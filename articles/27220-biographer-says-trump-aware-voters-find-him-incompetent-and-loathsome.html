Biographer says Trump aware voters find him 'incompetent and loathsome'

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 12, 20262 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that biographer Michael Wolff believes Donald Trump understood that voters increasingly regarded him as “incompetent and loathsome” after a broad political repudiation that extended into his own party. Speaking on the “Trump’s Brain” podcast, Wolff argued that Trump would answer his diminished position by intensifying his public persona rather than seeking compromise or legislative accomplishment.

Wolff predicted that Trump would spend his remaining two years in office pursuing attention and projecting strength while Democrats blocked his policies and investigated allegations of corruption and self-enrichment. He also said the next president could inherit what he called Trump’s “Iran mess.” These are Wolff’s judgments and predictions; the article does not establish Trump’s private awareness independently.

Power behind the performance

The central political fact is not Wolff’s amateur diagnosis of Trump’s mind. It is that the president still held executive power. Electoral rejection, congressional resistance and personal humiliation could constrain him, but none of them converted him into a mere entertainer.

Treating Trump principally as a wounded celebrity risks confusing temperament with authority. Cameras may have supplied the validation Wolff describes, but the presidency supplied the machinery: foreign-policy discretion, administrative control and the ability to impose costs that would outlast a news cycle.

Agency does not disappear into psychology

Wolff’s account explains Trump’s anticipated conduct through narcissistic injury: a man denied admiration escalates the performance. That may be an interpretation of his incentives, but it is not an alibi.

If a president chooses spectacle over governing, retaliation over compromise or self-promotion over public responsibility, those remain political choices. Calling the struggle “an ugly battle” inside his head makes the story vivid while shifting attention away from decisions, enablers and institutional consequences.

The enabling structure

A president cannot govern through persona alone. Party officials, executive appointees and political allies decide whether grievance becomes policy and whether institutional restraints hold. Wolff’s claim that repudiation had reached Trump’s own party therefore raises the more important question: what would that party do with its remaining power?

Private disgust and electoral anxiety are not resistance. Officials who continue facilitating a president they consider diminished are exercising agency, not merely surviving his personality.

The misdirection of humiliation

The article’s framing makes public rejection sound like the engine of the coming danger. But voters who repudiated a president are weaker than the officials still positioned to restrain, investigate or enable him. Blame belongs with the people who control institutions, not with an electorate cast as the trigger for presidential rage.

The fixation on whether Trump felt loved also shrinks politics into celebrity psychology. The consequential question is not whether he could remain relevant. It is what state power could still be used for while he tried.

The systemic error

Personal rule thrives when institutions treat governance as one man’s emotional weather. The spectacle becomes the explanation, the explanation becomes an excuse, and the officials converting impulse into state action disappear from view.

Wolff’s portrait points toward a broader failure: a political system can recognize incapacity, vanity and foreseeable damage yet continue granting their bearer enormous discretion. The scandal is not simply that a president might prefer attention to accomplishment. It is that so many powerful people can understand that preference and still help make it consequential.
