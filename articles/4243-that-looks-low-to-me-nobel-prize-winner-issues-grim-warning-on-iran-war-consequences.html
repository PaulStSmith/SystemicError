'That looks low to me': Nobel Prize winner issues grim warning on Iran war consequences

A Nobel Prize winner has warned there could be further spikes in the cost of oil, and it would be far worse than predicted.

Paul Krugman believes estimates from experts predicting how high the cost of oil will reach could be understating just how expensive it could get. Writing in his recent Substack post, the economist suggested there is a chance that oil prices could spike much higher than either experts or Donald Trump’s administration will admit.

Krugman claimed, “Given time to adjust, the world can conserve oil in many different ways. For example, gas mileage roughly doubled in the decades that followed the 70s oil shocks — and that was before hybrid and electric vehicles. In the long run, the world economy could make do without Persian Gulf oil, at minimal cost in terms of global GDP.

“But in the long run we are all dead. In the short run, the economic impact of a sustained loss of Gulf oil could be very ugly. In fact, it would have to get ugly to persuade the world to buy a lot less oil.”

“I’ve seen some alarmists warn that a long war in the Gulf could lead to oil at $150 a barrel. That looks low to me.” Though Krugman’s opinion may be a rude awakening for some, the economist believes change will only come if the price hike persuades people to give up driving.

He wrote, “Around 20 percent of the world’s normal flow of oil is bottled up inside the Strait of Hormuz — and as we’ve seen in the past day, even tankers and oil facilities inside the Strait are vulnerable to attack. If this blockade persists, it will be a much worse shock to world oil supplies than the 1973 embargo, the 1979 Iranian revolution, or the 2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine.”

“So what happens if the Strait remains closed for months? It’s a matter of supply and demand. If the quantity of oil supplied to world markets can’t rise — which, as far as we can tell, it won’t until the mullahs decide to let tankers through again — the price of oil will have to rise high enough to reduce the quantity demanded.”

“And how high would that price have to be? It would have to be high enough to persuade drivers to stop driving, trucks to stop trucking, airlines to stop flying.”

“In other words, the price of oil would have to rise enough to cause a global economic crisis even though the world is much less oil-dependent than it used to be.”

Our Analysis:

Analysis of Paul Krugman’s Warning on Oil Prices and the Role of Decision Makers

Identifying Institutional Power

In the discourse over rising oil prices and potential economic fallout, the institutional power lies with several key actors:

  • Governments controlling oil-rich regions, particularly those around the Persian Gulf, have direct influence over oil supply by virtue of their geopolitical and military decisions.

  • International organizations (like OPEC) that coordinate oil production levels among member states.

  • Major oil corporations that manage the extraction, distribution, and pricing of oil on the global market.

Decision Makers Impacting the Outcome

The immediate trigger for the scenario Paul Krugman discusses—the potential closure of the Strait of Hormuz—would be a decision made by the Iranian government or other entities capable of exerting military or political control over the region. However, the broader decision-making landscape includes:

  • Global leaders and international coalitions that could negotiate or apply pressure to keep such critical passages open.

  • Energy policy makers worldwide, whose strategies on oil dependency and alternative energy sources impact global readiness for oil supply shocks.

  • Consumers and industries that decide on their oil usage, affecting demand and thus pricing indirectly.

Misdirection of Responsibility?

The article, by focusing on Krugman’s analysis, does not explicitly misdirect responsibility but rather highlights a lack of immediate control by most actors in the face of geopolitical tensions. It indirectly suggests that entities like Donald Trump’s administration (at the time of the original concerns) or other current political leaders may underestimate the potential for crisis, implying a critique of preparedness and policy orientation.

However, it’s crucial to recognize that the ability of any single administration or policy body to unilaterally manage global oil prices or the strategic decisions of sovereign nations around the Persian Gulf is limited. The framing might suggest an overemphasis on the role Western governments can play in averting or mitigating these crises, without acknowledging the complex, multi-actor power dynamics at play.

Conclusion

The potential oil price spike scenario as discussed by Paul Krugman correctly identifies a systemic vulnerability in global energy markets to geopolitical disruptions. The real institutional power in preventing or exacerbating such a crisis is dispersed among international governments, organizations, and corporations, with significant influence also held by the strategic decisions of countries controlling oil chokepoints.

While the article might seem to imply that the underestimation of risks is a failure of specific administrations or policy bodies, the truth is that the scope for unilateral action by any single entity is limited in a tightly interconnected global system. The critique should not solely focus on specific political figures or policies but also address the broader need for international cooperation, diversification of energy sources, and long-term strategic planning to reduce global oil dependency.

In this complex scenario, misplaced blame or oversimplified narratives do not contribute to understanding or solving the underlying issues. Instead, a nuanced approach that recognizes the diverse actors and factors at play is crucial for navigating the challenges of global energy dependency and security.
