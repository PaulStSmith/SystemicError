Trump lawyers file appeal alleging 'abuse' by judge

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosAugust 19, 20263 min read

The State Trump Says He Runs, and the Law He Wants to Silence

A settlement with himself

The reporting here is straightforward enough: Trump’s lawyers want sanctions paused after Judge Kathleen Williams rejected their claim that the president had reached a valid “settlement” with the U.S. government over the IRS tax-return breach. Williams saw through the arrangement, sanctioned counsel, referred one lawyer to the Florida Bar, barred another from seeking admission in the Southern District of Florida for a year, and restricted discussion of the supposed settlement.

That is the surface. The political meaning is uglier: this was not a dispute between an injured private citizen and an overreaching state. It was a president trying to turn the machinery of the state into an instrument for his own grievance, then pretending the resulting conflict is proof of judicial misconduct.

Power was the point

Who had actual institutional power? Trump did. Not as a private plaintiff floating above government, but as the person who controls the executive branch and, by the article’s own account, was trying to claim a “settlement” with the same government he runs. That is the central fact the lawyers are trying to bleach out.

Once you start there, the rest falls into place. The issue is not whether a president can file suit in his personal capacity in some abstract sense. The issue is that Trump’s side allegedly tried to make the state negotiate against itself. Williams’ objection, as described here, is the obvious one: if the same political actor controls both sides, the “agreement” is not a settlement so much as a managed fiction.

The blame-shift machine

Trump’s lawyers are doing what this operation always does when caught in a contradiction: they rebrand accountability as persecution. The brief frames sanctions as an “extraordinary abuse,” a “sweeping, unconstitutional, and unlawful gag order,” and a threat to the public’s right to hear from the president. That is familiar misdirection. It turns discipline for misconduct into censorship, and a self-dealing maneuver into a constitutional drama.

Williams’ order, according to the article, did not arise from some mystical hostility to Trump. It arose because the court saw conduct that collapsed adversarial process into a one-sided arrangement. The lawyers now want the public to believe the injury is that a judge noticed. That is not a legal argument so much as a blame transfer.

The grievance economy

The proposed “anti-weaponization fund” is the kind of phrase that tells on itself. It converts political resentment into a pot of money, then invites anyone who claims to have been wronged by prior administrations to line up. The article says dozens of Jan. 6 attackers are already demanding millions, despite convictions or guilty pleas. That is not a side effect. It is the feature.

This is how authoritarian politics launders itself through bureaucracy. The most lawless actors are recast as victims. Criminality becomes entitlement. State power is repurposed not to repair harm, but to compensate the people who attacked democratic order in the first place. If that sounds like a parody of governance, that is because it is. The joke is on everyone forced to treat it as normal procedure.

The court saw the trap

The sharpest line in the source is not from Trump’s lawyers. It is the court’s conclusion that Todd Blanche’s apparent ability to speak for both plaintiffs and defendants showed there was only one party whose interests were represented throughout the case. That is the real scandal. The legal system is being asked to pretend that executive power and private grievance are separable when the political project is built on erasing that boundary.

IRS employees suing over the agreement’s “hidden legal trap” reinforces the point. The danger here is not merely a bad settlement clause. It is the use of institutional opacity to smuggle a patronage system into law, then demand deference because the paperwork exists.

What this reveals

This story is not about a judge being too aggressive. It is about a president and his legal apparatus trying to make personal power look like official process, then treating resistance to that fraud as abuse. That is how capture works: not by openly abolishing the rules, but by using the rules to conceal who they serve.

The deeper pattern is straightforward. Trump’s politics do not just test institutions; they seek to invert them, so that the state pays the people who threaten it, shields the officials who enable the scheme, and punishes anyone who insists on adversarial reality. That is not confusion. It is governance as self-exoneration.
