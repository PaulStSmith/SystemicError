Trump judge locks president into problematic December deposition

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 13, 20263 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that a federal judge kept Donald Trump on track for a December deposition in his $10 billion lawsuit against the BBC. District Judge Jeffrey Kuntz directed the parties to follow existing discovery deadlines and required any request for further changes or delay of Trump’s appearance to be filed by November 16.

Trump sued over a BBC documentary about the aftermath of the January 2021 Capitol attack, alleging defamation and violations of Florida’s deceptive-trade-practices law. Because Trump put an enormous damages claim at issue, the BBC argues that it may examine his wealth. U.S. Magistrate Judge Enjoliqué Lett had ordered him to answer questions about his family’s finances under oath.

Trump’s lawyers sought to stay that order after canceling an October 16 deposition. According to the BBC, an earlier July proposal also went unanswered until repeated follow-ups. Lett warned that continued noncompliance could result in dismissal of the lawsuit.

The plaintiff cannot claim immunity from his own case

Trump chose this litigation. He selected the defendant, asserted the injury and demanded $10 billion. That gives him agency—but it also gives the BBC ordinary discovery rights.

His presidency creates genuine logistical constraints, but it does not erase the central contradiction: Trump is invoking federal judicial power while resisting the evidentiary obligations that make civil litigation possible. A plaintiff cannot turn discovery into a one-way weapon, forcing a defendant to answer while treating his own testimony as optional.

The court holds the immediate power

Kuntz controls the schedule, Lett oversees discovery, and the judiciary can compel compliance or impose consequences. The BBC cannot make Trump testify by itself; it can request evidence, document delays and ask the court to enforce its rules.

That distinction matters because the story’s real institutional question is not whether BBC lawyers are “humiliating” Trump. It is whether judges will apply routine litigation requirements to a sitting president who voluntarily filed an extraordinary monetary claim. Personal embarrassment is spectacle. Judicial enforcement is power.

Delay is leverage

The reported pattern is concrete: an initial proposed deposition in July, delayed responses, an agreed October date, cancellation, and then an expedited effort to stay the December deposition and discovery deadline. Whatever Trump’s lawyers’ stated reasons, each delay consumes the BBC’s limited discovery period.

That gives procedural stalling a political function even without proving an improper motive. It raises the defendant’s costs, compresses its preparation and postpones the moment when the plaintiff must submit his claims to sworn questioning. Lett’s warning about possible dismissal identifies the only consequence capable of making a deadline more than a suggestion.

The wealth question is self-inflicted

The BBC did not introduce Trump’s finances as an unrelated fishing expedition, according to the account. Trump’s own $10 billion demand made the scale of his alleged damages relevant enough for the defense to seek financial testimony.

The article’s suggestion that his reluctance reveals fear of embarrassment goes beyond what the reported filings establish. The sharper, supportable point is simpler: Trump wants the court to recognize a vast injury while fighting disclosure that the defendant says it needs to test that claim. The conflict was created by the size and structure of his lawsuit, not by journalistic curiosity about his bank balance.

Litigation as asymmetric power

This case illustrates a recurring political pattern: powerful actors can use litigation not merely to win judgments, but to impose expense, uncertainty and delay on institutions that scrutinize them. The corrective is not faith in anyone’s restraint. It is a court willing to enforce reciprocal obligations.

If Trump complies, the BBC gets the sworn examination available to defendants under ordinary process. If he refuses, the court must decide whether deadlines and discovery orders carry real consequences. The systemic error would be allowing institutional status to convert a voluntary lawsuit into punishment for the defendant without accountability for the plaintiff.
