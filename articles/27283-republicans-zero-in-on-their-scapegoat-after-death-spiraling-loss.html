Republicans zero in on their scapegoat after death-spiraling loss

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 15, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A scapegoat chosen for ideological convenienceThune’s defense exposes the surrenderVoters were offered one governing project

Republicans have settled on Senate Majority Leader John Thune as the preferred scapegoat for what AlterNet describes as the party’s worst electoral defeat since Watergate. The charge is that Thune failed to push through the SAVE America Act, a Trump-backed voting bill. The more consequential failure, conservative writer Nick Catoggio argues, was the opposite: Thune and Senate Republicans spent two years surrendering their authority to Donald Trump, leaving voters no meaningful distinction between the president and the congressional candidates they rejected.

A scapegoat chosen for ideological convenience

Trump is the central figure in Republican politics, so holding him responsible would require his supporters to reconsider the political identity they built around him. Vice President J.D. Vance is both less visible and too important to the party’s succession plans. House Speaker Mike Johnson, meanwhile, has exercised so little independence that Catoggio compares blaming him to blaming a puppet instead of the person controlling it.

That leaves Thune: powerful enough to carry responsibility, but insufficiently identified with the Trumpist project to be protected from it. In a party largely purged of internal opposition, even limited ideological distance can make someone a convenient target.

The case now circulating against Thune centers on his failure to pass the SAVE America Act, which would have required millions of Americans to provide documentary proof of citizenship when registering or reregistering to vote. Catoggio notes that the resulting administrative burden might have fallen heavily on Trump voters as well. That practical complication matters less to the blame campaign than the bill’s symbolic value. It allows Republicans to portray an electoral defeat as the consequence of insufficiently aggressive voting restrictions rather than public rejection of the government they built.

Thune’s defense exposes the surrender

After the defeat, Thune went on Fox News and asked what Republicans could have done differently. Catoggio’s answer is extensive: Congress could have acted like a coequal branch of government.

According to Catoggio, Thune’s Senate permitted Trump to launch a major war without congressional authorization, accepted his erosion of legislative power, and confirmed nominees Catoggio considers manifestly unfit, including Pete Hegseth, Todd Blanche and Robert F. Kennedy Jr. He further argues that Senate Republicans tolerated Trump’s monetization of the presidency and his use of federal law enforcement for personal retaliation.

Those are Catoggio’s allegations and judgments, as relayed by AlterNet. But the institutional argument does not depend on his insults. Senate Republicans possessed confirmation power, legislative power and mechanisms for oversight. They made choices about how to use—or not use—those powers. Thune’s claim that he cannot imagine an alternative is less a defense than an admission that Republican leadership had stopped treating congressional independence as a responsibility.

The party did not merely fail to restrain Trump. On Catoggio’s account, its senators repeatedly enabled him, including through Cabinet confirmations. Power was available; compliance was chosen.

Voters were offered one governing project

That complicity created the electoral problem Republicans are now trying to assign to one man. By refusing to establish any governing identity separate from Trump’s, Republican members of Congress made themselves accountable for his presidency. Voters had no reason to treat House and Senate candidates as an independent check because those candidates had declined to behave like one.

In that limited sense, Catoggio does blame Thune for the loss—but not because he failed to impose a proof-of-citizenship requirement on voters. He blames Thune and Senate Republicans for making Trumpism the party’s only recognizable program, then acting surprised when the electorate judged the entire ticket accordingly.

The distinction is crucial. The scapegoating campaign says Thune failed to protect Republicans from voters. Catoggio’s argument is that he failed to protect Congress from the president. The first diagnosis points toward more barriers at the ballot box. The second points toward lawmakers who abandoned their own authority and now want someone else to absorb the consequences.

Thune is therefore both an opportunistic scapegoat and a legitimate participant in the failure. He did not single-handedly produce the defeat, and his colleagues cannot plausibly separate themselves from decisions they supported. But he led a Senate majority that, by Catoggio’s account, gave Trump nearly everything he wanted. Republicans wanted political unity without institutional accountability. The election made that bargain expensive, and their response has been to search for a dissenter weak enough to blame—not a decision they are willing to reconsider.
