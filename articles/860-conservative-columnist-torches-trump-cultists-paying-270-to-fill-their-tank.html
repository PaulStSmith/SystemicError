Conservative columnist torches Trump 'cultists' paying $270 to fill their tank

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosJune 15, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The ReceiptWho Actually Holds PowerThe Blame ShuffleCompetence as CoverThe Moral CollapseThe Pattern

Republicans Want Credit for Power Without Paying for It

The Receipt

The source column is built around a simple, ugly question: when Republicans defend Trump and his party by invoking “the policies,” what exactly are they defending? It points to a $270 diesel fill-up, then names the mess attached to Republican power: war in Iran, killings on the high seas, corruption, debt, inflation, and the promotion of unqualified loyalists like Bill Pulte. It ends by pairing Republicans with Ken Paxton and asking whether any of this feels smart.

Who Actually Holds Power

This is not a mystery story. The people with institutional power are Republicans in office and the Trump-aligned apparatus they keep elevating. They do not merely comment on events; they decide, appoint, authorize, and normalize them. If diesel is expensive, if the national ledger is getting heavier, if corruption is becoming ambient, the question is not whether voters are sufficiently enlightened. The question is who owns the machinery producing the outcome.

That ownership runs upward, not outward. It sits with party leaders, presidential authority, and the loyalists they install. The column’s central instinct is correct: stop pretending this is a collection of unfortunate weather systems.

The Blame Shuffle

What Republicans have perfected is not policy defense but accountability laundering. When the damage is visible, they retreat into “results.” When the results are ugly, they retreat into grievance about Democrats. When that fails, they retreat again into the claim that politics is about character and not outcomes, as if character were a decorative accessory instead of the operating system of power.

That is the misdirection. Deliberate decisions get described as confusion, and obvious failure gets rebranded as pragmatism. The point is not to explain the record. The point is to make the public accept the bill without asking who signed it.

Competence as Cover

Williamson’s column reaches for the old conservative trick of separating “policy” from personality, as though the two can be cleanly pried apart after years of corruption, incompetence, and loyalist promotion. But the source text also exposes the weakness of that dodge: the supposed policy case collapses the moment you name the actual consequences.

There is no clean policy defense for “the gross self-dealing and corruption,” no serious competence case for elevating “wildly unqualified yes-men,” and no honest appeal to results when the results include inflation, trade chaos, and war. At that point, “think of the policies” is just a polite way of telling the public to stop looking at the wreckage long enough to keep the grift moving.

The Moral Collapse

The most revealing part of the source is not the cost of diesel. It is the moral inversion. Republicans who claim to care about order, strength, and seriousness end up sponsoring the opposite: corruption dressed as governance, violence framed as necessity, and incompetence installed as loyalty. Their defenders then ask voters to judge by outcomes while refusing to name who caused them.

That is not confusion. It is political insulation. The party wants the authority of rule without the shame of responsibility. It wants credit for power and distance from consequence. That is why the column’s rhetorical question lands harder than its mockery suggests: the real insult is not that voters are supposed to feel smart. It is that Republicans keep assuming voters will be made to absorb the cost while the people in charge keep the keys.

The Pattern

This is the deeper pattern: modern reactionary politics does not merely lie about facts. It reorganizes blame. It concentrates power at the top, then disperses the damage outward to ordinary people, broken institutions, and whatever scapegoat is most convenient that week. The party in control turns dysfunction into identity, then asks for gratitude when the bill comes due.

That is the system the source article accidentally describes. Not a debate over moralism versus pragmatism, but a ruling project that treats public injury as branding and accountability as an inconvenience.
