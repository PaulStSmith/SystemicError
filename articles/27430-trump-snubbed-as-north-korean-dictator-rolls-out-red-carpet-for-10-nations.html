Trump snubbed as North Korean dictator rolls out red carpet for 10 nations

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 24, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A selective display of opennessTrump’s personal diplomacy lost its leverageKim turns protocol into a claim of authority

Kim Jong Un did not meet Donald Trump during the U.S. president’s latest trip through Asia. Days later, the North Korean leader personally welcomed ambassadors from 10 countries in Pyongyang, using a revived diplomatic ceremony to make the distinction unmistakable: North Korea is pursuing relationships it considers useful, and Washington is not currently among them.

The event was more than an elaborate snub. As reported by The Wall Street Journal from North Korean state media, it presented Kim as a leader choosing his diplomatic partners from a position strengthened by closer ties with China and Russia—not as an isolated ruler waiting for another summit with an American president.

A selective display of openness

Ambassadors from Russia, Sweden, Vietnam and seven other countries sat in bright red armchairs inside a red-carpeted hall as Kim accepted their credentials. He declared North Korea ready to expand “friendship and cooperation” with countries that respect its sovereignty and behave favorably toward it.

That language made diplomatic access explicitly conditional on Pyongyang’s security interests. Kim did not mention the United States, but the exclusion required little decoding. During a visit to Russia the previous week, senior North Korean official Jo Yong Won accused Washington of an “inveterate hostile policy” and said it was pushing the country’s security environment toward “unpredictable danger.”

Kim also attacked attempts to restrict the rights of sovereign states, a familiar North Korean description of U.S. pressure. The ceremony therefore paired an invitation with a warning: governments willing to engage on Pyongyang’s terms may receive an audience, while Washington’s demand for nuclear concessions no longer guarantees even a meeting.

Sweden’s inclusion gave the spectacle additional weight. The country has historically served as a channel between North Korea and the West and became the first Western nation to return diplomats in September 2024 after Pyongyang’s pandemic border closure drove out most Western embassies and international organizations. Even so, few permanent United Nations personnel have returned. This was not a general reopening so much as a controlled one, with Kim deciding who enters and under what conditions.

Trump’s personal diplomacy lost its leverage

Trump met Kim three times during his first term, turning negotiations over North Korea’s nuclear program into leader-to-leader theater. Those talks collapsed in 2019 without an agreement. Since then, according to the reporting, North Korea has expanded its military and nuclear arsenal and has said it will not resume negotiations unless it is recognized as a nuclear power.

That record undercuts the premise that Trump’s presence in Asia would itself draw Kim back to the table. A summit is valuable to Pyongyang only if it advances Pyongyang’s objectives. Kim has already received the prestige of meetings with a U.S. president; another photo opportunity offers less when Washington is not prepared to grant the status North Korea demands.

The balance around Kim has also changed. He appeared alongside Chinese President Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin at a military parade in Beijing last year. Former South Korean nuclear envoy Lee Do-hoon told the Journal that Chinese and Russian backing leaves Kim with little reason to hurry into another meeting with Trump.

Calling the episode merely a personal “snub” risks reproducing Trump’s preferred understanding of foreign affairs: powerful men exchanging attention, praise and access. The consequential development is not that Kim withheld a meeting from Trump. It is that the United States presently lacks sufficient leverage—or an acceptable offer—to make such a meeting worthwhile to him.

Kim turns protocol into a claim of authority

Sunday’s credential ceremony restored a practice no North Korean ruler had personally performed in decades. Kim Il Sung, North Korea’s founder and Kim Jong Un’s grandfather, routinely received ambassadors. After his death in 1994, that responsibility passed to the head of the legislature. A constitutional change in March returned the power to Kim Jong Un.

The revival consolidates symbolism as well as protocol. Kim was not simply hosting diplomats; he was publicly occupying a function associated with the regime’s founding patriarch and placing himself at the center of North Korea’s selective return to international contact.

State media supplied the imagery and the quotations, so the ceremony should be understood as a managed political presentation. Its intended message was nevertheless clear: North Korea can receive foreign representatives, deepen relationships and stage the rituals of state legitimacy without conceding anything to Washington.

Trump traveled through Asia hoping for another encounter. Kim instead assembled diplomats from 10 other countries and demonstrated that access to him is no longer a prize the American president can presume is available.
