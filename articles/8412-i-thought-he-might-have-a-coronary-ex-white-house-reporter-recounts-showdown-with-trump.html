'I thought he might have a coronary': Ex-White House reporter recounts showdown with Trump

By Paulo Santos

U.S. President Donald Trump speaks to the press as he arrives at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., July 13, 2025. REUTERS/Annabelle Gordon

A former White House reporter thought Donald Trump was going to have a coronary when the journalist didn’t listen to his demand to “shut up.”

Brian J. Karem, who famously asked Trump if he would commit to a peaceful transfer of power if he lost the 2020 election, over the weekend published an essay called “Trump still sounds like a child,” in which the reporter recalls “the time he told me to shut up or he’d leave; I don’t shut up and he didn’t leave.”

Karem used the recent development of Trump calling a reporter “piggy” to make his point that Trump was terrible to all reporters, women and men alike.

“He usually talks down to women, dismisses them and belittles them. Sometimes he admires their physical attributes in such a fashion as to make the rest of us in the room uncomfortable,” he wrote. “But it’s not limited to women. Donald Trump is worse than a misogynist. He’s obviously a racist, but that’s not the whole story. He’s a misanthrope. He absolutely loathes everyone - unless there’s something in it for him. He believes that makes him a renaissance man. I’ve heard him say that he’s an ‘equal opportunity offender.’ But, big deal. So is every other Medieval man.”

Karem says Trump didn’t like him very much at all.

“That being said, I guess I’ve never been able to do much for him, though during his first administration I was asked by a staffer where the ‘Playboy After Party’ was following the White House Correspondents Dinner. While we were put on alert, Trump never had the temerity to show up for the party,” he wrote. “Usually Trump yelled at me, or told me to sit down. Still he’d call on me and I’d ask him questions that would usually anger him to the point that I thought he might have a coronary. His face occasionally got [red]. he stammered. He pointed his finger. He never called me ‘Piggy’ but once he told me if I didn’t shut up, he’d leave his own press conference. I didn’t shut up. He didn’t leave.”

Karem then linked to a prior telling of the story, in which he wrote, “I have asked questions of every president since Ronald Reagan. I have been told to be quiet on more than one occasion. I’ve been told I shouldn’t be allowed into a press briefing. But before Tuesday I had never made anyone threaten to leave his own news briefing. Donald Trump did that.”

Our Analysis:

Trump’s Tumultuous Relationship with the Press: A Closer Look

Brian J. Karem’s recent essay, provocatively titled “Trump still sounds like a child,” serves as a stark reminder of the fraught relationship between former President Donald Trump and the White House press corps. Karem, known for his confrontational exchange with Trump over the peaceful transfer of power post-2020 election, uses his personal encounters to paint a broader picture of Trump’s demeanor towards reporters. But is there more to this narrative than just personal anecdotes? Let’s dive deeper.

Trump’s Disdain for Dissent

Karem’s recounting of his interactions with Trump, including an instance where Trump threatened to leave his own press conference if Karem didn’t “shut up,” underscores a critical point: Trump’s aversion to being challenged. It’s a theme as old as his presidency, yet Karem’s perspective adds a personal layer to the ongoing narrative of Trump’s contentious exchanges with the press. The use of the term “child” to describe Trump’s reactions isn’t just a throwaway insult; it’s a pointed critique of Trump’s inability to handle dissent or criticism, a trait alarmingly unbecoming for a leader of the free world.

Misogyny, Racism, and Misanthropy

Karem doesn’t stop at criticizing Trump’s handling of press interactions; he delves into the more sinister aspects of Trump’s personality. Labeling Trump as “worse than a misogynist” and a “racist,” Karem argues that Trump’s behavior goes beyond gender discrimination to encompass a broader disdain for humanity itself, except where personal gain is involved. This accusation is severe, yet it is backed by a litany of public records and Trump’s own statements. To dismiss Karem’s claims as mere hyperbole would be to ignore the substantial evidence that supports them.

The ‘Equal Opportunity Offender’

Trump has often branded himself as an “equal opportunity offender,” a moniker that Karem rightfully critiques. This self-description, intended to deflect criticism of Trump’s behavior, is emblematic of a deeper issue: the normalization of disrespect under the guise of fairness. By claiming to be indiscriminate in his offensive behavior, Trump attempts to sidestep accountability for specific instances of sexism, racism, and general rudeness. However, as Karem notes, being universally offensive is not a virtue; it’s a sign of a deeply flawed character.

A Renaissance Man?

Karem’s skepticism towards Trump’s self-proclaimed status as a “renaissance man” is well-founded. The Renaissance, a period marked by an explosion of art, culture, and intellectualism, seems an odd reference point for a president known for his disdain for expertise and the arts. This self-description by Trump is not just delusional; it’s a stark misrepresentation of what it means to be a learned or cultured individual.

Conclusion: The Real Story

Karem’s essay, while personal, sheds light on a larger truth about Donald Trump’s presidency: his fundamentally adversarial relationship with the press and, by extension, the truth. Trump’s interactions with Karem are symptomatic of his broader conduct in office—marked by hostility, evasion, and an often vitriolic disdain for accountability.

While some may argue that Karem’s portrayal is biased, the sheer volume of similar accounts from other reporters makes it difficult to dismiss his observations as mere personal vendettas. The pattern is clear, and the implications are troubling. A president who cannot tolerate scrutiny, who lashes out at dissent, and who views the press as an enemy is not just a problem for reporters; it’s a challenge to the very principles of democracy.

In the end, Karem’s essay is more than just a recounting of personal skirmishes; it’s a critical examination of a presidency that, time and again, showed itself to be at odds with the fundamental values of transparency, respect, and accountability. And that, readers, is the real story.
