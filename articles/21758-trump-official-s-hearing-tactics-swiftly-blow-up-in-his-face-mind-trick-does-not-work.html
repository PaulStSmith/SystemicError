Trump official's hearing tactics swiftly blow up in his face: 'Mind trick does not work'

Based on an article by David Edwards

Paulo SantosAugust 18, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Russell Vought’s Strategy Is Simple: Break the State, Then Lie About It

Russell Vought showed up before the Senate Banking Committee as the Trump-aligned official trying to hollow out the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, and Sen. Angela Alsobrooks came armed with his own words and his own record. He tried to recast the assault on federal workers and the CFPB as necessary discipline and partisan inconvenience. That framing is the point of the exercise. It is supposed to make deliberate institutional sabotage sound like administrative weather.

Who Actually Has the Keys

The source is clearer than the hearing itself: Republicans hold the levers. Vought is not some detached commentator on government dysfunction. He is the director of the Office of Management and Budget and the operative trying to gut the CFPB from inside the executive branch. The committee chair, Tim Scott, ended the exchange by cutting Alsobrooks off before Vought had to answer the substance of her question. That is not oversight. That is controlled damage.

Alsobrooks said the quiet part plainly: Republicans are in charge. That matters because Vought’s whole defense depends on erasing the governing majority and replacing it with a fog of procedural blame. The people with authority over staffing, enforcement, and agency direction are not Democrats. They are the ones trying to shrink the agency while pretending the wreckage is accidental.

The Misdirection Is the Message

Vought’s move was classic authoritarian bookkeeping: attack the institution, then describe resistance to the attack as the real cruelty. He told Alsobrooks she should blame Democrats for shutdowns, as if that somehow answers questions about his push for mass firings and the targeting of civil servants. It doesn’t. It is a dodge designed to move the argument away from what he chose to do.

That dodge matters because it converts accountability into theater. The injured party is told to discuss tone, process, and partisan etiquette while the actual power holder keeps dismantling the machinery of government. Vought even tried to split the difference between “bureaucrats” and the career civil servants doing the work. That distinction is not a defense. It is a laundering operation. If the bureaucracy is “weaponized,” then the answer is not mass fear campaigns against workers who process the public’s claims and enforce the law.

The Cruelty Was Not Hidden

Alsobrooks read back Vought’s own language from private speeches: he wanted bureaucrats “traumatically affected,” he wanted them to dread coming to work, he wanted them cast as villains. That is not reform language. That is intimidation language. It is an admission that the objective is not efficiency but demoralization.

He then tried to retreat into the familiar bureaucratic varnish: he was talking about bureaucracies, not career workers. But the source text already shows the effect on actual people, including federal workers lined up in the Capitol in tears. When a political project knowingly targets workers’ fear and livelihood, the cruelty is not incidental to the policy. It is the policy.

Alsobrooks was right to call it evil and depraved. Those are strong words, but the underlying conduct earns them. A government official using state power to terrorize public employees is not trimming waste. It is punishing the people who keep institutions functioning so that those institutions can be weakened without public resistance.

The CFPB Is the Target for a Reason

The CFPB has returned $21 billion to consumers since its creation. That is exactly why Vought wants it smaller, weaker, and easier to ignore. An agency that can force accountability onto predatory finance is intolerable to the people who see public regulation as an obstacle to private extraction.

The source says federal courts have repeatedly blocked Vought’s campaign to slash the enforcement division and that he has been trying to cut it to one-third of its staff. That is not a good-faith policy disagreement. It is a legal and political effort to make consumer protection optional. If the agency cannot enforce the law, then the law becomes a press release.

And notice the contempt built into the process. Alsobrooks and colleagues sent a letter in April asking for answers. Three months later, no response. That silence is part of the governing style: demand obedience from the public, ignore the legislature, then claim procedural innocence when questioned.

The Committee Helped Him

The hearing did not merely fail to constrain Vought. It gave him shelter. Alsobrooks pressed the point, asked how the CFPB could do its legal job with one-third of its enforcement staff, and then the chair shut the witness down from answering. That is a useful summary of how institutional decline works. The people doing the damage are allowed to speak in slogans. The people documenting the damage are denied the floor.

This is why pretend neutrality is so corrosive. The Senate was not watching a balanced exchange between equal claims. It was witnessing a Democratic senator trying to force an explanation from a Republican power broker who is actively reducing the capacity of government, while the committee structure itself protected the power broker from accountability.

The Pattern Is Sabotage, Then Blame-Shifting

The larger pattern is simple: weaken public institutions, terrorize the workers inside them, then blame Democrats for the instability that follows. That pattern runs through Vought’s conduct, his rhetoric, and his reliance on procedural control to avoid direct answers. It is not confusion. It is governance by damage.

Alsobrooks’ intervention mattered because she refused the script. She did not treat the assault on federal workers as an abstract management dispute, and she did not let Vought hide behind partisan noise. That is what democratic opposition has to look like in practice: naming power, naming the harm, and refusing to pretend that the people dismantling institutions are mere critics of them.

The real story here is not that one senator was sharper than a bureaucrat in a hearing. It is that an organized faction inside the Republican-controlled state is openly working to make government cruel enough that it can later be declared broken. That is how institutional collapse is sold in real time.
