When America Sneezes, the World Gets a Fever

By Paulo Santos

The United States is discovering that debt has an interest rate. Unfortunately, the rest of us may get the bill too.

It started, as these things often do, with a financial story that sounds terribly boring until you realize what the numbers actually mean.

The yield on the 10-year U.S. Treasury crossed 5 percent.

☕ Buy us a coffee

Normally, that sentence belongs somewhere between crop reports and quarterly earnings in the category of news capable of anesthetizing an entire room.

This time, pay attention.

The Congressional Budget Office had expected substantially lower long-term borrowing costs. Instead, Treasury yields have climbed above assumptions embedded in Washington’s fiscal projections. Meanwhile, gross federal debt has now crossed $40 trillion. As of September 3, 2026, it stood at roughly $40.1 trillion, including about $32.4 trillion held by the public.

And this is happening before anything resembling a sovereign-debt crisis.

The United States already runs enormous structural deficits. CBO projects debt held by the public rising substantially relative to GDP over the coming decade, while interest expense consumes an ever-larger share of federal revenue.

That is where the arithmetic becomes unpleasant.

The United States does not borrow money once and lock in the rate forever. Treasury securities continually mature and must be refinanced.

A bond issued years ago at 2 percent matures.

It gets replaced by debt yielding 5 percent.

Then another.

Then another.

Eventually, higher interest payments themselves require additional borrowing.

Which creates additional debt.

Which requires additional interest.

Which requires additional borrowing.

There is a technical term for this arrangement:

Bad.

I’ve Seen This Movie Before

For Brazilians of a certain age, discussions about deficits, rising interest costs, inflation expectations and monetary credibility produce a particular sensation.

We have seen a version of this movie.

No, the United States in 2026 is not Brazil in 1989.

The differences are enormous.

America borrows primarily in a currency it creates. The dollar remains the dominant international reserve and financing currency. Treasury markets are extraordinarily deep. The Federal Reserve retains enormous institutional and monetary power.

Those distinctions matter.

They are also not magic spells.

Brazil did not wake up one Tuesday morning with four-digit inflation.

The disaster accumulated.

Fiscal imbalances became harder to finance. Inflation became expected. Contracts adapted. Wages adapted. Prices adapted. Financial behavior adapted.

Eventually inflation itself became embedded in the machinery of the economy.

Anyone who lived through that period remembers something economists sometimes fail to communicate adequately.

Inflation at that level is not simply “prices rising quickly.”

It changes behavior.

You spend money immediately because tomorrow it will buy less.

Businesses constantly reprice goods.

Workers demand compensation for inflation that has not happened yet.

Suppliers shorten payment terms.

Lenders protect themselves.

Consumers protect themselves.

Everyone acts rationally according to their own circumstances.

Collectively, those rational defensive actions help perpetuate the disease.

That is why the American situation deserves attention long before anybody starts throwing around the word “hyperinflation.”

The danger isn’t that America suddenly wakes up with 1,000 percent inflation.

The danger is that fiscal deterioration gradually corners monetary policy.

The Trap Has a Name

Economists call one version of this problem fiscal dominance.

Normally, a central bank raises interest rates when inflation becomes excessive.

Simple enough.

Except imagine the government already owes an enormous amount of money.

Higher interest rates make servicing that debt vastly more expensive.

Those larger interest payments increase deficits.

Those deficits require additional borrowing.

Additional borrowing expands the debt.

Markets may then demand still higher yields.

Now the central bank faces an increasingly unpleasant choice.

Keep interest rates high enough to suppress inflation and exacerbate the government’s financing problem.

Or keep rates lower than price stability requires and risk allowing inflation expectations to become embedded.

That is where a fiscal problem can become a monetary problem.

And once inflation expectations become unanchored, putting them back into the bottle becomes considerably more expensive than keeping them there in the first place.

We Have Another Movie on Repeat

There is another piece of American history worth putting beside the Brazilian comparison.

Not because presidents control the economy like somebody turning knobs on a machine. They do not. Congress matters. The Federal Reserve matters. International shocks matter. Business cycles matter. Pandemics certainly matter.

But fiscal policy matters too.

And the sequence of the last quarter-century is revealing.

Clinton: surplus, not zero debt

Bill Clinton did not leave the United States debt-free.

That claim would be wrong.

What he did leave was a federal government running budget surpluses and a dramatically improved debt position.

From 1998 through 2001, the federal government recorded four consecutive surpluses. Debt held by the public fell by more than $450 billion and reached about 32 percent of GDP, its lowest level since the early 1980s.

That distinction matters.

America still owed trillions.

But relative to the economy, federal finances had become considerably more stable.

Then the direction changed.

Bush: tax cuts, wars and deficits

The George W. Bush administration enacted major tax cuts in 2001 and 2003 while dramatically increasing military expenditures after September 11.

By 2008, Congress had provided or was considering roughly $750 billion in cumulative appropriations for operations in Iraq, Afghanistan and related activities since 2001.

Those wars were not accompanied by equivalent tax increases.

Quite the opposite.

And therefore the United States did something rather extraordinary:

It cut taxes while fighting two wars.

Credit cards are marvelous things.

CBO later noted that extending the 2001 and 2003 tax cuts would substantially worsen long-term debt projections.

Then came the financial crisis.

Here precision matters again: it would be simplistic to say George W. Bush personally “caused” the Great Recession. The housing bubble, financial deregulation, mortgage securitization, leverage, monetary conditions and regulatory failures accumulated over years and across institutions.

But Bush left office with the economy collapsing around him.

Real GDP contracted 5.1 percent cumulatively between late 2007 and mid-2009, the deepest recession in the postwar quarterly GDP record at the time.

By fiscal year 2009, the federal deficit reached roughly $1.4 trillion, or 9.9 percent of GDP. Part of that reflected the recession itself; part reflected emergency measures; and part reflected fiscal imbalances that predated the crash. CBO explicitly identified all three.

That was the economy Barack Obama inherited.

Obama: recovery without a return to surplus

Obama did not restore Clinton-era surpluses.

Again, the numbers matter more than mythology.

The federal deficit remained substantial throughout his presidency.

But it fell dramatically from the crisis levels he inherited.

CBO recorded six consecutive years of declining deficits after 2009 before the trend reversed. By 2015, the deficit had fallen to about 2.5 percent of GDP; in 2016 it was roughly 3.2 percent.

Meanwhile, the economy emerged from the Great Recession and unemployment declined dramatically.

So the accurate description is not:

“Obama balanced the budget.”

He didn’t.

It is:

Obama inherited the worst economic contraction since the Great Depression, presided over a long recovery, and left behind an economy no longer in emergency conditions.

Then Donald Trump arrived promising, among other things, fiscal discipline.

That did not happen.

Trump I: deficits were rising before COVID

This point matters because COVID is often used as though it explains everything that happened to the federal balance sheet during Trump’s first term.

It does not.

Before COVID existed, the deficit was already climbing.

The federal deficit rose from 3.5 percent of GDP in 2017 to 4.6 percent in 2019, reaching $984 billion during an economy that was not in recession.

That is unusual fiscal behavior.

Large deficits during recessions are expected: tax revenues collapse, unemployment benefits increase, and governments deliberately spend to stabilize demand.

Running increasingly large deficits during an expansion gives the government less room when the emergency eventually arrives.

And Trump’s 2017 tax law contributed materially to that deterioration.

CBO and the Joint Committee on Taxation estimated that the legislation would increase deficits by roughly $1.5 trillion over ten years before macroeconomic feedback, with additional borrowing generating still more debt-service costs.

Then COVID arrived.

The 2020 deficit exploded to $3.1 trillion — 14.9 percent of GDP, the largest share since World War II. A substantial portion resulted from necessary emergency legislation enacted to prevent an economic collapse during the pandemic. CBO estimated pandemic-related legislation itself added approximately $2.3 trillion to that year’s deficit.

So COVID deserves an enormous asterisk.

But it cannot erase what happened during 2017–2019.

The deficit was already deteriorating before anybody had heard the words “social distancing.”

Biden: recovery, followed by an inflation shock

Joe Biden entered office while the pandemic was still disrupting the economy.

The unemployment rate was 6.4 percent in January 2021. By January 2025, it was 4.0 percent.

The recovery was accompanied by a major inflation episode.

That fact should not be sanitized.

U.S. consumer inflation reached 9.1 percent in June 2022, driven by a mixture of pandemic disruptions, enormous fiscal stimulus enacted under both Trump and Biden, supply constraints, energy shocks, housing pressures and rapidly recovering demand.

By December 2024, however, annual CPI inflation had fallen to 2.9 percent.

It would be inaccurate to say the United States simply had “the lowest inflation in the industrialized world” throughout Biden’s presidency. Different countries led or lagged at different moments, and by 2026 U.S. inflation was actually running above several other G7 economies.

But the broader record is clear enough:

Biden inherited a pandemic-disrupted labor market, presided over a substantial employment recovery, endured a severe inflation surge, and left office after inflation had fallen dramatically from its peak.

Then Trump returned.

And now the debt has crossed $40 trillion.

CBO estimates that legislation enacted during Trump’s second administration in 2025 will add roughly $4.1 trillion to cumulative deficits between 2025 and 2034 once additional debt-service costs are included.

That does not mean Donald Trump personally created all $40 trillion.

He obviously did not.

The current debt is the accumulated product of decades of decisions by presidents and Congresses of both parties, entitlement commitments, wars, recessions, tax policy, demographic change, financial crises, pandemic spending and compound interest.

But pretending that every administration contributed identically would be just as dishonest as assigning the entire debt to one president.

The ledger exists.

Read it.

The Most Fascinating Economic Indicator May Be Partisanship

And then we reach the part that should make anyone worried about democratic economic policymaking slightly nauseous.

People do not perceive economic conditions purely through bank statements and unemployment statistics.

They perceive them politically.

Pew has documented this repeatedly.

In September 2024, during the Biden administration, 41 percent of Democrats rated economic conditions positively, compared with only 10 percent of Republicans.

Then the presidency changed parties.

By February 2025—barely weeks after Trump returned to office—30 percent of Democrats rated the economy positively, versus 18 percent of Republicans.

By April, Republicans’ positive assessments had doubled to 36 percent, while Democrats had fallen to 11 percent.

The factories had not magically relocated overnight.

The housing stock had not been rebuilt.

The national debt had not vanished.

Millions of workers had not simultaneously changed jobs.

The president changed.

And perceptions moved with him.

By July 2026, Pew found the partisan divide still enormous: 41 percent of Republicans rated the economy excellent or good, versus 10 percent of Democrats.

That is an extraordinary demonstration of how political identity filters economic reality.

It also helps explain how economic reputations survive contradictory evidence.

Campaigns do not need to persuade voters to conduct longitudinal analyses of debt-to-GDP ratios, cyclically adjusted deficits, productivity growth and labor-force participation.

They need a story.

“Things were better when we were in charge.”

“The other guys ruined everything.”

“We inherited a disaster.”

“We created the greatest economy ever.”

Repeat it often enough and statistical memory starts losing to narrative memory.

No conspiracy is required.

Partisan media ecosystems, selective reporting, motivated reasoning and ordinary human tribalism can accomplish quite enough by themselves.

The polling demonstrates the result.

And Delayed Consequences Make It Worse

Fiscal policy contains a particularly cruel political defect.

The person who signs the bill does not necessarily receive the bill.

Tax cuts can increase borrowing today.

Debt accumulates gradually.

Interest rates rise years later.

Treasury securities refinance later still.

Debt service consumes increasing amounts of the budget.

Another administration may then inherit the consequences.

That government may have to raise taxes.

Cut spending.

Accept higher interest rates.

Endure slower growth.

Possibly tolerate a recession to control inflation.

And voters experience the pain during the treatment, not necessarily during the behavior that made the treatment necessary.

That creates an obvious democratic hazard.

Administration A loosens fiscal policy.

Administration B inherits the balance sheet.

Administration B administers unpleasant medicine.

Voters punish Administration B.

Administration A’s political heirs return promising relief.

The cycle begins again.

Goldfish everywhere are hereby absolved from comparison.

Except This Isn’t Just America’s Invoice

And this is where the Brazil analogy stops working.

Brazil could destroy the cruzeiro.

Brazil could create the cruzado.

Brazil could destroy that too.

We could create the cruzado novo, bring back the cruzeiro, invent the cruzeiro real, finally introduce the real, and subject ourselves to an economic naming convention that increasingly resembled somebody desperately clicking “Save As.”

The rest of the world would notice.

It would not collapse because of us.

The United States does not enjoy that luxury.

The dollar isn’t merely America’s currency.

It is infrastructure.

U.S. Treasuries aren’t merely bonds issued by one government among many.

They function as collateral throughout the global financial system.

Their yields establish benchmarks against which countless other assets are priced.

Central banks hold them as reserves.

Banks hold them as safe assets.

Pension funds hold them.

Insurance companies hold them.

Governments hold them.

Companies borrow relative to them.

Mortgages respond to them.

Capital moves around the planet in response to them.

That changes the entire nature of an American fiscal crisis.

If Brazil develops pneumonia, Brazil goes to the hospital.

If the United States develops pneumonia, somebody starts asking whether the hospital’s electrical system is connected to its lungs.

The Global Transmission Belt

Imagine persistent American inflation forces the Federal Reserve to raise rates aggressively.

Treasury yields climb.

Capital flows toward dollar assets offering those higher returns.

Currencies elsewhere weaken against the dollar.

Dollar-priced commodities become more expensive in local currencies.

Countries begin importing inflation they did not create.

Their central banks raise their own rates.

Borrowing becomes more expensive.

Investment slows.

Debt service rises.

Emerging markets experience capital pressure precisely when they most need financing.

Nobody in Brazil needs to have done anything particularly stupid for Brazil to feel the consequences.

Nobody in South Africa does.

Nobody in Indonesia does.

Nobody in Europe does.

That is the privilege—and danger—of operating the currency sitting at the center of the international financial system.

What Happens If the Safe Asset Stops Feeling Quite So Safe?

There is an even darker possibility.

Suppose investors don’t merely believe American inflation will remain elevated.

Suppose they begin believing that the United States will eventually solve part of its debt problem through inflation.

That changes the calculation completely.

A Treasury bond promises dollars in the future.

If investors begin expecting those future dollars to have significantly less purchasing power, they demand compensation.

Higher yields.

Those higher yields increase federal interest expense.

Which increases deficits.

Which increases borrowing.

Which reinforces concerns about future inflation.

There is our loop again.

And suddenly the question becomes much bigger than whether the U.S. government can technically repay its bonds.

Of course it can create dollars.

The more dangerous question is:

What will those dollars be worth when it does?

Then comes the question nobody particularly wants to contemplate:

If Treasuries cease being treated as the world’s unquestioned risk-free asset, what replaces them?

There is currently no obvious substitute possessing the Treasury market’s combination of size, liquidity and institutional integration.

That protects the United States.

It also makes everybody else dependent upon American fiscal competence.

Which Brings Us to Elections

Years ago, somebody made a joke that has circulated in various forms:

“American presidential elections are too important to be left to the Americans.”

The original attribution appears lost.

But whoever coined it understood something fundamental.

American elections are domestic in franchise.

They are not domestic in consequence.

American voters choose governments whose decisions affect sanctions, wars, alliances, trade restrictions, climate policy, financial regulation, monetary expectations and the borrowing costs faced by governments thousands of kilometers away.

That doesn’t mean foreigners should vote in American elections.

It means the rest of the world has every reason to watch them very, very carefully.

Because when Americans vote on economic policy, they are not making decisions whose effects stop politely at customs.

The dollar crosses borders.

Treasury yields cross borders.

Inflation crosses borders.

Capital flows cross borders.

Financial crises most certainly cross borders.

The Most Dangerous Phrase in Economics

Whenever somebody points out that the United States is exhibiting behavior associated with fiscal crises elsewhere, someone inevitably responds:

“Yes, but America is different.”

True.

America is different.

The dollar is different.

The Treasury market is different.

The American economy is different.

The Federal Reserve is different.

Those differences give the United States vastly more room for error than Brazil ever possessed.

They do not provide infinite room.

Reserve-currency status is not a law of physics.

Credibility is not hereditary.

Institutions do not remain trustworthy because they were trustworthy yesterday.

Markets do not provide favorable financing conditions out of patriotism.

And arithmetic has never once cared about American exceptionalism.

The frightening thing about the Treasury-yield story isn’t that the United States is about to become 1990 Brazil.

It isn’t.

The frightening thing is that the assumptions supporting America’s enormous debt burden are becoming less comfortable while that burden continues to grow.

And now the gross federal debt has crossed $40 trillion.

Not because one party created every dollar.

Not because one president owns every mistake.

But because America has spent decades discovering an apparently irresistible political formula:

Promise benefits.

Cut taxes.

Borrow the difference.

Let somebody else explain the invoice.

That formula works beautifully.

Until interest rates decide it doesn’t.

We Don’t Get to Be Spectators

For the rest of us, there is a final unpleasant realization.

America’s fiscal politics belong to Americans.

The consequences don’t.

A Brazilian government that makes catastrophic economic choices can devastate Brazil.

An American government that badly damages confidence in the dollar and Treasury market can destabilize the plumbing of global finance.

That is the difference.

The world doesn’t watch American elections merely because American politics has become some grotesque combination of reality television, courtroom drama and constitutional stress test.

We watch because we have money in this casino too.

Our currencies react.

Our central banks react.

Our pensions react.

Our borrowing costs react.

Our governments react.

Our economies react.

We simply don’t get a ballot.

Which brings us back to that wonderfully cynical line:

American elections are too important to leave just in American hands.

Not because the rest of the world should choose America’s government.

But because Americans are choosing something larger than they sometimes seem to realize.

They are choosing the people who will operate one of the central pieces of machinery holding the global economy together.

And machinery this important should not be operated on the assumption that somebody else will repair it after it breaks.

Because when America sneezes, the world gets a fever.

If America ever develops something worse, the rest of the world will discover just how interconnected the patient ward really is.
