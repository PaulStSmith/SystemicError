Georgetown University law professor Stephen Vladeck sounded the alarm this weekend over the Supreme Court and its increasing use of what legal scholars have dubbed the “shadow docket,” a process by which the court institutes rulings unsigned, and without oral arguments, explanations or written opinions.

The Supreme Court has utilized the shadow docket well over a dozen times since President Donald Trump retook the White House in January, at least 14 of which were in cases that tested the president’s power, with the court siding with Trump in the vast majority of its rulings.

Supreme Court Justice Brett Kavanaugh has defended the court’s use of the shadow docket, but instead has endorsed the term “interim docket,” citing the temporary nature of rulings decided using the tactic. Vladeck, citing the “massive and permanent effects” such rulings could have on the nation going forward, clapped back at Kavanaugh’s position.

“When you’re going to have rulings producing these massive and permanent effects, it seems kind of disingenuous to label them as interim,” Vladeck said, speaking with the Wall Street Journal in its report published Saturday night.

The New York Times spoke with 65 federal judges in an October report and found that 47 of them had argued the Supreme Court made inappropriate use of the shadow docket since Trump returned to office, with some describing the court’s use of the shadow docket as “a slap in the face to district courts,” and “incredibly demoralizing and troubling.”

The Supreme Court has been a key player in Trump’s consolidation of power within the executive branch, with the court ruling in Trump’s favor onexpanding presidential presumptive immunity, andprohibitinglower courts from instituting nationwide injunctions on Trump’s agenda.

Our Analysis:

The Shadow Docket: A Veil of Secrecy or a Necessary Evil?

In the labyrinth of American jurisprudence, the Supreme Court stands as a colossus, ostensibly above the political fray, yet its actions often suggest otherwise. The use of the so-called “shadow docket” has surged into the spotlight, thanks to Georgetown University law professor Stephen Vladeck. This mechanism, by which the court issues rulings without signature, oral argument, or explanation, has been wielded with increasing frequency and, alarmingly, to consolidate power within the executive branch under President Donald Trump.

The Shadow Docket Unveiled

The term “shadow docket” conjures images of a clandestine operation, far removed from the public’s prying eyes. Indeed, its use has expanded significantly since Trump’s inauguration, with the Supreme Court deploying it over a dozen times in cases that directly empower the presidency. The outcomes of these cases are not trivial; they have far-reaching implications, touching on everything from presidential immunity to the enforcement of nationwide policies.

Justice Brett Kavanaugh, in a feeble attempt at rebranding, proposes the term “interim docket”. This euphemism fails to mask the reality of the situation. As Vladeck astutely points out, the effects of these rulings are anything but interim—they are monumental and irreversible. The distinction Kavanaugh attempts to draw is not only disingenuous; it is downright deceptive.

A Chorus of Discontent

The discontent with the shadow docket is not confined to academic circles. A New York Times inquiry reveals that a substantial majority of federal judges believe the Supreme Court’s reliance on this mechanism is inappropriate. Descriptions of it as “a slap in the face to district courts” and “incredibly demoralizing and troubling” underscore the depth of the judiciary’s unease. This is not mere professional jealousy at play. These criticisms highlight a fundamental concern about the erosion of transparent judicial processes and the undermining of lower courts.

The Supreme Court as Trump’s Enabler

What emerges from the shadows is a picture of the Supreme Court as an active participant in Trump’s consolidation of power. The decisions made under the guise of the shadow docket have not only expanded presidential immunity but also insulated Trump’s agenda from judicial scrutiny. This is not the hallmark of a balanced system of checks and balances; it is the modus operandi of a court that has strayed from its constitutional moorings.

Conclusion

The Supreme Court’s increasing reliance on the shadow docket raises profound questions about the state of American democracy. Far from being a benign procedural tool, it has become a means of enacting significant policy changes without the usual scrutiny or accountability. The defense of such practices by justices like Kavanaugh only adds insult to injury, suggesting a court more concerned with expedience than with justice.

The shadow docket, then, is not just a quirk of judicial procedure. It is symptomatic of a deeper malaise—a court comfortable operating in the shadows, far removed from the democratic ideals it is supposed to uphold. The time has come for a reckoning, for sunlight is said to be the best of disinfectants. And the shadow docket is in dire need of illumination.
