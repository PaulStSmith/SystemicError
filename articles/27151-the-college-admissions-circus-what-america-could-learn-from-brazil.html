The College Admissions Circus: What America Could Learn From Brazil

The Great American Disappearing Student

American universities have discovered a terrifying new phenomenon: students who say they'll attend college sometimes change their minds.

Apparently, this requires a name, a strategy, and possibly an entire administrative department.

In a November 2026 Wall Street Journal article, “Colleges Struggle to Stop ‘Melt’: Students Who Commit, Then Don't Show Up”, reporter Roshan Fernandez describes a growing headache for American institutions.

Students accept admission offers, pay deposits, and then fail to appear when classes begin.

The colleges call this summer melt.

At Whittier College in California, 17% of students who had committed failed to show up. Some institutions are experiencing rates approaching 20%.

Meanwhile, students entering college in 2025 placed an average of 1.4 enrollment deposits, compared with approximately one in 2019.

Universities are responding with what they call freeze strategies: increasingly elaborate efforts to keep students engaged, committed, and emotionally attached to their chosen institution throughout the summer.

Faculty outreach. Personalized messages. Financial-aid counseling. Parent engagement. Orientation programs. Registration assistance.

All designed to prevent a student from committing the apparently unforgivable offense of taking their business elsewhere.

And somewhere in Brazil, a college administrator is probably wondering what all the fuss is about.

Meanwhile, in Brazil…

Imagine the following conversation at a typical, less-selective Brazilian private college.

Student: “I'd like to enroll.”

Administrator: “Certainly. Which degree program?”

Student: “Computer Science.”

Administrator: “Excellent. Here's the required documentation, the enrollment procedure, and your tuition. Classes begin next month.”

Student: “Here's my payment.”

Administrator: “Welcome to the college.”

End of story.

Naturally, there are formal admission requirements. Brazilian institutions cannot simply enroll anyone who walks through the door without satisfying educational and regulatory prerequisites.

But for many private colleges, those requirements are straightforward, and the admission process is designed to facilitate enrollment rather than transform it into an elaborate ceremony of institutional approval.

This is not a fringe characteristic of Brazilian higher education.

According to the 2026 Mapa do Ensino Superior, based on government education data, approximately 79.8% of Brazilian higher-education enrollment is in private institutions.

And those institutions have a very practical understanding of their relationship with prospective students.

They provide education. Students pay tuition.

The institution's responsibility is to establish whether applicants qualify, admit them when places are available, and deliver the education they purchased.

A remarkably uncomplicated arrangement.

Brazil Has Competitive Admissions. It Just Doesn't Confuse Them With a Personality Contest.

None of this means Brazilian universities have abandoned competitive admissions.

Far from it.

For decades, Brazilian students aspiring to prestigious institutions have faced the vestibular, an entrance examination that can be exceptionally demanding.

The University of São Paulo (USP) uses FUVEST. The University of Campinas (Unicamp) maintains its own examination. Other institutions have adopted national examination results or hybrid admission systems.

The national examination, ENEM (Exame Nacional do Ensino Médio), has increasingly become a gateway to higher education.

Through SISU (Sistema de Seleção Unificada), students compete for places at participating public institutions using their examination results.

Other programs use ENEM scores to determine scholarship or financing eligibility.

Even prestigious private universities can offer relatively straightforward admission routes. PUC-SP, for example, offered a 2026 supplemental admissions process based exclusively on ENEM scores.

Here's the crucial distinction.

Brazilian universities generally ask students to demonstrate academic eligibility and compete for available places. Many American universities additionally ask students to demonstrate why their personal histories make them worthy of admission.

In the American system, depending on the institution, applicants may need standardized test results, recommendation letters, extracurricular records, personal essays, supplementary essays, interviews, and financial-aid documentation.

The Common Application makes much of this easier to coordinate, but individual institutions can still impose their own combinations of requirements.

The applicant isn't merely demonstrating academic preparation.

They're selling a biography.

Somehow, American higher education decided that becoming a college student should sometimes resemble applying for a senior executive position at a Fortune 500 company.

And this raises an uncomfortable question:

How much of that complexity actually measures educational potential, and how much exists to reinforce the institution's perception of its own importance?

When Difficulty Becomes Prestige

There is a psychological trick at work in American higher education.

When something is difficult to obtain, people tend to assume it must be valuable.

Universities have spent generations cultivating this association.

Acceptance rates become bragging rights. Rejection becomes evidence of exclusivity. Admissions offices proudly announce record application numbers, even when the number of available places has barely changed.

An institution rejecting 95% of applicants sounds impressive.

But rejection itself doesn't educate anyone.

A university's acceptance rate tells us how many applicants it rejected relative to its applicant pool. It doesn't directly tell us how well its professors teach, how rigorous its examinations are, or how competent its graduates become.

An institution can reject thousands of qualified applicants and still deliver mediocre instruction.

Another can admit most qualified applicants and demand substantial academic achievement before awarding a diploma.

These are not contradictory possibilities.

They're different measurements.

Selectivity measures access. Academic rigor measures expectations. Educational quality measures something more complicated: what students actually learn.

Yet the American prestige economy frequently encourages people to treat these concepts as interchangeable.

And once complexity becomes associated with prestige, institutions have an incentive to preserve it, whether or not it serves students.

I suspect that some less-prestigious universities imitate elite admissions practices partly because those practices make an institution appear more selective.

That is an interpretation, not a universal diagnosis of administrators' intentions. But the prestige incentive is hardly imaginary.

For years, acceptance rates were included in the U.S. News college ranking methodology before being removed in 2018.

When rejection itself becomes a metric of success, the incentives are already upside down.

Brazilʼs Other Extreme: “Pagou, Passou”

Before anybody mistakes this for an advertisement for Brazilian higher education, we should acknowledge the country's own longstanding educational dysfunction.

Brazil has an expression for certain private colleges:

“Pagou, passou.”

Roughly translated: “You pay, you pass.”

It's the Brazilian version of a diploma mill.

For decades, some institutions have faced reputational criticism for prioritizing tuition revenue over educational standards.

The economic temptation is obvious.

If students are paying customers, failing them can be bad for business. A student who fails repeatedly may leave, transferring their tuition payments elsewhere.

The result can be pressure to keep paying students progressing regardless of whether they've adequately mastered the material.

But notice something important.

That failure has almost nothing to do with how difficult admission was.

It concerns what happens after enrollment.

Brazil also has respected private universities, including the Pontifical Catholic Universities, whose academic reputations demonstrate that accessible admission pathways and serious educational standards can coexist.

And even within those institutions, admission competitiveness varies substantially by program.

A university need not reject nearly every applicant to take education seriously.

Conversely, an extraordinarily selective institution is not automatically immune to grade inflation, lenient grading, or inadequate teaching.

The relevant question isn't how difficult it was to enter.

It's whether the institution is prepared to withhold a diploma from someone who hasn't earned it.

The proper measure of academic seriousness is not how many students you reject before classes begin, but what you require students to accomplish before graduation.

The Financial Reality Behind America's “Melt”

Returning to the original Wall Street Journal story, there's an even more revealing problem with the term summer melt.

It combines fundamentally different circumstances into one convenient administrative category.

A student who accepts an offer from University A and subsequently receives a better financial-aid package from University B isn't experiencing an educational crisis.

They're making an economic decision.

A student who intends to enroll but discovers that tuition, accommodation, or family circumstances make attendance impossible is facing a genuine financial barrier.

And a student who gets lost in a labyrinth of financial-aid forms, registration procedures, documentation requirements, and institutional deadlines is encountering an administrative failure.

These problems require entirely different responses.

Yet calling all three “melt” allows universities to describe them collectively as students disappearing.

Consider the competitive version.

American colleges routinely admit more students than they expect to attend. They calculate enrollment yields, maintain waiting lists, and compete for prospective applicants.

This is considered sophisticated enrollment management.

But when students place deposits at multiple institutions to preserve their options, administrators suddenly face an enrollment emergency.

Apparently, the university is permitted to hedge its bets.

The student, however, is expected to behave as though an acceptance letter were a marriage proposal.

There's a certain hypocrisy in that expectation.

American higher education has embraced market competition, elaborate tuition pricing, selective financial aid, and institutional branding.

Students have simply learned to behave like market participants.

If University B offers a better deal than University A, why shouldn't a student take it?

A diploma is an investment. For many American families, an extremely expensive one.

Prestige doesn't pay the bills.

When Bureaucracy Becomes the Obstacle

One of the most revealing examples in the original article involves Georgia State University.

The institution reportedly reduced its melt rate from 22% in 2024 to approximately 7% by simplifying registration and improving communication and support.

That's a remarkable improvement.

And it suggests that at least some students weren't abandoning higher education because they suddenly became irresponsible or unreliable.

They were failing to complete a complicated transition.

When institutions help students navigate that transition, more students show up.

Imagine that.

A university discovers that making enrollment easier increases enrollment.

Revolutionary.

Brazilian universities certainly have their own bureaucratic nightmares. Anyone who has dealt with Brazilian institutional paperwork knows that administrative absurdity is hardly an American monopoly.

But the widespread availability of relatively direct admission and enrollment pathways in Brazilian private higher education demonstrates that much of the elaborate American admissions experience isn't an unavoidable feature of university education.

It is a choice, shaped by institutional priorities, competition, financial structures, and tradition.

And choices can be changed.

A Personal Example: Back to College After Three Decades

I can offer a firsthand example of just how straightforward this process can be.

I'm currently pursuing my second bachelor's degree in Brazil, at the very same institution where I earned my first one more than thirty years ago.

Despite already holding a degree and having decades of professional experience, I enrolled as an ordinary incoming freshman, exactly as though I had just graduated from high school.

I went to the admissions office during the winter break, in the middle of the academic year, and asked about tuition and entrance examinations. After all, it had been more than three decades since I'd last gone through the process.

They directed me to the college's website, where I completed the application and paid the enrollment fee electronically. I was then scheduled for an entrance examination.

On examination day, I was handed a sheet of paper and asked to write an essay on a particular subject.

I couldn't tell you what that subject was today. I've completely forgotten it.

I wrote the required number of words, handed in the essay, and went home.

The following day, I received a notification informing me that I'd been accepted and inviting me to finalize my enrollment.

I returned to the admissions office, paid my tuition, and was told when classes would begin.

That was it.

No recommendation letters. No autobiographical essays about the adversity I'd overcome. No interviews about my ambitions, my character, or what unique perspective I might contribute to the student body.

And certainly no months-long campaign by the university to keep me from mysteriously “melting” before the semester began.

To be clear, this wasn't some obscure diploma mill desperate to collect tuition payments. It was Centro Universitário Fundação Santo André, a well-established, nonprofit institution with roots dating to 1953, created through municipal initiative and operating under the unusual legal structure of a public foundation governed by private law. It has a long academic tradition in São Paulo's industrial ABC region and has earned the highest institutional evaluation score awarded by Brazil's Ministry of Education.

And yet, despite its history, institutional credentials, and academic reputation, enrolling was remarkably straightforward.

The institution apparently understood something that many American universities struggle with: making admission unnecessarily complicated doesn't make an education more valuable.

But that's precisely the point.

The institution established that I satisfied its admission requirements, offered me a place, and enrolled me.

The entire process, from entrance examination to acceptance, took approximately twenty-four hours.

My previous degree didn't exempt me from the process. My professional experience didn't earn me preferential treatment.

And neither needed to.

I wanted to study. The college had a place available. I met its requirements.

Everything else could happen in the classroom, where education is supposed to take place.

The Comparison America Doesn't Want to Make

There are important differences between the two countries.

Brazilian public universities are generally tuition-free and frequently highly competitive. The United States has community colleges and less-selective institutions that also provide relatively straightforward admission.

Brazilian private institutions have their own financial pressures, and dropping out because of tuition or personal circumstances is hardly unusual.

Neither country has solved equitable access to higher education.

But comparing the systems reveals something worth examining.

In Brazil, the prestige of an institution can derive from academic tradition, examination performance, research, professional reputation, and the achievements of its graduates.

In the United States, those factors also matter, but the selectivity of the admissions process has become a particularly visible part of the prestige narrative.

The institution's ability to say “No” is treated as evidence of its value.

And that creates a peculiar relationship with students.

First, the university constructs an elaborate process requiring applicants to demonstrate why they deserve admission.

Then it congratulates those who survive the process.

Then it asks for a deposit.

And finally, when the student finds a better opportunity or encounters an insurmountable obstacle, it expresses astonishment that the commitment wasn't permanent.

The institution has spent months acting as though the applicant should feel privileged to be admitted.

Now it must spend the summer convincing that same applicant that attendance is worthwhile.

There's something almost poetic about the reversal.

Education Is Not an Exclusive Club

The purpose of a university should be to educate.

That sounds obvious, but higher education has developed an extraordinary capacity to complicate the obvious.

Admissions selectivity can serve legitimate purposes. Universities with limited places need mechanisms for choosing among qualified candidates. Competitive examinations, academic records, and other indicators can help make those decisions.

But complexity should have to justify itself.

If an additional essay, interview, recommendation, or administrative requirement doesn't meaningfully improve student selection, access, or educational outcomes, why impose it?

If a student meets the academic requirements, can secure a place, and is prepared to attend, why construct unnecessary obstacles?

And if institutions are struggling to retain students who have already accepted offers, perhaps they should begin by asking whether their own procedures, prices, and expectations are driving those students away.

The lesson from Brazil isn't that universities should admit everybody or hand out diplomas in exchange for tuition.

The lesson is considerably simpler.

Make it straightforward for qualified students to enter. Make the education worth attending. And make the diploma mean something when they leave.

American universities have become extraordinarily accomplished at measuring how difficult they are to enter.

Perhaps it's time they devoted comparable attention to demonstrating why students should want to stay.

After all, a university's purpose isn't to collect applications, celebrate rejection rates, or manufacture institutional mystique.

It's to teach.

And if a student finds a better educational opportunity somewhere else, that isn't necessarily a crisis.

Sometimes that's just competition.

Welcome to the market you've spent decades building.
