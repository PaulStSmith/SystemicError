George Conway claps back at 'idiot' Trump Jr. after Bolton dig: 'Just like your father'

"Someone’s gonna have to explain why George Conway was already at John Bolton’s house at the crack of dawn," Trump Jr. said, with a pondering emoji. He shared a Reuters photo of Conway with his phone as he was live-streaming outside the home of John Bolton, who the FBI was raiding.

Conway's stream was on Tim Miller's morning podcast for "The Bulwark." The time-stamp showed Conway was there at approximately 9:57 a.m. EDT. Sunrise in Bethesda was at 6:29 a.m. EDT.

"My God, you're such an idiot, just like your father!" Conway wrote back on X.

Conway spent the morning laughing about the Reuters photo that was taken of him.

He also joked to Trump Jr., "I went through Kash Patel's trash and underneath all the coloring books and enemies lists, I found the search warrant!!"

First on the scene was Lawfare's Benjamin Wittes, who spoke to local police outside the home.

Our Analysis:

The Spectacle of Political Theater: Conway vs. Trump Jr.

In the latest installment of the never-ending circus that is American politics, we have a scene that could easily be mistaken for a poorly written sitcom, but alas, it's real life. The characters? George Conway, a Washington lawyer and co-founder of the never-Trump organization, The Lincoln Project, and Donald Trump Jr., the eldest son of the former president. The setting? The home of John Bolton, amidst an FBI raid, no less.

The Ridicule Dance

Donald Trump Jr.'s attempt at a jab—questioning why George Conway was at Bolton's house so early—fell flat, showcasing a lack of wit one might find despairing or amusing, depending on one's tolerance for political banter. Conway's response, "My God, you're such an idiot, just like your father!" is a line so laden with disdain, it practically drips off the screen. This exchange, while it may seem petty and personal, is emblematic of the deeper, systemic issues plaguing American political discourse.

The Early Bird Gets the Worm?

The fact that Conway was at the scene of an FBI raid at what Trump Jr. inaccurately implied was an ungodly hour (9:57 a.m. is hardly the "crack of dawn") is a spectacle in itself. The sunrise time detail—6:29 a.m. EDT—served to underscore the absurdity of Trump Jr.'s attempt to stir the pot. Conway's presence, and his subsequent live-streaming for Tim Miller's morning podcast, is a vivid illustration of how political operatives and pundits now engage in real-time theatrics, leveraging every opportunity to score points against their adversaries.

The Underbelly of Political Commentary

Conway's joke about going through Kash Patel's trash is a stark reminder of the level to which political commentary has sunk. The mention of "coloring books and enemies lists" not only trivializes the serious nature of the search warrant but also highlights the juvenile tactics often employed by those in the political arena. It's a sad commentary on the state of our political discourse that such remarks are par for the course.

First on the Scene

Benjamin Wittes' role as the first on the scene speaks volumes about the voyeuristic nature of political journalism today. The rush to be first, to comment first, to judge first, has overshadowed the more critical need for accuracy, depth, and thoughtfulness in reporting. Wittes speaking to local police outside Bolton's home is a snapshot of the frenzied rush to gather crumbs of information, often before the full story is known or understood.

Conclusion: The Theater Continues

This entire episode is a microcosm of the broader dysfunction within American political culture. It's a culture characterized by quick jabs, personal attacks, and a relentless pursuit of the next sensational story. The exchange between Conway and Trump Jr., while it may provide momentary amusement or indignation, is ultimately a distraction from the substantive issues that require our attention.

The spectacle of Conway at Bolton's house, live-streaming for a podcast, encapsulated by the sharp-tongued exchange with Trump Jr., is not just political theater—it's indicative of the systemic error in how politics is conducted and consumed in America today. As long as the focus remains on these theatrical skirmishes rather than on meaningful dialogue and policy, the systemic error remains uncorrected.
