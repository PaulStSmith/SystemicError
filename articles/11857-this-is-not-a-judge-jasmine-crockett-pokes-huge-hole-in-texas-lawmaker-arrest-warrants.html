'This is not a judge': Jasmine Crockett pokes huge hole in Texas lawmaker arrest warrants

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 10, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:Texas Democrats' Flight: A Tangled Web of Power, Law, and Political Theater

Ali Velshi, Jasmine Crockett (MSNBC screenshot)

The warrants issued to arrest Texas Democrats who fled the state to stymie a GOP attempt to redistrict the state lack the legal weight to do what the Republicans want them do.

That is the opinion of Rep. Jasmine Crockett (D-TX) who was asked, as an attorney, about the FBI reportedly getting involved in tracking the lawmakers down.

Appearing with MSNBC host Ali Velshi on Saturday, she was asked, "Quick question for you: because you're a lawyer, why would [FBI director] Kash Patel take [Texas Republican] John Cornyn's phone call about getting the FBI to round up people? I don't know what the crime is."

"There is no crime, you already know this," the Texas Democrat replied. "And just so people understand, even the warrants that they put out for them, they are signed by the speaker of the [Texas] House. I mean, this is not a law, a law enforcement official. This is not a judge. This is not a prosecutor. This is no one whomsoever would normally sign off on a warrant."

"And that is because it is not a criminal warrant," she elaborated. "This is only giving license to someone who is found within the state of Texas, to then grab them and take them back to the [legislature] chamber. If they take them anywhere other than the chamber, then they are looking at a civil rights violation because they have not broken any laws."

"So this is just a matter of a civil situation and frankly, this is a family situation as far as i'm concerned, because basically, the family known as the legislators in the Texas House have decided that they don't want to come to the table and have a conversation about doing right by Texas," she added. "They have decided that they would steamroll and disrespect their colleagues as well as their constituents in this moment, so that they can appease the mad king."

Our Analysis:

Texas Democrats' Flight: A Tangled Web of Power, Law, and Political Theater

In the latest spectacle of political drama, Texas Republicans' attempt to use law enforcement to corral Democrats who fled the state to block a controversial redistricting effort is not just legally dubious—it's a full-blown mockery of democratic principles. According to Rep. Jasmine Crockett (D-TX), the warrants issued for their arrest hold about as much legal weight as a feather in a hurricane. Let's dissect this, shall we?

First off, the concept of arresting lawmakers for employing a political strategy, albeit dramatic, to halt legislation they find detrimental is, in itself, a darkly comic overreach. Crockett, leveraging her legal background, points out the glaringly obvious: the FBI has no business in this theater of the absurd. The notion that FBI Director Kash Patel would entertain a call from Senator John Cornyn (R-TX) to round up political opponents is as ludicrous as it sounds. The crime? Nonexistent. The motivation? Purely political.

But let’s dive deeper into the "warrants" themselves. Signed not by a judge, prosecutor, or any law enforcement official, but by the Speaker of the Texas House, these documents are about as legally binding as a note passed in high school. They're not criminal warrants but rather a bizarre form of political subpoena with the muscle of a wet noodle outside the state lines.

Crockett hits the nail on the head when she describes this debacle as a "family situation" within the Texas Legislature. This isn't about law and order; it's about political muscle, about the majority attempting to force its will upon the minority using whatever tools it perceives to have at its disposal—even when those tools are more symbolic than effective.

And let's talk about the elephant (or should we say, donkey) in the room: the Democrats' decision to leave Texas. This isn't a holiday; it's a last-resort measure to prevent what they view as a direct assault on democratic principles—the right to fair representation. By fleeing, they're not shirking their duty but rather embracing it in the only way they see left open to them, a dramatic stance against what they perceive as a "steamroll" of their rights and those of their constituents.

The term "mad king" used by Crockett might seem hyperbolic, but it's emblematic of the frustration felt by many. It's a vivid illustration of the perceived tyranny of the majority, where the rules of the game are being rewritten to serve the interests of a select few at the expense of the many.

In conclusion, this episode is less about the legal nuances of arresting lawmakers and more about the state of politics in Texas—and, by extension, America. It's a glaring example of how far some are willing to go to maintain power, even if it means stretching the bounds of legality and morality. The Republicans' attempt to use the FBI as a personal retrieval service is not just a failure in legal terms; it's a stark illustration of the lengths to which political warfare has descended. Democracy, it seems, is on a precipice, with Texas as its current battleground.
