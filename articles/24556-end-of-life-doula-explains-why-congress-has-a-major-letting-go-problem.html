End-of-life doula explains why Congress has a major letting-go problem

Based on an article by The Conversation

Paulo SantosOctober 8, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Congress’s “letting-go problem” is sustained by the identity incumbents derive from office and by institutions that reward them for staying. Parties prize incumbency, staff careers depend on sitting lawmakers, and constituents may value seniority. In Massachusetts, 80-year-old Sen. Ed Markey defeated 47-year-old Rep. Seth Moulton in the September 1, 2026, Democratic primary by nearly 30 percentage points; 11 octogenarians won congressional primaries that year.

The article, republished from The Conversation and written by management professor and trained end-of-life doula Nancy Forster-Holt, argues that succession should be planned before illness forces it. She contrasts Barbara Boxer’s advance retirement announcement with the crisis-driven exits or absences surrounding Dianne Feinstein, Joe Biden and Kay Granger, while noting current scrutiny of Mitch McConnell’s health and Chuck Grassley’s acknowledgment that dying in office could determine which party selects his successor.

Power does not surrender itself

The problem is not simply that elderly politicians cannot imagine retirement. Congressional office carries votes, committee authority, patronage, publicity and influence over legislation and appointments. The person occupying the seat controls those powers until resignation, defeat or death.

Age alone does not establish incapacity, and the article does not claim that every older lawmaker is unfit. But incumbents are not ordinary employees deciding when to leave a private job. Their refusal to plan can transfer the risks of illness and decline to constituents, colleagues and the functioning of government while preserving their own status for as long as possible.

The enablers have names

Parties keep backing incumbents because incumbency is electorally valuable. Staff members and longtime allies may avoid difficult conversations because their careers, access and shared projects are tied to the officeholder. Voters can reinforce the arrangement by preferring accumulated seniority to an orderly transfer of power.

Those incentives help explain why “not yet” so often survives until choice gives way to emergency. Feinstein’s nearly three-month health-related absence in 2023 left the Senate Judiciary Committee deadlocked on dozens of Biden judicial nominations after Republicans blocked a temporary Democratic replacement and Feinstein declined to resign. That outcome involved identifiable decisions, not an abstract failure to “let go.”

Age is the convenient decoy

Framing this chiefly as a gerontocracy story risks blaming biology for an institutional design that rewards indefinite tenure. Markey’s age did not award him the nomination; Massachusetts Democratic primary voters did. Parties did not merely observe these candidacies; they operated within a system that prizes incumbents’ fundraising, recognition and seniority.

The sharper question is not whether 80 is categorically too old. It is why lawmakers can retain consequential authority without credible succession plans, and why the people surrounding them often remain quiet until a medical or political crisis makes concealment impossible. Personal attachment matters, but institutional permission makes that attachment politically consequential.

Crisis is not succession

Boxer announced her departure early enough for candidates to organize and voters to choose among them. By contrast, Biden withdrew from the 2024 presidential race only after the primary process, following advice from his inner circle to seek reelection. Harris then became the Democratic nominee without participating in those primaries and lost to Donald Trump.

The reporting does not establish that the compressed succession caused that defeat. It does establish that delay deprived Democrats of a normal competitive selection process. When leaders and their entourages postpone transition, the public inherits fewer choices, less preparation and greater dependence on insiders making decisions under pressure.

An institution built around “not yet”

Forster-Holt’s palliative-care analogy is useful because it treats departure as something to prepare for while meaningful choice remains possible. But Congress does not need better therapeutic vocabulary nearly as much as it needs political actors willing to place institutional continuity above an incumbent’s preferences.

The larger pattern is a governing class that treats public office as personal identity while distributing the costs of that attachment to everyone else. The scandal is not that lawmakers grow old. It is that parties, aides and political institutions repeatedly allow foreseeable transitions to become emergencies—and then describe the resulting dysfunction as tragedy rather than the consequence of choices made by people with power.
