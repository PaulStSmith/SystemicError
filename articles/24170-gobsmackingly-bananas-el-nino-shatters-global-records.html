‘Gobsmackingly bananas’ El Niño shatters global records

Based on an article by Frida Garza, GristKate Yoder

Paulo SantosSeptember 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet, republishing Grist, reported that a developing El Niño had pushed temperatures in the Niño 3.4 region of the Pacific to 3.11 degrees Celsius above normal, surpassing the peak recorded in November 2015. Forecast models cited in the article put the chance of the event remaining “very strong” through the end of that year at 90 percent.

The report said the naturally occurring shift in Pacific winds and water was intensifying extreme weather against a background of rapid, fossil-fuel-driven warming. Scientists warned of consequences for public health, agriculture, hydropower and severe weather; one study projected as many as 451,000 heat-related deaths worldwide by the following February. The article also said California had declared a statewide emergency, but did not identify the issuing authority or provide the declaration’s date.

The atmosphere is not the decision-maker

El Niño redistributes heat. It does not approve drilling permits, finance fossil-fuel expansion or decide how much protection heat-exposed workers receive. The institutional power in this story belongs to governments, energy companies, financial institutions and regulators whose decisions determine emissions and preparedness.

The reporting acknowledges that fossil-fuel combustion supplies the warming backdrop, but its fascination with “super,” “Godzilla” and “gobsmackingly bananas” risks turning a political crisis into a vocabulary problem. The Pacific did not create the underlying vulnerability. Powerful institutions did—and continue to decide whether it deepens.

Spectacle can obscure responsibility

The argument over terminology is not meaningless. Officials must communicate danger without minimizing it or numbing the public through endless escalation. But a long discussion about whether “super El Niño” is scientifically respectable directs attention toward messengers while leaving decision-makers largely unnamed.

That is the central weakness in the framing. The article gives scientists names, measurements and quotations, yet identifies no government or corporate actor responsible for continuing the fossil-fuel dependence it invokes. Causation becomes atmospheric; agency disappears.

Disaster is administered politically

A forecast does not determine who dies. Heat protections, resilient electrical grids, public-health capacity, crop support and emergency planning shape the consequences. The projected death toll cited by the report was a study’s estimate, not a recorded outcome, but its scale makes the political stakes plain.

Those with the least institutional power are most likely to absorb failures made elsewhere. Workers cannot individually legislate heat standards. Farmers cannot stabilize global weather systems. Households cannot substitute personal preparedness for functioning infrastructure. Shifting the burden onto “awareness” would disguise a chain of public and private decisions as individual vulnerability.

Records are becoming alibis

Repeatedly calling each event unprecedented can make escalation sound unforeseeable. The reporting establishes the opposite: scientists were measuring the anomaly, modeling its persistence and warning about foreseeable effects. Uncertainty remained about the exact distribution and severity of those effects, not about the existence of serious risk.

The larger pattern is institutional delay followed by emergency management. Governments tolerate the conditions that magnify danger, then present declarations and disaster response as evidence of leadership. Preparation matters, but preparation without mitigation is management of a worsening baseline.

The system behind the weather

The useful political question is not whether “super” is dramatic enough. It is who can reduce the danger, who chooses not to use that power, and who pays for the choice.

El Niño is a natural phenomenon. A fossil-fueled climate, uneven protection and recurring institutional unpreparedness are not. Treating their combined damage as an astonishing act of nature launders political agency out of the story—and lets the institutions shaping the future pose as spectators to it.
