'Quite bad': Ex-prosecutor says Trump's slush fund woes are just beginning

The Slush Fund Met the Courtroom

Context

A federal judge has paused Donald Trump’s $1.776 billion “Anti-Weaponization” fund, and another has reopened the IRS case that helped create it. Preet Bharara’s point is straightforward: the courts are starting to inspect the machinery, and that is where the story gets dangerous for Trump.

Where Power Actually Sits

The real institutional power here is not with the loudest political actors; it is with the judges who can freeze money, reopen records, and force discovery. Trump can brand the fund however he wants, but the legal system still controls whether that money moves and whether the underlying arrangement survives scrutiny.

That matters because the fund did not appear out of thin air. According to the source, it emerged from a court-linked process that was “at the behest of and at the urging of 35 retired judges.” That is not a sideshow. It is the mechanism.

Who Enabled It

If this story has a central act of political enabling, it is the decision to turn judicial process into a pipeline for money and patronage. The source says the sitting judge now wants to know whether the system is being abused to help Trump pay his allies. That is the key allegation: not confusion, not technical friction, but the possible laundering of institutional authority into political advantage.

The responsibility does not sit with the judges who are now pausing and reopening things. It sits with the people who built the arrangement, defended it, and treated it as normal enough to move forward until someone finally looked closely.

The Convenient Blame Shift

The most revealing part of the response is where outrage goes. The source says Trump and his allies are more angry about retired judges “injecting themselves” into the case than about the improprieties those judges flagged.

That is classic misdirection. It shifts attention from the substance of the alleged abuse to the identity of the people exposing it. The complaint becomes the participation of judges, not the possibility that the court system was being used in a “too cute for school” scheme. That is not a defense. It is a diversion.

What the Story Is Really About

The pattern here is institutional cowardice meeting political opportunism. Trump’s side appears to treat legal process as a resource to be harvested, not a constraint to be obeyed. Then, when the process catches up, the same camp performs outrage at the people enforcing the rules.

Even Trump’s own public fixations, as Bharara notes, drift toward vanity theater, like complaining about not having his name on the Kennedy Center while the legal ground shifts under his feet. That contrast is not incidental. It shows a politics built to survive on distraction while others do the work of accountability.

The Larger Pattern

This is what modern power abuse looks like when it is dressed up as procedure. The strongest actors do not always need to break institutions openly; they can bend them, exploit them, and then accuse the referees of misconduct when the bending is noticed.

The deeper lesson is not that the courts are suddenly heroic. It is that institutions often tolerate abuse until it becomes too visible to ignore. By then, the damage is already done, and the fight becomes about whether accountability can arrive before the machinery is normalized.
