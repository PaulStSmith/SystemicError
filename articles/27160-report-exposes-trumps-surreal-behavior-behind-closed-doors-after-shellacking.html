Report exposes Trump’s surreal behavior behind closed doors after shellacking

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 9, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump responded to Republicans’ landslide defeat in the 2026 midterms with denial rather than public acknowledgment. Citing New York Times reporter Peter Baker, it describes Trump as “flat,” possibly dazed, and largely unwilling to recognize the electorate’s rejection while his party assessed its losses.

Trump signed a beam for a new White House ballroom, traveled to Florida for a space-program speech, and offered no concession comparable to George W. Bush’s “thumping” or Barack Obama’s “shellacking.” Meanwhile, the White House posted, “TRUMP IS STILL YOUR PRESIDENT,” as Trump faced the erosion of influence that commonly follows a president’s loss of congressional power.

Political analyst Allan Lichtman told AlterNet that the scale of the result invited comparisons with the congressional upheavals of 1994 and 1894. He attributed the 2026 rout partly to a sour economy and partly to an unpopular war, which he treated as a separate political liability.

The headline overpromises

The reporting does not expose much “behind closed doors.” Its evidence is chiefly Trump’s public schedule, his failure to acknowledge the defeat, a White House message, and Baker’s interpretation of his demeanor. No private remark or concealed episode establishes the headline’s promised surreal behavior.

That distinction matters. Trump’s internal emotional state is less politically important than his official response. Calling him dazed risks converting an exercise of presidential agency into a psychological spectacle. Whatever Trump privately feels, he chose not to acknowledge public discontent, and his White House chose to answer repudiation with an assertion of continuing status.

Power did not disappear

Republicans lost congressional power, but Trump remained president. The White House still controlled the executive branch, the machinery of administration, and the immense public authority attached to the office. “TRUMP IS STILL YOUR PRESIDENT” was therefore literally true, but politically revealing: it answered an electoral rebuke by emphasizing the power voters had not yet removed.

Institutional power does not vanish because its holder looks diminished. The election constrained Trump and weakened his leverage; it did not make him a bystander. Coverage centered on whether he seemed sad, stunned, or detached can obscure the more consequential question: how a repudiated president uses the authority he retains.

Denial is a governing choice

Bush and Obama publicly admitted major midterm losses. Trump reportedly did not. That contrast is not merely about humility or temperament. A president’s acknowledgment of defeat tells the governing party that voters imposed a constraint. Refusing that acknowledgment gives allies permission to treat the result as an inconvenience rather than an instruction.

The article notes that conservative groups were already arguing Republicans would have prevailed had Trump followed their advice. That is the familiar machinery of factional evasion: accept the vote count while rewriting its meaning so no one with power must accept responsibility. The president avoids the verdict; aligned organizations convert defeat into a tactical disagreement.

The electorate supplied the consequence

The decisive actors were voters, who stripped Trump’s party of congressional control amid economic dissatisfaction and opposition to war, according to Lichtman’s analysis. Trump dominated the political system for years, but dominance is not permanence. Once congressional majorities change hands, agenda control, oversight authority, and bargaining power change with them.

Historical analogies cannot establish what follows. The 1894 rout preceded a long Republican era, but Lichtman also identified important differences in economic conditions. The sound conclusion is narrower: this was a large transfer of institutional power, not proof of a settled political realignment.

The system rewards spectacle over accountability

The larger pattern is political journalism’s attraction to the ruler’s mood after the public has acted. A devastated party, a lost Congress, an unpopular war, and economic discontent become scenery for a personality study: Was Trump dazed? Could he admit his era was ending?

That frame shrinks democratic power into presidential psychology. The consequential story is not that Trump may have struggled emotionally with defeat. It is that voters imposed a material limit, the White House answered with defiance, and Republican interests began repositioning themselves without squarely accepting responsibility. The spectacle is his demeanor. The politics is the fight over whether an electoral judgment will constrain those who still hold power.
