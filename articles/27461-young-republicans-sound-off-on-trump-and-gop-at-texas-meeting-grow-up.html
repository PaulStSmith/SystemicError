Young Republicans sound off on Trump and GOP at Texas meeting: 'Grow up'

Based on an article by Brenda Alexander

Paulo SantosNovember 26, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A defeat the party cannot blame awayAffordability exposes the limits of spectacleThe loyalty structure is now the liability

Young Republicans meeting at a Texas brewery after the GOP’s 2026 midterm rout reached an unsparing conclusion: Donald Trump and his party had helped engineer their own defeat through broken promises, economic detachment, personal fealty and behavior several attendees described as childish. Their advice—“grow up”—was directed not at wayward voters but at the people who held power and squandered it.

The Monday gathering, organized by the Tarrant County Young Republicans and reported by The Independent, was less a rebellion against Republican politics than an argument over whether the party can function after years organized around Trump. These were activists and voters confronting a loss from inside the coalition, including a three-time Trump supporter, a Republican who sat out the midterms and a state Young Republican leader urging his party to stop blaming everyone else.

A defeat the party cannot blame away

“Everyone has someone to blame, and that finger is never pointed at ourself,” Derrick Wilson, head of the Texas Young Republican Federation, told The Independent. “So we're going to have to get past that and figure out, what did we do wrong?”

That question punctures the reflexive machinery of political scapegoating. Parties in power can blame hostile media, insufficiently loyal officials or an ungrateful electorate. What they cannot honestly do is pretend they lacked agency. According to the Republicans at this meeting, the GOP’s own conduct contributed to its losses.

The local chapter had already registered deeper disaffection before Election Day: its previous leader resigned ahead of the midterms after concluding that she could not vote for her party. Trevor Carr, a 25-year-old attending his first meeting, also sat out the election because he doubted Trump would fulfill his promises. He interpreted the result as a rejection of the party’s old guard and called for a movement built around a durable ideological program rather than allegiance to one man.

Carr credited Trump with disrupting the political establishment a decade earlier but said that period had exhausted itself. “It’s served its purpose, and it’s time to move on to be a serious political movement,” he said.

Affordability exposes the limits of spectacle

Robert Coe, 29, did not arrive as a Trump apostate. He voted for Trump three times and remained loyal to Republicans in the 2026 midterms. Yet he told The Independent that Trump appeared disconnected from the country’s affordability crisis and had offered no credible remedy.

“Me and millions of other Americans are just tired of that, and we’re not seeing any viable solution to it,” Coe said. “It’s almost as if the people in power don’t even want to try and solve it.”

Coe’s frustration identifies the governing failure beneath the party’s style problem. Name-calling and factional combat are not merely embarrassing manners. They consume attention while officials with institutional authority fail to address material pressures facing voters. When politics becomes an endless performance of grievance, spectacle can obscure the absence of results—but it cannot make rent, food or other necessities more affordable.

Coe said he was finished with “the high school-level name-calling” and infighting conducted “on an almost childish level.” He also said the party now lacked “decency.” Other attendees urged Republican leaders to take themselves seriously and show humility.

The loyalty structure is now the liability

The sharpest criticism at the meeting was not simply that Trump had become rude or ineffective. It was that the party had subordinated a coherent political identity to one person, then discovered that personal loyalty could not substitute indefinitely for governing.

Carr wants to help shape the GOP’s future, but his proposed reset would require party officials to surrender the organizing principle that has rewarded them for years: allegiance to Trump. That is a harder demand than asking politicians to improve their tone. It challenges the distribution of influence inside the party, where loyalty has served as a credential and dissent as a liability.

The young Republicans’ diagnosis therefore runs ahead of their prescription. “Grow up” captures their disgust, but maturity is not a policy platform, and humility is not an institutional reform. The officials and operatives who constructed this arrangement still hold the power to decide whether the GOP changes course. The brewery attendees can name the failure. They cannot, by themselves, compel the people who benefited from it to let go.
