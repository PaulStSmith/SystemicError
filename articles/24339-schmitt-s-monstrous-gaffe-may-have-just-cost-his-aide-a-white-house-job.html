Schmitt's monstrous gaffe may have just cost his aide a White House job

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet, citing Politico, reports that Ethan Harper, general counsel to Republican Sen. Eric Schmitt of Missouri, was expected to leave the senator’s office on Friday and join the White House Staff Secretary’s office the following week. After a Tuesday Senate Judiciary Committee hearing, however, three anonymous sources said the move was in doubt. Neither outlet established that the job had been formally withdrawn.

At the hearing, Schmitt tried to connect former special counsel Jack Smith with Fulton County District Attorney Fani Willis by claiming Smith had attended an Atlanta Hawks game in February 2024. Smith denied it. Schmitt accused him of perjury before the premise collapsed: the evidence concerned an Iowa Hawkeyes women’s college basketball game, not the Atlanta Hawks.

Harper stood behind Schmitt displaying a board of text-message screenshots, but Politico reported no evidence that Harper was responsible for the error. The White House declined to comment; Schmitt’s office and Harper did not immediately provide responses.

The senator held the power

Schmitt controlled the hearing time, delivered the accusation and invoked the grave charge of perjury. Smith was the witness compelled to answer. Harper held the visual aid, but Schmitt held the office, the microphone and the institutional authority behind the attack.

That hierarchy matters because the apparent employment consequence may fall on the aide whose visible role made him convenient to blame. Politico’s reporting does not establish who researched the allegation, approved the board or failed to verify the teams. It does establish who publicly converted that defective work into an accusation: Schmitt.

An accusation built before the facts

This was not merely a verbal slip. Schmitt arrived with posters and a theory intended to suggest coordination between prosecutors investigating Donald Trump. The display gave the performance the appearance of documented proof. The documentation did not prove the central claim.

When Smith denied attending the Hawks game, Schmitt escalated to accusing him of lying under oath. Sen. Amy Klobuchar’s response—that Schmitt should get his facts together before hurling insults—identified the basic failure. The accusation came first; factual control came later.

Accountability flowing downhill

Harper’s prospective White House role involved managing documents and materials sent to the president, while his work for Schmitt included interviewing judicial candidates. Accuracy is plainly relevant to such responsibilities. But the available reporting does not show that Harper created or approved the false claim, so treating his stalled transfer as a clean act of accountability would outrun the evidence.

What the episode does show is a familiar distribution of risk. Senior officials receive the cameras and political benefit from an aggressive attack. Staff members perform the supporting labor and may absorb the professional damage when the attack disintegrates. Institutional power protects the person who chose to speak while exposing the person standing behind him.

The framing softens the offense

Calling this a “gaffe” understates what happened. Confusing two basketball teams was the factual blunder. Accusing a witness of perjury on the strength of that confusion was Schmitt’s decision.

The distinction is political, not semantic. “Gaffe” language turns an abuse of senatorial authority into personal embarrassment. The substantive problem was that a senator used a congressional hearing to advance an unsupported insinuation about prosecutorial coordination and escalated a disputed premise into an allegation of criminal conduct.

Spectacle without responsibility

The larger pattern is investigative theater designed to produce a damaging clip rather than establish a fact. Visual aids supply certainty, confrontation supplies drama, and the correction arrives only after the allegation has been broadcast.

When that machinery fails, responsibility can be displaced onto anonymous staff work, faulty preparation or generalized confusion. But institutions do not make accusations by themselves. Schmitt made this one. Unless consequences reach the official who exercised the power, the system teaches politicians that reckless allegations are cheap—and teaches their aides that accuracy matters most when someone more powerful needs a scapegoat.
