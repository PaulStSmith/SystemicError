'We’re out of time': Farms warn Trump is about to usher in the worst crisis in decades

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 16, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Unnecessary CrisisThe Human CostConflict of Interest?Conclusion

A farmer stands in a field (Shutterstock).

President Donald Trump's trade policies have set up American farmers for an impending disaster, as China refuses to buy soybeans from the United States.

According to The New York Times, the move by the Chinese government to halt U.S. soybean imports "has had devastating repercussions for farmers in North Dakota, which exported more than 70 percent of its soybeans to China before Trump unveiled the new tariffs this year. Unless China agrees to restart its purchases as part of a trade deal, farmers that depend on the Chinese market will be facing steep losses that could fuel farm bankruptcies and farm foreclosures around the United States."

China usually consumes around half of the U.S. annual soybean harvest, and 70 percent of the harvest from North Dakota. The uncertainty has caused many farmers to fear a repeat of the 1980s farm crisis, a massive agricultural recession triggered by high interest rates, falling crop prices, and land value changes. That recession led to a huge wave of farm bankruptcies across the Midwest.

“The stress level is much higher now than it was then,” 44-year-old Jordan Gackle, who co-owns a 76-year-old soybean farm near Kulm, told The Times. “If we keep this going for very long, then we are going to see the kind of foreclosures that were happening.” He continued, “All of it is unnecessary. The U.S. was not forced into this by anybody.”

His brother Josh, who chairs the American Soybean Association, said, “The input costs for fertilizer, chemicals, cost of land, cost of equipment, all of those things are increasing. And the price of the commodity is decreasing.”

Justin Sherlock, a 37-year-old farmer in Dazey, had a more blunt assessment: “I feel like we’re out of time.”

Adding to the stakes, Trump's own Treasury Secretary, Scott Bessent, has financial interests in the region as he tries to negotiate a trade solution with China: "The Treasury Secretary owns thousands of acres of North Dakota farmland, worth up to $25 million. The properties grow soybeans and corn in a state that exports most of its agricultural products to China. The investments have earned Mr. Bessent as much as $1 million in rental income annually, according to his financial disclosure filings."

Nonetheless, the report noted, Bessent has many other sources of income, which is not the case for many of the struggling farmers in the area.

Our Analysis:

Trump's Trade Policy: A Recipe for Agricultural Despair

In an era where political decisions often have far-reaching consequences, President Donald Trump’s trade policies stand out for their particularly devastating impact on a group that can ill afford it: American farmers. The New York Times report paints a grim picture of the situation in North Dakota, where farmers, who once exported more than 70% of their soybeans to China, now face the abyss of economic disaster. This isn't merely a policy error; it's a catastrophic failure with real human costs.

The Unnecessary Crisis

Let's be clear: This crisis was avoidable. The decision to engage in a trade war with China was a choice, not an inevitability. Jordan Gackle’s assertion that the U.S. was not forced into this situation is a damning indictment of a trade policy that seems to prioritize political posturing over the livelihoods of American farmers. The stark reality is that the policies of the Trump administration have set the stage for a potential repeat of the 1980s farm crisis, with all its attendant misery and bankruptcy.

The Human Cost

The farmers' stories are a poignant reminder of the human cost of these policies. The stress, the fear of foreclosure, the increasing input costs versus decreasing commodity prices – these are not abstract economic concepts but the harsh realities of people's lives. Jordan Gackle's and Justin Sherlock's testimonies reflect a deep-seated anxiety about the future, a sentiment that is both understandable and heart-wrenching.

Conflict of Interest?

Enter Scott Bessent, Trump’s Treasury Secretary, whose financial interests in North Dakota farmland add a layer of complexity—and perhaps conflict—to the situation. It's almost Kafkaesque that Bessent, who stands to profit from the very policies that are driving farmers to the brink, is involved in negotiating a trade solution with China. This situation raises serious ethical questions. While Bessent may have diversified sources of income, the farmers do not have the luxury of financial cushions. Their farms are not just businesses but their homes and legacies.

Conclusion

The New York Times article shines a spotlight on a crisis that encapsulates the broader failures of the Trump administration's trade policies. It's a cautionary tale about the dangers of using tariffs as a blunt instrument in international relations. The consequences of these decisions ripple through the heartland of America, leaving economic devastation and shattered dreams in their wake.

The plight of North Dakota farmers is a stark reminder that policy decisions have real-world impacts. As the trade war continues to unfold, the question remains: How many more will have to suffer before pragmatism prevails over politics? The answer, unfortunately, may be far more than our farming communities can bear.
