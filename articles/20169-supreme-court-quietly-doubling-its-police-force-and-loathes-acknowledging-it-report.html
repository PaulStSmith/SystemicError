Supreme Court quietly doubling its police force — and 'loathes' acknowledging it: report

The Court That Judges Everyone Is Building Its Own Shield

Quiet Expansion, Loud Impunity

The Supreme Court is expanding its police force in near silence, apparently while its officials “loathe” discussing the matter. The reported rationale is security: rising political violence, threats to justices, and a court system that has not kept them safe. On the surface, that sounds administrative. In practice, it is a familiar institutional move: power that refuses scrutiny while spending more taxpayer money to protect itself from the consequences of its own legitimacy crisis.

Who Actually Holds Power

The answer is not the public, and not the lawmakers who sign the check. The justices do. They sit atop an institution with extraordinary authority and very little day-to-day accountability. When they decide they need more security, the public is expected to pay and stay quiet. That is the real structure here: a branch of government that can insulate itself from political fallout while remaining largely unanswerable for the political climate it helps produce.

Security as Self-Protection, Not Transparency

The source makes clear that the court’s police force, long under 200 officers, may soon double. That is not a minor adjustment. It is an institutional buildout. Yet the court’s response to public concern is concealment, not explanation. Rep. Rosa DeLauro’s complaint cuts to the core: Congress funds the court, but the court does not come up and account for what it is doing with that money. That is not just bad optics. It is a governance failure wrapped in judicial prestige.

The Blame Is Traded Downward

The article gestures toward “political violence” and “threats,” but the deeper issue is how easily powerful institutions shift the frame from responsibility to vulnerability. The justices become the endangered party, which may be true in specific cases, but the institution itself remains shielded from harder questions: why its legitimacy is deteriorating, why its decisions have inflamed public anger, and why the public is asked to absorb the costs of a court that treats transparency as optional. When institutions help create the conditions of distrust, they do not get to present themselves as innocent bystanders to the backlash.

The Larger Pattern

This is what elite institutions do when authority starts to look fragile: they harden the perimeter, expand the security state around themselves, and reduce accountability to a budget line. The court’s police expansion is not just about protection. It is about hierarchy. The message is simple: the judiciary can demand deference, spend more, and reveal less. In a political system already defined by unequal accountability, the Supreme Court is not resisting the pattern. It is refining it.
