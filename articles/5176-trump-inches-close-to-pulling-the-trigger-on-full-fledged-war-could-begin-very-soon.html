Trump inches close to 'pulling the trigger' on 'full-fledged war': 'Could begin very soon'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 19, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Specter of War: Unpacking the Rhetoric and Reality

U.S. President Donald Trump looks on as he speaks to reporters onboard Air Force One, on travel from West Palm Beach, Florida, to Joint Base Andrews, Maryland, U.S., February 16, 2026. REUTERS/Elizabeth Frantz

As a fleet of U.S. warships barrel toward Iran, the Trump administration appears poised to “pull the trigger” on a “full-fledged war” at any moment, and may do so sooner than “most Americans realize,” Axios reported Wednesday.

“Trump’s military and rhetorical buildups make it hard for him to back down without major concessions from Iran on its nuclear program,” writes Axios’ Mike Allen in the outlet’s report Wednesday.

“It’s not in Trump’s nature, and his advisers don’t view the deployment of all that hardware as a bluff. With Trump, anything can happen. But all signs point to him pulling the trigger if talks fail.”

The Trump administration met with Iranian officials in Geneva, Switzerland on Tuesday in the hopes of reaching a deal to avoid further escalations, but according to Vice President JD Vance, those talks stalled due to Iranian officials refusing to “acknowledge” some of President Donald Trump’s “red lines.”

Now, according to sources who spoke with Axios on the condition of anonymity, the United States could be engaged in “a major war in the Middle East,” and “very soon,” Axios reported.

Both the USS Gerald Ford and the USS Abraham Lincoln – two massive aircraft carriers that each carry dozens of aircraft and crews of up to 5,690 – are currently near Iran, BBC Verify and AntiWar.com have reported.

With the aircraft carriers are dozens of warships and hundreds of fighter jets. And, according to Axios, more than 150 military cargo flights have “moved weapons systems and ammunition” to the region. Within the past 24 hours as of Wednesday morning, the Trump administration has also moved 50 additional fighter jets to the region.

Our Analysis:

The Specter of War: Unpacking the Rhetoric and Reality

In an era where the drumbeat of potential conflict seems louder and more incessant, Axios brings to light the Trump administration’s latest foray into the theater of brinkmanship with Iran. The article paints a picture of an administration teetering on the edge of initiating “a full-fledged war,” a scenario that should alarm any observer due to its profound implications. However, beneath the surface of Axios’ reporting lie layers of complexity and contradiction that demand a closer examination.

“A fleet of U.S. warships barrel toward Iran” – This imagery, potent and provocative, sets the stage. The deployment of the USS Gerald Ford and the USS Abraham Lincoln, alongside an armada of supporting vessels and aircraft, is not a mere flexing of military muscle; it is a statement of intent. Yet, one must question the wisdom and the endgame of such a maneuver. Is the show of force meant to coerce Iran into concessions, or is it a prelude to conflict?

The Trump Administration’s Calculus – Mike Allen of Axios suggests that the administration’s military and rhetorical buildups corner it into a position where backing down without significant Iranian concessions seems unlikely. This assessment, however, presupposes that the administration’s only or primary goal is to dismantle Iran’s nuclear program through force or intimidation. Such a view is reductive and ignores the multifaceted nature of international diplomacy, where bluster often serves as a prelude to negotiation, not war.

The Geneva Stalemate – The article notes that recent talks in Geneva between U.S. and Iranian officials hit a wall, citing Vice President JD Vance’s commentary on Iran’s refusal to acknowledge Trump’s “red lines.” This impasse is troubling but not insurmountable. Diplomacy is a marathon, not a sprint, characterized by setbacks and breakthroughs. To frame this stalemate as a harbinger of imminent conflict is to ignore history’s many lessons on the power of persistence in diplomacy.

The Anonymity Conundrum – Axios relies on sources who “spoke on the condition of anonymity” to claim that the U.S. could be “engaged in a major war in the Middle East, and very soon.” While anonymous sourcing is a staple of political journalism, it also raises questions about the motives and biases of the sources. Are these individuals privy to the innermost deliberations of the administration, or are they speculating based on the same public posturing observed by the rest of us?

The Implications of War – Most glaringly, the article skirts around the colossal implications of a new major war in the Middle East. The human cost, the economic fallout, the geopolitical upheaval – these are not mere footnotes in the narrative. Any discussion of potential conflict must grapple with these realities, not as abstract possibilities but as the most profound consequences of the rhetoric and actions of those in power.

In conclusion, while Axios’ reporting shines a light on a potentially perilous moment in U.S.-Iran relations, it also falls into the trap of sensationalism, skimming over the depths of diplomatic nuance in favor of bold proclamations. As observers, we must sift through this narrative, questioning and challenging the assumptions it makes, lest we find ourselves swept up in the tide of history without a compass. War, especially one so fraught with catastrophic potential, should be the last resort, not an inevitable conclusion drawn from a temporary alignment of ships in the sea.
