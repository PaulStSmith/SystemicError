The Window Between Trump and Whatever Comes Next

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 11, 20267 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Congress Cannot Govern Around a Presidential VetoThe Filibuster TestThe Democratic Field Taking ShapeThe Post-Trump Danger

Democrats are positioned to take the House in 2026 and may have a path to the Senate. That would not save American democracy—but it could create the opening required to rebuild it.

The 2026 midterms could produce something that has seemed increasingly unfamiliar in Washington: consequences.

Democrats are strongly positioned to contest control of the House, where they need only a small net gain. The Senate is substantially harder. Republicans entered the cycle with a 53–47 advantage, leaving Democrats needing four additional seats for an outright majority. The available races make that possible, but hardly comfortable. (House forecast comparison, Senate battleground overview)

A Democratic House victory is not inevitable. Gerrymandering has eliminated many competitive districts. Voter suppression remains effective precisely because it usually operates through administrative friction rather than cartoon villainy. Democrats also possess a supernatural ability to transform favorable political environments into consultant-enrichment programs.

Still, the fundamentals point toward significant Republican vulnerability. Trump is unpopular. His administration has attached itself to economic pain, institutional chaos and policies that become much less charming when their consequences arrive at someone’s own workplace, hospital, school or retirement account.

The Senate remains closer to a genuine toss-up because land votes in the United States. Tens of millions of Democratic voters can be efficiently packed into a few populous states while sparsely populated Republican states receive the same two senators each. Democrats must therefore win a national majority and perform electoral needlework across several very different states merely to approach parity.

But suppose they do it. Suppose Democrats win both chambers.

Then what?

Congress Cannot Govern Around a Presidential Veto

A Democratic Congress taking office in January 2027 would not control the executive branch. Trump would remain president for another two years.

That means Democrats could pass legislation, but Trump could veto it. They would not possess the two-thirds majorities necessary to override him.

This matters because there are two major democratic-repair bills waiting to be enacted.

The John R. Lewis Voting Rights Advancement Act would restore and modernize the Voting Rights Act’s preclearance protections, requiring jurisdictions with histories of discrimination to obtain federal approval before implementing certain election changes. (John R. Lewis Act overview)

The Freedom to Vote Act would establish national standards for federal elections, protect election administration, improve campaign-finance transparency and restrict partisan gerrymandering of congressional districts. (Freedom to Vote Act overview)

Democrats should pass both immediately upon taking Congress.

Trump would almost certainly veto them. Let him.

Force him to place his name beneath the proposition that jurisdictions with histories of racial discrimination should remain free to manipulate voting rules without federal supervision. Force every Republican member of Congress to vote publicly for partisan map-rigging and election subversion.

Legislative defeat can still establish responsibility. It can define the program for 2028 and prevent Republicans from later pretending that nobody knew what was at stake.

But Congress would possess more than symbolic power.

A Democratic House could subpoena officials, expose corruption, investigate interference with election administration and reconstruct records that the executive branch would prefer to bury. A Democratic Senate could stop Trump from placing still more lifetime operatives on the federal bench. Both chambers could use appropriations, oversight and confirmation authority to constrain further damage.

They could not complete the repairs. They could stop the vandals from working entirely unsupervised.

The Filibuster Test

If Democrats regain the presidency and retain Congress in 2028, they will face the choice they evaded during the Biden administration.

Voting-rights legislation cannot survive the modern Senate filibuster without sixty votes. Democrats will not have sixty votes. Therefore, they must either create a voting-rights exception, reform the filibuster or admit that preserving a Senate custom is more important to them than preserving representative government.

There can be no further performance in which fifty-something senators “support” voting rights while lamenting that the parliamentarian, Senate tradition or the ghost of Robert Byrd has forbidden action.

Republicans do not treat procedural rules as holy objects. They retain rules when those rules serve Republican power and discard them when they do not. Democrats’ unilateral reverence does not protect institutions. It permits institutional arson while ensuring that the fire department observes proper decorum.

A 2026 congressional victory must therefore become preparation for a 2028 governing trifecta. The bills should be drafted, hearings completed, constitutional arguments developed and vulnerable provisions equipped with severability clauses. Democratic candidates should be forced to state before the election whether they will permit the filibuster to kill democratic reform.

No more winning first and discovering principles of helplessness afterward.

The Democratic Field Taking Shape

The 2028 presidential primary is already underway, although nobody has yet attached the words “presidential campaign” to the presidential campaigning.

Gavin Newsom currently looks like the clearest early frontrunner. He has visited South Carolina repeatedly, raised money for state Democratic parties and acknowledged that he is considering a run. His national operation is being assembled beneath the respectable fiction that he is merely helping with the midterms. (PBS NewsHour)

Newsom’s advantage is simple: he fights. While much of the Democratic establishment responds to authoritarianism by requesting a sternly worded panel discussion, Newsom understands that political combat requires entering the combat.

His liability is equally obvious: California. Republicans will construct a national horror film from homelessness, housing costs, taxation, crime footage and every absurdity produced by San Francisco municipal government. Much of it will be dishonest. Enough of it will be real to make the attack effective.

Josh Shapiro offers the establishment a governor from Pennsylvania with proven appeal in the state Democrats most desperately need. He may appear highly electable in November while facing a brutal Democratic primary over Israel, Gaza, school vouchers and his relationship with the party’s left.

Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez would represent generational and ideological change. By 2028, she will have nearly a decade of congressional experience, an enormous small-dollar fundraising base and communication skills few Democrats can match. Her central task would be persuading older and risk-averse primary voters that nominating her is not an act of electoral self-harm.

Wes Moore may be the most interesting compromise candidate: young, charismatic, executive, a veteran and capable of presenting progressive government through competence rather than ideological theater. He currently lacks the national organization and hostile-state victories of his rivals, but he has considerable room to grow.

Gretchen Whitmer, Andy Beshear, Pete Buttigieg and JB Pritzker all possess plausible lanes. Kamala Harris retains an organization and loyal constituency, although convincing Democrats to rerun 2024 would be difficult. Rahm Emanuel apparently believes the country has been waiting for the triumphant return of 2010.

Jon Ossoff is the wildcard. If he survives a difficult Georgia election in 2026, he can present himself as young, nationally tested and demonstrably capable of winning under hostile conditions. A convincing reelection could transform him from “promising senator” into a serious presidential contender almost overnight.

My provisional field has Newsom as the early frontrunner, Shapiro as the establishment alternative, AOC as the strongest insurgent and Moore as the candidate most likely to surge once voters begin searching for someone outside the initial duel.

The Post-Trump Danger

Whoever wins the Democratic nomination may face a Republican Party undergoing a succession war.

Trump is mortal. Trumpism has been embedded into the party’s electorate, media, fundraising and institutional apparatus.

No successor can easily reproduce Trump’s personal authority. Several will reproduce his cruelty, paranoia and contempt for law. They will compete to prove who inherited the movement by accusing everyone else of betraying its founder.

That could leave Democrats nationally dominant for several election cycles. But “dominant” is not synonymous with “secure.” Republicans would retain gerrymandered districts, state governments, federal judges, wealthy patrons and a media system capable of converting every inconvenience into evidence of national collapse.

More importantly, the Republican succession crisis may eventually produce something more dangerous than Trump: an authoritarian with discipline.

Trump is impulsive, narcissistic, intellectually vacant and frequently distracted by whoever praised him most recently. His incompetence limits the damage he can inflict even as his recklessness multiplies it. The next candidate may possess the same contempt for democracy without possessing the attention span of a Labrador loose in a butcher shop.

Democrats may therefore receive a narrow interval between Trump and a more competent successor.

They cannot spend it congratulating themselves for being the only nationally viable party. They cannot mistake Republican fragmentation for the permanent defeat of authoritarianism. And they cannot assume that voters will remain loyal merely because the alternative is appalling.

They must improve material conditions, protect elections, dismantle partisan gerrymandering, restore voting rights, defend election workers and construct institutions capable of surviving an executive who regards law as an obstacle.

The 2026 midterms may give Democrats the power to halt the demolition and document the crimes. A 2028 trifecta could give them the power to rebuild.

But only if they understand the difference between occupying office and exercising power.

The coming Democratic opportunity is not a reward. It is borrowed time.

If Democrats use it properly, they can make the next authoritarian assault substantially harder. If they waste it on civility theater, procedural worship and bipartisan fantasies, they will merely tidy the building before handing the keys to Trump’s more competent heir.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
