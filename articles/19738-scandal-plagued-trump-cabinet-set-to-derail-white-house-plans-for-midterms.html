The White House’s plan to dispatch members of President Donald Trump’s Cabinet to competitive districts for the midterm elections to aid Republican efforts is the best thing Democrats could hope for, said MS NOW Producer Steve Benen.

“[President Donald Trump’s] first-term Cabinet was a mess,” said Benen. “… But a month into the sixth year of Trump’s presidency, it’s probably fair to say that his second-term Cabinet is worse.”

Eleven months ago, New York Times’ Frank Bruni criticized Trump’s fledgling Cabinet in a scathing report, outlining the fact that Trump was not hiring for aptitude or intelligence. Trump, Bruni said, wanted bootlicks.

“Trump chose people for senior administration positions not because they had demonstrated the skills and disposition that those jobs required, not because they had paid their dues, not because they had proved their mettle. He wanted provocateurs. He wanted sycophants. … Competence didn’t enter the equation, so competence isn’t among the results. He got exactly what he paid for, and now a nation is paying the price,” Bruni wrote.

One year later Benen said the results are clear.

“Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem is at the center of a variety of ongoing controversies and faces bipartisan calls for her ouster,” said Benen. “Attorney General Pam Bondi recently humiliated herself during a congressional hearing. Around the same time, Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. talked about his past history of snorting cocaine off toilet seats against a backdrop of systemic problems at the federal department he ostensibly leads.”

But there’s more, said Benen.

Director of National Intelligence Tulsi Gabbard is at the center of overlapping controversies, while Benen describes Pete Hegseth as the “scandal-plagued nominee who became a scandal-plagued Pentagon chief.” Office of Management and Budget head Russ Vought is making enemies with Americans and his own party.

“And did I mention Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick? Because even some Republicans have raised concerns about his extensive ties to Jeffrey Epstein,” said Benen. “What’s more, this is just a sampling based on recent developments.”

Benen points out that last year, an NBC News poll was already finding a 52 percent majority of Americans disappointed with the people Trump had hired. This was the highest number the network had found across the past four administrations.

“It’s a safe bet that if that same question were included in a national survey now, the number would be even higher,” said Benen.

Our Analysis:

Trump’s Cabinet: A Masterclass in Ineptitude

The latest strategy from the White House to deploy members of President Donald Trump’s Cabinet to swing districts, ostensibly to bolster Republican efforts in the upcoming midterm elections, is being heralded as an unwitting gift to the Democrats. Steve Benen of MS NOW articulates a sentiment that is hard to ignore: Trump’s Cabinet, rife with controversy and incompetence, is arguably the best campaign asset the Democrats could wish for.

Trump’s penchant for loyalty over competence is costing the nation dearly. Eleven months ago, Frank Bruni of the New York Times delivered a blistering critique of Trump’s then-fledgling Cabinet. Bruni’s insights have proven prophetic, highlighting a fundamental flaw in Trump’s hiring philosophy: a preference for “provocateurs” and “sycophants” over individuals with the aptitude and intelligence necessary for high-stakes governance.

Fast forward to today, and the situation has devolved from bad to worse. Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem is embroiled in controversies that span the political spectrum, with bipartisan calls for her resignation. Attorney General Pam Bondi’s recent congressional hearing was nothing short of a humiliation, laying bare her inadequacies for all to see. Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.’s admission of past cocaine use starkly contrasts with the systemic issues plaguing the department he leads, illustrating a profound disconnect from the gravity of his role.

The list of Cabinet members whose tenure has been marred by scandal or controversy is alarmingly long. Director of National Intelligence Tulsi Gabbard and Pentagon chief Pete Hegseth are entangled in controversies that undermine the integrity of their offices. Meanwhile, Russ Vought, the head of the Office of Management and Budget, is alienating Americans and fellow Republicans alike, further fracturing an already divided party.

The most damning indictment of Trump’s Cabinet comes perhaps from Benen’s mention of Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, whose connections to Jeffrey Epstein have raised alarms even within Republican circles. This is indicative of a broader trend of questionable judgment and ethical blindness that seems to characterize Trump’s picks for key governmental positions.

An NBC News poll from last year found that a 52 percent majority of Americans were disappointed with Trump’s hiring decisions, a figure that, according to Benen, would likely be higher if measured today. This dissatisfaction underscores a growing public awareness and concern over the competency and integrity of those at the helm of the nation’s affairs.

In their misguided attempt to sway the midterm elections, the dispatching of Trump’s Cabinet members to competitive districts may indeed be the boon Democrats didn’t know they needed. It serves as a stark reminder of the administration’s failures and the dire need for competent, ethical leadership.

In essence, Trump’s Cabinet has become a parable for his presidency: a testament to the dangers of valuing loyalty over capability, and a case study in how not to govern. As the midterm elections approach, the American public is left to ponder the costs of such mismanagement and the importance of rectifying these systemic errors in governance.
