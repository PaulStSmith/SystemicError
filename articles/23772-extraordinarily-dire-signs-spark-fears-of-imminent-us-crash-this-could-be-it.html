'Extraordinarily dire' signs spark fears of imminent US crash: 'This could be it'

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

The Crash Would Not Come From Yemen. It Would Come From Washington.

A Chokepoint Exposes the Machine

The immediate facts are grim: Houthi control around the Bab al-Mandeb Strait threatens a major shipping route, an attack from Iraq damaged Saudi Arabia’s East-West oil pipeline, and prolonged disruption at the Strait of Hormuz has already driven fuel prices sharply higher. That is enough context. The political question is why the United States is so vulnerable to shocks its own government helped create.

Trump Owns the War

The actors disrupting shipping and oil infrastructure have agency. But they do not hold power over American war policy. President Donald Trump does.

The source identifies his war against Iran as a central cause of the inflationary pressure now colliding with damaged energy routes and weakened supply buffers. Treating the potential crash primarily as a “Middle East development” would therefore conceal the decisive chain of responsibility. Geography did not choose escalation. Militants did not commit the United States to war. The Trump administration did.

If inflation, unemployment, and market losses follow, they will not be inexplicable fallout from an unruly region. They will be foreseeable domestic consequences of a presidential decision.

The Cushions Were Not a Strategy

The economy remained relatively stable because temporary protections absorbed part of the damage: China reduced oil purchases, Washington drew from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, and Saudi Arabia moved barrels through its East-West pipeline. Those protections are now weakening or disappearing.

That distinction matters. The administration did not eliminate the risk created by war; it spent down buffers and benefited from favorable conditions elsewhere. Calling that stability mistakes delayed consequences for competent governance.

The reported loss of Trump’s ability to “market manipulate” is equally revealing. Reassuring statements and political messaging can postpone panic, but they cannot reopen a strait, repair a pipeline, refill a depleted reserve, or make diesel affordable.

An Economy Balanced on an AI Story

The second vulnerability is domestic and self-inflicted. According to the cited Deloitte finding, AI-related technology investment supplied roughly half of real American GDP growth since 2025. That is not broad prosperity. It is extreme dependence on one investment boom.

If AI companies slow spending while fuel costs hammer transportation, agriculture, manufacturing, and household budgets, the two shocks will reinforce each other. One removes a major source of headline growth; the other raises costs throughout the physical economy.

The political class has treated rising asset values and concentrated technology investment as proof of national strength. The reporting instead suggests an economy whose apparent resilience rests on a speculative sector continuing to expand while consumers absorb wartime prices.

“Confluence” Is a Word That Hides Decisions

Describing these pressures as a confluence of factors is analytically convenient and politically evasive. It places a presidential war, corporate investment choices, militant attacks, fragile shipping routes, and exhausted public safeguards into one weather system—as though they simply converged.

They did not. Different actors made different decisions with radically different levels of power. The Houthis can disrupt a chokepoint. Technology executives can reduce investment. But the federal government chooses war, manages strategic reserves, shapes sanctions and energy policy, and decides how much economic pain Americans will be forced to absorb.

The article’s crash framing risks turning accountability into market fortune-telling. Whether equities collapse tomorrow is less important than the structure already exposed: households carry the costs while political leaders gamble with military escalation and markets depend on a narrow technology boom to disguise underlying weakness.

War Abroad, Discipline at Home

This is the larger pattern. American leaders sell war as an exercise of strength, then describe its domestic costs as external shocks. Fuel spikes become the work of distant militants. Market instability becomes investor anxiety. Unemployment becomes a Federal Reserve problem.

That sequence launders policy through consequence. The people who authorize escalation retain institutional power; the people buying gasoline, driving diesel trucks, or losing jobs receive the bill.

A crash, if it comes, will not prove that Yemen controls the American economy. It will show how readily Washington exposes that economy to predictable harm—and how quickly its political and media systems rename responsibility as uncertainty.
