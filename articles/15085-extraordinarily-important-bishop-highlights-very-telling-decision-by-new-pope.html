'Extraordinarily important': Bishop highlights 'very telling' decision by new pope

One MSNBC guest believes there is a very telling reason Bishop Robert Prevost chose the name Pope Leo XIV.

Morning Joe guest Bishop Robert Barron, from the diocese of Winona, Rochester in Minnesota, pondered the name which was last selected by a pontiff in 1878.

Barron also grew up just 25 minutes from Pope Leo, and he is still in awe that there is an American Pope.

“I was one of those people saying it will never be an American. His name was mentioned [before the conclave]. Everyone knew his qualities, but everybody said it won't be an American,” Barron recalled.

“After I've gotten over the shock of it, and last night with the adrenaline going, I had trouble getting to sleep. I kept drifting back to his name. His name, I think, is extraordinarily important.”

Barron went through his thoughts, “If he was, you know, simply continuity with Francis, why wouldn't he be Francis II? He didn't choose that name. He could have chosen a name like John Paul III, like John the 24th, and would have given a very clear indication I'm on this side or that side.”

“The fact that he reached back to the very end of the 19th century to this figure, Leo XIII, is very telling. Leo was someone who, at a pivotal moment, engaged modernity in a creative way. The church had said no initially to the, you know, political reforms of the 18th century, the philosophical innovations of the 19th century, especially Marxism,” Barron said.

“Simply said, no Leo gave a very nuanced response that was both affirming and challenging, and therefore he sets the tone, I think, for a very intelligent creative engagement for the world.”

Our Analysis:

The Strategic Papal Name Game: Unpacking the Significance of Pope Leo XIV

In a recent appearance on MSNBC's Morning Joe, Bishop Robert Barron provided a fascinating analysis of the newly elected Pope Leo XIV’s choice of papal name, a decision that may seem trivial to the uninitiated but is loaded with ecclesiastical and historical significance. Barron’s insights into the implications of this choice are noteworthy, not just for their depth but also for the broader commentary they offer on the current state of the Catholic Church and its interaction with modernity. However, a closer examination of his comments and the situation reveals a layered narrative that demands a more critical lens.

The Name Game: A Symbolic Gesture or a Calculated Move?

First, let’s cut through the ecclesiastical euphoria surrounding the election of an American pope—a milestone, yes, but not the crux of the matter. The real meat of this discussion lies in why Bishop Robert Prevost, now Pope Leo XIV, eschewed names that would have signalled clear continuity with his immediate predecessors in favor of a name that harks back to a pontiff whose reign ended in 1878. Barron's musings, while insightful, beg a sharper question: In reaching back nearly a century and a half, is Leo XIV signaling a nuanced approach to modern challenges, or is this a veiled attempt to romanticize and repackage conservative responses to these challenges?

The Ghost of Leo XIII: Progressive or Protector?

Barron characterizes Leo XIII as someone who engaged with modernity in a "creative way." This is a neat, somewhat sanitized summary of a complex papacy. Leo XIII, indeed, offered the church’s response to the turmoil of modernization, notably through his encyclical Rerum Novarum, which is often hailed as the birth of modern Catholic social teaching. However, to frame Leo XIII's engagement with modernity as purely innovative glosses over the inherent conservatism of his responses. His was a balancing act, trying to steer the church through the shoals of modernity without capitulating to the era’s radical changes.

Thus, the selection of Leo XIV as a papal name is not just a nod to creative engagement but a potential signal of a desire to return to a period where the church sought to influence modernity on its own terms, rather than adapting or embracing it outright. The choice suggests a preference for a path that is both affirming of the church's traditions and challenging to the secular currents of the day.

Beyond Names: The Challenge of Modernity Revisited

It’s telling that Barron, and by extension, Leo XIV, view the engagement with modernity as a pivotal challenge. The church finds itself once again at a crossroads, grappling with issues ranging from the role of technology and social media to climate change, social justice, and the ongoing fallout from the sexual abuse crisis. The invocation of Leo XIII's legacy implies that Leo XIV may seek solutions in a nuanced, perhaps conservative, engagement with these issues rather than radical shifts or reforms.

A Reflection on Ecclesiastical Politics

Barron's reflections, steeped in admiration for the new pope, inadvertently shine a light on the intricate dance of ecclesiastical politics. The decision to bypass names that would have indicated a clear allegiance to the progressive or conservative wings of the church suggests a strategic positioning by Leo XIV. It’s an attempt to carve a middle path, perhaps, but also a reminder that the papacy, for all its spiritual significance, is not immune to the calculations and maneuverings typical of any position of power.

In Conclusion: A Calculated Leap into the Past?

In sum, the choice of the name Pope Leo XIV is a deeply symbolic act that signals a potential approach to the papacy: one that respects tradition, seeks engagement on its own terms, and perhaps, cautiously navigates the turbulent waters of modernity. However, while Barron views this as an "extraordinarily important" decision, we must not lose sight of the fact that names, while powerful, are but a precursor to action. Leo XIV's papacy will ultimately be judged not by the name he chose but by the actions he takes and the extent to which he can truly engage with and respond to the complexities of the modern world. In this, let us hope the spirit of innovation triumphs over the allure of nostalgia.
