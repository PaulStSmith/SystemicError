'Let the wheels fall off': Insiders say ousting Wiles could accelerate White House chaos

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 16, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Bill Kristol and Andrew Egger of The Bulwark believe removing White House chief of staff Susie Wiles could accelerate disorder inside the Trump administration. Their assessment is speculative: Politico reportedly said President Donald Trump had asked whether Wiles should be replaced and by whom, but the article does not establish that he decided to fire her.

Egger credits Wiles with limiting infighting, leaks and operational breakdowns. Kristol offers a harsher distinction: she has managed the White House efficiently without meaningfully restraining Trump, Stephen Miller or Pete Hegseth. Both suggest that her authority is already weakening, citing the hiring of Katie Zacharia over Wiles’s preferred candidate, Scott Jennings.

Kristol also relayed Minnesota Gov. Tim Walz’s account that Wiles said she could control White House operations only from 7 a.m. to 7 p.m.; afterward, Trump spoke with whomever he wanted and acted as he chose. The reporting provides no response from Wiles or the White House and no confirmed timetable for a personnel change.

The president holds the power

Wiles may control schedules, information flow and staff discipline, but Trump controls whether her decisions matter. The Zacharia appointment is significant not because it proves Wiles will be fired, but because it reportedly shows the president’s circle can bypass the official charged with running the operation.

That is the governing structure beneath the palace gossip. Wiles has administrative agency only while Trump lends her his authority. Trump chooses the advisers, rewards access and can erase her influence without formally removing her. If the operation deteriorates after her departure, responsibility would not belong to an abstract “chaos.” It would belong to the president who discarded the manager and to the officials who helped circumvent her.

Competence in service of what

The article’s most useful distinction is between restraining power and making power run smoothly. According to Kristol, earlier chiefs of staff sometimes checked Trump’s impulses. Wiles’s model, as Egger describes it, is closer to letting Trump act while containing the resulting disorder.

That may make the machinery quieter without making its decisions wiser. Fewer leaks and cleaner travel schedules are managerial achievements, not evidence of responsible government. An efficient chief of staff can reduce internal friction while increasing the administration’s capacity to carry out decisions that would otherwise encounter resistance.

Kristol points to a reported decline in White House approval as evidence that operational discipline has not produced political success. Whatever weight that figure deserves, the larger point stands: competence cannot be judged apart from the ends it serves.

Chaos is not an independent actor

Coverage of White House staffing often treats disorder like weather—a system rolling in when personalities clash. That framing launders agency. A president who encourages competition for personal favor, consults outside the formal chain of command and overrides his chief of staff is not merely afflicted by dysfunction. He is maintaining conditions in which dysfunction predictably flourishes.

Wiles should not be recast as the powerless adult in the room. She accepted and enforced a model built around Trump’s freedom of action. But blaming her for failing to control him would invert the hierarchy. The elected president possesses the institutional power; the chief of staff operates by delegation.

The seductive hope for collapse

Egger’s wish that Trump fire Wiles because it would hurt him exposes another bad political habit: treating administrative breakdown as satisfying because it may damage an opponent. The costs of a malfunctioning White House do not remain inside the president’s poll numbers. They spread through agencies, policy execution and every institution forced to respond to erratic decisions.

There is no public-interest victory in replacing disciplined harmful governance with undisciplined harmful governance. One may be easier to expose, but the exposure is purchased with consequences imposed on people who never entered the factional fight.

Personal rule consumes its managers

The larger pattern is a presidency organized around proximity to one person rather than durable lines of authority. Under that arrangement, loyalty never becomes security. An aide’s usefulness lasts only until independent judgment, competing access or simple exhaustion makes that aide inconvenient.

Whether Wiles remains or goes, the reported struggle reveals the same system: formal authority is conditional, internal discipline depends on presidential indulgence, and personnel become disposable explanations for outcomes created at the top. Firing the mechanic may make the engine knock louder. It does not absolve the person at the wheel.
