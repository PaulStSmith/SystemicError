Three Supreme Court Justices could be forced out at once under plan

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 7, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe headline outruns the planPower without accountabilityReform is not expulsionThe missing coalitionSpectacle substitutes for structure

What happened

AlterNet reports that Maryland Democratic Rep. Johnny Olszewski proposed a constitutional amendment requiring Supreme Court justices to retire after 18 years. If enacted as described, it would apply simultaneously to Chief Justice John Roberts and Justices Samuel Alito and Clarence Thomas.

The outlet says Democrats are expected to control Congress with at least 250 House seats and 54 Senate seats. That would let them consider court-reform measures, but it would not let them enact this amendment alone: constitutional amendments require two-thirds of each chamber—290 House votes and 67 Senate votes—followed by ratification in 38 states.

House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries said Democrats should “clean up the mess at the Supreme Court,” but the reporting does not establish that party leaders adopted Olszewski’s proposal or assembled the bipartisan and state-level support it would require.

The headline outruns the plan

“Could be forced out” is formally possible and politically misleading. The three justices would leave only after clearing constitutional thresholds far beyond the reported Democratic majorities. A proposal being newly discussable is not the same as its targets being remotely close to removal.

The story converts control of the congressional agenda into control of the Constitution. Democrats would possess the institutional power to hold hearings, schedule votes and compel members to take positions. They would not possess the power to impose this amendment.

Power without accountability

The underlying imbalance is real. Roberts, Alito and Thomas exercise life-tenured judicial power, while elected officials face recurring elections and ordinary legislation can stall behind the Senate’s 60-vote threshold. AlterNet links renewed reform efforts to a conservative majority that has overturned longstanding precedents and expanded executive power.

But “clean up the mess” is not a governing program. Jeffries named a grievance; Olszewski supplied one possible mechanism. Neither, on the evidence presented, showed a viable coalition. Political leaders often benefit from collapsing that distinction: supporters hear a promise of structural change while leadership retains ample room to blame constitutional arithmetic later.

Reform is not expulsion

An 18-year limit would establish a general tenure rule, not simply punish three disfavored justices. Its immediate effect would nevertheless be partisan because the sitting members exceeding that limit are three conservatives. Any serious defense of the proposal must address both facts.

The relevant question is not whether Democrats dislike the Court’s decisions. It is whether lifetime tenure concentrates too much public power without regular democratic renewal—and whether a uniform term limit is a legitimate structural answer. Framing the measure principally as a way to force out named justices weakens that case by making institutional reform look like a one-time partisan purge.

The missing coalition

The decisive actors would extend well beyond House Democrats. Republican senators, additional House members and legislators across 38 states would have to enable the amendment. The article supplies no evidence that this coalition exists. It even notes that Republican Rep. Tom Barrett advanced a separate 20-year proposal, then lost reelection, but one proposal does not establish broad bipartisan momentum.

That absence is the central political fact, not a procedural footnote. The people with blocking power are being treated as scenery while the weaker faction is portrayed as capable of immediate transformation.

Spectacle substitutes for structure

This is how structural politics gets flattened into personnel drama. A difficult argument about tenure, judicial supremacy and constitutional design becomes an enticing scenario in which three famous officials abruptly lose their seats.

The larger pattern is institutional evasion through exaggerated possibility. Politicians receive credit for entertaining reform, media receive a dramatic conflict, and the officials holding life-tenured power remain protected by thresholds the story itself shows are nowhere near being met. The Court may face intensified political scrutiny. On AlterNet’s evidence, Roberts, Alito and Thomas do not face imminent forced retirement.
