MAGA election loser suffers another humiliation

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 19, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Bo French’s latest rejection came not from voters but from River Crest Country Club, the Fort Worth institution whose board terminated his membership effective immediately. In a Nov. 10 letter obtained by Politico, the club cited “reasons relating to the reputation and general welfare of the Club.” River Crest did not specify what conduct prompted the decision, leaving any connection to French’s racist campaign rhetoric unconfirmed.

The expulsion followed French’s failed Republican campaign for Texas railroad commissioner, during which he built his candidacy around hostility toward immigration and questioned the Americanness of nonwhite Texans. Losing access to an elite golf club is hardly a public sanction. But it is another measure of how thoroughly French converted racial provocation into political and social liability.

A private institution protects its reputation

River Crest’s board had the power to remove French, and it used it. The club, founded in 1911, markets itself as a Fort Worth “who’s who” and operates a 6,368-yard golf course known as a training ground for prominent golfers.

That institutional self-image matters because the board framed French’s removal around the club’s interests, not any public principle. River Crest’s general manager declined to discuss the former member or the board’s reasoning, citing a policy against commenting on past, current or prospective members and club operations.

The available reporting therefore does not establish why French was expelled. Politico identified his campaign controversy as a possible explanation, not a confirmed cause. The distinction is essential: the club condemned nothing publicly. It acted to protect its “reputation and general welfare” and then withheld the facts behind that judgment.

French targeted students he could not identify

The controversy began when French reacted to a photograph of cheering University of Texas football fans that included many students of South Asian descent. He treated their appearance as grounds to question their place in American life.

The photograph did not reveal anyone’s citizenship or nationality. As National Review noted while denouncing French’s remarks, state law requires 90 percent of the university’s students to be Texans. His attack relied on a racial inference: people who looked South Asian were recast as foreigners, regardless of who they actually were.

French’s online defenders tried to redirect the dispute toward the university—claiming that it served too many foreign students or discriminated against white Texans. Those arguments did not fit the relevant facts. National Review observed that the university had no preferential admissions standards for Indian Americans. It also noted that French had previously joined other Texas Republicans in supporting an admissions system designed to eliminate consideration of race.

That retreat exposed the misdirection. Faced with criticism for judging students by appearance, French and his defenders shifted attention to admissions policy. The weaker actors in the story—the photographed students—were made to answer for an imagined institutional offense, while the candidate who racialized them posed as the aggrieved party.

Condemnation without ambiguity

Some Texas Republicans criticized French, with varying degrees of force. Bishop Michael Olson of Fort Worth was more direct.

“This is racist and sinful rhetoric that seeks to harm and to divide,” Olson wrote. “It is disturbing that it comes from a candidate for public office. An apology is called for.”

That rebuke prompted anger from MAGA supporters, illustrating the political function of French’s rhetoric. The point was not a serious inquiry into enrollment rules or citizenship. It was to make racial appearance a test of belonging—and then punish anyone who named the tactic plainly.

French failed to win the state office he sought. River Crest has now decided it no longer wants him as a member. The country club’s silence prevents its action from becoming a clear institutional repudiation of his campaign, but its letter still records the practical verdict: French’s presence had become, in the board’s judgment, incompatible with the club’s reputation and welfare.
