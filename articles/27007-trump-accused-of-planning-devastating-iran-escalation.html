Trump accused of planning 'devastating' Iran escalation

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 31, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Sen. Chris Murphy said he had been briefed on a post-midterm plan by President Donald Trump’s administration to escalate military operations against Iran. Murphy withheld operational details but called the plan “insane,” predicted “devastating escalation,” and said it would likely undermine the rule of law without achieving its objectives.

The report cites CNN’s earlier account that sufficient U.S. forces were already positioned in the region for attacks extending beyond limited strikes on replaceable Iranian military assets. It also quotes political scientist John Mearsheimer arguing that the United States lacked leverage, faced munitions constraints, and risked Iranian retaliation against Gulf oil and desalination infrastructure. The article supplies no administration response, documentary war plan, or independent account of Murphy’s briefing, so the plan’s precise scope and legal basis remain unestablished.

The power is in the White House

Murphy’s warning is politically important, but he is not the actor directing the military. The institutional power belongs to Trump and the executive branch officials preparing, recommending, authorizing, and executing military operations.

That distinction matters because political coverage routinely turns an impending use of state violence into a dispute among commentators. A senator calls a plan reckless; a political scientist calls the existing campaign a defeat; strategists calculate electoral damage. But the central fact is simpler: the administration reportedly possesses the forces and operational machinery to widen the conflict. Everyone else quoted is describing the consequences of power they do not control.

An election is not a legal authorization

The most damning element is the reported timing. CNN’s account, as summarized by AlterNet, says Trump intended to escalate after the midterms, when electoral consequences would be less immediate. If accurate, that makes the election a scheduling constraint on war-making—not a democratic mandate for it.

The report does not establish exactly what legal authority the administration would claim, and Murphy’s warning about the rule of law is a prediction rather than a disclosed legal analysis. Still, postponing a consequential military decision until voters have less leverage would reveal a governing logic in which public accountability is treated as an obstacle to maneuver around.

“Winning” conceals the actual stakes

The framing of a plan to “win” the war launders an enormous burden of proof. The reporting identifies no attainable political objective, no defined end state and no explanation of how broader attacks would produce either. Murphy explicitly said the plan would fail to achieve its objectives, while Mearsheimer argued that Washington had no available route to regain leverage.

Meanwhile, the foreseeable costs are concrete: another cycle of attacks, potential retaliation against regional infrastructure and possible damage to facilities essential for oil production and drinking water. Those consequences would be imposed by governments and military commands, then endured largely by people who had no role in choosing the escalation.

The coverage leaves crucial gaps

AlterNet’s account is built almost entirely from other reporting and hostile assessments of the administration’s strategy. It offers no direct evidence of the plan, no details from the briefing and no response from the officials accused of preparing it. Those are substantial evidentiary limits, not reasons to dismiss the warning.

But the article also weakens its own case by leaning on electoral speculation and unrelated criticism of Trump’s handling of Russia and Ukraine. That material shifts attention from the question that demands scrutiny: what military action is being prepared, who authorized its preparation, under what law, and with what assessment of civilian and regional consequences?

War power thrives on withheld answers

The larger pattern is executive war-making protected by secrecy, delayed accountability and the vocabulary of strategy. Officials can prepare escalation behind classified briefings while the public receives fragments from legislators, anonymous sources and outside analysts. By the time operational details become visible, the machinery may already be deployed.

That structure rewards the decision-makers and burdens everyone else. The president controls the timetable. Military planners control the operational information. Congress is reduced to scattered warnings unless it uses its own powers. Voters encounter the policy as rumor before an election and consequence afterward. The systemic error is not confusion about who is responsible; it is a political order that lets those preparing war conceal the choice until resisting it becomes harder.
