Trump has finally united Americans with one simple word

Based on an article by Joe Conason

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

The word is “corruption.” AlterNet argues that Donald Trump has helped unite Americans around it: cited polls found overwhelming majorities across party lines describing corruption as endemic or systemic, while separate surveys showed substantial concern about Trump’s use of presidential power for personal profit.

The outlet cites an August Reuters/Ipsos poll in which nearly 70% said Trump uses presidential power “inappropriately” for personal gain, a UMass-Amherst poll in which 53% called the presidency the federal government’s most corrupt element, and an Economist/YouGov poll in which respondents most frequently chose “corrupt” to describe him. AlterNet contends that Democrats could benefit in the midterms but warns that doing so credibly would require reforms within their own ranks.

Power has an address

General disgust with “Washington” can obscure responsibility. The presidency commands executive agencies, controls appointments and grants extraordinary access to state power. Congress, meanwhile, can investigate, legislate and impose financial-disclosure rules. These institutions are not spectators to corruption; they determine whether conflicts are exposed, constrained or normalized.

The polling described by AlterNet matters because respondents were not merely blaming an abstract government. In the cited UMass-Amherst survey, a majority identified the presidency as its most corrupt component. That finding directs scrutiny toward the office with the greatest concentration of individual federal power—and toward Trump’s reported use of that office.

Consensus is not accountability

A public can overwhelmingly perceive corruption while institutions continue permitting it. Ninety-seven percent calling corruption systemic, as reported in separate UMass-Amherst and Brennan Center surveys, does not itself produce an investigation, a prohibition or a penalty.

The decisive actors remain officeholders. Republicans surrounding Trump have the power to scrutinize his conduct or shield it. Democrats have the power to pursue enforceable reforms or reduce corruption to campaign language. Voters can change congressional control, but elected officials write the rules and decide whether oversight has consequences.

The bipartisan escape hatch

AlterNet acknowledges misconduct across party lines, then argues that Trump-era Republican conduct is unusually brazen and disproportionate. That distinction is essential. “Both parties” can be technically accurate and politically deceptive when it collapses differences in scale, proximity to power and institutional response.

Democratic stock trading is therefore neither irrelevant nor an excuse to blur responsibility. It is a test. A party asking voters to punish Republican corruption cannot preserve profitable conflicts for its own members without weakening both its case and any reform it claims to support.

Investigation is not conviction

AlterNet points to California Representative Robert Garcia’s promise that a Democratic House would investigate what he calls the “Trump crime family.” That is a partisan characterization and a proposed investigation, not a finding of criminal guilt.

The necessary distinction does not require timidity. Congress can examine financial relationships, conflicts of interest and uses of presidential authority without pretending the outcome is already established. Oversight becomes credible through evidence, public findings and rules that apply beyond one target—not through slogans that outrun the record.

Corruption thrives on abstraction

The larger pattern is institutional evasion. Political coverage often treats corruption as atmospheric decay: everyone smells it, nobody caused it, and responsibility dissolves into public cynicism. But corruption is not weather. It consists of identifiable decisions by people who possess power, assisted by officials who decline to restrain them.

AlterNet’s strongest point is not that Trump accidentally restored civic consciousness. It is that reported conduct associated with the country’s most powerful office has made corruption harder to discuss as a vague national defect. The remaining question is whether Congress will impose durable constraints—or merely convert public anger into another election-season asset.
