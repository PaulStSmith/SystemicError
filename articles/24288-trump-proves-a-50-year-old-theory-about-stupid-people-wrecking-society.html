Trump proves a 50-year-old theory about 'stupid people' wrecking society

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

What happened

AlterNet reports that New Republic writer Ross Rosenfield used Italian historian Carlo Cipolla’s 1976 theory of human stupidity to interpret Donald Trump’s administration. Rosenfield argues that Cipolla’s warning becomes especially relevant when people who harm others without benefiting themselves acquire control over armies, monetary policy, media influence, and regulatory institutions.

Rosenfield points to the administration’s conduct involving Iran, public health, artificial intelligence, and cryptocurrency, alongside Republican attitudes about January 6 and the 2020 election. His conclusion is cautious: the United States may be entering the institutional decline Cipolla described, but the eventual extent of that decline remains unknown.

Power Is the Material Fact

The useful part of Cipolla’s framework is not the insult. It is the recognition that destructive behavior changes scale when backed by the state.

Trump does not merely hold foolish opinions. As president, he directs an administration with military, regulatory, financial, and communications power. An inept private citizen can cause limited damage. An inept health secretary, in Rosenfield’s account, can weaken protections affecting millions. Institutional authority turns bad judgment into public policy.

“Stupidity” Hides Agency

The article’s central framing is also its central weakness. Calling political actors stupid can disguise decisions as cognitive accidents. It shifts attention from who appointed officials, who supports the administration, who weakens oversight, and who benefits from deregulation or public confusion.

Cipolla distinguishes “stupid” actors from “bandits” who profit while harming others. But political systems routinely join the two: powerful officials can produce reckless outcomes while industries gain from weakened regulation. The reporting mentions that possibility without examining which interests benefit from the administration’s approach to AI, cryptocurrency, health policy, or war.

The Republican Party Is an Enabler

Rosenfield cites polling in which only 20 percent of Republicans described January 6 as a violent insurrection, while 71 percent denied that Joe Biden won the 2020 election. Those figures do not establish why respondents hold those views, but they show that the problem described here is larger than one officeholder.

Trump possesses executive power, yet a political party sustains that power by validating his account of reality. Treating this as a mass outbreak of stupidity lets party officials, media figures, and institutional allies fade into the crowd. Their choices deserve individual scrutiny because organized denial is politically more consequential than private ignorance.

The Consequences Are Institutional

The foreseeable damage is not simply that government makes embarrassing mistakes. It is that institutions meant to constrain error become instruments for spreading it. Regulatory neglect exposes the public to risks; distorted historical narratives protect those responsible for political violence; military misjudgment places lives under decisions the public cannot easily reverse.

Rosenfield says it may be too early to know whether the United States is on Cipolla’s path of decline. That uncertainty is warranted. But uncertainty about the final destination does not erase the conduct already described or the officials empowered to carry it out.

A System Built to Amplify Failure

The larger pattern is a political order that concentrates enormous power while encouraging everyone to discuss its use as personality. “Stupidity” supplies a memorable label, but it is not an adequate theory of responsibility.

Systems fail when powerful people make damaging decisions, aligned institutions enable them, and public language converts agency into bewilderment. The scandal is not that destructive actors exist. It is that parties, offices, and industries can place state machinery in their hands—and then invite the country to mistake the result for mere confusion.
