White House official dodges questions as China calls bluff on Trump phone call claims

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 29, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

U.S. President Donald Trump uses his phone on Marine One at Leesburg Executive Airport in Leesburg, Virginia, U.S., April 24, 2025. REUTERS/Craig Hudson

It remains unclear whether President Donald Trump spoke with President Xi Jinping about tariffs on China and both sides continue to give different answers.

Last week, Trump told reporters that he was "actively" negotiating with China on a tariff deal, according to The Los Angeles Times. However, China responded, denying the claim, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent contradicted the president, stating that talks have yet to begin.

Trump reiterated that Xi had called him to discuss the negotiations.

"He's called. And I don't think that's a sign of weakness on his behalf," Trump is quoted as saying on Friday, but he didn't provide specifics, FoxNews.com pointed out.

Then, China's Foreign Ministry said on Monday that Xi had not spoken to Trump.

"As far as I know, the two heads of state have not called each other recently," Guo Jiakun, a ministry spokesperson, told Reuters. "I would like to reiterate that China and the U.S. have not conducted consultations or negotiations on the tariffs issue."

The back and forth continues to be a question, and CNBC's "Squawk Box" co-host Joe Kernen asked Bessent about it Monday.

"Okay. Where are we?" asked Kernen. "I know that there are a lot of questions. We just mentioned China. Let's start with China. Where are we with China? Are there negotiations? Was there a phone call with President Xi and President Trump?"

"You know, you know, Joe, treasury secretary does a lot of things, running the White House switchboard isn't one of them," Bessent replied. "So, they, you know, all aspects of government are in contact with China. And in terms of trade negotiations, we see where this goes. But, as I've repeatedly said, I believe that it's up to China to de-escalate because they sell five times more than we sell them. And so, 125%, 145% tariffs are unsustainable."

The comment comes less than a week after the Chinese Foreign Ministry told the U.S. to be more polite, Politico reported.

“If a negotiated solution is truly what the U.S. wants, it should stop threatening and blackmailing China and seek dialogue based on equality, respect, and mutual benefit,” the Chinese foreign ministry spokesperson said Wednesday before Bessent's speech to the International Monetary Fund (IMF). “To keep asking for a deal while exerting extreme pressure is not the right way to deal with China and simply will not work.”

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Tariff Tango: Trump, Xi, and the Art of Miscommunication

In the latest chapter of the U.S.-China trade saga, we find ourselves entangled in a web of contradictions, denials, and downright confusion that would make even Kafka blush. At the heart of this bewildering narrative are claims from President Donald Trump about negotiations with China over tariffs, a topic as volatile as it is vital to global economic stability. Yet, as we wade through the mire of statements and counterstatements, one thing becomes crystal clear: the truth is as elusive as ever.

Trump's Claims vs. Reality

Last week, The Los Angeles Times reported Trump's assertion that he was "actively" negotiating with China on a tariff deal. This claim was met with immediate skepticism, not just from China, but from within his own administration. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's contradiction—that talks have yet to begin—throws a wrench into the narrative machinery Trump has attempted to construct. It's as if Trump is playing a high-stakes game of telephone, where the message starts as a negotiation and ends up as a diplomatic faux pas.

Moreover, Trump's insistence that President Xi Jinping had called him to discuss negotiations adds layers of intrigue and ambiguity. FoxNews.com highlighted Trump's lack of specifics, which is less a sign of strategic ambiguity and more a hallmark of narrative disarray. China's swift denial of such a call, as reported by Reuters, only serves to muddy the waters further. If the goal here was to project strength or leverage, the result is a resounding failure, marked by global head-scratching and domestic embarrassment.

A Symphony of Missteps

The article also touches upon a less talked-about aspect of international diplomacy: tone and respect. Politico's report of the Chinese Foreign Ministry's call for the U.S. to be more polite underlines the cultural and diplomatic nuances often lost in the bluster of tariff talks. It's a poignant reminder that behind the economic figures and trade policies are fundamental principles of respect and equality. The U.S.'s approach, as criticized by China, of "asking for a deal while exerting extreme pressure" is akin to negotiating with a gun to one's head—not exactly the hallmark of a mutually beneficial dialogue.

Treasury Secretary's Reality Check

Bessent's commentary to CNBC's "Squawk Box" is a rare moment of clarity in an otherwise opaque situation. His admission that it's "up to China to de-escalate" because they "sell five times more than we sell them" is an acknowledgment of the power imbalance at play. Yet, it also highlights a fundamental misunderstanding or, at the very least, a strategic oversimplification of the complexities of international trade. To suggest that tariffs of "125%, 145%" are unsustainable is to state the obvious, but it does little to advance the cause of finding a viable resolution.

Conclusion: The Road to Nowhere?

As this article lays bare, the U.S.-China tariff negotiations—or the lack thereof—are emblematic of a broader systemic failure. It's a failure of communication, of diplomacy, and of strategy. The back and forth between Trump and Xi, between the U.S. and China, serves as a stark reminder of the challenges facing international relations in an era defined by unpredictability and volatility.

In the final analysis, the U.S.-China tariff saga is not just about tariffs. It's about the ability—or inability—of world leaders to navigate the complexities of global economics with foresight, respect, and a commitment to mutual benefit. As it stands, we are witnessing a spectacle of miscommunication that undermines these goals, leaving businesses, consumers, and entire economies in a state of anxious uncertainty. The only way forward is through clear, respectful, and genuine dialogue—a path that seems all too distant in the current climate.
