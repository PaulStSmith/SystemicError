Trump ordered his White House portrait be printed with golden border so it would 'glimmer': report

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 18, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

U.S. President Donald Trump smiles as he signs energy-related executive orders, at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., April 8, 2025. REUTERS/Nathan Howard REFILE - QUALITY REPEAT

President Donald Trump has reportedly ordered his official portrait in the White House be printed with a golden border so it would catch the light and "glimmer."

According to a report published in the Wall Street Journal Wednesday, the Government Publishing Office, which is responsible for printing the official presidential portraits, was told that Trump wanted his portrait to glimmer.

The WSJ quoted an administration official, who was not named, as confirming that the White House asked that Trump’s portrait, along with Vice President’s JD Vance’s, be printed with a golden border.

"The reprint required metallic gold ink and a specialized printer. And it delayed the completion of the portraits, some of which had already been printed with a more understated white border, according to an internal government document outlining the request," the report claimed.

Trump is being assisted by a "gold guy," a man he brought for the White House makeover, per the article.

"Those who have visited Mar-a-Lago say the gold touches in the White House look familiar. The Florida club is awash in gold, with the color covering nearly every ceiling, wall, picture frame and desk. The original designer, Joseph Urban, designed opera sets and took inspiration from the grand palaces of Europe," the report said.

Trump is said to have personally overseen the installation of the gold carvings on the mantel in the Oval Office. Gold cherubs from Mar-a-Lago were also installed in the White House, per the report.

"Prominently displayed next to the Resolute Desk is a large gold FIFA World Cup trophy. Seven gold vases and urns decorate the mantle. During the Biden administration and the first Trump administration, the mantle was adorned with plants," the WSJ said.

According to a recent visitor, whose identity was not disclosed in the repot, Trump has mounted a gold crest bearing his name above the entrance to the White House from the colonnade. Gold coasters featuring his name can also be found on side tables in the Oval Office.

Trump's love of gold is well-documented. Foreign leaders have presented him with gold-themed items. Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu gifted Trump a gold pager during his visit to the United States in February, referencing Israel's assault on Hezbollah last year with explosive electronic devices. Similarly, in the same month, Japanese Prime Minister Shigeru Ishiba presented Trump with a gold samurai helmet.

Our Analysis:

The Gilded Ego: Trump's Golden Portrait Saga

In a move that could only be conjured up from the depths of what one might call an insatiable vanity, President Donald Trump has reportedly mandated that his official White House portrait be encased in a golden border designed to catch light and "glimmer." This revelation, unearthed by the Wall Street Journal, showcases not just a penchant for ostentation but a deep dive into the psyche of a man whose thirst for luxury knows no bounds, even at the expense of tradition and, arguably, taste.

The Government Publishing Office, a body typically unaccustomed to the whims of metallic ink demands, found itself in the peculiar position of having to source a "specialized printer" capable of fulfilling Trump's golden demands. This wasn't a simple aesthetic choice; it was a statement, loud and clear: Trump's legacy must not only be remembered but must do so in the most flamboyant manner possible. The logistical headache this caused, delaying the completion of portraits that had already been printed with a more traditional and understated white border, is a testament to the disruption Trump continues to bring to the established norms of presidential decorum.

The article paints a picture (pun intended) of a man who has systematically infused the White House with his personal brand of luxury, or should we say, gaudiness. The “gold guy” brought in for the White House makeover, gold cherubs from Mar-a-Lago installed in the Oval Office, and a large gold FIFA World Cup trophy ostentatiously displayed next to the Resolute Desk—the narrative reads less like the setting of the world's most powerful office and more like the backdrop of a nouveau riche fantasy.

It's particularly telling when the article mentions how, under both the Biden administration and the first Trump administration, the mantle in the Oval Office was adorned with plants—a stark contrast to the seven gold vases and urns that now take their place. This shift from organic to metallic, from understated to flashy, encapsulates the transformation of the White House under Trump: a shift from a symbol of democratic leadership to a monument to one man's ego.

The international angle adds another layer to this golden onion. Foreign leaders, perhaps recognizing Trump's affinity for all things gold, have presented him with gold-themed gifts, from a gold pager by Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu to a gold samurai helmet by Japanese Prime Minister Shigeru Ishiba. These gestures, while diplomatic, also subtly underscore the perception of Trump's presidency as one that is more concerned with the sheen of gold than the substance of governance.

In a world rife with complex challenges and nuanced diplomacy, the focus on gold—be it in portrait borders, decor, or gifts—feels not just out of touch but dangerously self-absorbed. It's a visual representation of a presidency that, time and again, has shown itself to be more concerned with appearance than reality, with the glittering facade rather than the structural integrity beneath.

In conclusion, the golden portrait saga is not just a story about a portrait; it's a metaphor for the Trump presidency itself. It's a tale of excess, of prioritizing form over function, and of a relentless pursuit of personal aggrandizement over public service. As we look back on this chapter of American history, it's crucial to ask ourselves: What do we value? Is it the glitter of gold or the grit of governance? In answering that, we define not just the legacy of one man but the future direction of a nation.
