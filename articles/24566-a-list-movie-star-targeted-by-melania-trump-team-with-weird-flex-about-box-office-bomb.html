A-list movie star targeted by Melania Trump team with 'weird flex' about box office bomb

Based on an article by David McAfee

Paulo SantosOctober 8, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that Marc Beckman, Melania Trump’s senior adviser and a producer of her documentary, celebrated its per-theater opening performance by claiming it beat Tom Cruise’s Digger by about 67 percent. His comparison relied on a narrow metric: Digger opened in 1,543 more theaters.

The broader figures undercut the boast. According to reports cited by RawStory, Melania opened to just over $7 million and finished with roughly $16.6 million worldwide, while Amazon reportedly spent $75 million on production and marketing. Digger earned $8 million domestically and $20 million worldwide during its opening weekend against a reported $160 million budget.

The documentary also received a reported 9 percent critics’ score on Rotten Tomatoes, compared with a 99 percent audience score attributed largely to the first lady’s supporters. Melania Trump has since announced another Amazon Prime Video series about her life, tentatively suggested for November.

The metric is the message

Beckman did not rebut the criticism of Melania or demonstrate commercial success. He selected the denominator that made the project look strongest, then compared it with another expensive disappointment.

That is not evidence that Melania succeeded. It is political communication disguised as box-office analysis: isolate one favorable statistic, remove the financial context, and declare victory. The remarkable part is not the arithmetic. It is the expectation that loyal audiences will accept a curated fragment as the whole record.

Power financed the failure

Tom Cruise is the celebrity target, but he is not the consequential actor in this story. The institutional power sits with the first lady’s political operation and Amazon, which reportedly committed $75 million to the documentary and then remained in business with Melania Trump for a follow-up series.

That relationship matters more than a contest between two poorly performing films. A sitting first lady received an enormous commercial platform for a self-authored image project. Her adviser, who also produced it, then used his political position to market its reception as public affirmation. The roles of subject, political surrogate, producer, and promoter collapse into one influence operation.

Celebrity provides the decoy

The Cruise comparison turns scrutiny away from the question Beckman’s own numbers invite: what did Amazon buy, and why does weak financial and critical performance appear to carry so little consequence?

RawStory’s framing partly follows the bait by emphasizing an A-list star and a “weird flex.” The remark is certainly strained, but weirdness is not the central problem. Treating the episode as celebrity sparring shrinks a story about political access, corporate patronage, and reputation management into entertainment gossip.

Cruise’s reported box-office failure supplies a weaker target against which the Trump team can manufacture relative success. His film’s losses do not improve the documentary’s reported return, reception, or artistic standing.

Loyalty replaces accountability

Beckman previously told Fox News that “everybody embraced” the first lady. The reported figures do not establish anything so sweeping. They show a modest theatrical audience, severe critical rejection, and an unusually large divide between critics and audience reviewers.

That gap cannot by itself prove manipulation or even explain why viewers differed so sharply. But Beckman’s rhetoric reveals the political use of fandom: support from committed admirers is presented as universal approval, while contrary evidence is reduced to something that can be managed through selective metrics.

The durable product is access

The larger pattern is familiar: powerful political figures do not need an uncontested success when aligned institutions keep financing the next vehicle and loyal operatives can redefine the previous result. Failure becomes a branding problem, not a material constraint.

The Cruise boast is therefore less a victory lap than a demonstration of insulation. The documentary could reportedly cost tens of millions, earn far less worldwide, receive brutal reviews, and still lead to another Amazon series. In systems organized around access and political value, the numbers need not justify the project. They merely need to be rearranged until power can call itself popular.
