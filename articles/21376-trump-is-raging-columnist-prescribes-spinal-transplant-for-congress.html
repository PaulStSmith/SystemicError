'Trump is raging': Columnist prescribes 'spinal transplant' for Congress

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

Paulo SantosAugust 16, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Power Without a Spine

The Setup

A New York Times columnist says Donald Trump’s latest eruption over Iran has pushed the country toward catastrophe, with Congress lying “prostrate” and voters told to rescue the system in the midterms. The immediate context is simple: Trump is lashing out, the White House is sending contradictory signals about missiles and blame, and the next Congress could theoretically check him if Democrats retake the House.

Who Actually Holds Power

This story is not about American voters failing to notice danger. It is about institutional power being hoarded by the executive and surrendered by the legislature. Congress is the branch that can restrain a president, and French says so plainly. That matters because it identifies the real failure: not public inattentiveness, but legislative cowardice dressed up as helplessness.

Trump is the decision-maker here. He set the tone, escalated the conflict, and then raged when reality refused to obey his fantasy of “a few targeted airstrikes” followed by regime collapse. The administration’s job was to prevent this kind of recklessness from becoming policy. Instead, the system has been organized around his impulses.

The Misdirection

French’s warning still carries a familiar weakness: it leans too hard on voters as the emergency brake. That is a convenient moral pose for elite commentary, but it also misdirects responsibility away from the institutions already in the room. Voters can change the balance of power in the next Congress. They cannot, by themselves, force a spineless legislature to act once the crisis is underway.

The problem is not that the public needs more alarm. The problem is that elected officials keep acting as though alarm is someone else’s job. “Prostrate before the president” is not a metaphor for confusion. It is a description of surrender.

The Real Coalition

The only plausible democratic check in the source is the House under Democratic control. That is not partisan cheerleading; it is basic arithmetic. If the country is facing a president who treats war as a mood and Congress as furniture, then the democratic coalition has to use the one branch still capable of resistance.

This is where lazy neutrality becomes malpractice. There is no symmetry here between the party enabling authoritarian politics and the party that would at least have a mandate to block it. Democrats are not saviors. They are the minimum institutional defense against a president who mistakes force, volatility, and humiliation for strategy.

What This Story Reveals

The deeper pattern is familiar: elite institutions warn about disaster after they have already helped normalize the person causing it. They describe the danger in grave tones while treating the machinery of constraint as optional, delayed, or politically inconvenient. Then they hand the burden back to voters and call that democracy.

That is how democratic failure is laundered into commentary. Trump creates the crisis, Congress enables it, and pundits convert structural weakness into a sermon about civic vigilance. The real lesson is uglier: when institutions refuse to exercise power, they do not become neutral. They become accomplices.
