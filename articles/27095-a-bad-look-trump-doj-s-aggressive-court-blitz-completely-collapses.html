'A bad look': Trump DOJ's aggressive court blitz completely collapses

Based on an article by The Conversation

Paulo SantosNovember 5, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the Trump Justice Department’s effort to intervene in the 2026 midterm election largely failed in federal courts from Washington, D.C., to Anchorage. Judges rejected or blocked administration actions aimed at state election processes, and the Supreme Court declined to disturb a ruling barring federal intervention in California’s ballot count.

The administration had alleged that 20,000 California ballots were suspect, sent target letters to people allegedly connected to an Alaska ballot controversy, dispatched roughly 1,000 election observers, and filed lawsuits near Election Day. Former federal judge John E. Jones III told The Conversation that states hold the constitutional authority to administer elections and that courts generally avoid changing election rules once voting is underway.

Jones argued that Justice Department leaders had acceded to the president’s wishes without a sound legal basis. He also alleged that government lawyers had failed to meet professional standards, including by giving judges answers that were untruthful or intentionally disingenuous. Those are Jones’s assessments; the article does not independently establish the lawyers’ intent.

Power did not disappear

The institutional power here belonged to the executive branch. The Justice Department could deploy lawyers, issue target letters, announce investigations, send observers and force state officials to spend the final days of an election answering federal demands.

That campaign did not need to win in court to impose costs. State and local election workers still had to divert time and resources toward litigation. People named in unresolved investigations could still be exposed to public suspicion. Courts still had to process claims one judge characterized as part of an effort to “intimidate, threaten and coerce” election officials.

The courtroom losses matter, but “collapse” describes the legal outcome more accurately than the exercise of power. The administration acted; everyone else had to respond.

The decision came from above

Jones placed responsibility on Justice Department leadership for accepting the president’s demands and sending weak cases into federal court. That is the central chain of agency: the president sought federal involvement, department leaders enabled it, and government attorneys carried it out.

Calling the litigation clumsy risks reducing an abuse of institutional authority to poor craftsmanship. Competent lawyering is not the missing safeguard when political leaders order the Justice Department into election administration. The missing safeguard is leadership willing to refuse.

The reported breach was therefore not merely reputational. A department with criminal investigative power acted close enough to Election Day that allegations alone could shape public perception before evidence was tested or cases resolved.

The “bad look” is the least of it

Jones called the performance of government litigators a “bad look,” but aesthetics are not the governing issue. The constitutional allocation of power is. Article I assigns election administration to states, subject to congressional authority; it does not give the president a general power to supervise ballot counting.

The article also invokes the Purcell principle, under which federal courts generally avoid disrupting election procedures once voting is underway. By that point, many voters had already cast ballots in person or by mail. Late intervention would not have corrected a static process. It would have altered an election already in progress.

Framing the episode chiefly as professional decline understates the political consequence. When federal prosecutors announce investigations and invoke suspected criminality around voting, they can manufacture doubt without proving misconduct. The implication arrives immediately; legal resolution comes later.

Courts contained this attempt

Judges supplied the resistance that Justice Department leadership reportedly did not. They rejected intervention, questioned government representations and forced federal lawyers to substantiate claims that carried the authority of the United States.

But judicial containment should not be mistaken for institutional health. Jones described courts operating without their usual presumption that government attorneys are behaving regularly and answering candidly. If judges must treat federal representations as potentially unreliable, every dispute becomes slower and harder to adjudicate.

The courts prevented the reported campaign from producing the requested legal result. They could not erase the intimidation alleged by one judge, restore the resources consumed by emergency litigation or prevent unresolved accusations from circulating during the election.

The systemic error

This episode reveals how executive power can be used against an election without successfully overturning a single ruling. Investigations, threats of prosecution and emergency lawsuits can burden administrators, stigmatize participants and seed suspicion while preserving the defense that the government was merely asking legal questions.

The larger pattern is the conversion of institutional failure into political utility. Losing cases can still broadcast fraud allegations and establish federal intrusion as routine. Each attempted intervention tests which officials will comply, which lawyers will object and how much disruption courts will tolerate.

A legal defeat is therefore not self-correction. It is evidence that one institution stopped another after Justice Department leaders had already chosen to cross boundaries their predecessors treated as essential. The system held at the judicial stage because it had already failed at the executive one.
