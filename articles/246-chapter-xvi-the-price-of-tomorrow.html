Chapter XVI. The Price of Tomorrow

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 23, 202611 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Roth returned the next morning with two hours of sleep, three legal pads, and a face that had misplaced mercy.

Adrian had not slept.

Help fuel the work. Independent analysis doesn’t fund itself:
☕ Buy us a coffee

This did not impress him.

“Do not look martyred,” Roth said, closing the door behind him. “Martyrdom is for people with cleaner books.”

Adrian stood from the conference table. The ledger was already there, along with the list of certificate counterparties, the projected maturities, the trust structures, the newspaper holdings, the civic funds, the bank endowments, the clinic commitments, the school grants, the legal-defense reserves, the campaign rules, and every other instrument by which he had tried to make the future behave.

It covered the table like a map of a country no one had admitted invading.

“Coffee?” Adrian asked.

“No.”

“Breakfast?”

“Do not attempt hospitality while under moral indictment.”

Adrian sat.

Roth did not.

“I spent part of the night deciding whether to resign,” he said.

Adrian kept his hands still.

“And?”

“And resignation is attractive in the way cliffs are attractive to poets. Dramatic, clean, and useless to anyone standing below.”

He placed the first legal pad on the table.

“So instead I made a list.”

Adrian looked at the page.

The heading read:

Crimes, Torts, Sins, and Stupidities.

“That seems broad,” Adrian said.

“I was tired.”

Roth sat at last.

“We begin with the legal names, because if we begin with the moral names I may throw something. Fraud. Forgery. Securities misrepresentation, depending on jurisdiction and instrument treatment. Bank fraud, if any court decides the credit facilities were obtained under false pretenses, which they were. Conspiracy, if anyone decides the future has co-conspirators, which I advise against exploring. Tax exposure. Disclosure exposure. Fiduciary breach if any trust officer knew enough to know. Professional exposure for me, although my ignorance was previously useful and is now dead.”

“I am sorry.”

“You keep saying that as if apology is a solvent.”

“No.”

“Good. It is not.”

Roth turned the page.

“Now the moral names. Theft from trust. Consent obtained by deception. Public good built on private fraud. A benevolent coup conducted through instruments instead of soldiers. The creation of dependency by means no beneficiary could meaningfully approve. The possible corruption of every institution you hoped would remain clean.”

Adrian looked down.

“Look at me.”

He did.

Roth’s voice had gone flat. That frightened Adrian more than anger would have.

“Do you understand why I am not making jokes?”

“Yes.”

“Say it.”

“Because the joke would make it survivable too quickly.”

Roth nodded once.

“There may be hope for you yet.”

Outside the windows, New York moved through morning with its usual refusal to care. Cars hissed on wet pavement. Steam lifted from grates. Somewhere below, a delivery driver argued with a doorman in a language Adrian did not know and a tone he understood perfectly.

Life continued without waiting for the scale of anyone’s sin.

Roth opened the second legal pad.

“Now we discuss what cannot happen.”

“All right.”

“You cannot confess publicly.”

Adrian blinked.

Whatever he had expected, it had not been that.

Roth saw the surprise and disliked it.

“Do not look relieved. This is not absolution. It is triage. Public confession would destroy the newspapers, the bank, the clinics, the schools, the candidates, the legal-defense funds, the people whose mortgages exist because your impossible money passed inspection, and the witnesses who trusted institutions you capitalized. It would not produce justice. It would produce collapse disguised as honesty.”

“I know.”

“No. You hoped I would say the opposite because punishment would be simpler than stewardship.”

Adrian had no defense.

“You also cannot continue as before,” Roth said.

“No.”

“Every new obligation must be treated as additional weight on the lie. Every initiative now competes with redemption, not ambition. Every dollar spent on a dream is a dollar not used to make the foundation real.”

He slid a page across the table.

Required: Redemption Reserve.

Below it, in Roth’s controlled hand:

Independent accounting. Segregated assets. Counterparty map. Maturity ladder. Liquidity stress. Panic scenario. Fraud-disclosure scenario. Founder incapacity scenario. Founder death scenario. Founder idiocy scenario.

Adrian paused at the last line.

“Founder idiocy?”

“A broad category. Historically underpriced.”

“I see your jokes have returned.”

“Do not celebrate. They are not here for you.”

Adrian read the page again.

“How much?”

Roth handed him the third pad.

The number sat in the middle of the page like an accusation.

Adrian had known it already in outline. One hundred billion dollars in principal. Layers of interest, fees, substituted obligations, rollover risk, currency movement, political risk, legal contingency, and the cost of making a fraudulent balance sheet become true without anyone noticing the conversion.

Knowing was not seeing.

Seeing had weight.

“This is more than the certificates,” Adrian said.

“Yes.”

“By a lot.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because lies accrue carrying costs. You invented a very large one.”

Adrian sat back.

For the first time, the future did not look like a branching tree. It looked like an invoice.

“Markets,” he said.

Roth closed his eyes.

“Do not.”

“I know enough to--”

“No, you do not.”

“Benjamin--”

“No.” Roth’s hand came down on the table, not hard, but with the finality of a judge denying a motion. “You know a market that no longer exists. Every dollar you have moved has already damaged your map. Every newspaper you bought, every prosecution you altered, every candidate you funded, every bank branch you opened, every frightened donor who changed behavior because your institutions exist, all of it has taken your precious stock tables and fed them to history.”

Adrian said nothing.

“You can still use broad knowledge,” Roth said. “Computing will matter. Energy will matter. Medicine will matter. Communications will matter. Space may matter if it stops exploding expensively for long enough. But no more pretending you can gamble your way clean with a list of prices from a dead world.”

“I was not going to gamble.”

“You are always going to gamble. You call it intervention.”

That landed.

Roth knew it had.

He softened only by changing tools.

“There is another reason. Even if your market knowledge worked, it would teach you the wrong lesson.”

“Which is?”

“That redemption can be arbitraged.”

The room went quiet.

Roth turned the legal pad toward himself and wrote a line.

Then he pushed it back.

No speculative redemption.

“Productive enterprise only,” Roth said. “Factories. Patents. Services. Instruments people can use. Technology that earns money because it solves actual problems. If the lie is to be retired, it will be retired by making things, not by beating other gamblers to the window.”

Adrian read the line.

Something in him resisted.

Not because Roth was wrong.

Because Roth had found the one remaining fantasy Adrian had not admitted keeping: that foreknowledge might still rescue him from the slow indignity of competence.

“That will take years,” Adrian said.

“Yes.”

“Decades, maybe.”

“Almost certainly.”

“We may fail.”

“Now you are improving.”

Adrian laughed once, without humor.

Roth did not smile.

“Say it plainly,” he said.

“We need an industrial base.”

“Again.”

“We need an industrial base large enough to generate real surplus, durable enough to survive politics, useful enough to justify itself without the secret, and governed well enough that it does not become another private empire.”

Roth watched him.

“Better.”

Adrian stood and walked to the window.

New York had become, in the years since his arrival, both less mysterious and less forgiving. He knew which papers would call, which lawyers would stall, which bankers would flatter, which politicians would smile too long before asking for money. He had learned the routes by which public virtue became procurement language and procurement language became invoices.

He had also learned that the city could absorb anything except simplicity.

“Newspapers because lies travel,” he said.

Roth waited.

“Banks because exclusion compounds.”

Still Roth waited.

“Clinics because neglect kills before policy notices.”

“And this?”

Adrian looked back.

“A way to make the future harder to steal.”

Roth’s mouth tightened.

“No.”

Adrian almost smiled despite himself.

“Try again without the plaque?” he asked.

“Exactly.”

Adrian looked out at the city again.

“A way to repay the debt without hollowing out the institutions the debt created. A way to build capacity no election can repeal in one afternoon. A way to earn the money honestly enough that, someday, the honest money can replace the false money.”

Roth considered that.

“Acceptable as a first draft.”

“Generous.”

“Temporary.”

Roth turned to a clean page.

“Divisions.”

Adrian returned to the table.

“Computing.”

“Why?”

“Every institution we build will need records, audits, communications, analysis, security. The government will need it. Hospitals will need it. Banks will need it. Newspapers will need archives and transmission. Factories will need controls.”

“Semiconductors?”

“No skipped rungs.”

Roth wrote that down.

“Energy.”

“Because oil shocks will keep teaching the same lesson to people determined not to learn it. Efficiency, controls, heat pumps, grid systems, solar where it works, storage where it can be made honest.”

“Medical instruments.”

“The clinics keep showing us what breaks. We can build for the places the market treats as afterthoughts.”

“Aerospace.”

Adrian paused.

Roth looked up.

“Say it as if I am not already irritated.”

“Satellites first. Weather, communications, education, medicine, disaster response. Launch later. Reuse later still. Stations much later. No flags. No founder mythology. No pretending glory pays invoices.”

“Rockets explode.”

“Yes.”

“They explode expensively.”

“Yes.”

“They explode in public.”

“If we do it wrong.”

“That was not reassuring.”

“It was not meant to be.”

Roth wrote for a while.

The list became less like a dream as it acquired columns. Capital required. Competence required. Time to revenue. Public benefit. Reserve contribution. Governance risk. Founder temptation.

Every line had that last column.

Adrian noticed.

“You are enjoying that.”

“Founder temptation? Immensely.”

“It is a little pointed.”

“So are scalpels.”

By noon, the table held the skeleton of Continental Technical Group.

The name arrived after six rejected alternatives, four of them by Roth, one by Adrian, and one by both men simultaneously for being too close to a sermon.

“Not Camelot anything,” Roth said.

“I was not going to suggest that.”

“You were going to think it.”

Adrian let that pass because it was true enough to be annoying.

“Continental,” Roth said at last. “Boring. Large. Geographic without declaring conquest. Technical Group because it sounds as if it belongs in a file cabinet.”

“Aspirational.”

“You are forbidden from saying that near stationery.”

The first charter provision took longer than the name.

No founder override of reserve review.

Roth wrote it, underlined it, then wrote it again on a separate page labeled Non-Negotiable.

“Explain,” Adrian said.

“No new major initiative, acquisition, facility, division, or political commitment above a threshold I will define after coffee may proceed without reserve impact review. You do not get to build tomorrow by endangering the people already standing on yesterday’s fraud.”

“That may slow us down.”

“That is the point of brakes.”

“Brakes can stop a vehicle.”

“Only if the driver is aiming at a wall.”

Adrian accepted the page.

The second provision followed.

Independent technical authority.

“You do not overrule engineers on safety,” Roth said. “Ever.”

“I know.”

“You know many things in theory. I am collecting them in writing before theory becomes schedule.”

The third:

No personal naming.

The fourth:

Public benefit covenants for technology developed with foundation capital.

The fifth:

Whistleblower protection independent of division management.

The sixth:

No intelligence service, no private police, no covert political operations through Continental assets.

“You already wrote that once,” Adrian said.

“And yet history remains repetitive.”

Afternoon became evening.

They ate sandwiches Roth ordered because he no longer trusted Adrian’s instinct for hospitality. They argued over whether aerospace belonged in the first wave or the second. Roth wanted second. Adrian wanted first. Roth called that evidence. Adrian countered with satellites. Roth countered with explosions. Adrian countered with revenue. Roth countered with insurance. The argument ended, as many of their arguments did, not with victory but with a clause.

Aerospace could begin only as a satellite systems and research division until independent review certified a staged launch program.

“You have turned wonder into committee procedure,” Adrian said.

“You are welcome.”

Near midnight, Roth’s anger finally arrived.

It came without warning.

He was reviewing the counterparty map when his hand stopped moving.

“You understand,” he said, quietly, “that I would have helped you if you had told me less impossibly.”

Adrian looked up.

“What?”

“If you had come to me with money of dubious origin and a good enough purpose, I would have built walls. I would have hated parts of it, but I would have built walls. I have done worse for worse men with smaller ambitions.”

“Benjamin--”

“No. Listen.”

Adrian did.

Roth tapped the ledger once.

“You did not only lie because you had to. You lied because no one could stop you if they did not know what they were stopping.”

The sentence entered the room and stayed there.

Adrian felt the first impulse to defend himself rise and die before it reached his mouth.

“Yes,” he said.

Roth’s eyes hardened.

“Do not become noble in response. It will annoy me.”

“I was going to agree.”

“Good.”

“You are right.”

“Worse.”

“What?”

“I am necessary.”

Adrian looked at him.

Roth gathered the pages into stacks, each one aligned with unnecessary precision.

“That is why I am angry. Not because you lied. Men lie. Not because the lie was large. Men with access to banks become imaginative. I am angry because the lie made me necessary after the fact, and I dislike being drafted by a catastrophe that has already selected my conscience for me.”

Adrian had no answer for that either.

Roth stood.

“Tomorrow we begin identifying engineers.”

“Already?”

“Unless you have discovered a cheaper method of manufacturing a hundred billion dollars plus interest without fraud.”

“No.”

“Then yes. Already.”

He picked up his coat.

At the door, he paused as he had the night before.

“One more condition.”

Adrian waited.

“You do not get to call this redemption.”

“What do I call it?”

“Repayment.”

Roth opened the door.

“Redemption is for other people to decide.”

He left Adrian with the pages, the debt, and a future that had finally stopped pretending to be memory.

By morning, the next room would not contain bankers, editors, ministers, or senators.

It would contain engineers.

And engineers were harder to impress.
