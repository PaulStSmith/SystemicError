New York Times columnist David French, a former writer for the conservative National Review, wrote an article on Sunday called, “Trump Is Dragging Us Down to His Level” in which he argues, Donald Trump has a role in the major GOP scandal that saw young professionals engaging in racist and sexist behavior.

“There’s a larger story here,” French wrote. “When you combine all the elements — yet another Republican racism scandal, death wishes from a Democratic politician, Vance’s decision to excuse the inexcusable — you can see the ways in which 10 years of Trumpism have twisted the American soul.”

The columnist continued:

“I know very well that there were corrupt and venal politicians and political staff members before Donald Trump. I know that America has endured periods of more extreme anger and polarization than we’re enduring today (and not just in and around the Civil War). But we’re currently trapped in a dynamic that is tempting many millions of Americans to indulge their worst impulses.”

According to French’s essay, “Every year, more Americans are coming of political age in this dreadful time. By the time Trump leaves office in 2029, he will have been the dominant American political figure for roughly 14 years — since the weeks after he announced his first run for president and surged to the top of Republican primary polls.”

“That means 14 years of escalating political stakes. That means 14 years of politics that pushes the best people out of public life. That means 14 years of Trump as the model of the most successful politician of the era. At the end of that time, what will remain?” he asked Sunday. “The story of this past week is the story of this past year. It’s the story of this decade. If your political opponents represent ultimate evil, then the only morality left is the morality of victory. The only true sin is the sin of defeat.”

Our Analysis:

The Trump Effect: A Deep Dive into American Political Decay

In a recent op-ed for The New York Times, David French, a figure known for his conservative viewpoints and past association with the National Review, casts a long shadow over the current state of American politics, attributing a significant portion of its decline to Donald Trump’s influence. The piece, titled “Trump Is Dragging Us Down to His Level”, serves as a critical examination of the moral and ethical degradation that has seemingly become intertwined with the fabric of today’s political landscape. French’s argument, centered around a scandal involving young Republicans and their engagement in racist and sexist behavior, unfolds to reveal a broader, more disturbing trend that he attributes to a decade of Trumpism.

A Scandal Beyond Just Bad Behavior

French’s exploration of the young Republican group chat scandal is not just a critique of the individuals involved but a condemnation of the environment that fosters such behavior. This isn’t merely about a few bad apples; it’s about an orchard that has been poisoned. To French, Trump isn’t just a participant in the political arena; he’s a catalyst for its corrosion. The scandal, in his view, is symptomatic of a larger disease – one that has been incubating in the body politic for the better part of a decade, fed by the rhetoric and actions of Trump himself.

The Moral Decay of Trumpism

One of the more chilling assessments French makes is regarding the moral decay that has taken root in American politics, suggesting that Trumpism has twisted the American soul. This isn’t just about the normalization of corruption or the amplification of political polarization – though those are significant issues. It’s about a fundamental shift in how Americans perceive right and wrong within the political sphere. If politics is a reflection of society, then what does it say about a society that increasingly indulges its worst impulses under the guise of political allegiance?

The Future in Trump’s Shadow

The prognosis French offers is bleak. With Trump poised to be the “dominant American political figure” for a span of 14 years, the long-term effects on American political culture and the collective psyche are deeply concerning. What happens to a nation that for over a decade has been steeped in a political ethos that drives the best away from public service and elevates the most divisive among us?

The Morality of Victory

Perhaps the most profound observation French makes is about the shifting moral compass in American politics. In a landscape where political opponents are not just disagreed with but demonized, the only morality becomes the morality of victory. The implication here is terrifying in its simplicity: in the pursuit of victory, all actions become justifiable, and the only true sin is defeat. This reduction of political engagement to a zero-sum game not only impoverishes the democratic process but erodes the foundational principles upon which it stands.

In Conclusion

David French’s op-ed is not just a critique of Donald Trump or the Republican Party; it’s a wake-up call to the American people. The issues at hand are not ones that can be solved by a single election or by removing one man from office. They are symptomatic of deeper, more systemic problems that require a collective reckoning. As we move forward, the question becomes: Are we capable of such self-reflection, or will the allure of victory continue to blind us to the cost of our moral compromise?
