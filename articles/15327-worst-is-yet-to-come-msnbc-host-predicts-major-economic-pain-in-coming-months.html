'Worst is yet to come': MSNBC host predicts major economic pain in coming months

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 3, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:Conclusion: The Bitter Pill of Reality

MSNBC screenshot

Major retailers, from Walmart to Dollar Tree to Home Depot, are being candid about having to raise their prices because of President Donald Trump's steep new tariffs. And some chains are already raising prices.

During an Friday morning, May 2 appearance on MSNBC's "Morning Joe," Stephanie Ruhle — who hosts MSNBC's late-night show "The 11th Hour" — warned that for U.S. consumers, things are going to get much worse in the weeks and months to come.

Ruhle told "Morning Joe" host Joe Scarborough and his colleague Willie Geist, "What we're doing with China mano a mano is devastating for businesses. And when you talk about the unemployment number, we don't know what it's going to be yet. But the worst is yet to come."

Ruhle added, "When you've got Apple, one of the most profitable countries in the world, that got one of the first exemptions from the president….. When they're telling you they're going to take a $900 million hit, what do you think it's going to do for the toy store in your downtown?"

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent is echoing Trump's claim that even if the tariffs create some short-term financial pain, they will lead to widespread prosperity in the long run. Geist asked Ruhle if Bessent really believes that, and the "11th Hour" host didn't hesitate to question his sincerity.

Ruhle told Geist, "From the sources that I speak to, who are Scott Bessent's former colleagues, competitors, peers — no, not one word of it. And I think that's the hardest thing. We all know it's difficult to be on TV, but the reason he might appear awkward on television is because he knows he's not telling the truth. And, you know…. he just said earlier this week: we're not going back to the jobs of the past. We're bringing back industrial jobs of the future. "

Ruhle continued, "Willie, if that were the case, then wouldn't we see exemptions for apparel companies? Because the jobs of the future are not bringing back sweatshops, clothing manufacturers. We are a manufacturing hub. We're the second largest manufacturer in the world. What we have figured out over time is how to optimize our manufacturing."

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Tariff Tumult: The Bitter Truth Behind the Price Hikes

In an era where political decisions ripple through the economy with unprecedented velocity, the recent revelations about major retailers, from Walmart to Dollar Tree to Home Depot, raising their prices in response to President Donald Trump's steep new tariffs, serve as a stark reminder of the interconnectedness of global trade and domestic welfare. Stephanie Ruhle's appearance on MSNBC's Morning Joe peeled back the layers of optimistic government rhetoric to expose a grim reality: the so-called trade war with China isn't just a game of high stakes poker; it's a strategy that's already beginning to inflict pain on American businesses and, by extension, consumers.

The Hard Truth: Short-term Pain with Questionable Long-term Gain

Ruhle's commentary, grounded in conversations with insiders and experts, paints a picture of a policy decision that's not just misguided but potentially devastating. The mention of Apple, "one of the most profitable countries in the world," taking a $900 million hit is emblematic of the broader economic impact. This isn't just about the big players, though; it's the small, local businesses—the backbone of the American economy—that stand to suffer the most. The toy store down the street doesn't have the luxury of a presidential exemption.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's statements, as reported, epitomize the administration's attempt to spin the narrative. The promise of "widespread prosperity in the long run" is a classic political maneuver: promise the moon, in the hopes that the electorate forgets when it never lands. Ruhle's skepticism of Bessent's claims is more than just journalistic cynicism; it's a reflection of a deeper, systemic issue where officials pedal optimism that their own advisers and colleagues don't buy into.

The Future of Manufacturing and the Irony of Industrial Jobs

Ruhle's point about the lack of exemptions for apparel companies is particularly telling. It dismantles the administration's narrative about bringing back "industrial jobs of the future." The reality, as Ruhle deftly points out, is that the future of manufacturing in the U.S. isn't about reverting to past practices. It's about innovation, optimization, and moving forward—not backward. The notion of returning to an era of sweatshops and clothing manufacturers as a cornerstone of American industry is not just outdated; it's out of touch.

The Larger Implication: A Misguided Trade Policy

This story isn't just about tariffs; it's about a broader trade policy that seems to be predicated on a misunderstanding of global economics and a misguided sense of nationalistic protectionism. The administration's approach, as highlighted by the price hikes and the commentary surrounding them, suggests a lack of coherent strategy and an underestimation of the global market's complexity.

Conclusion: The Bitter Pill of Reality

As Ruhle concluded her segment, the message was clear: the worst is yet to come. This isn't just fear-mongering; it's a logical deduction based on current trends and policies. The administration's tariff strategy is a gamble where the stakes are the economic wellbeing of the American people. And as the major retailers begin to raise their prices, it's a stark reminder that in this game, it's the average consumer who's left holding the bag.

In the grand chessboard of international trade, the administration's moves are proving to be less like strategic gambits and more like blunders. The real question now is not if, but how deeply, these policies will cut into the fabric of the American economy. The hope, faint though it may be, is that this realization dawns sooner rather than later, before the full brunt of these decisions is felt by the very people they purportedly aim to protect.
