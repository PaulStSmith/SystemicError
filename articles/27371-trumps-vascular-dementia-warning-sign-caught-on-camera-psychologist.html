Trump’s ‘vascular dementia’ warning sign caught on camera: psychologist

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 20, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

An unexplained sound becomes a diagnosis-shaped headlineMore footage, more inferenceSpeculation is not accountability

The footage does not establish that President Donald Trump has vascular dementia. It shows Trump making an unexplained sound during remarks in the Philippines, after which psychologist John Paul Garrison offered several possible neurological explanations—including vascular dementia—while explicitly acknowledging that he had not conducted a psychological evaluation and was expressing an opinion.

That distinction is the story. AlterNet’s headline turns a speculative interpretation of video into a “warning sign caught on camera,” granting diagnostic weight to an episode that Garrison himself described far more cautiously. A president’s capacity to perform the job is a legitimate matter of public concern. Remote diagnosis dressed up as political reporting does not answer it.

An unexplained sound becomes a diagnosis-shaped headline

At a luncheon hosted by Philippine President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. at Malacañang Palace, Trump was discussing the history shared by the United States and the Philippines when he made a sound that Garrison said was not a word. Trump continued speaking without an apparent reaction.

Garrison interpreted that absence of acknowledgment as evidence that the sound was involuntary. He also said Trump subsequently appeared to construct his sentence one word at a time. From those observations, he raised “a wide variety of potential neurological explanations,” including brain scarring and different forms of dementia.

That is a hypothesis with an expansive range, not a finding. Garrison said the video was “not a psychological evaluation of any kind” and characterized his conclusions as “just my opinions.” His strongest claim was narrower: he felt confident that Trump had not made the sound intentionally.

AlterNet’s framing collapses those categories. A clinician naming vascular dementia among multiple possibilities is not the same as identifying a clinical sign of vascular dementia in a patient. The article supplies an interpretation of behavior, not a diagnosis, and its headline outruns the qualification contained in its own reporting.

More footage, more inference

Garrison, a clinical and forensic psychologist who describes himself as a body-language expert, has spent months publishing videos about what he views as Trump’s neurological decline. During Trump’s arrival in China, he identified what he called a “myoclonic jerk” in Trump’s right arm. He also attributed an apparent collision with Chinese President Xi Jinping to reduced coordination.

Garrison further interpreted Trump’s near-embrace of Xi as “social disinhibition,” arguing that difficulty reading social cues can accompany the kinds of neurological dysfunction he has discussed. He said the behavior appeared to be progressing and that Trump seemed to struggle during the Asia trip, which also brought him face to face with Xi and Russian President Vladimir Putin at the APEC summit in Shenzhen.

These observations may justify questions. They do not settle them. A movement can be described from video; its medical cause cannot simply be read off the screen. The same limitation applies to an awkward encounter between heads of state. Calling it evidence of neurological decline gives one interpretation the authority of clinical language without the examination needed to support a clinical conclusion.

Speculation is not accountability

The presidency concentrates extraordinary coercive, diplomatic and administrative power in one person. That makes credible evidence about a president’s health consequential, especially when the reported behavior occurs during meetings with foreign leaders. It also raises the standard for reporting, rather than lowering it.

The political temptation is obvious: translate an unsettling moment into a hidden medical explanation, then make the explanation carry the burden of criticizing the president. But Trump’s public decisions, statements and uses of executive power can be evaluated directly. Assigning them to an unestablished neurological condition risks replacing political accountability with amateur pathology.

It can also misdirect responsibility. If aides or officials have concerns about a president’s capacity, they possess access, information and institutional duties that a YouTube commentator does not. If they have no such concerns, a psychologist’s review of clips cannot substitute for evidence from people positioned to observe Trump consistently or assess him clinically. Either way, the relevant power lies with the president and the officials surrounding him—not with viewers being invited to diagnose a sound.

Garrison is entitled to describe what he believes he sees. AlterNet is responsible for representing the limits of that claim. The footage captured an unexplained vocalization and other moments Garrison considered abnormal. It did not catch vascular dementia on camera.
