Businesses are flying blind into a historic El Niño —thanks to Trump

Based on an article by The Conversation

Paulo SantosNovember 27, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A historic warning, followed by disappearing dataThe administration cut the tools, not the exposureConsumers receive the bill later

An unusually powerful El Niño is colliding with a federal government that has dismantled or degraded parts of the public climate-information system businesses use to prepare for extreme weather. NOAA forecasters identified the danger months in advance, but Trump administration cuts have made the more detailed data needed to estimate losses, protect workers and reinforce supply chains harder to obtain—especially for smaller firms that cannot buy private substitutes.

The result is not simply less climate research. It is a transfer of risk: Washington saves money by withdrawing a public service, large corporations purchase replacements, and smaller suppliers and consumers absorb more of the damage.

A historic warning, followed by disappearing data

NOAA’s Climate Prediction Center had forecast a very strong El Niño since spring 2026. Its October update placed the probability that the event would become the strongest since records began in 1950 at 83 percent, with conditions likely to persist into early 2027.

That advance notice matters. Retailers, farmers, food processors and transportation companies can use it to increase inventories, arrange backup suppliers and reroute shipments before drought, flooding or severe storms interrupt production. El Niño patterns in North America are associated with Pacific storms as well as fires, floods and droughts, and historically strong events have imposed trillions of dollars in global economic costs.

But a broad forecast is not a substitute for the observation networks, historical records and regular analysis needed to decide where, when and how to prepare. Those resources have been steadily weakened.

NOAA stopped updating its Billion-Dollar Weather and Climate Disasters database in May 2025 and retired it after data through 2024. The database had tracked the cost of major American weather disasters for more than four decades, giving researchers and businesses a historical foundation for estimating future losses and economic disruption.

The federal government also shut down Climate.gov in 2025. Its El Niño blog has not been updated since June of that year. National Climate Assessments were removed from government websites, while staffing shortages led the National Weather Service to reduce weather-balloon launches at several locations, depriving forecast models of observations used in daily prediction.

These losses come as expensive disasters grow more common. The United States averaged fewer than five billion-dollar disasters annually during the 1980s; it now experiences more than 20 a year, even after adjusting for inflation. The totals in 2023 and 2024 were the highest recorded.

The administration cut the tools, not the exposure

The Trump administration has defended the cuts by pointing to administrative expense and possible errors in predictive models. Models are imperfect. Eliminating public data does not correct that imperfection; it leaves decision-makers with less information while the physical and economic hazards remain.

That distinction exposes the misdirection in the administration’s rationale. The relevant choice is not between flawless federal forecasts and wasteful ones. It is between a shared information system—available to businesses and researchers regardless of wealth—and a fragmented market in which access depends increasingly on the ability to pay.

Public weather intelligence runs quietly through the economy. Companies use current and near-current information to plan inventories, improve crop yields, select transportation routes and keep employees out of dangerous conditions. Insurers use it to price commercial coverage and handle claims. Risk Management Solutions, a catastrophe-modeling company owned by Moody’s, uses NOAA hurricane data in its models. Walmart maintains specialists and technology dedicated to monitoring weather threats to its supply chain.

The federal retreat does not make those needs disappear. It creates customers for private providers such as WindBorne Systems, Sofar Ocean, Tomorrow.io, Black Swift Technologies and Saildrone, which sell specialized observations and forecasting services.

Private data can replace some lost capacity for companies with sufficient budgets. It cannot reproduce equal access. Major retailers and insurers can pay subscription fees; local farmers, independent truckers and small suppliers often cannot. Those businesses occupy precisely the vulnerable links where a failed harvest, closed road or unsafe workplace can begin a much larger disruption.

Some scientists have tried to preserve the abandoned public work. The researchers behind NOAA’s El Niño blog moved their effort to a nonprofit, and another nonprofit has continued the disaster-database project. That salvages expertise, but it also reveals the institutional choice: functions once supported by the federal government are now dependent on private organizations to survive.

Consumers receive the bill later

Weather shocks rarely appear at the checkout counter on the day they occur. They move through farms, factories, warehouses and freight networks before emerging as shortages and higher prices months later.

The pattern is already visible. Extreme weather in the Americas, including Florida, helped push U.S. orange-juice prices to a record in fall 2024; prices remained elevated after Hurricanes Helene and Milton damaged Florida groves. A Pacific Northwest heat dome harmed Christmas-tree farms in 2021 and added an estimated 5 to 10 percent to prices that year. Poor rainfall in West Africa more than doubled cocoa prices in 2024, with the effects still reaching American consumers as U.S. chocolate prices rose 14 percent in 2026.

Suvrat Dhanorkar, a Georgia Institute of Technology operations-management professor writing in The Conversation, warned that diminished federal climate information could produce cascading failures across companies and supply chains before reaching consumers as inflation. That is not a claim that better data can prevent every crop failure or storm closure. It can, however, give businesses time to reduce foreseeable losses.

The 2026 El Niño has already disrupted crops and supply chains internationally, including through a delayed monsoon season in India. Its arrival during the erosion of American climate infrastructure turns the coming months into a test the Trump administration chose to make harsher: not whether weather intelligence has value, but who can still afford it after the government withdraws it from everyone else.
