'Absurd and unprincipled': MAGA meltdown erupts as GOP senator vows to block Blanche

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

The Justice Department Is Being Turned Into a Loyalty Office

A vote that exposed the system

Lisa Murkowski said she would not support Todd Blanche for attorney general because she did not trust him to check the White House’s worst impulses. That is the relevant fact. The MAGA response was not to defend independence inside the Justice Department, but to demand obedience to Donald Trump and smear Murkowski as disloyal, corrupt, and self-serving.

Who actually holds power

The power in this story sits where it always sits in an authoritarian setup: with the president and the machinery around him. Blanche is not being judged as a neutral legal steward. He is being evaluated as a vehicle for Trump’s agenda. Murkowski said the quiet part out loud: the office is supposed to restrain executive abuse, not ratify it.

That is why her objection matters. It is not procedural trivia. It is a refusal to pretend the attorney general is just another ornament in the palace.

The loyalty test is the point

The attacks on Murkowski are revealing because they do not engage her concern. They punish it. Mike Howell calls the politicization of the Justice Department “verifiably false” while the source itself notes a Protect Democracy tracker reporting that Trump’s Justice Department has targeted dozens of political opponents with criminal charges, even if most cases failed. The argument is not about evidence. It is about enforcing a required belief: if Trump’s apparatus is doing the targeting, the targeting must not count.

That is not legal analysis. It is faction discipline.

Projection dressed up as outrage

The same people accusing Murkowski of corruption and sabotage are defending a department already used as a political weapon. Benny Johnson frames her vote as defiance of “the will of the voters,” as if one senator’s refusal to bless a loyalist is equivalent to subverting democracy. That is a cheap inversion. The actual subversion is the attempt to turn institutional power into a one-way mirror: Trump’s choices are framed as legitimacy, while any resistance is framed as betrayal.

The accusation of “corruptly seek[ing] financial benefit” is especially telling. When a movement is this invested in personal rule, it projects corruption outward at the first sign of institutional independence.

The weaponization story is not a distraction

Murkowski criticized Blanche’s handling of the Epstein files release and the broader “weaponization” of the Justice Department under his leadership. That framing is significant because it names a pattern, not an isolated personnel dispute. The issue is not whether Blanche is polite, qualified on paper, or loyal enough to reassure the base. The issue is whether he will serve law or serve Trump.

The MAGA response makes the answer obvious. Their outrage is not aimed at restoring legitimacy. It is aimed at punishing anyone who still treats the Justice Department as something other than a tool of the ruler.

What this really shows

This is a loyalty-state conflict wearing the costume of Senate confirmation politics. One side is trying to preserve a minimal boundary between prosecutorial power and presidential revenge. The other side is screaming that the boundary itself is illegitimate. That is the larger pattern: authoritarian politics does not merely seek power; it demands that institutions abandon the pretense of independence and then attacks anyone who notices the theft.

Murkowski’s vote matters because it interrupts that demand. The scandal is not that she refused Trump. The scandal is that refusing Trump now has to be defended as if it were corruption.
