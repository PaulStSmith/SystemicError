That's according to a Wednesday article in the Daily Caller — a prominent conservative news outlet — which reported that Trump's plan to stop enforcing a key section of the Corporate Transparency Act (CTA) is worrying many in the law enforcement community. The U.S. Treasury Department officially announced the rule change last month, which may end up helping drug cartels operating in the United States.

The CTA's Beneficial Ownership Information (BOI) reporting requirement was put in place during the late part of former President Joe Biden's administration and just went into effect in January, and is aimed at preventing the financing of illegal activity. This includes "money laundering, the financing of terrorism, proliferation financing, serious tax fraud, human and drug trafficking, counterfeiting, piracy, securities fraud, financial fraud, and acts of foreign corruption."

Supporters of the decision to not enforce BOI requirement say that the rule was cumbersome for small businesses. The National Federation of Independent Businesses (NFIB) argued that having to follow through on those requirements out them at risk of cyberattacks that could compromise their sensitive personal information, and Trump called the rule an "economic menace" in a Truth Social post.

However, law enforcement professionals disagree, and say that the CTA and its BOI reporting section are essential for stopping cartels operating shell companies designed to facilitate money laundering. San Diego District Attorney Summer Stephan — who is president of the National District Attorneys' Association (NDAA), told the Daily Caller that she's personally aware of cartels in her jurisdiction running jewelry stores, real estate investment offices and law offices, among other front businesses. She added that cartels will often strong-arm U.S. citizens into putting their names on these ventures to avoid being tied to the companies themselves.

"They are going to try to operate by influencing domestic American citizens through maybe threats to their families in Mexico or through money promises," she said.

Additionally, the American Conservative Union's (ACU) Frank Russo Jr., who leads the ACU's Center for Combating Human Trafficking, said that it was "quite easy" for criminals to set up a shell company in the U.S. And as NDAA's former director of government and legislative affairs, Russo personally lobbied for the passage of the CTA, saying it was an important tool in the fight against the illicit drug trade.

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Irony in Trump's Border Security Policy

The recent revelation that President Donald Trump plans to cease enforcement of a critical section of the Corporate Transparency Act (CTA) is a masterclass in contradiction and irony. Trump has vociferously championed tougher border security measures under the guise of halting the influx of dangerous drugs like fentanyl into the United States. Yet, this move, as reported by the Daily Caller, a conservative bastion, could inadvertently bolster the very scourge he vows to combat.

The Facade of Border Security

Trump's rhetoric on border security, heavily laced with promises to stem the tide of narcotics, has always been a potent element of his political persona. However, actions speak louder than words, and this latest policy shift screams of a glaring disconnect between proclaimed intentions and actual policy implications.

The CTA and its Crucial Role

The Beneficial Ownership Information (BOI) reporting requirement of the CTA, a policy put into effect during the waning days of the Biden administration, serves as a bulwark against the dark underbelly of illegal finance. Its primary aim is to prevent a range of illicit activities, including money laundering and the financing of terrorism, alongside drug trafficking. By Trump's administration sidelining this requirement, they inadvertently roll out the red carpet for drug cartels to launder money with impunity through shell companies in the U.S.

The Economic Menace Excuse

Trump's dismissal of the BOI requirement as an "economic menace" is a classic example of prioritizing perceived economic gains over the safety and well-being of American citizens. The argument, parroted by the National Federation of Independent Businesses (NFIB), that this rule places small businesses at risk of cyberattacks is a thinly veiled excuse. It conveniently ignores the broader picture: that without such regulations, we're essentially leaving the door wide open for cartels to operate domestically with greater ease.

Law Enforcement's Cry in the Wilderness

San Diego District Attorney Summer Stephan's insights paint a vivid picture of how cartels exploit the lack of stringent financial transparency to run legitimate-looking businesses, from jewelry stores to law offices, as fronts for their nefarious activities. The strong-arming of U.S. citizens to act as fronts for these operations is a stark reminder of the cartels' reach and the critical need for regulations like the CTA's BOI.

The Globalist Effort Behind Drug Trafficking

Frank Russo Jr. of the American Conservative Union highlights a critical aspect often overlooked in discussions about drug trafficking: its nature as a "multinational, corporate globalist effort." The ease with which criminals can establish shell companies in the U.S. is a gaping hole in our defense against the drug trade. Trump's policy, by exempting domestic companies from the BOI requirement, not only undermines this defense but potentially exacerbates the fentanyl crisis plaguing our communities.

Conclusion

In an era where the opioid epidemic, fueled by drugs like fentanyl, ravages communities across the United States, the need for comprehensive strategies to combat this menace is undeniable. Trump's decision to weaken the CTA's reporting requirements is a step back in this fight, demonstrating a profound misalignment between his administration's actions and the well-being of the American people. It's a stark reminder that in the realm of political rhetoric, the chasm between words and deeds can be both vast and deadly.
