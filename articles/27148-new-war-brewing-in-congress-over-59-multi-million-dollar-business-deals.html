New war brewing in Congress over 59 multi-million dollar business deals

Based on an article by Amy GoodmanDemocracy Now

Paulo SantosNovember 8, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet’s report says the incoming Democratic majorities in the House and Senate will face pressure to investigate President Donald Trump’s private business activity, including 59 businesses that CREW president Donald Sherman said Trump created after winning the White House in 2024. Sherman also said Trump made $117 million through foreign business deals after returning to office. The reporting does not identify 59 separate multimillion-dollar deals; the headline overstates what the supplied text establishes.

In a Democracy Now! interview reproduced by AlterNet, Sherman called for congressional scrutiny of Trump’s finances, administration policy, corporate donors and foreign influence. He cited a $500 million purchase of a 49% stake in World Liberty Financial by an investment vehicle backed by the United Arab Emirates’ national security adviser, followed months later by the administration’s transfer of sensitive AI-chip technology to the UAE despite national-security concerns. The report does not establish that the investment caused that policy decision.

Sherman argued that Congress should investigate possible links between official actions and private gain, including tariffs, cryptocurrency, stock holdings and foreign investments connected to Jared Kushner. He also urged lawmakers to restrict their own stock trading, strengthen congressional ethics enforcement and examine what corporations received after financing presidential projects.

The power belongs to Congress now

Trump holds executive power and retains the machinery of federal policy. But according to the report, Democrats won both chambers in the midterm elections, giving them subpoena power, committee control and authority over legislation during the remaining two years of his administration.

That transfer of congressional power eliminates the excuse of helplessness. Democrats cannot prevent every executive action, and presidential immunity limits some avenues of criminal liability for official conduct. But oversight is not prosecution. Congress can compel records and testimony, trace financial relationships, expose conflicts and legislate against future profiteering.

The decision ahead belongs to Democratic leaders: investigate aggressively, or convert an electoral mandate into another cycle of hearings calibrated for television and designed to expire without structural reform.

The conflict is not merely personal

The easiest framing is that Trump is unusually shameless about mixing public office with private wealth. The more consequential issue is the market surrounding him.

Sherman’s allegation is not simply that corporations and foreign interests gave money. It is that they operated in what he called a “pay-to-play environment” and may have obtained policy advantages in return. The UAE transaction and subsequent chip decision therefore demand investigation, not a prewritten verdict. Timing creates a serious question; it does not, by itself, prove an exchange.

Corporate actors are not spectators trapped near presidential misconduct. They possess money, lawyers, lobbyists and direct access to government. If congressional investigators examine only Trump while treating donors and investors as passive witnesses, they will conceal half the power structure.

The headline blurs the evidence

The supplied reporting supports a claim about 59 businesses and $117 million in foreign deals. It does not document “59 multi-million dollar business deals.” That distinction matters because inflated framing makes legitimate scrutiny easier to dismiss.

The same discipline must govern the investigations themselves. Sherman alleged connections involving tariffs, trademarks, oil and gas trading, private-prison investments and the war in Iran. Those claims require records capable of distinguishing suspicious overlap from provable abuse. Moral certainty is not a substitute for an evidentiary chain.

Weak framing also misdirects attention toward public disgust as though anger were the political actor. Voters did not create the conflicts described here. Officials permitted them, companies exploited access, and Congress left rules weak enough for public authority and private enrichment to occupy the same room.

Reform cannot stop at Trump

Sherman’s strongest point is the one Congress has the greatest incentive to evade: lawmakers must investigate their own institution. Banning individual-stock ownership and trading, extending public-records requirements to Congress, creating an independent Senate ethics office and restricting former members’ lobbying would threaten practices that benefit members of both parties.

That is why an anti-corruption majority can still produce cosmetic accountability. It is politically cheap to investigate an opposing president. It is materially harder to surrender lawmakers’ own financial privileges and close the revolving door through which congressional service becomes private leverage.

A serious response would connect presidential conflicts, corporate influence and congressional self-dealing as parts of one governing system. Anything narrower turns corruption into a personality defect and leaves its infrastructure intact.

The system rewards access until power intervenes

The larger pattern is not public confusion about corruption. It is institutional tolerance of arrangements that make suspicion rational: private businesses proliferating around a presidency, foreign money entering enterprises connected to public officials, corporate financing of presidential projects and lawmakers trading assets while shaping policy.

The report presents Democrats with authority and a choice. They can treat corruption as campaign language directed at Trump, or as a distribution of power that includes donors, corporations and Congress itself. The first produces spectacle. The second would require officials to dismantle advantages they may eventually want for themselves.
