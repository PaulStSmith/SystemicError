White House fires federal prosecutor just five hours after his swearing in

Deputy U.S. Attorney General Todd Blanche reacts during a press conference at the Justice Department in Washington, D.C., U.S., January 30, 2026. REUTERS/Elizabeth Frantz

President Donald Trump’s White House fired a federal prosecutor on Wednesday just five hours after he was sworn in by a federal judge.

Donald T. Kinsella, 78, was appointed by a panel of judges to serve as an interim U.S. Attorney in the Northern District of New York, which happened the day after the term of Trump’s current U.S. Attorney, John A. Sarcone III, expired. Kinsella, a Republican, was sworn in during a virtual ceremony by Chief Judge Brenda K. Sannes, The Times Union reported.

Kinsella has more than 50 years of experience as a prosecutor, including as an assistant U.S. attorney in Albany and a lawyer for the Organized Crime Drug Enforcement Task Force, the outlet reported.

Even so, Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche said in a post on X that Kinsella was “fired” because Trump had not appointed him.

“Judges don’t pick U.S. Attorneys, @POTUS does. See Article II of our Constitution. You are fired, Donald Kinsella,” Blanche posted.

It is unclear whether Kinsella’s firing will be challenged in court. LawFare’s Senior Editor Anna Brewer reported that the announcement of Kinsella’s appointment was still live on the court’s website at the time Blanche “fired” him.

Our Analysis:

The Arbitrary Dismissal of Donald T. Kinsella: A Stark Illustration of Trump’s Governance

In a move that reeks of autocratic whimsy, President Donald Trump’s administration has once again shown its blatant disregard for established norms and processes within the United States’ legal framework. The firing of federal prosecutor Donald T. Kinsella, merely five hours after his swearing-in, is not just a slap in the face to the judiciary but a glaring testament to the chaotic and capricious nature of Trump’s tenure.

The Facts: A Swift and Baseless Termination

Donald T. Kinsella, a veteran with over 50 years of prosecutorial experience, was appointed as an interim U.S. Attorney for the Northern District of New York by a panel of judges. This appointment came as a routine transition after the term of Trump’s appointee, John A. Sarcone III, came to an end. Kinsella, despite his Republican credentials and a storied career that includes significant roles in combating organized crime, found his tenure cut absurdly short.

The rationale for this abrupt dismissal? A tweet from Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche, proclaiming that “Judges don’t pick U.S. Attorneys, @POTUS does.” This simplistic and reductive interpretation of the Constitution overlooks the nuanced and sometimes necessary role judicial panels play in interim appointments, especially to maintain continuity in crucial prosecutorial positions.

The Implications: Undermining Judicial Independence

This incident is not merely about the firing of an experienced prosecutor; it’s emblematic of a broader trend under Trump’s governance — the undermining of judicial independence and the erosion of the checks and balances that are foundational to American democracy.

The Deputy Attorney General’s statement, “You are fired, Donald Kinsella,” delivered via social media, is particularly galling. It trivializes the importance of the role and the individual holding it, reducing a significant legal and constitutional process to a reality TV catchphrase. This is not just unprofessional; it’s profoundly dangerous, signaling a disrespect for legal norms and processes.

The Constitutional Misinterpretation

The claim that “Judges don’t pick U.S. Attorneys, @POTUS does” simplifies a complex constitutional and historical context. While the President nominates U.S. Attorneys, the process involves a balance of powers, with the Senate confirming these nominations and, in certain circumstances, courts stepping in to fill temporary vacancies. This ensures the continuity of justice and the non-political administration of law — principles that seem to be of little concern to the current administration.

What Next? A Legal Challenge?

The article hints at the possibility of Kinsella’s firing being challenged in court. Such a challenge could become a significant legal standoff, testing the limits of executive power and the integrity of the U.S. legal system. The outcome could set a precedent, for better or worse, regarding presidential authority over prosecutorial appointments.

In the end, this incident is a stark reminder of the systemic errors plaguing Trump’s administration. It underscores a governance style marked by caprice, disregard for legal norms, and an unsettling inclination towards autocratic tendencies. This is not merely about the firing of a federal prosecutor; it’s a symptom of a much deeper malaise — one that threatens the very pillars of American democracy.
