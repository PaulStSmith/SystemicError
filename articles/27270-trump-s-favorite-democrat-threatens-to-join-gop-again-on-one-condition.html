Trump's favorite Democrat threatens to join GOP again — on one condition

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosNovember 15, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

An ultimatum built around Israel“Country over party,” with Trump sparedA Democrat by ballot, an ally by choiceThe voters he left behind

John Fetterman says he could leave the Democratic Party if Senate Democrats replace Chuck Schumer with Chris Van Hollen, whose criticism of Israel the Pennsylvania senator considers disqualifying. That is the condition behind Fetterman’s latest flirtation with a party switch: not a break over LGBTQ rights, marijuana policy or domestic economics, but a warning that his allegiance may depend on whether his caucus chooses a leader willing to challenge Israel and confront Donald Trump more aggressively.

The ultimatum, delivered Saturday on Fox News, also clarifies what Fetterman’s cultivated political estrangement is doing. He is using the possibility of defection to pressure Democratic senators over their own leadership decision while presenting resistance to Trump as partisan spectacle and opposition to Israeli policy as hostility toward Israel itself.

An ultimatum built around Israel

Asked about the prospect of Van Hollen replacing Schumer, Fetterman said a caucus decision favoring the Maryland senator would be “troubling.”

“Yeah, absolutely,” he said when asked whether it could push him out of the party. Fetterman described Van Hollen as having made his name by being “very … hostile to Israel.”

That wording collapses several distinct positions into one politically useful accusation. According to AlterNet, Van Hollen has gained support from voters who want stronger opposition to the Trump administration and its harshest policies. Fetterman’s objection, however, centered on Israel and on Democratic lawmakers who favor withholding or conditioning assistance.

“Both in the House now and the Senate now, their first instincts are to punish Israel and to withhold these kinds of aid,” Fetterman said, arguing that Iran should instead be held accountable.

Fetterman has been one of the Senate’s staunchest supporters of Israel. Schumer shares that broad alignment, and Fetterman is backing him in the leadership contest. His intervention therefore is not merely an observation about feeling unwelcome among Democrats. It is an attempt to attach a potential institutional cost—his departure—to the caucus’s choice of leader.

“Country over party,” with Trump spared

Fetterman also defended the Trump administration from blame for high gasoline prices, saying that although Americans were suffering, he continued to blame Iran as well. He called his position “a political loser” but framed it as “country over party.”

That slogan does substantial work. It casts Fetterman’s own breaks with Democrats as principled independence while depicting investigations of Trump as partisan retaliation. He urged lawmakers to embrace bipartisanship during the administration’s final two years and warned against turning Congress into “a circus of constant hearings about payback.”

Congressional oversight, however, is not inherently payback. Hearings are among the legislature’s few direct tools for investigating executive conduct. By preemptively describing sustained scrutiny as circus politics, Fetterman adopts a framing that benefits the official whose administration would face examination: Trump. The people demanding accountability become the source of dysfunction; the administration exercising federal power recedes from view.

The same misdirection operates in Fetterman’s discussion of Israel. Lawmakers considering restrictions on aid are exercising authority over American resources and policy. Labeling that position punishment or hostility avoids the underlying dispute: whether unconditional U.S. support should continue and whether Democratic leaders may impose limits without being accused of antagonism toward an allied country.

A Democrat by ballot, an ally by choice

Fetterman entered the Senate after winning election in 2022 as a progressive. Since then, AlterNet reports, he has moved sharply right on several prominent issues, repeatedly broken with his caucus and supported Trump, particularly where U.S. policy toward Israel is concerned.

He has also said he would be an awkward fit in the Republican Party because he continues to support LGBTQ rights and marijuana legalization. Those positions complicate the partisan branding but do not erase the practical effect of his conduct. Party identity is less consequential than how a senator uses power: whom he protects, which policies he enables and what price he tries to impose on colleagues who dissent.

AlterNet reports that fears of a Fetterman defection have become less urgent after Democrats won 56 Senate seats in the midterms. That larger majority reduces his leverage over control of the chamber. His threat can still influence a leadership fight, but it no longer carries the same capacity to throw the majority into immediate chaos.

That may explain the increasingly explicit terms. A vague reputation for independence produces attention; a conditional threat names the demand. Keep Schumer, reject Van Hollen and preserve a leadership posture Fetterman regards as sufficiently supportive of Israel—or risk losing him.

The voters he left behind

Fetterman is expected to face serious Democratic primary opposition when he is next up for election in 2028. AlterNet also reports that his approval among Democratic voters has collapsed, putting a potential reelection bid in jeopardy.

That political weakness matters because Fetterman’s alienation is often narrated as though the party simply drifted away from him. The chronology points in the other direction. He won office as a progressive, changed his posture after securing a six-year term and now invokes leaving the party when its members consider leadership he opposes.

His Fox News appearance turns that record into an ultimatum aimed upward at Senate Democrats and outward at dissatisfied voters: accommodate his priorities or accept his departure. But with Democrats holding 56 seats, the caucus may have less reason to treat one senator’s threatened defection as a veto. The more immediate judgment belongs to Pennsylvania Democrats, who will eventually decide whether the label Fetterman still carries bears any meaningful relationship to the senator he has chosen to become.
