The Presidency Is Not a Body-Language Puzzle

A Diagnosis the Reporting Cannot Support

An article built around psychologist John Paul Garrison’s video commentary claims that Donald Trump’s altered eye contact and response to negative coverage may indicate neurological decline. Garrison cites clips from “the past week,” including an Oval Office exchange about a press ban, but the article supplies no date, medical examination, diagnosis, or corroborating clinical evidence. It also reports his speculation that Trump may have suffered a stroke “sometime last year.” Those are serious claims resting on observations made through video.

Speculation Disguised as Accountability

Garrison is identified as a clinical and forensic psychologist and body-language expert. That establishes his credentials; it does not turn remote observation into a neurological assessment.

He associates Trump’s reported difficulty maintaining eye contact with conditions including stroke, dementia, frontal-lobe dysfunction, and attention deficits. Yet the article provides no evidence that Trump has any of those conditions. Behavioral change can justify scrutiny. It cannot, by itself, establish a cause.

The framing is especially weak because it repeatedly moves from “appears” and “suggests” toward the broader premise that Trump’s health “continues to decline.” The evidence presented does not sustain that certainty.

The Press Ban Is the Exercise of Power

The politically consequential fact is not where Trump looked while answering questions. It is that the president was questioned about what the article calls his “new press ban.”

A president controls executive-branch access through institutional authority. Restricting the press is therefore an official use of power with consequences for scrutiny and public information. Whether Trump acted because criticism upset him, confused him, or no longer served his political interests is Garrison’s interpretation. The reported action does not require a speculative medical explanation to matter.

By treating the ban as a symptom, the article risks converting an accountable political decision into a clinical mystery.

Illness Is Not an Alibi

Garrison argues that Trump once used attacks on “fake news” to excite his supporters but now seeks to exclude unfavorable coverage because his resilience and clarity have weakened. The article offers no evidence establishing that internal explanation.

Even if a health problem were eventually documented, it would not erase agency elsewhere. A president does not govern alone. Officials implement access restrictions, advisers defend them, and institutions decide whether to resist or comply. None of those actors appears in this account.

That omission shifts attention from a power structure to one man’s face. It also grants unnamed enablers the convenience of invisibility.

Access Journalism Turns Inward

The fixation on eye contact illustrates a familiar failure in political coverage: when powerful officials restrict scrutiny, journalists can become absorbed in decoding personality instead of documenting machinery.

This is misdirection toward a weaker target—not Trump, who holds presidential power, but observable mannerisms that cannot answer questions or authorize policy. The meaningful inquiry is concrete: what did the reported ban prohibit, who ordered it, who enforced it, and what information became harder to obtain? The article answers none of those questions.

Power Does Not Need a Medical Explanation

The larger pattern is institutional evasion. Political conduct is psychologized, while the chain of authority behind it disappears.

The article may intend to expose incapacity. Instead, it weakens accountability by building its case on an unverified diagnosis and leaving the exercise of presidential power largely unexamined. A press restriction is not principally evidence about gaze control. It is evidence that someone with state authority restricted access—and that others enabled the restriction. That is the system worth investigating.
