Ex-aide reveals 'crazy' backstory behind Hegseth's Pentagon makeup room

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 18, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A Fox News imperative enters the Pentagon“Lethality” for the institution, vanity for the bossThe image campaign has personnel consequences

Pete Hegseth wanted a makeup studio inside the Pentagon because, according to his former spokesman, a friend of Hegseth’s wife said the defense secretary expected one for media appearances. The request matters less as a story about cosmetics than as evidence of how Hegseth reportedly runs the country’s military headquarters: public image elevated into an operational priority, even as he sells himself as an enemy of institutional softness and enforces ideological discipline on others.

John Ullyot, Hegseth’s former spokesman, told The Daily Beast Podcast that the proposed studio became emblematic of disorder inside the Pentagon. Ullyot described a defense secretary intensely attentive to presentation, from his light-blue suits to his flag-themed pocket squares—an image strategy that Ullyot suggested had helped Hegseth advance at Fox News.

A Fox News imperative enters the Pentagon

The request reportedly came through a friend of Jennifer Hegseth, the secretary’s wife. Ullyot recalled her saying, “Pete expects to have a makeup studio if he’s going to be talking to the media.” He said he found the demand unprecedented and “crazy,” adding that he had never known another defense secretary to install such a facility.

Makeup itself is not the scandal. Television officials routinely prepare for cameras, and looking polished does not impede military administration. The revealing part is the expectation that the Pentagon should create a dedicated studio around one official’s media persona—and that a friend of his wife apparently had enough access to convey the demand to his spokesman.

That arrangement blurs the line between public administration and personal brand management. The Pentagon is not a cable-news greenroom, and the defense secretary’s communications apparatus exists to inform the public about military policy, not to reproduce the production conditions that sustained his television career.

“Lethality” for the institution, vanity for the boss

Ullyot identified the contradiction directly. Hegseth speaks in the language of “lethality” and attacks “wokeness,” yet reportedly wanted a private makeup studio in his Pentagon office. The inconsistency is not that a man might wear makeup on television. It is that Hegseth’s political posture treats presentation, identity and perceived softness as legitimate grounds for policing other people while exempting his own image-conscious demands from the ridicule he directs outward.

That asymmetry is the point of the performance. “Wokeness” functions as a flexible accusation against disfavored personnel and priorities; it does not impose a coherent standard on the secretary himself. Hegseth can cultivate pocket squares, camera lighting and a dedicated cosmetic space while presenting his opponents as distractions from combat readiness. The branding remains masculine because the person with authority gets to define masculinity.

A story focused only on the room’s absurdity risks letting the spectacle conceal the power behind it. The consequential issue is not that Hegseth reportedly wanted to look good on camera. It is that the official empowered to shape military leadership appears, in his former spokesman’s account, to have made personal presentation part of the Pentagon’s institutional business.

The image campaign has personnel consequences

AlterNet also reports that Hegseth has purged dozens of officers for purportedly ideological reasons, with women and people of color disproportionately represented among those fired or denied promotion. That is where the politics of image cease to be merely embarrassing. Hegseth’s preferred picture of the military is backed by appointment, promotion and removal power.

The administration’s pursuit of Lt. Gen. Brett Sylvia further illustrates the imbalance between the official controlling the message and the officers subjected to that control. Citing earlier reporting by The Daily Beast and The Washington Post, AlterNet says the military was preparing criminal charges against Sylvia after Hegseth fired him the previous month. Sylvia, who led plans and policy and reported to Joint Chiefs Chairman Gen. Dan Caine, was accused of sharing information with journalists.

The reported case does not establish Sylvia’s guilt, and the supplied account does not specify the contemplated charges. But The Daily Beast characterized the effort as unusually aggressive punishment of a senior military leader over alleged contact with the press. Set beside Ullyot’s account, it exposes the governing hierarchy: Hegseth’s own media presentation reportedly warrants special accommodation, while unauthorized communication by subordinates can bring dismissal and potential prosecution.

The makeup room is therefore not the central abuse. It is the clarifying detail. Hegseth’s Pentagon, as described by his former spokesman and in the reporting on Sylvia, is preoccupied with controlling what the public sees, who gets to speak and which officers fit the leadership’s ideological image. The cosmetic studio was simply the most literal expression of that priority.
