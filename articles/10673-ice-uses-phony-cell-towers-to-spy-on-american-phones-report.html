ICE uses phony cell towers to spy on American phones: report

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 10, 20255 min read

President Donald Trump's administration has reinstated a program from the first presidency to trick cell phones into connecting to fake towers run by U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement to target immigrants. The problem, however, is that data from Americans is getting scooped up in the process, according to privacy advocates.

Forbes cited on Tuesday that "ICE purchased nearly $1 million worth of 'cell site simulator vehicles' in May this year." At the same time, ICE also has a contract worth as much as $4.4 million with Stingray manufacturer, Harris Corporation, to use its “equipment to determine the location of targeted mobile handsets.”

It was tipped off with the unsealing of a search warrant that was part of ICE's attempts to find an individual in Orem, Utah, from Venezuela. The warrant alleges that the man was in prison there for murder and is now suspected of participating in gang activity there.

"When the government got the target’s number, they first got a warrant to get its location," the report explained. "However, the trace wasn’t precise–it only told law enforcement that the target was somewhere in an area covering about 30 blocks. That led them to ask a court for a Stingray-type device to get an accurate location."

The American Civil Liberties Union, or ACLU, has attacked these kinds of systems, saying that in the process of gathering data from the target, Stingray devices are used. In the process, they "ensnare ... bystanders’ phones as well, raising serious privacy concerns."

The Detroit News discovered unsealed documents in 2017 that requested the search warrant to use the device to find a particular person from El Salvador. The ACLU noted, "search warrants are generally available only in criminal investigations, and here the government alleged that the immigrant was wanted for the crime of unlawfully re-entering the country after being deported."

At that time, the ACLU was concerned that ICE was also using "Stingrays for civil immigration enforcement operations." Forbes didn't clarify whether the warrant for the Venezuelan man in Utah was for civil enforcement or if another criminal investigation was involved.

Both deals were also used under the Biden administration. Trump used the programs as part of his operation to deport 3.13 million people. Despite allegations of an "open border," Biden's administration deported more people than Trump (4.4 million), CNN reported, citing the annual report from Homeland Security.

Our Analysis:

The Stingray Conundrum: A Tangled Web of Privacy, Surveillance, and Immigration

In an era where the boundaries of privacy are constantly being redrawn, the Trump administration's revival of a controversial surveillance method serves as a stark reminder of the pervasive encroachment of state power into the lives of individuals, particularly immigrants. The use of cell site simulator vehicles, colloquially known as "Stingrays," by U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) has raised significant alarm among privacy advocates, and for good reason. The implications of this program extend far beyond its intended targets, ensnaring the data of countless Americans in its wide net.

The Stingray: A Double-Edged Sword

At the heart of this issue lies the Stingray device, a tool that masquerades as a cell phone tower to trick phones into connecting with it. Originally touted as a precision tool for targeting specific individuals, the reality is far murkier. As reported by Forbes, the acquisition of nearly $1 million worth of cell site simulator vehicles, coupled with a $4.4 million contract with Stingray manufacturer Harris Corporation, underscores a significant investment in surveillance capabilities aimed at pinpointing the location of mobile handsets associated with immigrants.

The search warrant unsealed in the case of a Venezuelan individual suspected of gang activity in Orem, Utah, reveals the lengths to which ICE will go to locate a person. Despite obtaining a warrant for the target's location, the imprecision of initial methods led to the deployment of a Stingray to narrow down the search area. This incident illuminates a troubling aspect of Stingray use: the indiscriminate collection of data from all mobile phones within its reach, not just the intended target.

Privacy at the Crossroads

The American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU) has been vociferous in its criticism of Stingray devices, highlighting the serious privacy concerns they pose. The technology does not discriminate between the phone of a suspect and that of an innocent bystander, leading to the unwarranted collection of data from individuals unconnected to the investigation. This dragnet approach to data collection is emblematic of a broader disregard for individual privacy rights under the guise of immigration enforcement.

Moreover, the ACLU's discovery of documents requesting a search warrant for a Salvadoran individual wanted for unlawfully re-entering the country after deportation raises questions about the deployment of Stingrays beyond criminal investigations to civil immigration enforcement. This blurring of lines between criminal and civil matters represents a significant overreach, potentially subjecting individuals to invasive surveillance without the requisite legal thresholds being met.

A Bipartisan Affair

While the reinstatement of this program under the Trump administration might suggest a partisan issue, the reality is more complex. The continued use of Stingrays under the Biden administration, which has deported more individuals than its predecessor, indicates a bipartisan consensus on the utility of these surveillance tools in immigration enforcement. This continuity underscores a troubling trend towards the normalization of invasive surveillance tactics, irrespective of the administration in power.

Conclusion: A Call for Accountability

The use of Stingray devices by ICE raises fundamental questions about the balance between national security interests and the right to privacy. The indiscriminate collection of data from both targets and bystandans underlines the need for greater transparency, oversight, and accountability in the deployment of surveillance technology. As we navigate the murky waters of privacy in the digital age, it is imperative that we scrutinize the tools and tactics employed by law enforcement to ensure they do not infringe upon the civil liberties that form the bedrock of our democracy. The Stingray saga is a cautionary tale of what happens when the pursuit of security comes at the cost of our privacy and freedom.
