White House can’t back Trump claim as Fed’s own data shows opposite

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosAugust 19, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Tariffs as Theater, Costs as Policy

Trump’s tariff push is being sold as industrial revival, but the numbers in the source point the other way: higher prices, no demonstrated manufacturing boom, and declining factory construction spending from late 2024 into June 2026. When the White House was asked to support Trump’s claim of “the largest number of plants ever being built,” it could not produce a source. That is the central fact. The rest is branding.

Who Actually Holds Power

This is not a mystery story about market forces gone awry. The actor with power here is the president, backed by the White House and the tariff authority of the federal state. Trump made the claim. Trump set the policy. Trump owns the consequences.

The people absorbing those consequences do not control the terms. Workers, manufacturers, and consumers get to live inside the price shock and the uncertainty. Journalists can ask for evidence. Economists can explain the mechanism. None of them can set the tariffs.

The Manufacture of Confidence

The administration is trying to convert a political preference into a fact by repeating it loudly enough. That is not analysis; it is propaganda dressed as economic triumphalism. If the White House cannot back up a claim that a manufacturing boom is underway, then the claim is not evidence-based policy talk. It is an attempt to create confidence where the data show decline.

The decline matters. Federal Reserve figures cited in the source show manufacturing construction spending falling from $250.089 billion in November 2024 to $172.674 billion in June 2026. That is not the profile of an unfolding renaissance. It is the profile of hesitation, delay, and capital sitting still.

Uncertainty Is the Pointed Weapon

Robert Shapiro’s explanation is the least theatrical and most useful part of the source. Tariffs do not just raise direct costs; they poison planning. Investment depends on knowing what inputs will cost, what demand will look like, and whether today’s trade arrangement will be blown up tomorrow by presidential whim.

That is the damage Trump’s tariffs create: not “discipline,” not “resilience,” but uncertainty. Businesses that sell into Canada have to ask whether retaliation will make their goods less competitive. Firms deciding whether to build, hire, or expand have to price in conflict that the White House itself is manufacturing. This is not industrial policy. It is self-inflicted instability sold as strength.

Blame Flipped Upward

The political misdirection here is obvious. Tariffs are imposed from the top down, but the costs are pushed downward and outward, as if consumers, workers, and manufacturers are the ones responsible for the fallout. Then the administration tries to claim the opposite outcome anyway: more plants, more growth, more strength.

That is how bad policy survives in a propaganda environment. The harm is real, but the blame is scrambled. Prices rise, investment cools, construction falls, and the president speaks as if repetition can overwrite consequence. The White House’s inability to source its own boast should not be treated as a minor embarrassment. It is the operating method.

The Larger Pattern

This story is bigger than tariffs. It is about a governing style that treats the economy as a stage set for nationalist performance. The public gets slogans about rebuilding. Businesses get uncertainty. Consumers get higher prices. And the administration asks everyone to mistake disruption for strategy.

That pattern should be recognized for what it is: executive power used to impose costs, then narrate those costs as progress. The promised manufacturing revival is not being blocked by mystery forces. It is being undercut by the policy itself.
