Trump floods Supreme Court with 13 new problems

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 26, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe administration chose this battlefieldSix justices hold the decisive powerThe headline obscures the machineryA pipeline for presidential power

What happened

AlterNet reports that the Trump administration has asked the Supreme Court to take up 13 additional cases involving major parts of its second-term agenda. The disputes include an asylum ban, transgender military service, foreign-student deportations, university grant cuts, immigration bond hearings, federal prosecutors’ removal, nationwide injunctions, land use, horseracing, legal fees, and damages against government officials. The justices decide which petitions to accept, so none of these cases was guaranteed a hearing when reported.

Citing The Hill’s docket analysis, AlterNet says some disputes could be resolved as soon as the following year. It also portrays the Court’s six-member conservative majority as generally receptive to Trump’s agenda, while acknowledging administration losses involving tariffs and transgender medical care for prisoners.

The administration chose this battlefield

The “13 new problems” are not problems that simply arrived at the Court. They are petitions from an administration asking the justices to intervene. Trump’s executive branch made or defended the underlying policy choices, lost ground or encountered resistance elsewhere in the legal system, and then sought review from a Supreme Court whose ideological composition gives it reason to expect a favorable audience.

That is executive agency, not docket weather. The administration is using litigation as an instrument of governance across immigration, military policy, universities, federal employment, and official liability. The breadth matters because victories could do more than preserve isolated decisions: they could enlarge presidential control while narrowing the institutions capable of restraining it.

Six justices hold the decisive power

Trump can petition, but the Supreme Court controls access and outcomes. Its six conservative justices can choose which disputes to hear and, when sufficiently aligned, determine the governing law. The three liberal justices can object, dissent, and describe the consequences; they cannot set the result without conservative votes.

Justice Elena Kagan’s criticism, as quoted by AlterNet, targets that institutional reality. She argued that the Court has failed to account adequately for how its rulings damage the political system. Justice Clarence Thomas offered a competing historical rationale, presenting the Court’s conservative direction as a response to progressive ideas introduced into the political mainstream more than a century ago. That is not judicial neutrality. It is an openly ideological account of constitutional power.

The headline obscures the machinery

Calling these cases “problems” makes the administration sound burdened by legal complications. The reporting underneath describes something more consequential: the executive branch is affirmatively inviting a sympathetic Court to validate contested exercises of government power.

The framing also risks turning a coordinated legal strategy into a miscellaneous list stretching from asylum to horseracing. Subject matter can distract from structure. The recurring question is whether courts will permit broader executive authority, weaker remedies, or fewer constraints on officials. The people affected by deportation, military exclusion, detention, grant cancellations, and government misconduct hold far less power than the institutions deciding what protections remain available to them.

A pipeline for presidential power

The larger pattern is a feedback loop between executive ambition and judicial permission. The administration acts aggressively, lower courts test or block those actions, and the Supreme Court is asked to revise the boundaries. Each ruling can become precedent for the next assertion of authority.

AlterNet’s account does not establish that the administration will win these 13 petitions, or even that the Court will hear them all. It does establish who is driving the process and who possesses the final institutional leverage. The central political fact is not that Trump has delivered problems to the justices. It is that his administration sees the Court as a viable route for converting disputed presidential actions into durable national power.
