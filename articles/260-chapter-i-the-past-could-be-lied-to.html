Chapter I. The Past Could Be Lied To

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 23, 202612 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

By the time the banker in Zurich said yes, Adrian Vale knew the past could be lied to. Across the desk, the man held one hundred million dollars in gold that had never existed and found no reason to doubt it.

The banker did not say yes all at once. Men like him were trained against visible surrender. He said it first with the small softening at the corners of his mouth, then with the second glance at the seal, then with the way he placed the certificate back into its leather folder as though returning a relic to a chapel.

“The custody chain is unusual,” the banker said.

His name was Herr Keller. He was perhaps fifty, perhaps sixty, the sort of man whose age had been hidden under good tailoring and clean habits. His office looked out over a city that understood quiet wealth as a form of weather. Rain tapped at the windows. Beyond the glass, Zurich moved in gray November light, trams sliding along their lines, umbrellas bending in the wind, the lake somewhere beyond the buildings like a sheet of hammered pewter.

Adrian folded his hands in his lap.

“Unusual is why I am here.”

Keller allowed himself no laugh, but a little acknowledgment passed across his face. “Yes. Of course.”

On the desk between them lay the first of the receipts, though receipt was too small a word for what it pretended to be. It was a deferred-delivery bullion custody certificate issued in 1936 by a private house whose records, in this world, would survive every reasonable inquiry. It carried the name of a family trust whose beneficiaries had spent three generations avoiding governments, creditors, newspapers, marriages, and each other. It bore signatures of dead men. It referred to vault ledgers in Geneva, London, and Antwerp. It contained clauses that made immediate delivery nearly impossible without the consent of parties who had been designed, very carefully, to be dead, incapacitated, unavailable, or in dispute.

It was beautiful work.

Adrian hated it.

Keller lifted a second sheet, the legal opinion from a firm in Vaduz. That opinion was also false, though less false than the gold. The firm existed. The partner existed. The language belonged to the period, to the profession, to the species of caution that private wealth used when it wished to look dull. There were no flourishes. No declarations of grandeur. No single word that sounded like a story.

That was the art of the forgery. Not brilliance. Boredom.

“You understand,” Keller said, “that our bank would not treat such instruments as liquid assets in the ordinary sense.”

“I would be disappointed if you did.”

“They are restricted.”

“Yes.”

“The delivery covenants mature in stages. The first material window does not open until 1991.”

“That is my understanding.”

Keller looked at him over the rim of the paper. “You are a patient man, Mr. Vale.”

Adrian smiled in the careful way he had practiced, the smile of a man who had learned to let other people mistake restraint for breeding.

“I have been patient all my life.”

It was almost true, depending on where one decided his life had begun.

Keller read for another minute. Adrian let him. He had learned that silence was the native language of rooms like this. If you filled it, you looked hungry. If you defended yourself too quickly, you looked frightened. Money did not plead. Money waited.

The first month had taught him that. Or perhaps the future had. He was no longer sure where knowledge ended and discipline began.

In his coat pocket, folded twice and worn soft along the crease, was a sheet of hotel stationery covered in dates. November 1969. December. January 1970. August 1974 circled in black. August 1971 underlined once. June 1972 underlined twice. Beside each date, not instructions exactly, but weather signs.

Gold window.

Break-in.

Pardon.

Reagan.

Tulsa.

Tuskegee.

He had written them the first night in Zurich, after waking at three in the morning with his heart trying to claw through his ribs. For several seconds he had not known where he was. There had been no ceiling fan, no distant hum of an old refrigerator, no blue-white glow from a phone left charging on the bedside table. There had only been the heavy curtains of the Baur au Lac, the cold weight of European bedding, and the sound of church bells striking an hour that should have been impossible.

He had sat up, turned on the lamp, and found the date on the newspaper.

Monday, November 17, 1969.

The paper had carried, below the fold, a small syndicated column about the Kennedy years and the word Camelot, already polished by grief into something cleaner than government had ever been. Adrian had stared at it longer than the column deserved. America, he had thought, was still teaching itself to remember a court instead of an administration, a promise instead of a machine.

That was dangerous.

It was also why he had come.

He had laughed then. Once. Quietly. It was not joy. It was the sound a mind made when reality stopped negotiating.

Now Herr Keller laid the legal opinion down and aligned it precisely with the certificate beneath it.

“The question,” Keller said, “is what you wish to do.”

Adrian looked at the man’s hands. Clean nails. A wedding band. A faint dark dot near the knuckle of the right index finger, old ink perhaps, or a flaw in the skin. Human details were dangerous. They made it harder to remember that systems did not have faces until they needed someone else to pay.

“I wish to establish a managed credit facility.”

“Against the certificate?”

“Against a basket of instruments. This is the first.”

Keller’s expression changed by a degree so small that only a room like this could have noticed. “There are others?”

“Yes.”

“Of similar character?”

“Some. Not all.”

“And similar value?”

Adrian let three seconds pass.

“In aggregate, larger.”

The banker did not move. The rain filled the silence for him.

“How much larger?”

“Large enough that I am not looking for a single bank.”

That, at least, pleased Keller. A man in his position did not want to be told he was unneeded, but neither did he wish to be made solely responsible for a fortune that arrived without a history he could gossip about. Shared risk was respectability. Distributed uncertainty could pass for prudence.

“And the purpose of this facility?”

“Acquisitions. Endowments. Long-term industrial investments.”

“In Europe?”

“Some.”

“America?”

“Most.”

Keller leaned back. For the first time, his gaze left the papers and settled fully on Adrian’s face.

“America is not short of men with money.”

“It is short of institutions that cannot be bought by the wrong ones.”

The sentence landed more heavily than Adrian intended. Keller studied him with a private banker’s polite dislike of sincerity. Adrian regretted it immediately. Not the thought. The exposure.

He softened his tone.

“I am interested in newspapers,” he said. “Local papers, mostly. Some broadcasting. Community banking. Education. Health care. Aerospace.”

Keller blinked once at the last word.

“That is a wide field.”

“It is one field.”

“And what field is that?”

Adrian almost answered truth. He almost answered time. He almost answered America, and knew that would be worst of all.

“Continuity,” he said.

Keller accepted the word because it sounded like something a wealthy man might say when he wanted his vanity treated as philosophy.

“You will need counsel in New York.”

“Yes.”

“And counsel in London.”

“Yes.”

“Tax.”

“Naturally.”

“Communications law, if you are serious about broadcasting.”

“I am.”

“Banking regulators, if community banking is not a metaphor.”

“It is not.”

Keller touched the folder with two fingers. “These instruments can open doors, Mr. Vale. They will also invite questions.”

“That is why I am beginning here.”

“In Zurich?”

“With people who understand the value of not asking every question at once.”

Keller’s mouth almost became a smile again. “There are questions one asks now. There are questions one asks later. And there are questions one records in a memorandum to be opened by one’s successors.”

“I would prefer the first two categories.”

“Most clients do.”

Keller stood and moved to a side cabinet. He unlocked it with a small brass key, removed a tray, then a loupe, then a pair of cotton gloves. Adrian watched the ceremony. The banker had already examined the certificate once before the meeting began; the second examination was theater, but not empty theater. Institutions believed through ritual. A seal became real by being inspected. A signature became authoritative by making men in suits pause before it.

Adrian had forged not only paper, but behavior.

That was the part that frightened him most.

Keller bent over the certificate. “The aging is remarkable.”

“The documents were stored well.”

“Clearly.”

The banker turned it under the light. The watermark appeared and vanished. Fibers caught the glow. A blind stamp rose from the page with all the mute confidence of law.

Adrian knew the exact moment in the future when that stamp had been made. He knew the room, the heat of the machine, the woman in nitrile gloves who had held it up to the light and said, “For a film prop, this is an obscene amount of work.”

He had answered, “The director is obsessive.”

She had laughed at that. Why not? In the future, a certificate maturing in the 1990s was not a financial instrument. It was set dressing for a period drama, an antique fiction, a dead promise made harmless by time. No bank would lend against it. No vault would open for it. No auditor would care.

Only Adrian had known that the harmlessness depended on the century.

The others had made paper, ink, seals, signatures, ledgers, and dull little histories. Adrian had made purpose. That was the part no artisan, archivist, printer, prop master, or document consultant had been asked to see. They had been good people, most of them, which was precisely why he had not trusted himself to let them understand the shape of the excuse.

The limits had come later, written alone in rooms where sleep would not come. No direct editorial command. No private candidate ownership. No bank loan by personal order. No permanent founder veto. No intelligence service. No police force. No exceptions for emergencies without review.

The founder is a threat model, one of them had written across the top of the draft charter.

Adrian had kept the page.

Keller straightened. “We would begin conservatively.”

“I expected nothing else.”

“A preliminary facility well below face value. Subject to verification.”

“Of course.”

“And relationship management through a restricted committee.”

“Discreet?”

“Discreet is not the same as invisible.”

“I am aware.”

“Good. Invisible clients are often more trouble than visible ones.”

Adrian looked at the certificate. One hundred million dollars in nonexistent gold. One thin tile in a mosaic of lies that, if handled correctly, could become schools, clinics, newspapers, bank branches, legal defense funds, rockets, and perhaps someday a flag no child would be forced to salute in fear.

If handled incorrectly, it would become what all hidden power became when frightened: an empire with better slogans.

“Herr Keller,” he said, “I need to be clear about something.”

The banker waited.

“The work I intend to fund will attract enemies. Political enemies. Commercial enemies. Men who are accustomed to pressure as a first language. I will require structures that survive pressure.”

“That is what structures are for.”

“No,” Adrian said, more sharply than he meant to. “Most structures exist to hide pressure. I need structures that reveal it.”

Keller’s face cooled.

There it was again. Too much truth in the room. Adrian could feel the future in him like a fever, always trying to speak in a language that had not been invented yet.

He adjusted.

“Forgive me. I mean that I prefer robust governance. Audits. Separation of operational control. Defined authorities. I do not want everything dependent on my signature.”

Now Keller understood, or thought he did. Wealthy eccentrics often wished to be protected from heirs, mistresses, governments, creditors, and themselves. Self-restraint, properly invoiced, was a respectable service.

“That can be arranged.”

“Good.”

“Though most men in your position eventually discover they enjoy control more than they expected.”

“I am counting on it.”

Keller raised an eyebrow.

Adrian let the mask slip a millimeter, just enough for the older man to see fatigue.

“That is why the restraints must be real.”

For the first time, something like curiosity touched the banker without being immediately hidden. Perhaps he saw a client with unusual discipline. Perhaps he saw a young man afraid of inheritance. Perhaps he saw only a puzzle, and bankers were not immune to vanity when invited to solve one.

“You are a strange man, Mr. Vale.”

“I hope not fatally.”

This time Keller did smile.

He returned the certificate to its folder and closed the leather cover. The sound was soft, almost tender.

“I believe we may proceed.”

There it was.

Not the salvation of anything. Not yet. Only a sentence in a room built to make sin look administrative.

Adrian felt no triumph. Relief, yes, but it passed quickly. Beneath it was the old nausea, the one he had carried from the future into every polished hallway of the past. He had not built a school. He had not saved a paper. He had not put a doctor in a poor county or a fair loan in a Black family’s hands. He had not stopped a pardon, exposed a massacre, broken a myth, or made a rocket rise clean and reusable into a sky that belonged to everyone.

He had only proved that a lie could live.

Keller extended his hand.

Adrian stood and took it.

“Welcome to Zurich, Mr. Vale.”

“Thank you.”

The banker’s grip was dry, professional, and brief. Outside, the rain went on washing the streets of a city that would never know what had just begun inside one of its quieter rooms.

When Adrian stepped back into the corridor, a young assistant was waiting to escort him out. She carried herself with the precise efficiency of someone still new enough to be proud of it. Adrian wondered, absurdly, whether she would live long enough to see a different century. Whether she would see the old one bend. Whether any of them would know, when the first branch opened or the first headline landed or the first child looked down at Earth from orbit, that it had begun with paper and cowardice and a man pretending not to tremble.

At the lobby doors, he paused.

The assistant asked, “Is everything satisfactory, Mr. Vale?”

He looked through the glass at the rain, the trams, the people moving under umbrellas, each of them carrying a future he had no right to command.

“No,” he said.

She hesitated.

Adrian turned from the window and gave her the practiced smile.

“But it may become so.”

Outside, Zurich smelled of wet stone and exhaust. He raised his collar and walked without hurry toward the waiting car.

In his pocket, the hotel stationery pressed against his chest like a second heart.

August 1971.

June 1972.

August 1974.

Dates from a dead world.

Warnings from a future he had already betrayed.

By the time the driver opened the door, Adrian had begun arranging the next lie in his mind.
