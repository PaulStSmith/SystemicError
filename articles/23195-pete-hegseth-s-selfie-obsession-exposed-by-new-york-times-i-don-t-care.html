Pete Hegseth's 'selfie' obsession exposed by New York Times: 'I don't care'

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

A Photo, a Grudge, and the Rotten Logic of Power

The Trigger Was Never the Point

Pete Hegseth allegedly set out to punish a four-star general because he fixated on an Army photo he mistook for a selfie. That is the surface absurdity. The real story is worse: a defense secretary using the machinery of state to settle a grievance built on misrecognition, vanity, and the fantasy that loyalty is measured by bloodletting.

Who Actually Had Power

The decision-maker here was Hegseth. Joe Kasper, his chief of staff, tried to interrupt the delusion with facts, even preparing a notecard laying out Donahue’s record and correcting the basic error about the photo. Hegseth glanced at it and returned it. “I don’t care” is not confusion. It is the governing principle.

That is what institutional power looks like when it rots: a subordinate can present reality, and the official with authority can simply reject it. Facts do not matter when the office is being used as a loyalty machine.

The General Became a Sacrifice

Gen. Christopher Donahue was not responsible for security at the bombing site where 13 American service members were killed. But he was a top commander at the airport, and that was enough for Hegseth to decide he “needed to go,” allegedly because Trump wanted “heads to roll.”

That is not accountability. It is scapegoating dressed up as command discipline. The target was not chosen because the facts demanded it, but because the political mood demanded a body. The logic is primitive and familiar: someone must be made to suffer so the people at the top can look strong.

The Photo Was a Decoy

The photograph from Kabul mattered only because it gave Hegseth a prop. It was taken at 11:59 p.m. on August 30, 2021, one minute before the withdrawal deadline, and four days after the ISIS bombing. Those details do not support Hegseth’s fixation; they expose it.

The image was not evidence of vanity. It was evidence of departure, delay, catastrophe, and the end of a failed war. Turning that into a grievance about a supposed selfie is political misdirection with a childish face on it. The point was never the image. The point was to convert shame into blame and blame into obedience.

The Pattern Is the Message

This is what authoritarian-adjacent politics does inside institutions: it substitutes personal resentment for standards, spectacle for responsibility, and loyalty tests for judgment. A decorated officer with a three-decade career, more than 20 deployments, and a role in the effort to support Ukraine gets reduced to a target because he fits somebody’s narrative.

The deeper pattern is not incompetence. It is the deliberate degradation of institutional seriousness. When the people running the institution do not care whether the facts are true, the institution stops being a professional structure and starts becoming a theater for impulse, punishment, and propaganda.

What This Really Shows

The source story is not about one mistaken photo. It is about a defense secretary treating the Pentagon like a grievance chamber. That is how systems fail before they visibly break: the wrong people are empowered, the right people are ignored, and reality is treated as an obstacle to management.

If this is how power is used at the top, the public should not be fooled by talk of discipline or strength. This is not strength. It is institutional cowardice with a uniform on.
