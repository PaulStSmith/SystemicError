'Appalled' Gold Star mom: If Trump thinks we’ll stay silent — he’s 'wrong'

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosSeptember 19, 20262 min read

Trump’s Arch Would Turn Arlington’s Dead Into His Backdrop

The Monument Is the Message

The proposed 250-foot arch at Memorial Circle is not an innocent piece of infrastructure. It is a 25-story assertion of presidential vanity placed between the Lincoln Memorial and Arlington National Cemetery. The administration acknowledges that it would obstruct the existing view and answers that tourists could enjoy a “new” one from an observation deck—as if the purpose of Arlington were to furnish better sightseeing.

Power Belongs to the Builders

The people buried at Arlington have no agency in this decision. Their families have little institutional power over it. Donald Trump’s administration advanced the project, and the commission scheduled to vote on it holds the authority to permit or reject the transformation.

That structure matters. Gold Star mother Karen Meredith and retired Major Gen. Paul Eaton can object, but they cannot issue permits. When coverage frames the dispute chiefly as veterans “fighting” an arch, it risks obscuring the officials choosing to impose it. The burden belongs on the decision-makers, not on grieving families forced to defend a military cemetery from the government.

Grief Is Not a Tourist Attraction

Arlington restricts filming, photography, and other intrusions around funerals because burial ceremonies are private acts of mourning. An observation deck overlooking the cemetery would undermine that boundary by design. Meredith’s objection is concrete: tourists could watch funerals from above.

The administration’s promise of a “new view” exposes its priorities. It converts a sacred landscape into a viewing platform and private loss into scenery. That is not confusion about the cemetery’s purpose. It is a decision to subordinate that purpose to spectacle.

Three Years of Disruption

Eaton says construction would continue for 20 hours a day over three years. Arlington conducts burials every weekday. Those two facts describe the consequence more clearly than any architectural rendering: noise and industrial activity imposed beside repeated military funerals so that Trump can erect an enormous signature monument.

Calling the project controversial is inadequate. The relevant question is who decided that years of disruption to grieving families were an acceptable price for presidential self-commemoration.

A Monument That Erases the Landscape

The arch would interrupt the visual relationship between the Lincoln Memorial and the cemetery created during the Civil War. Cultural landscape expert Charles Birnbaum says it would alter the character of that ground; Eaton says it borders on sacrilege. A similar arch proposal was abandoned in 1916 after Frederick Law Olmsted Jr. argued that it would insult the Lincoln Memorial.

The proposed structure therefore does more than occupy space. It overwrites an existing memorial landscape—one connecting national fracture, military death, and the preservation of the Union—with a colossal object identified with a living politician.

The Politics of Appropriation

Authoritarian politics does not merely honor sacrifice. It appropriates sacrifice, using the dead to enlarge the leader while disregarding the people who bear the loss. Trump’s arch follows that pattern: state power, public ground, military symbolism, tourist spectacle, and personal grandeur fused into one structure.

The deeper institutional failure would be approval by officials who recognize the damage and authorize it anyway. The scandal is not that families must object loudly enough. It is that a government body could require Gold Star families to defend Arlington’s dignity against presidential vanity in the first place.
