Alarm as chasing bizarre theories becomes major White House time suck: report

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 15, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Social Security SideshowThe South African Farmer FarceChasing Shadows and Phantoms

A view of the White House by night in Washington, U.S., November 16, 2019. REUTERS/Yara Nardi/File Photo

Six months into his presidency, Donald Trump has turned the federal government into a conspiracy theory factory, deploying taxpayer resources to investigate debunked claims while his administration grinds to a halt chasing internet fantasies, the Washington Post reported Monday.

When Trump falsely claimed millions of dead people were collecting Social Security, his administration spent 11 weeks overhauling the agency's database. When he bought into racist conspiracy theories about White South African farmers being "systematically massacred," U.S. officials developed plans to resettle 1,000 of them as refugees.

Most recently, the Justice Department wrapped up a months-long investigation using "considerable FBI resources" to debunk internet conspiracy theories about Jeffrey Epstein's death—concluding what three previous investigations already determined: Epstein had no client list and died by suicide.

"We used to say, 'Well, it's just some guy in his basement, he doesn't have the power to do anything,'" said Joseph Uscinski, a University of Miami political scientist. "But people in government have power to do seriously damaging things. That is the issue now."

The Social Security fiasco exemplifies Trump's dangerous approach. Despite no evidence of fraud, the administration forced agency employees to spend months moving 12.3 million long-dead people to different databases.

"For at least a month or two, that was multiple people's only job," one employee revealed to the Post.

Former Social Security administrator Martin O'Malley told the Post, "They are spending a lot of effort and a lot of money to give cover to the big lie—in this case, the big lie that lots of dead people are receiving Social Security."

Trump's obsession with South African farmers proved equally baseless. He confronted South African President Cyril Ramaphosa in the Oval Office, showing him a video of what he claimed were "more than 1,000 graves of murdered farmers"—which turned out to be protest props, not actual graves.

The administration has also launched investigations into Biden's use of an autopen machine, despite such devices being standard presidential tools since George W. Bush.

Under RFK Jr., Health and Human Services is now investigating the thoroughly debunked link between vaccines and autism, with Kennedy promising a "massive testing and research effort" involving "hundreds of scientists."

"When people who have power act on conspiracy theories, especially if they are in government, they can do so in a way that can seriously harm others," Uscinski warned. "When you start chasing phantoms, you will end up chasing real people."

Our Analysis:

A Dive into the Depths of Trump's Conspiracy Theory Factory

In what reads like a script ripped straight from the most far-fetched political thriller, the Washington Post's latest exposé reveals an unsettling reality: under Donald Trump's presidency, the U.S. federal government morphed into a bona fide conspiracy theory manufacturing plant. This isn't just about political squabbles or ideological differences. It's a stark illustration of how the highest echelons of power can be mobilized to chase after windmills, squandering taxpayer dollars and governmental resources on debunked, sometimes dangerously racist fantasies.

The Social Security Sideshow

Let's start with Trump's baseless assertion that millions of dead people were somehow cheating the system to collect Social Security benefits. No evidence? No problem. The administration didn't hesitate to overhaul the Social Security agency's database, a wild goose chase that commandeered the agency's focus for 11 weeks. According to an agency employee, this farce was the sole responsibility of multiple staff members for months. Martin O'Malley, a former Social Security administrator, didn't mince words, labeling the effort a colossal waste designed to "give cover to the big lie."

The South African Farmer Farce

Trump's fixation on the supposed "systematic massacre" of White South African farmers is another chapter in this saga of delusion. Despite the claim being rooted in racist conspiracy theories rather than facts, it prompted U.S. officials to draft plans to resettle 1,000 of these farmers as refugees. Trump even went as far as confronting South African President Cyril Ramaphosa with a video of "graves" that turned out to be nothing more than protest props. This incident underscores not just a detachment from reality, but a willingness to strain diplomatic relations over unfounded allegations.

Chasing Shadows and Phantoms

Perhaps the most damning indictment of this conspiracy theory industrial complex is the Justice Department's investigation into Jeffrey Epstein's death. Utilizing "considerable FBI resources," this inquiry simply echoed the conclusions of three prior investigations: Epstein died by suicide, and no mythical client list existed. This futile exercise epitomizes the administration's propensity to indulge internet rumors at the expense of focusing on real and pressing issues.

Joseph Uscinski, a political scientist from the University of Miami, captures the crux of the problem: individuals in government wielding their power to pursue baseless theories can cause tangible harm. The article illustrates this through the Social Security debacle, the absurdity surrounding South African farmers, and the baseless investigations into Biden's use of an autopen and the thoroughly debunked link between vaccines and autism under RFK Jr.'s direction at Health and Human Services.

These aren't just harmless eccentricities or political machinations. They represent a dangerous diversion of government focus and resources from genuine issues that affect the lives of millions. When the administration prioritizes debunked theories over data-driven policy, it not only erodes public trust but also squanders the opportunity to address the myriad challenges facing the nation.

In conclusion, the Washington Post's report isn't just a revelation of a government's descent into conspiracy theory madness; it's a clarion call for rationality, evidence-based policy making, and the dire need for accountability at the highest levels of power. When those in charge chase phantoms, it's the public that bears the cost.
