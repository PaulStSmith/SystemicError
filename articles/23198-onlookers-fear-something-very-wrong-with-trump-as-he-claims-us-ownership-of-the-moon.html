Onlookers fear ‘something very wrong’ with Trump as he claims US ownership of the moon

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosSeptember 7, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Office Is the StoryDelusion as Political StyleMisdirection by the Pressed PanicThe Real EnablerWhat This Moment Reveals

A President Posting the Empire

Donald Trump spent Sunday spraying the internet with dozens of AI-fed fantasies: the moon as U.S. property, Iran redrawn to flatter his profile, military robots with glowing eyes, and images of destruction packaged as boasts. The source is not subtle about the spectacle. What matters is that the spectacle came from the sitting president, the one person who still holds the codes to nuclear force.

The Office Is the Story

The central fact is not that Trump acted erratically online. It is that he occupies the most powerful political office on earth and uses it like a personal propaganda channel. That is the institutional reality here. The posts are absurd, but the authority behind them is real. The problem is not merely a president with a damaged public persona. It is a presidency that can metabolize delusion into official gravity.

Tom Nichols’ warning in the source gets the scale right: this is not a random celebrity meltdown. It is a man with unilateral destructive power broadcasting fantasies of domination, conquest, and annihilation. That is not a sideshow. That is the office.

Delusion as Political Style

Trump’s posts are not just incoherent. They are imperial in shape. “The moon is ours,” redraw Iran, rename New Mexico, blow up the earth, buy stocks in the Oval Office. The common thread is ownership, conquest, and impunity. This is how authoritarian aesthetics work when they are translated into social media sludge: every image says the same thing, which is that borders, institutions, and reality itself exist to be bent around one man’s appetite.

Calling this “bizarre” is accurate but incomplete. Bizarre is the surface. Power is the substance. The point is to normalize a sovereign who treats the world as an editable file.

Misdirection by the Pressed Panic

The usual framing invites readers to focus on Trump’s personal instability, as if the main issue were a strange man having a strange day. That is too small. Personal instability matters because the system places enormous force in his hands anyway. The deeper failure is institutional: a political order that can watch a president post AI war fantasies and still leave him in command of state violence.

This is where the commentary often goes soft. It turns deliberate projection into medical mystery, and political danger into a personality quiz. Trump is not just confused. He is performing domination in a medium built to reward noise, and the state is still attached to him.

The Real Enabler

The enabler is not mystery. It is concentration of power. One man can publish fantasies, summon attention, and remain the single authority over nuclear use. That is the structure that makes the spectacle dangerous. The internet is merely the stage; the presidency is the weapon.

The source’s strongest detail is also the most damning: the same figure posting about the moon also has access to the mechanisms of annihilation. That combination should be treated as a constitutional emergency, not as viral content. When public life reduces presidential conduct to “bonkers,” it softens the fact that a republic has handed extraordinary coercive power to an erratic performer.

What This Moment Reveals

This story is not about one bad evening online. It is about a political system that keeps mistaking domination for leadership and noise for governance. Trump’s posts reveal a broader pattern: authoritarian politics does not always arrive as a coup. Sometimes it arrives as a feed, a throne image, a conquest fantasy, and a public so inundated with spectacle that the concentration of power feels normal.

The lesson is not that the president is strange. The lesson is that the office remains dangerously overpowered, and the people around it have built too much of American governance around tolerating whatever comes out of it next.
