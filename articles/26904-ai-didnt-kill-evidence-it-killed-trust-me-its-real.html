AI Didn’t Kill Evidence. It Killed “Trust Me, It’s Real.”

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 28, 20267 min read

A 15-year-old girl in Florida apparently wanted to frame her ex-boyfriend.

Not long ago, accomplishing that convincingly might have required considerable technical ability. Fake an account. Manipulate an image. Reproduce the interface convincingly. Make everything line up well enough that somebody would believe it.

In October 2026, according to the Hillsborough County Sheriff’s Office, she allegedly used artificial intelligence to alter an image of an Instagram post so that it appeared her ex-boyfriend had threatened a shooting at Riverview High School.

School administrators received it.

Police became involved.

The boy was questioned.

And he hadn’t written it.

Investigators eventually traced the supposed evidence back to his ex-girlfriend, who authorities say admitted sending the email knowing that the image had been altered using AI.

That story will inevitably be presented as another warning about how dangerous artificial intelligence has become.

It is.

But not for quite the reason people think.

The frightening development isn’t simply that AI can fabricate evidence.

People have fabricated evidence since evidence existed.

The revolution is that fabrication has become cheap, fast, accessible and good enough that the old shortcut—“look at this”—is rapidly becoming worthless.

And that changes considerably more than Photoshop.

The Screenshot Used to Be the Evidence

For most of the digital era, authentication operated on a largely reasonable assumption.

Manipulating convincing evidence required effort.

You could fake photographs before Photoshop. You could fabricate audio before voice cloning. You could forge documents before computers.

But doing it convincingly required skill, equipment, time or access that most people simply didn’t have.

That scarcity created an implicit evidentiary advantage for recorded material.

Someone produced a photograph.

Someone produced an audio recording.

Someone produced a screenshot.

Unless there was some specific reason to suspect manipulation, we generally started from the proposition that the thing probably represented something that actually happened.

That world is disappearing.

The Florida teenager didn’t need an effects studio.

She apparently needed an AI tool and sufficient teenage spite.

That is an entirely different threat model.

Baltimore Showed Us What Comes Next

The Florida case could almost be dismissed as a particularly stupid teenage revenge scheme.

A case from Maryland cannot.

In 2024, an athletic director at Pikesville High School was accused of creating an AI-generated recording impersonating Principal Eric Eiswert.

In the fake recording, “Eiswert” appeared to make racist and antisemitic remarks.

It spread.

The principal was removed from the school.

He received threats.

Police reportedly maintained a presence around his home.

People became furious at a man for words he had never spoken.

And here is the important part:

Investigators did not solve the case because somebody listened closely and declared, “Aha! At 23.7 seconds you can hear the characteristic frequency distortion of an AI voice synthesizer.”

They followed evidence.

They traced purchases.

They examined browsing activity.

They connected network activity.

They found source material.

In other words, when the recording itself became unreliable, investigators stopped treating the recording as the end of the investigation.

They investigated its provenance.

That is the future.

Not magical AI detectors.

Forensics.

“But That Could Be AI” Is About to Become the New “I Don’t Recall”

There is another problem, however, and it may ultimately be worse than fake evidence.

Once everyone knows that photographs, video and audio can be fabricated, people caught by real photographs, video and audio suddenly acquire a wonderfully convenient defense:

“That isn’t me.”

“That recording is fake.”

“That video was generated.”

“You can’t prove I actually said that.”

This is sometimes called the “liar’s dividend.”

The better synthetic media becomes, the more valuable it becomes not only to people creating fake evidence, but also to people trying to escape genuine evidence.

We have already seen the argument attempted in court.

In 2023, attorneys for Tesla resisted efforts to question Elon Musk about recorded public statements concerning Autopilot. Among their arguments was that Musk, being famous, was frequently the subject of deepfakes and therefore recordings attributed to him might not necessarily be genuine.

Judge Evette Pennypacker was not impressed.

She called the argument “deeply troubling.”

Quite rightly.

Because taken to its logical conclusion, the existence of deepfakes would provide every sufficiently famous—or sufficiently shameless—person with permanent plausible deniability.

Did you say this?

“Could be AI.”

Did you do this?

“Could be AI.”

Here is a video of you doing it.

“Deepfake.”

Here is another angle.

“Also deepfake.”

Here are thirty people who watched you do it.

“Well, perhaps they’re AI.”

At some point evidentiary skepticism stops being skepticism and becomes Calvinball.

Courts Were Built for a Different Technological World

The traditional rules for authenticating evidence were developed when convincing fabrication was comparatively difficult.

That does not mean courts simply accepted anything somebody waved around.

But the threshold for establishing that a photograph, screenshot or recording was what its proponent claimed could often be relatively modest.

A witness could testify that the image accurately represented what they saw.

Someone could testify that they took the screenshot.

A participant could identify the voices on a recording.

Then the opposing side could challenge its reliability and the jury could decide what weight to give it.

That framework made sense when sophisticated forgery was exceptional.

It makes substantially less sense when fabrication can be performed during a coffee break.

The federal Advisory Committee on Evidence Rules has been wrestling with exactly this problem.

As of October 2026, it is considering a proposed Rule 901(c) aimed specifically at deepfakes.

The basic idea is sensible.

If the opposing party provides enough evidence to raise a credible question that an item has been artificially fabricated, the burden shifts toward the party offering it to demonstrate that it is probably authentic.

That is not an attack on evidence.

It is an acknowledgment that the technological assumptions underneath the old standard have changed.

Evidence Is Becoming a Chain Instead of an Object

For decades, we tended to think of evidence as an object.

Here is the photograph.

Here is the recording.

Here is the screenshot.

Increasingly, that will not be enough.

The evidence will be the photograph plus its provenance.

Where did it originate?

Where is the original file?

What device created it?

When?

What metadata exists?

Was it uploaded directly from the recording device?

Who possessed it between creation and presentation?

Do server logs corroborate it?

Do messages from the same period reference the event?

Are there independent recordings?

Are there witnesses?

Does location data align?

Does network traffic establish where it came from?

Can the supposedly originating platform confirm that the post ever existed?

The file becomes one component in an evidentiary chain rather than an oracle descending from Mount JPEG.

And frankly, that is probably healthier.

The Detector Arms Race Won’t Save Us

There will inevitably be enormous demand for software promising:

99.8% ACCURATE AI DETECTION.

We have already watched this circus play out with AI-generated text.

Someone builds a generator.

Someone else builds a detector.

The generator improves.

The detector improves.

The generator adapts to the detector.

The detector adapts to the generator.

Around and around we go.

Detection may be useful as one forensic tool.

It cannot become the foundation of our concept of truth.

If your standard for determining whether a recording is real is asking another neural network whether the first neural network made it, congratulations: you have not established provenance.

You have created two computers arguing with each other.

The stronger defense is external evidence.

A synthetic video can imitate what happened inside the frame.

It has considerably more difficulty fabricating the entire surrounding universe.

The camera file.

The cloud backup.

The message sent thirty seconds afterward.

The cellular tower records.

The security camera across the street.

The person standing beside you.

The server log.

The transaction.

The timestamp from an unrelated system.

The browser history of the person who created the fake.

That is precisely why investigators in these early cases were able to unravel them.

The fake may be sophisticated.

Its creator still lives in reality.

AI Has Not Destroyed Truth

There is a temptation whenever this subject appears to throw up our hands and announce that soon nobody will know what is real.

That conclusion is both melodramatic and dangerous.

Because it hands exactly what bad actors want to them for free.

If everybody accepts that “nothing can be trusted anymore,” then fake evidence becomes useful—and authentic evidence becomes useless.

That is the liar’s dividend taken to its endpoint.

The correct lesson is almost the opposite.

We can no longer afford intellectual laziness.

“Here is a screenshot” cannot automatically settle an argument.

Neither can “that might be AI” automatically destroy one.

Both claims require evidence.

We are moving from an era in which authenticity was frequently assumed unless challenged to one in which authenticity increasingly must be demonstrated through provenance and corroboration.

That will make investigations harder.

It will make journalism harder.

It will make courts work harder.

It will make ordinary people slow down before destroying somebody’s life over the viral outrage of the afternoon.

None of those things is necessarily bad.

AI did not make truth obsolete.

It made “trust me, I have a screenshot” obsolete.

And perhaps that particular death was overdue.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
