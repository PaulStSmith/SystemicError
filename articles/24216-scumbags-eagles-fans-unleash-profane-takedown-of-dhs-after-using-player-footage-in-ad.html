'Scumbags!' Eagles fans unleash profane takedown of DHS after using player footage in ad

Based on an article by Matthew Chapman

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power belongs to DHSBorrowed glory, redirected at migrantsThe NFL had leverageThe outrage is not the storyA government dressed as a franchise

What happened

The Department of Homeland Security used footage of former Philadelphia Eagles player Brian Dawkins in a charged social-media post about reports of an approaching migrant caravan. RawStory reports that the post declared “our time has come,” prompting the NFL to request its removal.

Eagles fans and other commenters denounced the use of the footage and urged the team and league to respond. The reporting does not establish who inside DHS approved the post, whether the Eagles or Dawkins authorized it, or whether DHS ultimately removed it.

The power belongs to DHS

The angry fans are the headline, but they are not the powerful actors. DHS is a federal department with enforcement authority over immigration. It chose to wrap that authority in the emotional language and imagery of professional football.

That distinction matters. Fans supplied backlash; government officials supplied the propaganda. Treating the episode chiefly as a profane online quarrel shrinks an exercise of state messaging into a culture-war spectacle.

Borrowed glory, redirected at migrants

The post appears designed to transfer the drama of athletic combat onto immigration enforcement. Dawkins’s image supplied the heroic spectacle; reports of migrants supplied the adversary.

The article does not establish the caravan’s size, location, composition, or legal circumstances. Yet DHS’s language cast its approach as a moment for confrontation. That is political misdirection: complex questions about human beings and government power are converted into a team-versus-enemy narrative.

The NFL had leverage

The NFL’s takedown request was more consequential than any insult in the replies. The league controls valuable footage and therefore had institutional leverage against its use. RawStory does not report the legal basis for the request or its outcome, but the intervention challenged DHS’s attempt to conscript the league’s imagery into federal messaging.

The Eagles and Dawkins, meanwhile, are central to the symbolism but absent from the reported response. Calls for them to condemn the post reflect a real question: whether institutions whose cultural authority is appropriated by government will defend the boundary or quietly benefit from ambiguity.

The outrage is not the story

RawStory foregrounded fans calling DHS officials “scumbags.” That captures anger but obscures agency. The essential event was not that citizens swore at a department. It was that a department with coercive power used sports footage to dramatize migrants as an approaching opponent.

This is how authoritarian-style propaganda becomes ordinary: state power borrows familiar entertainment, strips vulnerable people of context, and presents enforcement as a righteous contest. The spectacle encourages audiences to choose a jersey before asking what the government is doing.

A government dressed as a franchise

DHS did not need Eagles footage to communicate policy or public-safety information. Its use served a political aesthetic—heroic defenders, looming outsiders, and an appointed hour of action.

That pattern replaces accountable government with branding. Officials become warriors, enforcement becomes entertainment, and migrants become narrative equipment. The institutional danger lies beyond one deleted—or undeleted—post: agencies with immense power learn that spectacle can conceal the people, decisions, and consequences underneath it.
