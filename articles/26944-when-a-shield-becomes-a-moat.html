When a Shield Becomes a Moat

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 2, 202610 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Senator Bill Hagerty wants Jack Smith disbarred.

On September 30, Hagerty filed a professional-misconduct complaint against the former special counsel, accusing Smith of abusing his authority when investigators obtained telephone records belonging to members of Congress during the federal investigation into efforts to overturn the 2020 election. Hagerty argues, among other things, that Smith failed to adequately account for the Constitution’s Speech or Debate Clause when prosecutors obtained those records and secured orders preventing telecommunications companies from notifying the affected lawmakers.

There is a legitimate constitutional principle buried inside that argument.

There is also a potentially extraordinary expansion of it.

The Speech or Debate Clause exists to protect Congress from interference by the other branches. A senator cannot be prosecuted because of a vote. A representative cannot be dragged before a grand jury and forced to explain why she introduced a bill. Prosecutors cannot simply turn congressional deliberation into evidence because they find those deliberations useful.

That protection is broad for a reason.

It is not unlimited for exactly the same reason.

The Supreme Court warned more than half a century ago against extending the privilege to everything a member of Congress can somehow connect to legislative work. In United States v. Brewster, the Court observed that legislators perform countless political and official activities that are not themselves legislative acts. In Gravel v. United States, the Court described protected activity as conduct integral to the “deliberative and communicative processes” through which Congress actually performs its constitutional functions. The Congressional Research Service summarizes the doctrine similarly: communications outside the legislative process generally do not acquire constitutional protection merely because a legislator participates in them.

That distinction matters enormously in the Jack Smith controversy.

Because Smith did not, in the episode at the center of Hagerty’s complaint, obtain recordings of senators’ telephone conversations.

Investigators obtained toll records.

According to records released by the Senate Judiciary Committee, the data for several lawmakers covered January 4 through January 7, 2021. It showed information such as when calls occurred, who was contacted and how long the calls lasted. It did not contain the conversations themselves.

Four days.

January 4.

January 5.

January 6.

January 7.

That is not a random slice of the calendar.

And Smith testified on September 29 that investigators sought those records because communications involving members of Congress were relevant to the investigation. He emphasized that toll records are metadata rather than content and said his office consulted the Justice Department’s Public Integrity Section before issuing the subpoenas to determine whether doing so was appropriate.

None of that automatically establishes that every subpoena was proper.

But it does expose the danger in the constitutional theory now being advanced against Smith.

The Phone Call Is Not the Legislative Act

Suppose a senator is discussing an upcoming vote with another senator.

That discussion may well implicate the Speech or Debate Clause.

Now suppose a telephone company possesses a record saying only:

4:37 p.m.
Senator A called Person B.
Duration: 17 minutes.

That record does not tell investigators what legislation they discussed.

It does not tell investigators how the senator intended to vote.

It does not reveal a caucus strategy.

It does not reveal committee deliberations.

It does not reveal the conversation at all.

It establishes that a communication occurred.

If the mere existence of that record becomes constitutionally protected because the call might have involved legislative business, the privilege has changed character.

Investigators would first need to know what the conversation concerned before they could determine whether the conversation was protected.

But they could not investigate the communication because it might be protected.

And they could not obtain the metadata that might establish its relevance because that metadata would also be treated as protected.

The argument eats its own tail.

“You may not obtain the record because the call might concern legislative activity.”

Fine.

How do we determine whether it did?

“You would have to investigate the communication.”

Fine.

May we do that?

“No. It might concern legislative activity.”

At that point, the Speech or Debate Clause is no longer protecting legislative acts.

It is protecting the evidentiary perimeter surrounding a legislator.

That is something very different.

The Supreme Court confronted a related problem when Senator Lindsey Graham challenged questioning arising from the Georgia investigation into efforts to alter the 2020 election result. Lower courts protected questioning concerning genuine legislative fact-finding while allowing inquiry into matters outside that protected sphere. The Supreme Court declined to disturb that approach. The government’s filing in the case pointed to the danger of letting legislators immunize communications merely by connecting them somehow to legislative activity.

That is precisely why “a senator was involved” cannot be the end of the analysis.

Congressional status is not a constitutional force field.

Then Comes the Gag Order

Hagerty’s complaint goes further.

Smith’s prosecutors did not merely obtain the records. They obtained nondisclosure orders preventing the telephone companies from telling the affected lawmakers about the subpoenas.

Presented without context, that sounds ominous.

Secret subpoenas.

Secret collection of senators’ records.

A judge ordering telecommunications companies to keep quiet.

But secrecy is not some exotic invention Jack Smith devised for this investigation.

Congress wrote it into federal law.

Under 18 U.S.C. § 2705, a court may prohibit a communications provider from notifying someone about a government request when the court finds reason to believe notification could cause specified harms. Those include destruction or tampering with evidence, intimidation of witnesses, flight from prosecution, and—critically—conduct that would “otherwise seriously jeopardiz[e] an investigation or unduly delay[] a trial.”

There is an obvious reason for that provision.

Imagine investigators attempting to reconstruct a network of communications during a suspected criminal scheme.

They want to know who contacted whom.

In what sequence.

At what time.

How frequently.

And while they are doing that, they notify everyone whose records they are examining:

Hello. Federal investigators are mapping your communications.

Anyone who receives that warning can change behavior immediately.

Switch phones.

Use another application.

Move conversations to an intermediary.

Stop calling a particular person.

Delete material.

Warn somebody else.

The notification itself can alter the evidence environment the investigators are trying to understand.

That is why nondisclosure exists.

Not because secrecy is inherently virtuous.

Because sometimes an investigation ceases to work if everyone involved receives advance notice of what investigators are looking at.

The Senator Does Not Have to Be the Suspect

There is another linguistic sleight of hand worth avoiding.

Having your telephone records subpoenaed does not necessarily mean you are the target of the investigation.

Investigators may be interested in Person A and discover evidence that Person A repeatedly communicated with Senator B during the period under investigation.

They might want Senator B’s toll records to establish whether those communications occurred.

That does not necessarily mean they believe Senator B committed a crime.

It may simply mean Senator B sits on one end of a relevant communication.

So consider the investigative logic:

We are investigating A.

Evidence indicates A communicated with B during the alleged scheme.

We want metadata establishing when those communications occurred.

We do not want B notified because B could alert A or otherwise compromise the investigation.

Nothing conceptually extraordinary is happening there.

Change “Senator B” to “Accountant B,” “Attorney B,” “Businessman B” or “Neighbor B,” and nobody would have difficulty understanding the logic.

The constitutional question arises because legislators possess a real and important privilege protecting legislative acts.

But that raises the question Hagerty’s theory threatens to skip:

What exactly is the protected act?

The senator’s vote?

Protected.

Congressional deliberation?

Potentially protected.

Committee work?

Protected within the constitutional sphere.

A conversation integral to that process?

Potentially protected.

The bare fact that a telephone call occurred?

That is a much harder argument.

And the proposition that investigators must notify the senator before obtaining that fact?

That moves even further away from protecting legislative activity.

From Privilege to Early-Warning System

Follow the progression.

First:

A legislative act is protected.

That is constitutional doctrine.

Then:

A communication concerning a legislative act may be protected.

Still defensible.

Then:

Metadata showing that the communication occurred must itself be treated as protected because the underlying discussion might have involved legislative activity.

Now the perimeter is expanding.

Then:

Investigators must alert the member of Congress before obtaining that metadata so the member can challenge the subpoena.

Now something fundamental has changed.

The legislator has acquired something approaching an investigative early-warning system.

And that is where the argument becomes dangerous as a matter of institutional design.

Imagine this rule stripped of names and parties.

A president is suspected of participating in an unlawful attempt to interfere with an electoral proceeding.

Investigators develop evidence that people working with the president repeatedly communicated with senators during the critical period.

The government wants several days of non-content telephone metadata to establish the communication pattern.

But investigators cannot obtain it secretly.

The senators must first be notified.

Why?

Because their conversations might have concerned legislative activity.

Those senators can then challenge the subpoenas—and, merely by being notified, learn that investigators are examining that communications network.

It requires no imagination to understand how such a rule could impair an investigation involving political officials.

And there is no reason to assume the rule would remain confined to Jack Smith, Donald Trump or Republican senators.

Constitutional rules survive their immediate controversies.

That is the point.

Every power granted to Bill Hagerty today becomes a power available to Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez tomorrow.

Every investigative restriction imposed on a Democratic Justice Department becomes a restriction imposed on a Republican one.

Every privilege expanded to protect one congressional faction becomes available to the other.

That is why doctrine matters more than personalities.

The Difference Between a Shield and a Moat

The Speech or Debate Clause is a shield.

It prevents the executive and judiciary from punishing legislators for performing legislative functions.

That shield is indispensable.

But there is a difference between protecting the legislative act and constructing an exclusion zone around the legislator.

A shield says:

You may not prosecute me for this vote.

A wider shield says:

You may not interrogate me about protected legislative deliberations.

A still wider shield says:

You may not obtain communications containing those deliberations.

But eventually we arrive at:

You may not obtain records showing that I communicated with someone because the communication might have involved legislative business.

And then:

You must warn me before attempting to obtain those records.

That is no longer merely a shield around legislation.

It is a moat around the legislator.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly resisted that transformation. Brewster explicitly warned against stretching Speech or Debate protection to every activity a member can somehow relate to legislative work, because almost anything a legislator does can ultimately be described that way.

That warning seems remarkably prescient here.

A senator calls somebody.

The call may concern legislation.

Therefore the call may be privileged.

Therefore the metadata may implicate privilege.

Therefore the subpoena may implicate privilege.

Therefore secrecy surrounding the subpoena may prevent assertion of the privilege.

Therefore the senator must be notified.

And suddenly a constitutional protection designed to preserve legislative independence has produced a practical mechanism by which legislators receive advance warning when investigators examine communications touching them.

That is a very different constitutional architecture from the one we started with.

Hagerty May Still Have a Case to Make

None of this means Smith’s conduct is beyond scrutiny.

Hagerty alleges that Smith failed in his duty of candor to the court when seeking nondisclosure and should have specifically alerted the judge to potential Speech or Debate issues. That is a concrete allegation and can be evaluated against the actual applications, legal arguments and representations Smith’s team submitted.

If prosecutors misled the court, omitted legally material facts or sought records exceeding lawful investigative needs, those would be serious issues.

They should be examined on the evidence.

But “members of Congress were involved” cannot substitute for that examination.

Neither can “the subpoenas were secret.”

Federal law expressly contemplates secret investigative demands when a judge determines disclosure could jeopardize an investigation.

Neither can “the records involved telephone communications.”

The records at issue included non-content metadata rather than recordings of the calls themselves, and the Senate Judiciary Committee has described the relevant period for several lawmakers as the four days surrounding January 6.

And neither can Speech or Debate become a magic phrase that converts every trace of congressional communication into privileged material.

The Constitution protects lawmakers when they legislate.

It does not say that lawmakers become invisible when investigators draw a communications map.

That distinction is not hostile to congressional independence.

It is what prevents congressional independence from quietly becoming congressional immunity.

And once a privilege becomes immunity, the people most likely to benefit from it are precisely the people investigators may occasionally need to investigate.

A constitutional shield is essential.

A constitutional moat is something else entirely.
