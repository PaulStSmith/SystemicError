Biggest loser: Nightmare begins for Musk after he blows $500M on the midterms

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosNovember 7, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet published Robert Reich’s assessment of the 2026 midterms as a sweeping Republican defeat despite record spending by wealthy donors and super PACs. Reich singled out Elon Musk, reporting that he spent nearly $500 million supporting unsuccessful Republican campaigns, including Ken Paxton’s Texas Senate bid.

Reich argued that Musk’s political losses could bring institutional consequences once Democrats control Congress: investigations into data Musk allegedly obtained while running the Department of Government Efficiency, scrutiny of his authority over Ukraine’s access to Starlink, and questions about the government’s reliance on his satellite network. AlterNet also reported sharp post-election declines in Tesla and SpaceX shares, though the article provided no figures or evidence establishing that the election caused those movements.

Money bought access, not obedience

Musk possesses extraordinary private power: immense wealth, control of major companies, government contracts, and crucial communications infrastructure. Spending almost half a billion dollars on elections was an attempt to convert that private power into public authority without standing for office or answering to voters.

According to Reich’s account, that attempt failed at the ballot box. But calling Musk merely the “biggest loser” risks mistaking one electoral setback for the collapse of his influence. A billionaire who can spend that amount and retain control over strategically important companies has not been expelled from politics. He has demonstrated how much political intervention the American system permits one person to purchase.

The decisions belong to Republicans

Musk supplied money, but Republican candidates and leaders chose the campaigns it financed. Trump, JD Vance, RFK Jr., and their allies deployed the attacks Reich describes: anti-Muslim allegations against Abdul El-Sayed, insinuations about James Talarico’s sexuality, and an assault on Amy Acton built around childhood trauma.

Those were decisions, not electoral “roughness” or messaging errors. The politicians authorized the appeals; donors funded them; campaign organizations distributed them. Reich says voters rejected those tactics. That result does not transform cruelty into a strategic accident. It means the people exercising power calculated that prejudice and humiliation could mobilize enough voters—and calculated badly.

Representation is not the whole victory

The reported election of Black, Latino, Native American, Muslim, gay, and female candidates is politically significant. It shows that Republican efforts to organize power around exclusion did not prevail in the races Reich highlights.

But representation alone does not redistribute authority. Governors and senators still enter institutions shaped by concentrated wealth, corporate lobbying, racial gerrymandering, immigration enforcement, and donor access. Describing the outcome as a victory for “decency” may capture the contrast in campaign conduct, but it can also soften the harder fact: voters changed who controls state offices and Congress. The consequential question is what those officials do with that power.

Accountability requires more than a hot seat

Reich anticipates congressional scrutiny of DOGE, Starlink, government contracts, and Musk’s handling of sensitive information. Those are concrete questions of public authority. If Congress investigates, it should establish what Musk obtained, what legal powers he exercised, who authorized those powers, and whether public policy became dependent on the preferences of a private executive.

Nationalizing Starlink, which Reich raises, would be a major policy decision rather than a rhetorical punishment. The article does not establish that Democrats have adopted it or that investigations have begun. A “nightmare” for Musk therefore remains a forecast. Accountability exists only when institutions conduct oversight, publish findings, enforce law, and reduce dangerous dependencies—not when politicians promise discomfort for a defeated donor.

The system invited the billionaire in

The larger pattern is not that money suddenly stopped mattering. It is that concentrated money can lose elections while continuing to dominate the conditions under which politics operates. Musk’s reported spending failed to deliver the results he wanted, but the system still allowed one unelected billionaire to pour nearly $500 million into campaigns while controlling businesses intertwined with national infrastructure.

That is not legalized influence at the margins. It is a governing structure that grants private wealth repeated opportunities to capture public decisions. One bad election for Musk does not correct it. The real test is whether the new majority dismantles that structure—or merely enjoys having its most conspicuous beneficiary on the losing side.
