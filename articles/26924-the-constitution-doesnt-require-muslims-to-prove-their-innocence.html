The Constitution Doesn’t Require Muslims to Prove Their Innocence

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 19, 202610 min read

Sixty-six years after John F. Kennedy had to defend his loyalty to America against suspicions about his Catholicism, a Muslim Senate candidate is being asked essentially the same question. Apparently, some Americans still haven’t read the Constitution.

There is a particular kind of political question that isn’t really a question at all.

It’s an accusation disguised as an inquiry. A demand that someone defend themselves against a suspicion for which no evidence has been presented. A rhetorical trap designed so that refusing to participate looks like guilt, while participating means accepting the legitimacy of the accusation.

Michigan Democratic Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed recently encountered exactly this kind of question.

At a campaign event, a man named Tommy confronted him with what he apparently considered a devastating challenge:

“Will you disavow the Muslim Brotherhood?”

El-Sayed’s answer was immediate.

“Yeah, absolutely.”

That should have settled the matter.

It didn’t.

Because El-Sayed understood something his questioner apparently hadn’t considered: the most revealing part of the exchange wasn’t the answer. It was the assumption behind the question.

And for the next four minutes, he proceeded to expose that assumption with the clinical precision of someone taking apart a poorly assembled argument.

Guilty Until Proven Otherwise — If You’re Muslim

El-Sayed asked Tommy why he had assumed the candidate wouldn’t disavow the Muslim Brotherhood.

Tommy acknowledged that he had made that assumption.

His justification?

He hadn’t heard El-Sayed disavow the organization before.

Think about the breathtaking absurdity of that reasoning.

Have you personally heard your mayor condemn the Ku Klux Klan?

Has your congressman specifically disavowed the Lord’s Resistance Army?

Has your governor publicly rejected every violent extremist organization operating anywhere on Earth?

No?

Well, apparently we’d better start investigating.

Under Tommy’s standard, the absence of a public condemnation becomes grounds for suspicion. Never mind that there are thousands of organizations, movements, and ideologies a politician has never publicly discussed. Never mind that nobody has time to maintain a comprehensive, constantly updated catalog of everything they oppose.

And, most importantly, never mind that this standard somehow seems to become relevant when the candidate is Muslim.

The burden of proof has been turned upside down.

Instead of requiring evidence that someone supports an extremist organization, the questioner demands that the person prove they don’t.

And that demand isn’t distributed equally.

A Christian politician is ordinarily presumed to be an American who happens to be Christian.

A Muslim politician, apparently, must establish that he’s an American first.

One gets the benefit of the doubt.

The other gets an ideological background check conducted by someone who thinks asking a question is the same as establishing a fact.

We’ve Seen This Movie Before. In 1960.

El-Sayed then reached for an extraordinarily appropriate historical comparison: John F. Kennedy.

In 1960, Kennedy was running for president while facing widespread suspicion about his Catholic faith.

For generations, American anti-Catholic prejudice had portrayed Catholics as potentially disloyal citizens whose ultimate allegiance belonged not to the United States but to the Pope.

Sound familiar?

Kennedy addressed those suspicions directly in his famous September 12, 1960, speech to the Greater Houston Ministerial Association.

He stated:

“I believe in an America where the separation of church and state is absolute.”

Kennedy also explained that he wasn’t running as the representative of the Catholic Church. He was the Democratic Party’s candidate for president who happened to be Catholic.

His political obligations would be determined by the Constitution and the national interest, not instructions from the Vatican.

Sixty-six years later, a Muslim candidate is being asked to provide substantially the same assurance.

The institution has changed. The religion has changed. The insinuation hasn’t.

Your religion makes you suspect. Prove that America comes first.

What makes the parallel especially damning is that Kennedy explicitly warned that religious suspicion wouldn’t necessarily end with Catholics.

He recognized that once Americans accepted religious identity as grounds for questioning someone’s loyalty, the same weapon could be turned against virtually anyone.

Apparently, that warning wasn’t sufficiently memorable.

Or perhaps it was simply more convenient to forget it.

The Moment the Interrogation Changed Direction

Then El-Sayed did something even more effective.

He asked Tommy about his own religion.

Tommy identified himself as Christian and affirmed that he tried to live according to Christian doctrine.

Fair enough.

El-Sayed then asked whether he, as a Muslim, was required to live according to Christian doctrine.

The answer, of course, was no.

Why not?

Because religious freedom means individuals get to choose their own religious beliefs and practices.

It’s a remarkably simple concept.

And yet watching the exchange unfold, one gets the impression that Tommy hadn’t quite considered what happens when the constitutional protections he enjoys are applied to someone whose religion he doesn’t share.

El-Sayed explained that the Constitution protects Tommy’s right to practice Christianity and his own right to practice Islam.

And he made an important promise: he would defend Tommy’s religious freedom, even though they didn’t share the same faith.

That is the point.

That is the entire damn point.

Religious liberty isn’t a privilege that Christians generously extend to everyone else. It’s a constitutional right that belongs to everyone, including people Christians disagree with.

The First Amendment doesn’t say Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, provided the religion in question is sufficiently popular.

It doesn’t say free exercise applies only to faiths that make the majority comfortable.

And it certainly doesn’t require Muslim Americans to submit periodic declarations of loyalty before being permitted to participate in public life.

Religious freedom means the state doesn’t get to tell Tommy what to believe.

It also means Tommy doesn’t get to use the state to tell El-Sayed what to believe.

And neither gets to impose his religious doctrine on the rest of us.

Including those of us who don’t believe in any gods whatsoever.

Let’s Discuss the Extremism Question Without the Hypocrisy

There is nothing inherently wrong with asking a political candidate about extremist organizations.

Quite the contrary.

If credible evidence suggests that a candidate has connections to, supports, or receives assistance from an organization advocating political violence or the destruction of constitutional government, that deserves serious investigation.

Christian, Muslim, Jewish, Hindu, atheist — take your pick.

The candidate’s personal faith doesn’t provide immunity from scrutiny.

But neither does it constitute evidence.

And that’s the crucial distinction.

Questions about specific associations, financial relationships, statements, policy positions, or documented organizational connections are legitimate subjects for political inquiry.

Questions whose underlying premise is simply “You’re Muslim, so how do we know you’re not secretly loyal to Muslim extremists?” are something else entirely.

They’re religious profiling dressed up in the respectable clothing of political accountability.

There have been allegations about El-Sayed’s associations with the Muslim Brotherhood. Those allegations can be investigated on their merits, and El-Sayed’s responses can be scrutinized like those of any other candidate.

But membership in Islam isn’t evidence supporting those allegations.

Neither is failure to make a declaration nobody had previously requested.

And if someone wants to claim that the Muslim Brotherhood poses a particular danger, they should be specific about which organization, branch, activity, or association they’re discussing, rather than pretending every Muslim on Earth belongs to some ideological hive mind.

The same evidentiary standard should apply to Christian extremism.

If a candidate openly embraces Christian nationalism, advocates subordinating civil law to biblical doctrine, or argues that non-Christians should enjoy fewer political rights, those are legitimate reasons for scrutiny.

Not because the candidate is Christian.

Because of what the candidate actually advocates.

See how that works?

It’s called judging people by their actions and positions rather than by the religious category into which you’ve placed them.

A revolutionary concept, apparently.

And Then CBS Entered the Circus

As if the exchange itself weren’t revealing enough, CBS News chief legal correspondent Jan Crawford initially praised the questioner on social media and portrayed El-Sayed’s response as a political failure.

Yes, really.

A Muslim candidate immediately disavowed the organization he was asked about, explained why the presumption of divided loyalty was objectionable, and defended the constitutional rights of the Christian man questioning him.

And somehow that was supposed to constitute a self-inflicted defeat.

What exactly was the preferable answer?

Should El-Sayed have thanked Tommy for giving him the opportunity to demonstrate that he wasn’t a secret extremist?

Should he have apologized for being Muslim?

Should he have produced a notarized certificate of constitutional loyalty, preferably signed by three Christian witnesses?

Crawford subsequently deleted her post and apologized, acknowledging that it violated CBS News standards.

Good.

But the episode illustrates a broader problem with political commentary: sometimes the obsession with identifying who “won” an exchange overwhelms the considerably more important question of whether the underlying argument makes any sense.

A viral confrontation isn’t automatically a successful confrontation.

A provocative question isn’t necessarily an intelligent question.

And a candidate refusing to accept a loaded premise isn’t evidence that the premise was correct.

Sometimes the person asking the question is the one who needs to answer for it.

The Constitution Is Not a Christian Membership Card

Let’s address the larger problem directly.

The United States is not constitutionally a Christian nation.

It is a nation in which Christians are free to practice Christianity.

Those are two very different propositions.

The Constitution establishes no religious qualification for public office. Article VI expressly prohibits religious tests for federal public office, and the First Amendment protects religious exercise while restricting governmental establishment of religion.

Those protections exist precisely because religious majorities cannot be trusted to protect minority rights merely through goodwill.

That isn’t an insult to Christians.

It’s an acknowledgment of how power works.

Give any religious majority the authority to determine which beliefs deserve legal protection, and minority religions become dependent on the majority’s tolerance.

And tolerance is a very poor substitute for rights.

Today, the question might concern a Muslim candidate.

Tomorrow, it could concern a Jewish candidate.

Or a Hindu.

Or a Buddhist.

Or an atheist who refuses to pretend religious belief is a necessary qualification for public service.

The moment we accept that candidates must demonstrate religious conformity to earn the presumption of loyalty, we’ve abandoned one of the country’s foundational constitutional principles.

And here’s an irony that deserves particular attention.

Some of the loudest contemporary defenders of America’s supposed Christian identity also claim to be passionate defenders of the Constitution.

Yet the Constitution explicitly prevents the government from establishing the very religious hierarchy they seem to want.

You cannot simultaneously demand constitutional fidelity and insist that Christians possess some privileged claim to American political legitimacy.

Well, you can.

But that’s not constitutional reasoning.

That’s hypocrisy.

What El-Sayed Actually Demonstrated

None of this establishes that Abdul El-Sayed would make a good senator.

He still needs to answer questions about healthcare, taxation, immigration, foreign policy, national security, civil liberties, and every other issue relevant to the office he seeks.

His policy proposals deserve scrutiny.

His political associations deserve scrutiny when there are reasonable grounds for it.

His record deserves scrutiny.

That’s how democratic accountability works.

But his religion is not a substitute for evidence.

And what he demonstrated in this exchange was something every public official ought to understand: constitutional protections aren’t supposed to depend on whether the person exercising them belongs to your preferred group.

He didn’t respond by suggesting that Christians were untrustworthy.

He didn’t argue that Tommy should be denied religious freedom because his questions were objectionable.

He did something much better.

He defended the right of the man questioning him to practice a religion he himself doesn’t follow.

And he insisted on receiving the same protection in return.

That shouldn’t be remarkable.

It should be the bare minimum of constitutional literacy.

But in an era when religious identity is increasingly treated as a political credential — or a political disqualification — apparently even the bare minimum qualifies as a memorable moment.

The Question America Should Have Settled Decades Ago

In 1960, John F. Kennedy had to explain that being Catholic didn’t make him an agent of the Vatican.

In 2026, Abdul El-Sayed has to explain that being Muslim doesn’t make him an agent of the Muslim Brotherhood.

Sixty-six years of supposed progress, and we’re still recycling the same suspicion with a different religious label.

The question isn’t whether El-Sayed has sufficiently demonstrated his loyalty to the Constitution.

He answered the original question immediately and explicitly affirmed his constitutional commitments.

The more troubling question is why some Americans continue to regard their fellow citizens’ religious identities as presumptive evidence of disloyalty.

The Constitution already provides an answer to the underlying issue.

It doesn’t require Americans to share a religion.

It doesn’t demand that politicians abandon their faith.

It doesn’t grant Christians an exclusive franchise on patriotism.

And it doesn’t require Muslims to prove their innocence merely because they’re Muslim.

The Constitution protects Tommy’s right to be Christian. It protects El-Sayed’s right to be Muslim. It protects my right to believe neither of them has the slightest evidence that their gods exist.

And it requires the government to respect all three.

That’s not a complicated principle.

The complicated part, apparently, is getting people who claim to love the Constitution to actually accept what it says.
