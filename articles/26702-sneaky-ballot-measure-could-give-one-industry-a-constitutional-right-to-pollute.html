Sneaky ballot measure could give one industry a 'Constitutional Right' to pollute

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosOctober 19, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedA right designed for an industryWho has powerThe misdirection is constitutional languageThe alternative requires power tooThe larger pattern

What happened

AlterNet reports that Colorado voters were set to consider Amendment 82, a proposed state constitutional “right” for the natural-gas industry to sell its products. According to the outlet, that language could be used to challenge state pollution controls and local restrictions on gas wells, pipelines, appliances, and other forms of consumption.

The measure was promoted by Advance Colorado, which AlterNet describes as a dark-money group whose original donors were undisclosed. The article does not provide the election date, the amendment’s full text, an opposing legal interpretation, or the eventual vote result, so it establishes neither whether the measure passed nor precisely how courts would apply it.

The remainder of the piece argues for publicly backed rooftop-solar financing: government-guaranteed loans, temporary premium payments from utilities for surplus electricity, and tax credits intended to make installations cost-neutral for homeowners.

A right designed for an industry

The decisive feature is not that natural gas would receive favorable policy. Industries receive subsidies, exemptions, and regulatory indulgence routinely. The escalation is the attempt, as AlterNet describes it, to elevate commercial access to customers into a constitutional right.

Rights ordinarily restrain concentrated power. This proposal would invert that logic by arming concentrated economic power against regulation. If AlterNet’s interpretation is correct, elected governments could still formally enact public-health or safety rules, but the gas industry would gain constitutional grounds to attack measures that reduced consumption. Corporate market share would become the protected interest; clean air, local control, and fire precautions would become potential infringements.

Who has power

Voters would possess the formal power to approve or reject the amendment. But the power to design the proposal, finance its promotion, and choose the language presented to voters belongs to its organizers and funders. Advance Colorado is the named vehicle. The people supplying its original money are not named in the reporting.

That distinction matters. Calling this simply a voter decision would erase the actors who placed an industry-specific entitlement on the ballot. Voters exercise agency inside a political market whose agenda has already been shaped by organizations with money, lawyers, and access. Undisclosed funding makes that asymmetry harder to inspect.

The article speculates about who is behind the group but does not establish the donors’ identities. The secrecy itself is the supported fact. Motive and sponsorship beyond that remain unproven.

The misdirection is constitutional language

The proposal’s framing, as reported, converts regulation into victimization. A company facing limits on pollution or infrastructure is recast as a rights-holder being denied the freedom to sell. Communities living with the consequences are reduced to obstacles in the company’s path.

That is the political trick: separate the transaction from its costs. Gas is sold by one actor, but pollution, fire risk, infrastructure burdens, and enforcement costs are distributed across the public. A constitutional sales right would privilege the seller’s revenue while weakening the institutions responsible for managing those consequences.

AlterNet’s own framing also overreaches in places. It treats its broad legal interpretation as settled without supplying the amendment text or expert analysis in the excerpt. A serious indictment does not need that shortcut. The core issue is already stark: a political organization sought constitutional protection for one industry’s commercial activity while concealing its original funding.

The alternative requires power too

The rooftop-solar proposal usefully exposes what market fatalism conceals. Energy systems are built through law: loan guarantees, utility rules, ownership structures, tax policy, and public investment. Fossil-fuel dependence is not merely a sequence of private consumer choices, and a solar transition would not happen through individual virtue alone.

But the article presents its financing model more confidently than its evidence here permits. It proposes government-backed loans and above-retail utility payments, yet supplies no detailed assessment of grid upgrades, program costs, regional variation, default exposure, or distributional effects. Its claim that the arrangement could cost homeowners nothing is a policy design goal, not an established universal result.

The important political point survives that weakness. Government can use its balance sheet and regulatory authority either to lock in incumbent energy sales or to spread ownership of competing infrastructure. Neutrality is not on the menu. The allocation of risk, subsidy, and legal protection is the policy.

The larger pattern

Amendment 82, as described, reflects a broader strategy of entrenchment: when an industry risks losing ordinary legislative fights, it can try to rewrite the rules above the legislature. Constitutional status would make future regulation harder, slower, and more expensive even when public preferences change.

Dark money compounds the maneuver by obscuring who is purchasing that durability. The public sees a ballot question; the beneficiaries seek a shield against future public decisions. That is not deregulation in any honest sense. It is regulation rewritten to protect the regulated industry from everyone else.
