Corporations Deduct Their Operating Costs. Why Can’t Humans?

An American tax reform proposal: recognize the cost of staying alive, protect a larger share of lower incomes, and stop pretending that every dollar on a worker’s paycheck is disposable income.

Imagine two taxpayers.

One is a corporation.

The other is a human being.

The corporation earns $200,000 in revenue and spends $150,000 on legitimate deductible operating expenses.

It reports $50,000 in net income before any further tax adjustments.

The human being earns $200,000 in wages and spends $150,000 supporting a household.

Housing. Food. Transportation. Utilities. Healthcare. Clothing. Everything required to maintain a functioning life.

Does the Internal Revenue Service automatically recognize that person’s remaining $50,000 as their taxable income?

Of course not.

The worker receives whatever deductions, exclusions, and credits the tax code allows. But ordinary groceries, rent, and most other living expenses generally don’t qualify as deductible income-producing expenses.

The corporation explains that generating revenue costs money.

The government agrees.

The worker explains that remaining alive and capable of generating wages also costs money.

The government calls those costs personal consumption.

Apparently, maintaining a machine that generates revenue is a business expense. Maintaining the human being who generates revenue is a personal responsibility.

Now, there are legitimate economic and accounting distinctions between business expenses and household consumption.

Businesses purchase inputs to produce goods and services. Household spending also delivers personal benefits, and allowing unrestricted deductions for every purchase would create obvious opportunities for abuse.

But recognizing those differences doesn’t require pretending that a worker’s entire paycheck represents money available for taxation.

What if the tax code recognized something simpler?

A basic fact of human existence:

Before people can contribute income taxes, they must earn enough to sustain themselves.

And what if we designed a deduction around that principle?

The Tax Code Already Understands the Concept

The American tax system isn’t unfamiliar with the idea of protecting some income from taxation.

It has a standard deduction.

For the 2026 tax year, the federal standard deduction is:

  • $16,100 for single filers.

  • $24,150 for heads of household.

  • $32,200 for married couples filing jointly.

These amounts reduce taxable income, not taxes dollar for dollar. And some taxpayers benefit from itemized deductions, tax credits, additional exemptions, or other exclusions.

But the standard deduction is fundamentally a fixed amount determined by filing status, not a calculation of what it actually costs someone to live.

A single worker earning $40,000 and another earning $150,000 generally begin with the same $16,100 standard deduction.

One may be struggling to afford rent.

The other may be saving substantial amounts each month.

The tax code does not ask how much either actually spends on the necessities of life.

Nor should it necessarily require them to submit grocery receipts, rental agreements, utility bills, and transportation records merely to receive basic tax relief.

That would create an administrative nightmare.

But perhaps we can do better than a fixed deduction that provides little recognition of how much income must be consumed before a household can achieve financial stability.

Our proposal is simple.

Replace the ordinary standard deduction with an automatic, progressively declining percentage of earned income exempted from federal income taxation.

The less someone earns, the larger the share protected.

The more someone earns, the smaller the percentage of each additional dollar protected.

No grocery receipts.

No rent documentation.

No government official deciding whether a household spent too much on food.

Just a clear deduction, calculated automatically.

The Progressive Living-Expense Deduction

Here’s how it would work.

Instead of a fixed standard deduction, earned income would receive a living-expense allowance determined by four marginal income bands.

For illustration, consider the following structure for a single taxpayer:

\(\begin{array}{l|r} \textbf{Portion of Annual Earned Income} & \textbf{Deductible Share} \\ \hline \text{First }\$50{,}000 & 50\% \\ \text{Next }\$50{,}000 & 40\% \\ \text{Next }\$150{,}000 & 30\% \\ \text{Above }\$250{,}000 & 20\% \end{array} \)

These thresholds are illustrative policy choices, not existing IRS brackets. In an enacted system, they would need to be calibrated using household data, adjusted for filing status, and indexed annually to inflation.

The essential feature is that the percentages apply marginally.

Everyone receives a 50% deduction on their first $50,000 in earnings.

Only earnings above that threshold enter the next band.

Nobody loses their existing deduction merely because their income crosses a bracket boundary.

That distinction matters enormously.

Suppose someone earns $50,000.

Their living-expense deduction would be $25,000.

Now suppose they earn $50,001.

The first $50,000 still receives the 50% deduction. The additional dollar receives a 40% deduction.

The worker doesn’t suddenly lose thousands of dollars in deductions because of a one-dollar raise.

That’s how a properly designed marginal system should operate.

And that makes our proposal predictable, transparent, and resistant to the absurd financial cliffs that poorly designed benefit and tax systems can create.

Let’s Do the Math

Consider four workers earning different annual salaries.

Worker A: $40,000

The entire salary falls within the first band.

The worker deducts 50%, or $20,000.

Their remaining income subject to the ordinary income-tax calculation is $20,000.

Worker B: $80,000

The first $50,000 receives a 50% deduction: $25,000.

The next $30,000 receives a 40% deduction: $12,000.

Total deduction: $37,000.

Remaining income: $43,000.

Worker C: $150,000

The first $50,000 produces a $25,000 deduction.

The next $50,000 produces a $20,000 deduction.

The remaining $50,000 receives a 30% deduction: $15,000.

Total deduction: $60,000.

Remaining income: $90,000.

Worker D: $500,000

The first $50,000 produces a $25,000 deduction.

The next $50,000 produces a $20,000 deduction.

The next $150,000 produces a $45,000 deduction.

The remaining $250,000 receives a 20% deduction: $50,000.

Total deduction: $140,000.

Remaining income: $360,000.

Here’s the comparison:

\(\begin{array}{r|r|r} \textbf{Annual Earnings} & \textbf{Living-Expense Deduction} & \textbf{Income Remaining} \\ \hline \$40{,}000 & \$20{,}000 & \$20{,}000 \\ \$80{,}000 & \$37{,}000 & \$43{,}000 \\ \$150{,}000 & \$60{,}000 & \$90{,}000 \\ \$500{,}000 & \$140{,}000 & \$360{,}000 \end{array} \)

These are calculations of the proposed deduction, not estimates of final taxes owed. Actual federal tax liability would still depend on tax rates, other applicable provisions, filing status, and credits.

Look at the relative treatment.

The worker earning $40,000 protects 50% of their earnings.

The worker earning $500,000 protects 28%.

Both receive relief.

But the lower-income worker receives proportionately more.

That’s what progressive treatment should look like: recognize that the first dollars someone earns are more essential to their survival than the last dollars earned by someone already comfortable.

No Receipts. No Permission Slips. No Moral Lectures.

One of the most important parts of this proposal is what it doesn’t require.

We wouldn’t ask workers to prove how much they spend on groceries.

We wouldn’t require receipts for rent.

We wouldn’t audit whether they bought store-brand cereal or something more expensive.

We wouldn’t decide whether taking their family to dinner was a legitimate living expense.

Because that isn’t the government’s business.

The allowance would be automatic.

You earned the money.

The appropriate percentage is excluded from taxable income.

End of discussion.

And if a worker manages to live frugally and save some of that protected income?

Good.

That’s their money.

The allowance isn’t a government reimbursement for documented expenditures. It’s a recognition that individuals require a reasonable portion of their earnings to sustain themselves.

A deduction designed to recognize the cost of living should not become another opportunity for government to supervise how ordinary people live.

Why Not Simply Deduct Half of Everyone’s Income?

Because that would introduce a different inequity.

Someone earning $30,000 would deduct $15,000.

Someone earning $3 million would deduct $1.5 million.

And because deductions reduce taxable income, the actual tax savings can be greater for taxpayers facing higher marginal rates.

A universal 50% deduction would therefore deliver substantial absolute benefits to high earners.

Our marginal schedule reduces that problem.

It does not eliminate it entirely.

A wealthy taxpayer would still receive a larger deduction in absolute dollars, because they earn more and continue receiving deductions on higher bands.

That is a deliberate feature of the proposed schedule, not something we should conceal.

But their deductible share declines as their earnings increase.

And the existing progressive income-tax rate schedule would still apply to the income remaining after deductions.

We could also evaluate a ceiling on the allowance for exceptionally high incomes, depending on the fiscal and distributional analysis.

The objective isn’t to punish people for earning more.

It’s to recognize that earning more does not mean every additional dollar deserves the same protection from taxation as the money needed for basic survival.

This Isn’t the Same as Eliminating Income Taxes for the Poor

There’s another crucial point.

A deduction can reduce income-tax liability only to zero.

It doesn’t automatically put money into the hands of people who owe no federal income tax.

And some low-income workers still pay payroll taxes even when they owe little or no federal income tax.

That means our proposal must not be presented as a complete solution to poverty, low wages, or household financial insecurity.

Refundable programs such as the Earned Income Tax Credit and the refundable portion of the Child Tax Credit serve different purposes and should not be casually discarded.

A carefully designed reform should preserve effective low-income credits and avoid inadvertently reducing their value through changes in the definition of income.

It should also account for Social Security and Medicare payroll taxes, which operate separately from the ordinary federal income-tax deduction.

And it should protect households whose income is already too low to benefit substantially from an additional deduction.

Tax relief for earnings and direct support for people facing poverty are complementary tools, not interchangeable ones.

The goal is to make income taxation more realistic and more equitable—not to pretend that changing a tax return can replace an adequate social safety net.

The Obvious Objection: Wouldn’t This Cost a Fortune?

Possibly.

And anyone proposing a serious tax reform must be willing to confront that question.

A living-expense deduction of this scale could substantially reduce individual income-tax revenue.

The actual effect would depend on how it replaced existing deductions, how filing statuses were treated, how taxpayers responded, and whether other provisions changed.

We don’t yet have a credible ten-year revenue estimate.

We shouldn’t invent one.

Nor should we pretend that economic growth would automatically compensate for every dollar of revenue lost.

That’s a familiar rhetorical escape hatch used to justify tax reductions without adequately examining their fiscal consequences.

We’re not going to reproduce it.

The proposal would require an official distributional and revenue analysis.

The Congressional Budget Office, Joint Committee on Taxation, Treasury, and independent researchers could help evaluate its effects.

But here’s the critical distinction.

The existence of a fiscal cost is not, by itself, an argument against a policy.

Every tax preference has a cost.

Every public service has a cost.

And collecting taxes imposes economic costs of its own.

The relevant question is whether the benefits justify those costs—and whether the resulting distribution of burdens is defensible.

The Tax Policy Center has repeatedly documented that many individual tax preferences deliver substantial benefits to higher-income households. In its October 2026 distributional resources, it examines advantages ranging from itemized deductions to preferential rates on capital gains and dividends.

If Congress wants to protect federal revenue while granting workers a more generous living-expense allowance, it can examine those preferences.

It can reconsider tax provisions whose benefits are concentrated among the wealthiest households.

It can evaluate preferential treatment of investment income.

It can scrutinize unnecessary corporate subsidies and inefficient tax expenditures.

And it can consider adjusting rates on very high incomes.

Those are policy choices requiring analysis, not automatically sufficient financing mechanisms.

We would need to calculate their effects before claiming that any combination pays for our proposal.

But that is precisely what responsible policymaking looks like.

The question shouldn’t be whether America can afford to recognize workers’ living expenses. It should be what combination of taxation and public spending best reflects the country’s economic priorities.

Don’t Punish Marriage or Families

Another concern requires attention.

Our illustrative brackets apply to individual income.

But the United States allows joint tax returns for married couples and gives heads of household different treatment from single taxpayers.

Simply applying our single-filer thresholds to every household would produce undesirable results.

Imagine two married individuals each earning $60,000.

If they file separately, each might receive deductions across the first two income bands.

But if they file jointly and their combined $120,000 is treated as one person’s income, the deductions could be substantially smaller.

That would effectively penalize marriage.

We should avoid that.

One straightforward solution would be to double the thresholds for married couples filing jointly.

Under that approach, the first $100,000 of combined earned income would qualify for the 50% deduction, the next $100,000 for 40%, and so forth.

Heads of household could receive appropriately adjusted thresholds, recognizing the additional costs of maintaining a household with dependents.

There would still be technical questions about single-earner versus dual-earner couples, income shifting, and eligibility.

Those require careful design.

But the principle should be clear.

Tax reform intended to recognize the cost of supporting a household should not arbitrarily punish people for forming one.

Wages, Business Income, and the Problem of Loopholes

Our proposal also raises an important question about the meaning of income.

Should it apply only to wages?

What about a self-employed plumber?

A freelance programmer?

A small-business owner?

Someone operating through a limited liability company?

If the deduction were available only to W-2 employees, it could create another artificial distinction between economically similar workers.

A sensible version would therefore cover qualifying earned labor income, whether received as wages or through genuine self-employment.

But it would need to prevent double deductions.

A self-employed person already deducts legitimate business operating expenses in calculating net business profit.

They shouldn’t be able to characterize the same expenses as both business deductions and living-expense deductions.

Likewise, a wealthy shareholder shouldn’t be able to relabel passive investment income as wages merely to obtain another tax advantage.

Rules governing reasonable compensation, related-party transactions, and income classification would matter.

And capital gains, dividends, and other investment income would need separate consideration.

Otherwise, a reform intended to help people who earn their living through work could become another opportunity for sophisticated tax planning.

A tax reform is only as equitable as the rules preventing its benefits from being captured by those best equipped to exploit it.

Why Not Just Raise the Standard Deduction?

That would be the simpler alternative.

Congress could increase the ordinary standard deduction from $16,100 for single filers to $20,000, $25,000, or another amount.

That approach would be easier to administer.

It would also provide a predictable exemption regardless of a person’s precise earnings.

And unlike our proposal, it would not require calculating a separate marginal deduction schedule.

Those are genuine advantages.

But a fixed deduction and a progressive percentage-based deduction embody different principles.

A fixed deduction says:

“Everyone should have at least this much income exempt from federal income tax.”

Our proposal says:

“A progressively declining portion of earned income should be protected because maintaining a working life requires resources.”

Both principles have merit.

A fixed deduction may be particularly effective at exempting very low incomes.

A percentage-based allowance can provide increasing relief as earnings rise, while protecting a declining share of income.

One could even combine the approaches, providing a minimum guaranteed deduction with a marginal living-expense allowance above it.

But stacking the full existing standard deduction on top of our proposed allowance without restrictions would substantially increase the fiscal cost.

So our initial model should be evaluated as a replacement for the ordinary standard deduction, with a minimum allowance considered as a possible refinement.

And we should be willing to revise the design if the evidence shows that another structure better achieves our objectives.

That’s how serious tax policy should be developed.

Not through ideological devotion to a particular formula, but through clearly stated goals and measurable outcomes.

The Human Cost of Generating Income

There’s something almost absurd about the way we routinely describe economic productivity.

A corporation buys equipment.

That’s an investment.

It maintains machinery.

That’s an operating expense.

It purchases materials.

Those are production inputs.

It trains employees.

That’s a business cost.

Now consider the employee.

They need food to perform physical and intellectual labor.

They need shelter to remain healthy and productive.

They need transportation to reach their workplace.

They need electricity, heating, and increasingly internet connectivity.

They need clothing, medical care, and time to recover between working days.

And if they have children, they need to arrange for those children to be cared for.

None of those expenses is necessarily optional.

Yet most are treated as personal consumption for federal income-tax purposes.

There is an economic justification for distinguishing a firm’s expenses from household consumption.

A business expense is generally incurred to produce income; a person’s food and shelter also provide benefits unrelated to their employment. Both can be true.

But perhaps our tax policy has drawn that distinction without giving enough consideration to the unavoidable economic costs of sustaining workers.

Labor doesn’t emerge from nowhere. It comes from human beings who have to survive before they can produce anything.

That is the principle our proposal attempts to recognize.

Not by asking the IRS to decide which grocery purchases qualify as work-related necessities.

Not by pretending every household expense is equivalent to a business deduction.

But by acknowledging that a meaningful share of earned income is committed to the basic requirements of life.

A standard living-expense allowance would make that acknowledgment explicit.

The Tax System Should Serve People, Not Just Accountants

There is one final advantage to our proposal.

Ordinary taxpayers could understand it.

Everyone would know that their first $50,000 of earned income qualified for a 50% deduction.

They would know that the next $50,000 qualified for 40%.

They would understand that additional income received progressively smaller deductions.

And payroll withholding could be adjusted to account for those allowances throughout the year.

No annual shoebox full of receipts.

No elaborate spreadsheet calculating how much of the household electricity bill was used for work.

No anxiety over whether the IRS would accept a particular grocery purchase as necessary.

No requirement to hire a tax professional merely to claim the basic costs of being alive.

Of course, a comprehensive implementation would still require rules addressing filing status, other deductions, credits, income classification, and interactions with federal benefits.

Tax law cannot be reduced to four percentages.

But the central allowance could be simple enough for almost anyone to understand.

And transparency matters.

A system ordinary people can understand is easier to scrutinize, easier to debate, and harder to manipulate through obscure provisions.

Complexity should be justified by necessity, not preserved because it benefits those who can afford specialists to navigate it.

Tax What People Can Afford to Contribute

America’s tax debate has spent decades arguing about marginal rates.

Should the highest rate be 35% or 37%?

Should corporate profits be taxed at 21% or something else?

Should capital gains receive preferential treatment?

Those are important questions.

But perhaps we’ve neglected a more fundamental one.

How much of a person’s earned income should be considered available for taxation in the first place?

A worker’s paycheck is not pure disposable income.

It supports a household.

It buys necessities.

It maintains the worker’s ability to continue earning.

And for many Americans, very little remains after those obligations are met.

Our proposal doesn’t pretend that every household spends precisely the same percentage of income on living expenses.

It doesn’t attempt to calculate each person’s actual cost of survival.

And it doesn’t eliminate the need for progressive tax rates, refundable credits, or social assistance.

Instead, it establishes a simple, testable principle.

The first dollars earned deserve the greatest protection.

Subsequent dollars deserve progressively less.

And the state should recognize that sustaining human life is not some optional luxury that can be ignored when calculating the tax burden on labor.

The precise percentages and income thresholds can be debated.

The revenue effects must be calculated.

The consequences for different households must be modeled.

And any final design should be evaluated against simpler alternatives.

But the principle deserves serious consideration.

Because a tax system that understands why corporations must deduct the cost of maintaining their operations should also understand why people need resources to maintain their lives.

And a government capable of designing extraordinarily sophisticated tax preferences for capital should be capable of developing a straightforward living-expense allowance for workers.

A corporation can explain that revenue is not profit.

A worker should be able to explain that a paycheck is not disposable income.

The tax system should recognize both realities.

Not because humans and corporations are economically identical.

But because human beings are the reason an economy exists in the first place.
