Fox News lets profane opinion of Trump slip out on live TV

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 18, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Fox News aired interviews about President Donald Trump’s decision to cancel a planned livestream of Nidal Hasan’s firing-squad execution. Trump had initially supported the broadcast plan announced by Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, but reversed course after learning Hasan was partially paralyzed and after polling reportedly found that eight in 10 Americans opposed a public execution.

The interviewees largely welcomed the reversal, describing the proposed spectacle as inhumane, politically damaging and reminiscent of North Korea. One woman said Trump wanted to avoid looking bad before concluding with a profane condemnation of him that Fox allowed to air.

Hasan, a former Army psychiatrist, killed 13 people and injured 32 at a Texas military base. AlterNet also cites a Fox poll placing Trump’s job approval at 36 percent—the lowest Fox had recorded for either of his terms—while noting that polling alone does not establish why he abandoned the livestream.

Power ordered the spectacle

The decisive actors were not pedestrians answering questions for a television segment. Hegseth announced that the Pentagon would broadcast an execution, Trump initially endorsed that decision, and Trump later overturned it. Institutional power ran from the defense secretary to the president; everyone else was reacting to what they authorized.

That distinction matters because canceling an abuse does not erase responsibility for approving it. The administration brought the proposed spectacle into public life, tested its acceptability and withdrew it only after backlash. Whatever ultimately drove Trump’s reversal, the restraint came from the same office that had first said “absolutely.”

A reversal packaged as reassurance

Fox framed the interviews as public approval of Trump’s decision. But the comments AlterNet recounts express relief that the government stepped back from broadcasting state killing—not admiration for presidential judgment.

That framing relocates attention from the administration’s conduct to Trump’s supposed responsiveness. The central fact is not that people appreciated the cancellation. It is that senior officials considered televising an execution, publicly promoted the idea and possessed the authority to carry it out.

The final profane remark punctured the segment’s intended mood, but the editorial distortion preceded it. Fox converted rejection of an administration proposal into a favorable story about the official who had supported it.

Cruelty as political theater

Hasan’s crimes and sentence do not answer the question of whether the state should turn an execution into mass entertainment. The administration’s plan would have added an audience to punishment, making public spectacle part of the penalty.

The interviewees understood that distinction. They called the proposal inhumane, compared it with authoritarian practice and dismissed its claimed deterrent value as “shock effect.” Their objections were not sympathy for Hasan. They were objections to a government using its power to stage death for viewers.

The reported sequence suggests a recurring political method: propose an extreme display of force, measure the reaction, then present retreat as prudence. The evidence does not establish Trump’s private motive, but it does establish that his reversal followed his initial support and overwhelming reported opposition.

The profanity was not the scandal

A vulgar insult slipping through Fox’s edit is an embarrassment for a broadcaster. A Pentagon plan to livestream an execution is an exercise of state power. Treating the former as the remarkable event trivializes the latter.

The obscenity also tempts coverage toward personality drama: an angry voter, a television mishap, a president with poor approval numbers. Those details are vivid, but they are politically secondary. No interviewee commanded the military justice system. Hegseth and Trump did.

The system tests the boundary

This episode reveals how authoritarian spectacle can be normalized through bureaucratic announcement, presidential endorsement and media reframing before public resistance forces a retreat. The machinery does not need to complete the act to expose itself. The proposal, approval and promotional enthusiasm already show what officials considered permissible.

When power cancels its own cruelty, it often asks to be credited for moderation. That is the misdirection. The relevant standard is not whether officials eventually listened. It is why the country had to rely on polling and public revulsion to stop them.
