Supreme Court disbars 'nonfunctional' MAGA lawyer and key Trump court ally

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 10, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power was judicialA repeat player meets a boundaryThe headline overstates the punishmentThe system rewards delay

What happened

AlterNet reports that the U.S. Supreme Court declined to overturn a Florida Supreme Court ruling imposing a two-year suspension on conservative lawyer and Judicial Watch founder Larry Klayman. Despite the headline’s use of “disbars,” the reported sanction is a suspension, not permanent loss of his law license.

Florida disciplined Klayman after finding that he violated ethical rules while representing a client for whom he developed romantic feelings. The state court cited writings in which he said his emotions had rendered him “nonfunctional as a lawyer,” along with findings of repeated misconduct, bad-faith obstruction of disciplinary proceedings and submission of false evidence. It also considered his earlier sanctions.

The Supreme Court’s action leaves the Florida punishment intact and complicates Klayman’s pending representations, including Laura Loomer’s defamation suit against HBO and Bill Maher and his $150 million lawsuit against Hasan Piker.

The power was judicial

The decisive actors were not Klayman’s political critics, online commentators or litigation targets. Florida’s highest court exercised its authority to regulate lawyers, and the U.S. Supreme Court refused to disturb that judgment.

That distinction matters because professional discipline is not a popularity contest. The operative facts are the findings about Klayman’s conduct as a lawyer: compromised representation, obstruction and false evidence. His MAGA alliances explain his political significance, but they are not the stated basis for the sanction.

A repeat player meets a boundary

Klayman built influence through litigation, founding Judicial Watch in 1994 and repeatedly using courts to pursue conservative political causes. AlterNet describes him and the organization as prominent participants in election-denial claims, attacks on diversity policies and conspiracy-driven politics.

But access to a courtroom is institutional power, not merely speech. Lawyers can compel responses, impose costs and convert partisan accusations into formal proceedings. When courts tolerate serial misconduct from politically connected litigators, legal process itself becomes leverage against their targets. Here, disciplinary institutions finally imposed a substantial limit after earlier suspensions and courtroom bans had failed to end the pattern described by the Florida court.

The headline overstates the punishment

AlterNet’s framing commits an avoidable error: a two-year suspension is not disbarment. The difference is legally significant. Suspension removes a lawyer’s authority to practice for a defined period; disbarment is a more severe sanction with a different path, if any, back into the profession.

Calling the result “disbarment” makes the consequence sound more final than the article’s own facts support. It also distracts from the stronger, supportable point: multiple courts had already sanctioned Klayman, and Florida acted after finding another pattern of professional misconduct. Precision does not soften that record. It makes accountability harder to dismiss as partisan exaggeration.

The system rewards delay

The broader pattern is institutional patience for powerful political operatives and immediate cost for everyone forced to answer them. Klayman accumulated sanctions across jurisdictions while continuing to file politically charged cases and represent prominent right-wing figures. Each proceeding consumed defendants’ money, court time and public attention before professional discipline narrowed his reach.

That is how legal systems can enable political abuse without formally endorsing it: repeated access becomes its own weapon, while accountability arrives piecemeal and late. The lesson is not that one suspension purifies the courts. It is that institutions often recognize a pattern only after years of allowing the pattern to do its work.
