Trump told them to 'pretend' —now it’s the one thing they are trying to hide

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosOctober 13, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Donald Trump has urged Republican voters to “pretend I’m on the ballot” during the midterm campaign, while some Republican candidates are trying to make their campaigns look less connected to him. The outlet says a dozen candidates removed references to Trump from campaign websites and cites Susan Collins and John Sununu as declining to campaign with him, while Michigan Senate candidate Mike Rogers publicly opposed Trump’s tariff conflict with Canada.

Democratic candidates are making the association explicit. In recent debates, Troy Jackson blamed Collins for failing to oppose Trump’s Iran war and tariffs; Jon Ossoff tied Michael Collins to Trump’s agenda; and Abdul El-Sayed called Rogers a Trump “sock puppet.” Those are campaign accusations, and the article does not provide voting records or other evidence sufficient to establish each charge independently.

The story’s answer to what Republicans are trying to hide is their political connection to Trump. AlterNet argues that his weak approval ratings have turned that connection into an electoral liability, even as Trump continues presenting the midterms as a referendum on himself.

Power does not disappear from a website

Trump holds the institutional power that matters here: the presidency, command of executive policy and enormous influence over Republican politics. Candidates can delete his photograph, avoid the word “MAGA” and keep him outside their states. None of that changes who imposed the tariffs they now criticize or who directs the administration they have spent years navigating.

The candidates retain agency too. They decide which policies to support, when to object and whether opposition arrives before a policy causes damage or only after polling makes loyalty expensive. The reporting does not document every candidate’s record, so it cannot prove that each Republican now creating distance enabled every Trump decision. But it does reveal an unmistakable campaign strategy: conceal the association without necessarily accounting for it.

Distance without a reckoning

Rogers’s beer-can denunciation of the tariff conflict is politically useful because it isolates one unpopular consequence while leaving the larger relationship unresolved. Sununu’s spokesman similarly framed Trump’s absence as a commitment to campaigning with New Hampshire figures, not as a substantive break with Trump.

That is not repudiation. It is brand management. These candidates want the freedom to benefit from Trump’s political coalition while denying opponents an easy visual reminder of the bargain. Their problem is not merely that Democrats keep mentioning Trump. It is that Republican control of Congress would affect Trump’s ability to govern, face oversight and withstand impeachment. The connection is institutional, not cosmetic.

The article’s evidence has limits

AlterNet is strongest when it identifies the contradiction between Trump’s demand to become the symbolic ballot choice and Republican candidates’ efforts to reduce his visibility. It is weaker when it treats his approval rating as the established cause of every act of distancing. The article supplies suggestive examples, but not enough candidate-by-candidate evidence to prove motive in every case.

Its account of Trump’s rallies also relies heavily on Robert Reich’s interpretation that Trump is campaigning principally for personal adulation. The reported speeches—about his intelligence, hair, polling, crowds and possible impeachment—support the narrower conclusion that he devotes substantial attention to himself. They do not, by themselves, prove his internal motive.

A party trapped by its own leader

Trump’s reported conduct makes the conflict unusually stark. He asks voters to treat congressional races as votes for him, then reportedly spends campaign appearances discussing himself far more than the candidates whose victories would determine control of Congress. AlterNet also reports that he agreed to have MAGA Inc. pay for self-promotional advertising after bipartisan objections to its taxpayer funding, while declining to commit to reimbursing costs already incurred.

The larger pattern is a governing party trying to separate responsibility from identity. Republican candidates cannot plausibly claim Trump’s coalition when useful, obscure him when costly and then describe the resulting backlash as unfair guilt by association. If they supported his power, declined to restrain it or seek office in order to extend it, the association is not a Democratic trick. It is the political consequence of their own choices.
