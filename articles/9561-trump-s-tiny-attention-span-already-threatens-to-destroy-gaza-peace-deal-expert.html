Trump's tiny attention span already threatens to destroy Gaza peace deal: expert

No sooner had the ink dried on Donald Trump’s signing of a peace plan for the Middle East than “concerns” were raised that he will move on to some other subject that he feels requires his attention — and the whole framework will collapse.

According to New York Times analyst Karen DeYoung, the president’s plan has gaping holes which are already being singled out by experts in diplomacy, and worries are growing that the brain trust that pushed it through will be moving on to try and solve the Russia/Ukraine impasse.

According to DeYoung, Trump could have done himself a favor by sharing ownership and responsibility with Democrats, but instead shoved them to the side.

“Rather than pledging a renewal of the historic bipartisan U.S. support for Israel that has fractured over Netanyahu’s scorched-earth operations in Gaza, Trump rambled through his usual insults of his Democratic predecessors, saying they had a “hatred toward Israel” and could never have accomplished what he did,” she wrote, before adding that Aaron David Miller, a veteran Middle East negotiator under Republican and Democratic administrations, sees trouble ahead.

“If I was running this, I wouldn’t leave [the region] until the president had assurances of at least four working groups, all headed by a senior American official,” he explained. “If they don’t approach it this way, I don’t see how it’s going to work. I can’t tell you in 25 years, even though our efforts mostly failed, how many peace conferences and big gatherings I went to. It’s the day after the peace conference where serious people wake up and say … what do we do now?”

The Times’ DeYoung added, “One of the greatest fears, Miller and others said, is that as the immediacy of the shooting war and humanitarian disaster that have riveted the world for two years fades, so will the forward momentum.”

With DeYoung suggesting Trump believes he has “solved” the Gaza situation — with no firm framework in place to go forward — there is a belief he will move on and, “the administration may not have the bandwidth to stick with the day after.”

Our Analysis:

The Mirage of a Middle East Peace Plan: Unpacking the Illusions

No sooner had Donald Trump put pen to paper on what he touted as a groundbreaking Middle East peace plan, than the cracks began to show, wide and gaping for all who cared to look. Karen DeYoung of The New York Times provides a sobering analysis that not only exposes the plan’s inherent flaws but also underscores a chronic ailment in Trump’s approach to diplomacy: a hit-and-run strategy that prioritizes momentary triumphs over sustainable solutions.

The Illusion of Success

Trump’s self-congratulatory tone, as described by DeYoung, paints a picture of a president more concerned with the spectacle of success than its substance. By sidelining Democrats, Trump missed an opportunity to fortify his plan with bipartisan support, opting instead for a solitary victory lap that alienated potential allies. This isolationist approach to diplomacy—viewing critical international negotiations as a zero-sum game—only serves to underscore the fragility of the proposed peace.

A Scorched Earth and Scorched Opportunities

Trump’s disregard for the bipartisan tradition of U.S. support for Israel, highlighted by his unfounded accusations of Democratic hostility towards the country, not only distorts historical realities but also ignites partisan divisions. Such divisive rhetoric from the Oval Office does little but fan the flames of an already polarized political landscape, making the arduous journey towards peace even more treacherous.

The Devil is in the (Lack of) Details

Aaron David Miller’s insights reveal a fundamental flaw in Trump’s strategy—or the lack thereof. The absence of a concrete, actionable framework for the day after the peace announcement is not just an oversight; it’s a critical failure that jeopardizes the entire endeavor. The comparison of this effort to previous, more meticulously planned attempts at peace in the Middle East, which themselves often fell short, highlights a reckless disregard for the complexities of diplomacy.

A Fleeting Focus

DeYoung’s portrayal of Trump’s fleeting attention span speaks to a larger issue within his administration: the lack of sustained commitment to resolving deeply entrenched conflicts. The suggestion that Trump believes he has “solved” the Gaza situation is not just overly optimistic; it’s dangerously naïve. Without a dedicated effort to navigate the post-agreement landscape, the peace plan is nothing more than a house of cards, vulnerable to the slightest breeze of geopolitical reality.

Conclusion: A Precarious Path Forward

As the immediacy of conflict fades, so does the urgency to act, leaving a vacuum where leadership and dedication should be. The fear, as Miller and others express, is not just that the plan will collapse, but that the momentum towards peace will dissipate, leaving the region no better than it was before—and perhaps, in some ways, worse off.

In the final analysis, Trump’s Middle East peace plan emerges not as a blueprint for lasting peace, but as a stark illustration of his administration’s failure to grasp the nuances of international diplomacy. It’s a sobering reminder that in the complex arena of global politics, good intentions—or in this case, even questionable ones—are far from enough. The road to peace requires more than a signature; it demands a steadfast commitment to the painstaking process of building and maintaining it. Without this, Trump’s plan is destined to be remembered not for what it achieved, but for what it failed to understand.
