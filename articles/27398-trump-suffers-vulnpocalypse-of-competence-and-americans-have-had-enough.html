Trump suffers 'vulnpocalypse' of competence — and Americans have had enough

Based on an article by Terry H. Schwadron

Paulo SantosNovember 21, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Reassurance without an explanationOfficials insulated from the breakdownA crisis of credibility, not a proven “no confidence” vote

A five-day run of disruptions affecting flights, banking, email and government communications has exposed a Trump administration unable to provide either a convincing explanation or credible reassurance. AlterNet describes the episode as a “vulnpocalypse,” but the reporting establishes something narrower and more consequential: essential systems were failing, senior officials responded with indifference and speculation, and the FBI did not open an investigation until the interruptions had persisted for five consecutive days.

The outages may have damaged public confidence, but AlterNet offers no polling to establish that Americans “have had enough.” Its stronger case lies in the administration’s conduct. President Donald Trump publicly said he did not care about the interruptions, according to the outlet, while his own travel and bank access remained unaffected. That is not proof of a national revolt. It is evidence of a president treating failures that disrupt other people’s lives as somebody else’s problem.

Reassurance without an explanation

The administration reportedly offered assurances that the disruptions were not part of an organized cyberattack or coordinated effort to probe American systems. Yet the account supplies no corresponding technical explanation for what caused them.

That gap matters because the government’s cyber defenses had already been weakened by Trump administration firings, AlterNet reports. Officials were therefore asking the public to trust a conclusion from an apparatus the administration itself had diminished. Whether the outages resulted from hostile activity, copycats, routine denial-of-service attacks or unrelated technical failures remained unresolved in the reporting.

Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin filled that vacuum with conjecture. Based on a single conversation with what he called “a technology expert,” Mullin speculated that artificial intelligence might be outpacing conventional programming and producing backups severe enough to interrupt services. The article also cites reports of people using ChatGPT to search for ways into other computers, but it does not connect those reports to these outages.

Artificial intelligence thus became a convenient possibility before investigators had supplied a cause. Officials could gesture toward an emerging and poorly understood technology while avoiding the more immediate question: why did institutions paid to protect critical systems fail, and why did the federal response take so long to become investigative rather than rhetorical?

Officials insulated from the breakdown

Mullin’s behavior sharpened the contrast between public disruption and official privilege. During the nationwide airport scramble, AlterNet reports, he used a federal jet to attend a college football game and sat with a lobbyist who conducts business with his department.

The significance is not that one official watched football during a crisis. It is that the official responsible for Homeland Security reportedly enjoyed government transportation while ordinary travelers confronted delays and cancellations—and did so alongside someone with a financial interest in his department’s decisions. That arrangement invites scrutiny even without evidence that the lobbyist received any favor.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy, meanwhile, responded by demanding $50 billion for airport work extending beyond the safety-related conversion of aging electronics to digital systems. AlterNet characterizes the demand as extortionist and reports that several Republican senators reacted poorly to it. Whatever upgrades airports need, using an unfolding disruption to press for a vastly broader spending package substitutes leverage for an accounting of failure.

Trump’s reported indifference completes the picture. The president was more engaged, AlterNet argues, in defying a court order so he could build a golf course on national parkland in Washington than in systems affecting Americans’ ability to work, pay bills or reach family events. The contrast is politically revealing because presidential attention is itself an exercise of power: it tells agencies what matters, officials what will be rewarded and the public whose inconvenience counts.

A crisis of credibility, not a proven “no confidence” vote

AlterNet ties the outages to unfavorable midterm results and continuing complaints about Trump family enrichment, health care, measles, war and diplomacy. Those issues may form part of a broader judgment about the administration, but the article moves too quickly from signs of discontent to a declaration that public trust has collapsed across the country.

There is no survey in the supplied reporting measuring confidence in the administration’s response to these interruptions. Nor does the account establish that the outages shared a cause or were too coordinated to be coincidental. Calling the episode a “vulnpocalypse,” as the Drudge Report did, captures the atmosphere of exposure; it does not diagnose the systems involved.

The more defensible indictment does not require inflated certainty. Americans watched critical services fail while the president reportedly dismissed the consequences, the homeland security secretary traveled to a football game and speculated from one expert conversation, the transportation secretary sought $50 billion, and federal investigators waited until the fifth consecutive day of interruptions to act.

Those are decisions by people with institutional authority, not an impersonal evaporation of “Trust.” The unanswered question is whether the FBI investigation will identify a common cause. The answer already supplied by the administration’s conduct is that the people in charge did not need certainty about the threat to display urgency—and chose not to.
