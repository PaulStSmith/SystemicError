'Full-out brawl': Trump health official linked to death at fraternity in new report

Based on an article by David Edwards

Paulo SantosSeptember 20, 20263 min read

The Administration’s Vetting Standard Is Loyalty, Not Judgment

The Allegation Is Not the Whole Story

William Boothby, now the White House liaison overseeing nearly 300 political appointees at the Department of Health and Human Services, was accused in a wrongful-death lawsuit of punching University of New Hampshire student Vincenzo “Vinny” Lirosi during a fraternity-house brawl. Lirosi, heavily intoxicated and allegedly left dazed, was later found drowned. His death was ruled accidental, no criminal charges were filed, and Boothby was removed from the civil case through a settlement that assigned no judgment to either party.

Power Belongs to the People Who Promoted Him

The lawsuit cannot establish Boothby’s guilt now that it has been settled. It can establish something else: the Trump administration knew—or had every institutional opportunity to know—that its appointee had been accused of violence during events preceding a young man’s death.

The administration nevertheless brought Boothby into HHS in January 2025 and promoted him six months later. That decision belongs to the officials responsible for White House personnel and departmental appointments. Boothby did not acquire authority over hundreds of appointees through bureaucratic weather. Someone evaluated him, accepted the risk, and elevated him.

“Escalation” Is Language That Conceals Agency

Boothby told police that events “escalated.” That familiar construction makes violence sound self-propelled. But the lawsuit alleged specific conduct: Boothby pushed Lirosi down, several fraternity members fought him, and Boothby punched him in the head, leaving him disoriented. Boothby offered a different account, saying Lirosi attacked another fraternity member and that he pushed Lirosi while removing him from the house.

Those competing claims matter. So does the euphemism. “Escalation” collapses individual decisions into atmosphere. It obscures who struck whom, who saw an impaired person leave, and who failed to intervene afterward. The civil case’s closure means the allegations were never adjudicated at trial; it does not transform those decisions into an inexplicable accident.

The Institution Closed Ranks

The fraternity-house response described in the lawsuit reflects a small institution protecting itself through collective inaction. Lirosi’s mother alleged that nobody ensured her visibly intoxicated and injured son’s safety. The bar, meanwhile, was accused of selling him 17 drinks in under four hours, of which investigators said he consumed at least nine.

Then the larger institution supplied its own version of the same reflex. Asked about Boothby, HHS declared that every department member remained focused on Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.’s priorities. That statement answered no question raised by the reporting. It converted scrutiny of an official’s judgment into an irrelevant affirmation of obedience.

This is institutional cowardice in press-release form: loyalty to the program is presented as a substitute for accountability.

Patronage Explains the Promotion

Boothby’s path to power ran through Kennedy’s presidential campaign and MAHA Action before reaching the department Kennedy now leads. That history does not prove favoritism, but it makes the governing incentive plain. Political staffing systems reward service to the faction controlling appointments, and the administration promoted Boothby after the lawsuit had already placed his alleged conduct in the public record.

The essential qualification appears to have been political reliability. HHS’s response reinforces that inference by defending neither Boothby’s conduct nor its vetting process, only his continued focus on Kennedy’s agenda. The department effectively described compliance as the relevant credential.

Accountability Disappears Upward

The easiest version of this story is a lurid account of a fraternity fight. That framing keeps attention on drunken students and a dead young man while minimizing the officials who later conferred federal power.

The larger pattern is more consequential: politically connected operatives move from campaign work into public authority while institutions treat serious questions about judgment as public-relations inconveniences. Settlements end litigation. Euphemisms blur decisions. Loyalty statements replace explanations. Then promotion converts institutional silence into institutional approval—not necessarily of every alleged act, but of the proposition that none of it should obstruct power.
