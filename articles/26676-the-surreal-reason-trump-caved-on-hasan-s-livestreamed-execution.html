The surreal reason Trump caved on Hasan's livestreamed execution

Based on an article by Chauncey DeVega

Paulo SantosOctober 17, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Donald Trump abandoned a plan to livestream the December 3 firing-squad execution of Nidal Hasan after overwhelming public opposition made the spectacle politically costly. AlterNet reports that Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced the broadcast on October 8 and called it a “no-brainer”; Trump initially approved, then announced on October 15 that the execution would proceed without a livestream. The article attributes the reversal to backlash and suggests that concern about the optics of executing a paralyzed man in a wheelchair—and about approaching midterm elections—also influenced the decision.

Hasan, a former Army psychiatrist, was convicted of killing 12 soldiers and one civilian and wounding 32 others at Fort Hood in 2009. According to AlterNet, his execution would be the first carried out by the military in 65 years. Immediate relatives of his victims may attend in person.

Opposition extended well beyond death-penalty critics. A cited ABC News/Washington Post/Ipsos poll found 80 percent of Americans, including 64 percent of Republicans, opposed broadcasting the execution. Catholic bishops condemned the plan, Vice President JD Vance said he would not watch, and Republican lawmakers Thom Tillis and Austin Scott publicly denounced it.

Power proposed the spectacle

Hasan had no agency over the proposed broadcast. Neither did the public officials and religious leaders who objected to it. The institutional power belonged to the Trump administration: Hegseth announced the plan, Trump endorsed it, and Trump claimed the final decision.

That chain of responsibility matters. This was not an unfortunate misunderstanding about military procedure. Senior officials deliberately proposed turning an execution into government-distributed content. Trump’s later description of the broadcast as a possible global deterrent confirms that its audience was central to the idea. The state was not merely going to kill Hasan; it was considering using the killing as a message.

The distinction between punishment and spectacle is the political core of the story. A sentence can be carried out without converting death into mass entertainment. Livestreaming would have made spectatorship part of the punishment and presidential performance part of the event.

The reversal was damage control

AlterNet argues that Trump’s reversal reflected political calculation rather than moral reconsideration. The evidence supports the narrower conclusion that organized opposition changed the administration’s decision: Trump continued to defend the execution, called Hasan “SCUM,” and presented the abandoned broadcast as a potentially useful deterrent.

What the reporting does not independently establish is Trump’s private motive. Its claim about the wheelchair optics and midterm timing is an interpretation, not a documented admission. But no hidden motive is needed to identify the observable pattern. The administration embraced the broadcast while it appeared politically usable and dropped it after the costs became unmistakable.

Calling that a “change of mind” would conceal more than it explains. The policy changed; the underlying endorsement of state killing as political theater did not. Public disgust imposed a boundary that the officials holding power had declined to recognize themselves.

Cruelty was the governing language

The proposed livestream did not appear in isolation. AlterNet places it alongside Trump’s reported statement that people who leak information should be shot and his circulation of videos depicting fatal animal violence and deportees sent to foreign prisons. Those examples vary substantially in legal and factual character, but the political language connecting them is domination: punishment displayed, enemies degraded, force marketed as strength.

The article goes too far when it treats expert speculation about Trump’s childhood or psychological condition as explanatory evidence. Remote diagnosis is not necessary to assess official conduct, and it can distract from the institutions that convert a leader’s preferences into policy. Hegseth did not need access to Trump’s psyche to announce the broadcast. He needed authority, compliance and an administration willing to test the proposal.

Personal pathology is a weaker explanation than organized power. The relevant fact is not that one politician may enjoy violent imagery. It is that government officials were prepared to attach the machinery of the military state to that imagery and distribute the result.

Victims were used as moral cover

Trump said victims’ immediate families deserved to witness “JUSTICE.” Their suffering is real, and the reporting does not establish that they collectively requested a livestream. Invoking them therefore cannot settle the separate question of whether the government should broadcast an execution to the world.

That move collapses justice, vengeance and audience consumption into a single emotional claim. It also shifts attention away from the officials who designed the spectacle. Opposition to broadcasting Hasan’s death did not excuse his crimes or diminish his victims. It challenged the administration’s decision to turn punishment into propaganda.

The article is strongest when it keeps that distinction clear. It is less disciplined when it reaches for sweeping historical analogies. Hasan’s case and the 1936 public execution of Rainey Bethea arose from radically different crimes, legal orders and racial conditions. The defensible connection is limited but serious: public killing allows the state to make hierarchy visible and invite a crowd to participate symbolically in its violence.

The cost of every test

The administration retreated, but only after demonstrating that a livestreamed execution was available for consideration at the highest levels of government. That matters even though the broadcast was canceled. Each such proposal measures institutional resistance, identifies reliable enablers and shifts the next debate from whether cruelty is permissible to how publicly it should be displayed.

The larger pattern is government by provocation followed by tactical retreat. Officials introduce an extreme act, defenders normalize its premise, opponents spend political energy restoring the previous boundary, and the architects claim restraint when they withdraw only the most grotesque feature.

Public opposition worked here. It did not transform the officials who proposed the spectacle; it raised its price. In a system dependent on external restraint, that is a real victory—and an indictment of the people entrusted with restraint from within.
