America’s real problem with 'madman' Trump has nothing to do with him: analysis

Former Cedar Rapids Gazette columnist and The Rumpus managing editor Lyz Lenz said the nation’s problem with an obviously “madman” president is not the biggest problem facing it. It is the powerful people who put the madman in charge.

On Monday, at a Congressional Medal of Honor ceremony, Trump began ranting about the curtains, said Lenz.

“He was talking about the war on Iran, and he said that he doesn’t get bored. Then, he turned and pointed at the gold curtains. ‘See that nice drape? When that comes down right now you see a very, very deep hole, but in about a year and half from now, you’re gonna see a very, very beautiful building. And there’s your entrance to it right there. In fact, I think I’ll even, I’ll save money on the doors because you can’t get more beautiful than that. I picked those drapes in my first term. I always liked gold, but I think we can save a lot of money. I just saved … I just saved curtains.’”

“America is being led by a madman. But that’s not the concerning part. Because if he were just a madman, just someone ranting about curtains, he’d never be able to unilaterally start a war,” said Lenz. “He’s a madman who is supported by an entire system — an entire administration; an entire media ecosystem, too — that believes in what he is doing.”

“This administration wouldn’t exist without Elon Musk’s and Jeff Bezos’ stacks of cash,” said Lenz. “Every Trump rally I’ve been to in the past year has had advertising from Amazon, Coinbase, Lockheed Martin, and Palantir This administration wouldn’t exist without the Heritage Foundation’s endless stream of policy papers that seek to make the horrendous seem intellectually reasonable. It wouldn’t exist without Karoline Leavitt’s furious defenses, or David Brooks’ passive hand-wringing about the left.”

If the Epstein files teach U.S. voters anything the problem is “never just one man, one moment or one action,” said Lenz. It is the hundreds or thousands of other people who willingly enable the one-man problem so they can profit from it.

“It doesn’t matter whether Trump is out of his mind or not,” said Lenz. “It doesn’t matter if he’s a narcissist, if he had a stroke if he secretly has cancer, or anything else. Because the problem with America isn’t just the problem of one man. It’s a whole system that props him up, does his bidding and sponsors his propaganda. It’s all the sane people behind him.”

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Ecosystem of Power: Beyond the “Madman”

Lyz Lenz offers a scorching critique of the current political landscape, emphasizing a point that should be glaringly obvious but is often overlooked: the real danger is not the “madman” at the helm but the system that enthrones and sustains him. This isn’t just about a president’s erratic behavior at a Congressional Medal of Honor ceremony; it’s about the infrastructure of power, money, and ideology that legitimizes his tenure.

The Madman as a Symptom, Not the Disease

The anecdote about the president’s ramblings over curtains during a solemn ceremony is emblematic of his erratic leadership style. Yet, Lenz astutely points out that the focus on these ramblings misses the forest for the trees. The concern isn’t that America is being led by a madman—history is replete with eccentric leaders—but that this madman is propped up and enabled by a complex network of powerful entities.

The Complicity of Cash and Ideology

Lenz draws direct lines to the enablers: Elon Musk, Jeff Bezos, major corporations like Lockheed Martin and Palantir, and ideological think tanks like the Heritage Foundation. These aren’t just passive bystanders; they are active participants in the maintenance of this administration. The mention of advertisements from major corporations at political rallies is a stark illustration of how deeply entwined corporate interests and political power have become. This isn’t merely about financial support; it’s about legitimizing and normalizing through visibility and association.

The Intellectual Whitewashing

The Heritage Foundation’s policy papers are highlighted as a mechanism for making “the horrendous seem intellectually reasonable.” This is a critical point: the ability of think tanks and certain public intellectuals to provide a veneer of legitimacy to extreme policies cannot be underestimated. It’s the intellectual whitewashing of policies that, under scrutiny, often reveal themselves to be antithetical to the principles of democracy and human rights.

The Role of Media and Public Discourse

Lenz also implicates the media ecosystem and certain commentators who, through either furious defense or passive hand-wringing, contribute to the normalization of the abnormal. This is a significant aspect of how dangerous policies and leaders gain traction: the erosion of public discourse, where the outrageous slowly becomes mundane through repetition, defense, and equivocation.

The Systemic Error

The crux of Lenz’s argument—that the problem isn’t just one man but the “whole system that props him up”—is a clarion call to look beyond the individual. It’s a systemic error, a failure of the checks and balances purportedly designed to prevent such a concentration of unaccountable power. The mention of the Epstein files serves as a metaphor for this broader issue: a network of complicity and silence that enables harm.

In conclusion, Lenz’s analysis isn’t just an indictment of a president; it’s an indictment of the entire ecosystem that supports him. It challenges the reader to consider how various elements of society—corporate interests, ideological machinery, and media landscapes—converge to prop up leaders who, in any healthy democracy, would be considered untenable. This isn’t just about changing the figurehead; it’s about addressing the systemic dysfunctions that allowed him to rise and remain in power.
