Trump appointee pleads with Iceland to forgive his 'totally inappropriate' 52nd state joke

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 13, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Faux Pas of Diplomacy: Billy Long’s Icelandic Joke

Trump nominee for Ambassador to Iceland Billy Long, Image via Public Domain

Donald Trump’s administration and allies are rarely known to retreat from controversial statements, but that is precisely what Billy Long, nominee to be ambassador to Iceland, has done after backlash to a joke about making the country the “52nd state,” according to Politico.

Long previously served as a Republican congressman from Mississippi for over a decade, departing the office to make a failed Senate bid in 2022. He also served as Trump’s IRS commissioner after being confirmed by the Senate in June, but was removed from that office just two months later as the president prepared to nominate him for the Iceland ambassadorship.

Long’s path to confirmation hit a significant bump last month when he joked to members of the House that Iceland ought to become the “52nd state,” with himself serving as governor. Reports of this remark set off a firestorm of controversy, with the Icelandic Ministry for Foreign Affairs pressing the U.S. embassy with questions about it and the Icelandic public launching a petition for Long’s nomination to be rejected.

During a hearing before the Senate Foreign Relations Committee on Thursday, Long apologized for the joke and pleaded with the people of Iceland for “a second chance.”

“It was totally inappropriate,” Long said. “I just hope that the people in Iceland will give me a second chance to make a first impression.”

He also added: “There was nothing serious about that, I was with some people, who I hadn’t met for three years, and they were kidding about Jeff Landry being governor of Greenland and they started joking about me and if anyone took offense to it, then I apologize.”

While Long insisted that the remark was meant purely in jest, it came in the wake of Trump’s unprecedented pursuit of power over foreign nations and territories.

Early on, the second Trump administration severely stressed relations with Canada after making repeated assertions that the nation should give up its sovereignty and become the “51st state.” Long’s joke also came not long after the U.S. seized a measure of authority over Venezuela after a military operation ousted its president, Nicolás Maduro, and in the midst of Trump’s insistence that the U.S. must annex the Arctic island territory of Greenland from Denmark. The latter situation set a major international backlash after Trump refused to rule out the use of military force, risking the dissolution of the NATO alliance.

Our Analysis:

The Faux Pas of Diplomacy: Billy Long’s Icelandic Joke

In an era where political controversies come and go with the wind, some statements manage to cut through the noise with the precision of a poorly aimed dart. Billy Long, a nominee for the ambassadorship to Iceland, provided such a moment, offering a glaring example of the diplomatic ineptitude that seems to be a hallmark of certain factions within Donald Trump’s administration and allies.

Billy Long’s “Joke”: Long’s career, marked by a transition from a Republican congressman to a short-lived IRS commissioner, and finally to a nominee for the ambassador to Iceland, has been anything but smooth. However, it’s his recent attempt at humor, suggesting Iceland could become the “52nd state” of the U.S., that has landed him in hot diplomatic waters. This comment, beyond its face value of absurdity, reveals a deeper lack of understanding and respect for the sovereignty of other nations—a trait worryingly prevalent among some in the Trump era.

The Reaction: Understandably, Long’s remark did not sit well with Iceland. The Icelandic Ministry for Foreign Affairs and the public’s pushback, including a petition to reject Long’s nomination, highlights a broader international exasperation with the U.S.’s cavalier attitude towards other nations’ autonomy. Long’s apology and plea for a “second chance” during the Senate Foreign Relations Committee hearing might have been sincere, but the damage was already done. His defense that the remark was made in jest does little to mitigate its implications.

A Pattern of Controversy: This incident cannot be viewed in isolation. It comes on the heels of the Trump administration’s aggressive posturing—be it the audacious suggestion that Canada relinquish its sovereignty or the military intervention in Venezuela. Most notably, Trump’s obsession with annexing Greenland, to the extent of not ruling out military force, underscores a dangerous disregard for diplomatic norms and international stability. Long’s joke about Iceland, thus, is not merely a bad attempt at humor but a symptom of a larger, more concerning trend.

The Implications: Long’s gaffe and the subsequent fallout are emblematic of a broader issue within certain political circles in the U.S.—a fundamental misunderstanding of diplomacy and international relations. The notion of suggesting, even in jest, that a sovereign nation could become a U.S. state is not only disrespectful but also diplomatically damaging. It projects an image of the U.S. as a bully, ready to absorb smaller nations rather than respect and engage with them as equals.

In Conclusion: While Long has attempted to walk back his comments, the incident leaves a sour taste. It underscores the need for a more nuanced, respectful approach to international relations—one that recognizes the sovereignty and dignity of other nations. The world is not a Monopoly board for powerful nations to divide and conquer. It’s high time certain figures within the U.S. political landscape realize this, lest they continue to erode the country’s standing on the global stage.
