Not 'our job': Rubio dodges question about Kimberly Guilfoyle cash shakedown

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 9, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that U.S. Ambassador to Greece Kimberly Guilfoyle allegedly asked Eric Deters, a former Kentucky congressional candidate, to secretly pay her $100,000 American Express bill shortly before her July 9, 2025 confirmation hearing. According to a Wall Street Journal report cited by AlterNet, Guilfoyle suggested she could connect Deters—who wanted help with personal tax and legal matters—to officials at the IRS and Justice Department. Deters did not pay.

Guilfoyle’s lawyer reportedly disputed the messages’ “authenticity” without denying that the exchanges occurred. A U.S. Embassy official defended her integrity and cited her Senate confirmation. When reporters questioned Secretary of State Marco Rubio in Greece the day after the report appeared, he said the department had not had time to assess it and repeatedly attacked the press’s method of seeking comment. He did not address the substance of the allegations.

The power is not mysterious

Guilfoyle is not merely a former television personality with famous connections. She is a Senate-confirmed ambassador, and the alleged exchange occurred days before her confirmation hearing. The reported offer concerned access to two institutions capable of imposing or relieving enormous legal pressure: the IRS and the Justice Department.

That imbalance matters. Deters allegedly wanted government intervention in personal disputes; Guilfoyle allegedly wanted a private debt paid without public visibility. If the messages are authentic, the issue is not generic “influence” or embarrassing fundraising. It is an apparent proposal to exchange proximity to state power for private financial relief.

Rubio changed the subject

Rubio had a legitimate basis for saying he could not authenticate a newly published report within hours. That did not require a lecture about reporters playing a “game.” He could have stated what the State Department knew, what it did not know, and whether it would examine the allegations.

Instead, he treated scrutiny as the misconduct. Reporters had not created the alleged messages, the credit-card request, or the proposed introductions. Asking the secretary responsible for an ambassador whether his department would investigate is ordinary accountability. Rubio’s complaint about timing displaced the relevant question: what safeguards apply when an ambassador is accused of monetizing access to federal law-enforcement and tax officials?

Confirmation is not exoneration

The embassy’s defense relied partly on Guilfoyle having been vetted by the Senate Foreign Relations Committee and confirmed by the Senate. That answers the wrong question. Confirmation establishes that senators approved an appointment based on the record before them; it does not authenticate disputed messages, resolve later reporting, or immunize an official from investigation.

Invoking institutional approval as proof of personal integrity turns oversight into a ceremonial warranty. It also exposes the weakness of the vetting argument: the reported request was sent six days before the confirmation hearing. If senators did not know about it, confirmation proves nothing about it. If relevant officials did know, the questions become more serious.

Accountability deferred

AlterNet’s framing sometimes outruns the evidence by declaring Guilfoyle “caught” in a “cash shakedown.” The supplied reporting establishes allegations supported by reported messages, not a completed payment, adjudicated misconduct, or proven quid pro quo. Deters refused to pay, and Guilfoyle’s lawyer contests authenticity.

But evidentiary caution does not justify institutional evasion. The appropriate response to a grave, unresolved allegation is examination—not premature conviction, and not hostility toward those asking whether examination will occur. Rubio’s performance converted uncertainty from a reason to investigate into a reason to resent the question.

The protective reflex

The larger pattern is how governing institutions protect insiders before establishing facts. A potentially compromised official receives testimonials about her values. Senate approval is presented as retrospective absolution. The cabinet secretary attacks the process by which questions reached him.

That sequence shifts the burden away from officials who control public power and onto journalists seeking answers. Whether the allegations are ultimately substantiated remains unresolved in the supplied reporting. What is already visible is the institutional reflex: when access to government may have been offered for private benefit, the first machinery activated is not accountability but insulation.
