Roger Stone, a longtime adviser to President Donald Trump, warned that "Satanic" Democrats could take control of Congress in the midterm elections.

During a Sunday interview on Real America's Voice, host Shemane Nugent told Stone that Republicans were in "spiritual warfare" with Democrats.

"We control the House of Representatives, I think now by two seats," Stone noted. "So the House could flip back to the Democrats in the midterm election as repugnant an idea as that is."

The Trump adviser predicted investigations and impeachment of the president if the Democrats were successful.

"But anybody who thinks that we will hold on to power forever, I think, misunderstands the dynamics here," he explained. "These people are demonic. They are Satanic. There are no moderates in the Democrat [sic] Party. This is a radical clique of Marxists. And they are committed."

"Be certain that we are doing God's will," he added. "Pray for the safety and the wisdom of our president and those he is surrounded around him."

Stone also accused former special counsel Robert Mueller of giving his wife cancer.

"I've had as many as five miracles in my life in a short period of time," he insisted. "My wife survived aggressive stage four cancer, which I think was a gift from Robert Mueller and his thugs."

"I mean, two and a half years under intense pressure, that kind of stress triggers cancer, in my opinion, and the opinion of many doctors."

Our Analysis:

The Theater of the Absurd: Roger Stone's Latest Rant

In the latest installment of what can only be described as political theater at its most ludicrous, Roger Stone, a figure whose allegiance to former President Donald Trump is as unwavering as it is infamous, took to the airwaves to issue a dire warning: the imminent takeover of Congress by what he describes as "Satanic" Democrats.

During a Sunday interview on Real America's Voice with host Shemane Nugent—a platform that has increasingly become a haven for the most outlandish and baseless of claims—Stone painted a picture of the upcoming midterm elections that felt more like a script out of a B-rated horror movie than a political analysis. According to Stone, we are on the brink of a Democratic coup, led not by politicians with differing views on governance but by a cabal of "demonic" and "Satanic" radicals, hell-bent on the destruction of American values.

The Art of Fearmongering

Stone's rhetoric is nothing new; it's a page straight out of the fearmongering playbook. By labeling Democrats as not just opponents but as evil incarnate, Stone and his ilk attempt to morph political discourse into a battle between good and evil. This isn't just hyperbole; it's a dangerous and calculated attempt to demonize a whole segment of the political spectrum. The implications of such narratives are far-reaching, fostering division and hatred that transcends mere political disagreement.

The "Satanic" Democrats?

Calling Democrats "Satanic" and "demonic" is a tactic as ludicrous as it is laughable. Stone's unfounded claim that there are no moderates in the Democratic Party, but rather a "radical clique of Marxists" is not just factually incorrect, it's intellectually lazy. It ignores the wide spectrum of ideologies within the Democratic Party, from centrist to progressive, and everything in between. It's a blanket statement designed to alarm rather than inform, a tactic that has unfortunately become all too common in today's political environment.

A Divine Mission or Delusional Grandeur?

Perhaps most troubling is Stone's assertion that he and his allies are "doing God's will" and his call for prayers for the safety and wisdom of the president and his advisors. This messianic messaging not only blurs the lines between church and state but also elevates political figures to near-divine status, suggesting that opposition to them is tantamount to opposition to divine will. This dangerous conflation of religious zealotry with political loyalty only serves to deepen the divide, making compromise and dialogue nearly impossible.

Bizarre Accusations and "Miracles"

And then there's the matter of Stone accusing Robert Mueller of giving his wife cancer—an accusation so bizarre and devoid of any scientific basis that it would be laughable if it weren't so tragic. Stone's claim plays into the narrative of victimhood and conspiracy that has been cultivated by some on the far right. It's a narrative that eschews personal responsibility and factual evidence in favor of sensationalism and scapegoating.

Conclusion

Roger Stone's latest tirade is a stark reminder of the depths to which political discourse in America has sunk. His unfounded and outrageous claims do more than just misinform; they actively sow division and hatred among the American populace. In an era where truth and facts are more important than ever, we must remain vigilant against such baseless fearmongering and strive for a political dialogue grounded in reality, not in the fantastical imaginings of those who would see America divided for their own gain.
