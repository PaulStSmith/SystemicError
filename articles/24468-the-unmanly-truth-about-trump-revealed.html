'The unmanly truth' about Trump revealed

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Paul Waldman, writing for MS NOW, identified the “unmanly truth” as the Trump administration’s failure to deliver the economic security it promised young men and other voters who backed Donald Trump in 2024. Citing the latest federal jobs report, Waldman argued that prices remain burdensome, manufacturing employment is lower than when Trump took office, and the promised factory revival has not materialized.

Waldman also attributed higher gasoline prices to Trump’s war with Iran, with sharper consequences for rural residents who drive farther and often use less fuel-efficient vehicles. Meanwhile, he wrote, employment growth has occurred in fields such as home healthcare and social services—work that clashes with the administration’s preferred image of masculinity. With an election reportedly one month away, Waldman suggested that the gap between Trump’s promises and his record may help explain his poor polling, though the article does not establish that explanation as causal.

The power belongs to the president

Young men may have cast decisive votes, but they do not command federal economic, military, or communications policy. Trump and his administration do. The president made the sweeping promise to reduce prices “starting on Day One,” promoted a restoration of manufacturing employment, and took the military action that Waldman links to higher fuel costs.

That distinction matters because political coverage often treats disappointed constituencies as the central characters after an election. They are not. Voters supplied authority; the administration exercised it. The relevant question is not why young men wanted stable work. It is what Trump did with the power obtained by promising it.

Nostalgia was substituted for policy

The manufacturing vision described here was not merely a jobs proposal. It offered status, identity and social restoration through an economy imagined as physically demanding, male and industrial. But a president cannot recreate a vanished labor market through posture. According to Waldman, the measurable result is fewer manufacturing jobs than when Trump entered office, not the factory boom he advertised.

The growing sectors expose the fraud in the fantasy. Home healthcare and social services meet real needs and provide actual employment, yet Waldman argues that Trump’s gender politics make such work difficult for his movement to celebrate. The administration’s cultural script therefore works against economic adaptation: it tells men to recover dignity through jobs that are not appearing while demeaning the kinds of work that are.

Masculinity is the misdirection

AlterNet’s framing risks turning a governing failure into a psychological story about men. The deeper issue is not that young male voters placed too much emotional weight on Trump. It is that a politician made concrete promises, acquired institutional power and, by Waldman’s account, pursued choices that worsened costs without producing the promised employment gains.

The imagery of testosterone, pickups and “manly” labor is politically relevant because it helped sell the program. But it can also obscure accountability. A worker struggling to afford gasoline is experiencing a material consequence, not a crisis of symbolism. Culture-war masculinity offers an explanation for why the sales pitch resonated; it does not excuse the officials who sold it or replace scrutiny of their decisions.

Grievance cannot provide a paycheck

Waldman describes an administration and allied media ecosystem promoting manhood through bullying, violence and anxiety. His interpretation is sharpest when connected to the economic void beneath that performance. If secure work and affordable necessities remain unavailable, theatrical dominance becomes a cheap substitute for material provision.

The predictable beneficiaries are politicians and influencers who can keep converting insecurity into attention and loyalty. The voter receives an identity to defend while the promised economic transformation fails to arrive. Even the reported disappointment can then be redirected toward vulnerable groups rather than toward the officials controlling policy.

The systemic error

This is a recurring political bargain: concentrated power promises to reverse structural economic change, packages the promise as cultural restoration, and leaves ordinary supporters to absorb the failure privately. When the numbers disappoint, public discussion drifts toward voter psychology—alienation, impatience, masculinity—rather than staying fixed on presidential agency.

The consequential divide is not between masculine and unmasculine work. It is between those who make promises and decisions and those who pay for them. A politics organized around lost status can win power. It cannot manufacture an economy merely by insisting that the past deserves to return.
