Brace yourselves: The midterms are about to go thermonuclear

Based on an article by John Stoehr

Paulo SantosSeptember 21, 20263 min read

Impeachment Is Not a Principle Until Power Finds It Useful

The Power Is Already in the Room

Donald Trump commands the executive branch. Republicans supply the congressional protection that makes his conduct politically survivable. The Supreme Court has supplied an expansive shield against criminal prosecution. Democratic leaders control whether opposition becomes an institutional confrontation or remains an election message.

The source describes war, deportations, taxes, corruption and rising energy costs while Washington debates whether impeachment is practical. That is enough context. The central issue is not whether the system contains a remedy. It is whether the people empowered to use it believe Trump has become more costly than useful.

Republicans Are the Deciding Institution

Removal requires Republican senators. That fact strips away the decorative language about constitutional principle.

The scenario offered here—devastating midterm losses, Trump’s approval collapsing toward 25 percent, another attempted seizure of power—is really a forecast about Republican incentives. The party might abandon him once he threatens its 2028 prospects, not when his actions harm the country. The threshold is not misconduct. It is electoral liability.

That is how impunity becomes durable: allies tolerate conduct until the bill reaches them.

Democrats Are Marketing Around the Emergency

Hakeem Jeffries’ emphasis on affordability is presented as strategy for vulnerable districts. Perhaps it is. It is also an admission that Democratic leaders believe accountability must first survive message testing among persuadable Trump voters.

Affordability is not irrelevant. The source links war and energy shocks to diesel prices, food costs, heating bills and recession risk. But treating those consequences as a separate, safer subject obscures agency. Prices do not rise in a political vacuum. If presidential decisions produce economic pain, discussing the pain while softening who caused it is not discipline. It is political laundering.

Jeffries may change course if a large Democratic majority and Republican defections make impeachment viable. That flexibility would reveal the actual governing logic: leaders call accountability divisive when it is difficult and necessary when it becomes achievable.

“Let Him Cook” Means Let Others Burn

The most cynical calculation is that Democrats might benefit by leaving Trump in office to damage the Republican brand. Under that theory, continued chaos becomes opposition-party campaign material.

But the costs described in the source are not paid by strategists. They fall on people buying food, heating homes and absorbing the consequences of war. Foreign civilians do not become acceptable losses because presidential recklessness might improve Democratic margins.

Allowing preventable harm to continue for electoral advantage would not be restraint. It would be institutional cowardice disguised as patience.

Accountability by Convenience

The proposal to let Republicans choose the grounds for removal is tactically sound and morally revealing. If their votes are required, the charge capable of attracting them will shape the case.

That does not make removal illegitimate. It demonstrates that impeachment is a political instrument governed by power, even when responding to grave misconduct. The Constitution provides the mechanism; partisan incentives determine whether anyone touches it.

The first Trump impeachments already exposed this structure. Conduct alone did not produce removal. Republican protection did. Any later removal would likewise require Republicans to withdraw that protection.

A System Built to Wait for Elite Permission

The deeper pattern is not that constitutional machinery has mysteriously failed. Institutions are operated by people making choices. Republican officials choose when loyalty ends. Democratic officials choose when confrontation begins. Judges choose how much immunity executive power receives.

A system that answers spectacular harm only after approval ratings collapse is not enforcing accountability. It is pricing it.

That is the systemic error: official consequences arrive, if they arrive at all, only when powerful actors decide that impunity has become inconvenient to them.
