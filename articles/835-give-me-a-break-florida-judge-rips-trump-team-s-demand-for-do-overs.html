'Give me a break': Florida judge rips Trump team's demand for 'do-overs'

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosJune 15, 20263 min read

Trump’s DOJ Treats Courts Like a Test Lab

The Source, Briefly

A federal judge in Florida ordered immigrant detainee Dmitrii Iastrebov a bond hearing, the government initially agreed he was entitled to one, then let an immigration judge ignore that ruling and later came back asking for the opposite outcome. Judge Kyle Dudek called the move legally unsupportable and ordered Iastrebov released because the government had shown it could not be trusted to follow the court’s directions.

Power Sat Where It Always Sits

The real power in this story is not the detainee, and it is not even the immigration judge caught in the procedural mess. The power sits with the executive branch: Trump’s DOJ, its federal lawyers, and the immigration machinery that can detain first and litigate later. That is the structure. A person in immigration detention does not control the process. The government does.

That matters because the article’s drama is framed as a legal blunder, when the deeper fact is institutional leverage. When the state can confine someone and then improvise around a court order, the issue is not merely competence. It is the use of custody as pressure.

The Decision Was Made in Washington

The decisive acts all belong to the government. Trump’s DOJ conceded the bond-hearing point. Its counsel then let that concession evaporate. Later, facing renewed challenge, the same government reversed course and tried to resurrect a mandatory-detention theory it had already abandoned.

That is not confusion in the ordinary sense. It is a chain of choices. The government chose the first position, chose not to defend the court’s mandate, and then chose to seek a do-over once the tactical value shifted. Judge Dudek’s anger is not theatrical; it is the correct response to an executive branch that appears to treat legal positions as disposable tools.

This Is Not a Mistake, It Is a Method

The article’s weak point is its tendency to describe the situation as a collection of “bad decisions,” “foul-ups,” and “legal gaffes.” That language softens what is actually on display: administrative cynicism. A court order was treated as something to manage rather than obey.

That is the pattern. The government says one thing to close a case, lets its own process defy the result, then returns demanding a reset. This is how institutional bad faith works in practice. It exploits delay, procedural confusion, and the assumption that courts will tolerate a second bite at the apple after the first concession has already served its purpose.

Immigration as a Site of State Impunity

Immigration detention is built for this kind of abuse. The detainee sits inside a system where the government controls the cage, the paperwork, and the timetable. If the executive branch can casually reverse itself after a court ruling, detention stops being a legal status and becomes a bargaining instrument.

That is why the judge’s order for immediate release lands as a rebuke to the entire machine. The issue was never simply whether Iastrebov qualified for a hearing. The issue was whether the government had any intention of honoring the legal process it claimed to support when it was convenient.

The Pattern Behind the Slapdowns

The larger political pattern is authoritarian sloppiness backed by bureaucratic power. Trump’s DOJ, as described here, is not just underperforming; it is being politicized, hollowed out, and staffed in a way that makes compliance less reliable and contempt more likely. Experienced attorneys are leaving. The institution is losing memory and discipline. Into that vacuum comes a posture of improvisation, reversal, and disregard.

Courts can correct a single case. They cannot by themselves repair a federal apparatus that treats legal restraint as negotiable. The real story is not that one judge got angry. It is that anger has become necessary because the government is no longer behaving like a law-bound actor.
