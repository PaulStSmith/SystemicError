Mike Johnson's sickening silence catches him in a shameful lie

Based on an article by Pat Dennis, American Bridge 21St Century

Paulo SantosSeptember 7, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Power, Not ConfusionWhat Johnson Refused to SayThe Pattern Is the StoryMoral Language, Tactical SilenceThe Real Standard

Mike Johnson’s Real Constituency

The source is simple: Republican leaders are backing Max Miller despite serious allegations from multiple women, including allegations of violence against his ex-wife and harm to his daughter. The point is not the allegations alone. The point is that the Speaker of the House has the power to set a standard and has chosen not to use it.

Power, Not Confusion

Mike Johnson is not a bystander. As Speaker, he has real leverage over his caucus, over the public posture of the party, and over what conduct gets normalized. He is not “waiting for more information” in some innocent sense. He is deciding that keeping a seat is worth more than drawing a line around violence against women and children.

That is the governing logic here: protect the majority, protect the machine, protect the men who make the machine run. Everything else is branding.

What Johnson Refused to Say

Johnson did not call for Miller to withdraw. He did not condemn the behavior. He praised him as a “great member of Congress” and backed his reelection effort. When pressed on Miller and other Republicans facing ugly allegations, his answer was the bureaucratic cop-out of the moment: “allegation.”

That word is doing propaganda work. It turns serious claims into fog. It asks the public to treat documented patterns, committee findings, and repeated accusations as if they are all just noise until the party decides otherwise. That is not caution. It is institutional cowardice.

The Pattern Is the Story

Miller is not isolated in Johnson’s orbit. The source points to Rep. Chuck Edwards, who withdrew from reelection after the House Ethics Committee found “substantial reason to believe” he violated rules against sexual harassment. It points to Rep. Cory Mills, under ethics investigation for dating violence allegations and accused of threatening to release explicit images. It points to Tony Gonzales, and to Johnson holding off on pressure until Gonzales’s political position was already collapsing.

This is not one bad apple. It is a caucus culture in which allegations against powerful men are treated as a communications problem, not a moral or institutional one.

Moral Language, Tactical Silence

Johnson’s website talks about “morality” and a “just government.” That rhetoric is decorative. When the moment comes to act, the Speaker’s morality disappears behind fundraising, loyalty, and Trump-era discipline. The source is right to connect this to Johnson’s weeks-long effort to block the Epstein files at Trump’s direction. The pattern is consistent: shield the powerful, delay accountability, preserve the coalition.

That is what partisan corruption looks like when it wears a sermon.

The Real Standard

The standard is not whether Republican leaders can say the word “family” while excusing abuse. The standard is whether they are willing to use office to stop dangerous men from being protected by the institution that empowers them. Johnson has that power. He is using it in the opposite direction.

The larger lesson is blunt: when a party treats violence, harassment, and intimidation as manageable public-relations damage, it is not suffering a values crisis. It has already chosen its values. And in this case, the chosen value is the seat, not the child.
