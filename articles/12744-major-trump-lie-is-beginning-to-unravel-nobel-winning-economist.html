Major Trump 'lie is beginning to unravel': Nobel-winning economist

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 15, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

U.S. President Donald Trump speaks to the press as he arrives at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., July 13, 2025. REUTERS/Annabelle Gordon

A Nobel Prize-winning economist believes Americans are souring on the Trump administration's draconian immigration policies because they finally realize "they've been lied to" about the inherent "criminality" of people fleeing oppression and looking for steady work inside the United States.

In a new Substack article, Paul Krugman cited a recent Gallup poll showing that "When asked if immigration is generally a good thing or bad thing for the country, a record-high 79% of U.S. adults call it a good thing; a record-low 17% see it as a bad thing."

In addition, "30% of Americans want immigration decreased, down from 55% a year ago," with more Americans rejecting Trump's border wall and mass deportation policies.

Krugman maintained that Trump's initial call for mass detentions and deportations was always based on the lie that "America is facing a huge immigrant crime wave." After all, last week, Department of Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem declared that Los Angeles was not so much a city of immigrants as one of "criminals." And yet, LA officials reported last week "that LA is on track to have the fewest homicides in 60 years."

As Americans start to parse fact from fiction being fed to them from the Trump administration's claims that rapists, murderers, and the worst-of-the-worst from "insane asylums" were being released into the streets, Krugman wrote, "it seems to me that the lie is beginning to unravel as it becomes clear that ICE is having a really hard time finding violent immigrants to arrest."

"Why aren’t they rounding up more undocumented criminals?" Krugman asked. "Because that would be hard work, and anyway there aren’t that many of them. Preliminary numbers found that "only around 78,000 undocumented immigrants with criminal records, and 14,000 convicted of violent crimes," had made their way over the border.

"Meanwhile, Stephen Miller is demanding that ICE arrest 3,000 people a day. Do the math, and you see why they’re grabbing farm workers and chasing day laborers in Home Depot parking lots," Krugman wrote.

"So, Americans may be turning on Trump’s immigration policies in part because they’re starting to realize that they’ve been lied to. But an even more important factor may be that more native-born Americans are beginning to see what our immigrants are really like, rather than thinking of them as scary figures lurking in the shadows," he concluded.

Our Analysis:

Analyzing Krugman's Critique of Trump's Immigration Policies

In a scathing critique of the Trump administration's immigration policies, Nobel Prize-winning economist Paul Krugman pulls no punches in his latest Substack article. He sheds light on a significant shift in American public opinion, leveraging recent Gallup poll data to underscore a growing disenchantment with the draconian measures previously championed by the administration. But let's dissect the truths, the hyperboles, and the underpinnings of Krugman's arguments to really understand the narrative here.

The Myth of the Immigrant Crime Wave

At the heart of Trump's immigration rhetoric was the alarmist and unfounded claim that America was being inundated by a wave of immigrant crime. This narrative, as Krugman rightly points out, has been foundational to justifying harsh policies including mass detentions and deportations. Yet, the evidence—or rather, the stark lack thereof—tells a different story.

Kristi Noem's characterization of Los Angeles as a city overrun by "criminals" rather than immigrants is contrasted sharply by the actual crime statistics. Los Angeles is reportedly on track to record the fewest homicides in 60 years—a fact that flies in the face of the fear-mongering propagated by figures like Noem and, by extension, the Trump administration.

The Real Numbers

Krugman highlights an essential discrepancy that gets to the very core of the issue: the actual number of undocumented immigrants with violent criminal records is vastly lower than the administration's rhetoric would suggest. With around 78,000 undocumented immigrants with criminal records and only 14,000 convicted of violent crimes, the incessant focus on immigrant criminality seems not just exaggerated, but deliberately misleading.

Stephen Miller's demand for ICE to arrest 3,000 people a day appears not just unrealistic but absurd when faced with these numbers. It's no wonder then that, as Krugman notes, ICE agents are "grabbing farm workers and chasing day laborers in Home Depot parking lots." This isn't a policy aimed at keeping Americans safe from criminals; it's a policy aimed at fulfilling deportation quotas and fueling a narrative of fear.

Shifting Public Opinion

Perhaps the most telling aspect of Krugman's analysis is the significant shift in American public opinion regarding immigration. Citing a Gallup poll, he notes that a record-high 79% of U.S. adults now see immigration as a good thing for the country, with a decreasing number advocating for reduced immigration. This shift suggests that the American public is not only rejecting Trump's policies but also the underlying narrative of immigrant criminality.

Conclusion: The Unraveling of a Lie

Krugman's argument is both compelling and grounded in a reality that the Trump administration often seemed keen to distort. The so-called immigrant crime wave has been revealed as a constructed bogeyman, used to justify extreme policies that have done more to sow division and fear than protect American citizens.

As Americans increasingly come to see immigrants for what they overwhelmingly are—individuals seeking safety and opportunity, not criminals lurking in the shadows—the narrative pushed by Trump and his allies loses its grip. The unraveling of this lie, as Krugman suggests, may not only mark a turning point in public opinion but also signal a broader rejection of fear-mongering as a substitute for genuine policy-making.

In essence, Krugman’s article doesn’t just critique a set of policies; it challenges an entire narrative built on misinformation and fear. It's a narrative that, if the recent Gallup poll is any indication, is losing its power over the American psyche. And in its place, a more nuanced, compassionate, and fact-based understanding of immigration seems to be taking root.
