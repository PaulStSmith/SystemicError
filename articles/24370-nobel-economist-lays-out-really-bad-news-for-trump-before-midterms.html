Nobel economist lays out 'really bad news' for Trump before midterms

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 3, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Paul Krugman’s “really, really bad news” for Donald Trump and Republicans is the widening gap between the administration’s economic sales pitch and voters’ reported experience one month before the midterms. As AlterNet reports, Krugman pointed to worsening economic sentiment, perceptions of a weaker job market, and a sharp rise in mortgage rates.

In an AP-NORC survey of 2,140 adults conducted Sept. 24–28, 74 percent called the national economy poor and 73 percent disapproved of Trump’s handling of it. Fifty-five percent said their local economy was worse than when Trump took office in 2025. Separately, the Conference Board reported declining confidence and deteriorating perceptions of job availability in September, while Freddie Mac put the average 30-year fixed mortgage rate at 7.28 percent.

Krugman acknowledged that some headline employment measures remained comparatively strong. His argument was narrower: prices, housing costs, and anxiety about finding work were moving against the White House’s claim of a “Golden Age.”

The power belongs to the White House

Trump is not a spectator watching an unfortunate economy arrive. He controls the executive branch and has made economic prosperity central to his political claim. Republicans seeking to retain power benefit from that claim when voters accept it and inherit the consequences when they do not.

That does not mean every price, hiring decision, or mortgage-rate movement originates in the Oval Office. The reporting does not establish such a causal chain. But an administration that claims sweeping credit for prosperity cannot plausibly declare itself powerless when households report deterioration.

Propaganda has met local evidence

The most damaging finding is not merely that voters dislike the national economy. During the Biden administration, Krugman observed, Americans often rated conditions near them more favorably than the national picture. In the September survey, that cushion had eroded: 55 percent said their local economy was worse, while only 20 percent said it was better.

That distinction matters because national economic opinion can be shaped by partisan messaging. Local conditions are harder to dismiss when people encounter them through rent, mortgage quotes, household bills, and job searches. The White House’s “Golden Age” branding asks voters to subordinate those experiences to presidential advertising.

The numbers require discipline

AlterNet’s strongest evidence concerns public judgment, not proof that the economy has collapsed. Polling measures what respondents believe; it does not establish the cause of those beliefs or predict the midterm result. The Conference Board figures likewise describe consumers’ perceptions of job availability, while the article says official employment measures remained relatively strong by historical standards.

That qualification does not rescue the administration’s message. It exposes its dishonesty of scale. An economy can produce respectable aggregate statistics while distributing insecurity through unaffordable credit, stubborn living costs, and fear of unemployment. Declaring victory from the top line is a political choice to discount the conditions people actually have to survive.

Accountability is not misdirection

The article largely identifies the correct center of political responsibility: the president and the party governing with him. It does not redirect anger toward workers, consumers, or some conveniently powerless group. Krugman’s critique targets the White House’s response—insisting on triumph while its own promised beneficiaries report strain.

Still, electoral damage should not be mistaken for economic accountability. A bad poll is a threat to officeholders; a 7.28 percent mortgage rate can shut households out of ownership. The political class tends to recognize material pain most clearly when that pain begins endangering its control of government.

The systemic error

This is the recurring failure of economic politics: institutions treat national indicators as reality and household experience as mood. When the two diverge, those in power often try to correct the public’s perception before confronting the public’s conditions.

Krugman’s warning is therefore larger than a forecast about one midterm. Political authority becomes brittle when leaders demand credit for abstractions and deny responsibility for consequences. The White House can call the moment golden. It cannot compel people paying its price to see the shine.
