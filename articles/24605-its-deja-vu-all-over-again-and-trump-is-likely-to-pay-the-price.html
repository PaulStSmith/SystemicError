'It’s déjà vu all over again' — and Trump is likely to pay the price

Based on an article by The Conversation

Paulo SantosOctober 10, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Donald Trump’s low approval ratings and voter dissatisfaction over the economy, health care, foreign policy, and the Iran war have put Republican control of Congress at risk in the 2026 midterms. The “price” is prospective, not yet paid: analysts cited in the article expect Democrats to win the House, while control of the Senate remains uncertain.

Charlie Cook projects a Democratic gain of at least five to 15 House seats. The article says Democratic Senate candidates have also become competitive in Republican-leaning states, but Democrats need a net gain of at least four seats and could lose seats of their own in New Hampshire or Michigan.

The comparison is to the backlash against second-term presidents in 2006 and 2010. AlterNet argues that a Democratic Congress could restrict Trump’s appointments and legislation, investigate his finances and use of executive power, and shape the contest for the White House in 2028.

The power is still in Washington

The article’s electoral framing risks getting ahead of its institutional analysis. Trump holds the presidency. Republicans hold the Senate 53–47. Until voters change that arrangement, the administration retains enormous executive authority and Republicans retain the power to confirm judges, cabinet officers, and ambassadors.

A House defeat would impose oversight and legislative resistance, but it would not remove Trump’s executive power. Even the article calls that outcome only a “modest catastrophe” for him if Republicans keep the Senate. The distinction matters: losing political prestige is not the same as losing governing capacity.

Republicans made their choice

Trump did not force congressional Republicans to make his presidency their political identity. They chose to sustain his agenda while their party controlled Congress. If voters punish Republican candidates for conditions they associate with his administration, those candidates are not innocent bystanders caught in presidential weather. They are members of the governing coalition.

Trump is also making the referendum more explicit. According to AlterNet, he has asked voters to “pretend I am on the ballot” and is campaigning in states he carried by double digits in 2024. That strategy may mobilize supporters, but the article argues that it can just as easily mobilize the larger share of voters who disapprove of him.

His response to adverse polling is denial: he called the numbers fake and the pollsters corrupt. That does not change the measurements. It tells Republicans that loyalty to the leader should take precedence over information about the electorate.

Polling is evidence, not destiny

AlterNet’s framing is strongest when it describes the structural danger to Republicans and weakest when it turns forecasts into near-certainty. A 32 percent approval rating, a Democratic advantage on the generic ballot, and competitive races in traditionally Republican states are serious warning signs. They are not election results.

The Senate picture described in the article is especially unstable. Possible Democratic gains in Texas and Ohio coexist with possible losses in New Hampshire and Michigan and a potential failure in Maine. Calling the political atmosphere “catastrophically bad” may capture the national mood measured by the cited analysts, but it cannot settle individual races.

The headline’s prediction should therefore be read accurately: Trump is likely to pay an electoral and institutional price if the reported polling environment holds through Election Day. The reporting does not establish that he already has.

The consequence is constraint

If Democrats win only the House, they gain subpoena power, control of committees, and the ability to block Republican legislation. If they win both chambers, the consequences widen: Trump’s nominees would need Democratic support, his legislative program would face a congressional veto point, and Democrats could send him bills on costs, energy, and health care that force visible presidential decisions.

That is the real political stake. Elections do not merely deliver a verdict on presidential popularity. They allocate the machinery that confirms officials, writes laws, conducts investigations, and exposes decisions an administration would prefer to make without hostile scrutiny.

The recurring system

The déjà vu is not simply that presidents often lose seats in midterms. It is that American government repeatedly concentrates attention and accountability in the presidency while congressional allies help exercise power, then present themselves as passengers when the public turns against the results.

The 2006 and 2010 comparisons show how backlash can rearrange Congress and prepare the next presidential contest. They do not guarantee a replay. What the present evidence reveals is a governing party tied to an unpopular president, an opposition positioned to convert dissatisfaction into institutional leverage, and an election whose importance lies less in political humiliation than in who will possess the power to obstruct, investigate, appoint, and govern.
