The Stranger Danger Scam: We Were Looking for Monsters in the Wrong Places

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 18, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Remember “stranger danger”?

If you grew up during the 1980s or 1990s, you probably do.

We were warned about sinister strangers lurking around playgrounds, suspicious vans cruising residential streets, and strange men offering candy to unsuspecting children.

The message was relentless: strangers were dangerous, children were vulnerable, and the world outside the family home was crawling with predators.

Parents were frightened. Children were frightened. Television amplified the fear. And an entire generation absorbed the idea that safety meant staying close to home, under adult supervision.

There was just one rather inconvenient problem.

The statistics were pointing in almost exactly the opposite direction.

According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, approximately 90% of child sexual abuse is committed by someone known and trusted by the child or the child’s family.

Not the mysterious man in the van.

Not the stranger waiting behind the playground.

Someone familiar.

A relative. A family friend. A trusted adult. Someone who has been welcomed into the child’s life.

And here’s the truly infuriating part: this wasn’t some revolutionary discovery made decades after the fact.

The evidence was already emerging while the “stranger danger” campaign was in full swing.

A CDC study published in 1985 found that 91% of the known sexual offenses examined involved family members, friends, or acquaintances. Only 9% involved strangers.

Of course, that individual study wasn’t a nationally representative census of every abusive incident. But its findings were consistent with the broader pattern that subsequent research would repeatedly establish.

We spent decades teaching children to fear the people they didn’t know while failing to adequately confront the danger posed by some of the people they did.

The Numbers Behind the Nightmare

Let’s examine another central obsession of the era: stranger abduction.

Government research examining 1999 estimated approximately 115 cases of what researchers called “stereotypical kidnappings” in the United States.

These were especially serious abductions by strangers or slight acquaintances involving circumstances such as prolonged detention, long-distance transportation, ransom, or murder.

One hundred and fifteen.

That’s 115 too many. Every single case represents a terrible crime, and many ended tragically.

But it was never evidence that strangers were routinely snatching children off every street corner in America.

The same research estimated a much larger number of abductions under broader definitions, including incidents involving family members and acquaintances.

Yet the cultural image that dominated childhood safety education wasn’t the trusted adult exploiting their position.

It was the sinister stranger.

And that distinction matters enormously.

Because when you teach children to associate danger primarily with unfamiliar faces, you risk leaving them less prepared to recognize abusive behavior from someone they already trust.

The Most Dangerous Words: “He Would Never Do That”

There is another aspect of this problem that deserves considerably more attention.

What happens when a child actually reports inappropriate behavior by a trusted adult?

Sometimes the response is disbelief.

“He’s your uncle.”

“She’s known our family for years.”

“He’s a respected member of the community.”

“He would never do something like that.”

And perhaps the most devastating response of all:

“Are you sure you didn’t misunderstand?”

Think about the contradiction.

We tell children they must report suspicious behavior to adults.

Then, when the suspicious adult is someone the family knows and trusts, some families suddenly decide that the adult’s reputation deserves more protection than the child’s testimony deserves consideration.

The child is expected to respect authority.

The adult benefits from that authority.

And the family may become so invested in believing that its trusted relationships are safe that it struggles to confront evidence suggesting otherwise.

That is not protection.

That is the betrayal of the very trust on which childhood safety depends.

A child’s allegation must be taken seriously, handled carefully, and investigated fairly. Taking a report seriously doesn’t mean automatically pronouncing anyone guilty. It means refusing to dismiss a child’s account merely because the accused adult is familiar, respected, or beloved.

Trust should never be a substitute for scrutiny.

The Stranger Was Never the Whole Problem

To be clear, stranger abductions and assaults are real. They can be horrific, and children absolutely should learn how to respond to suspicious approaches.

But effective safety education should be grounded in actual risks, not frightening caricatures.

Teach children about boundaries.

Teach them that nobody has an automatic right to touch them inappropriately, demand secrecy about abuse, or make them uncomfortable simply because they’re older.

Teach them that they can refuse an adult’s inappropriate request.

Teach them how to recognize manipulation.

Teach them how to seek help.

And, above all, teach them that the rules apply to everyone, including relatives, family friends, teachers, coaches, and other trusted adults.

A trusted adult isn’t entitled to a special exemption from a child’s personal boundaries.

Quite the opposite.

The closer an adult is to a child, the greater the responsibility to respect those boundaries.

And parents must make it unmistakably clear that their children can report inappropriate behavior without being punished, ridiculed, or accused of disloyalty.

Fear Is Not a Safety Strategy

Now consider the extraordinary irony.

A child like me, traveling downtown on a familiar bus route, surrounded by hundreds of ordinary commuters, might be portrayed as endangered because no parent was physically standing beside him.

Meanwhile, a child sitting quietly at home with a trusted adult could be in genuine danger, even though every superficial requirement of parental supervision had been satisfied.

That’s not to suggest that homes are inherently dangerous or that unsupervised city streets are always safe.

It illustrates something much simpler.

Safety cannot be determined merely by asking whether a child is at home or whether a stranger is nearby.

It depends on circumstances, behavior, preparation, and the ability to recognize and respond to danger.

A child who knows how to navigate public transportation, identify inappropriate requests, seek assistance, and assert personal boundaries may be far better prepared for real-world challenges than one whose entire safety education consists of “Don’t talk to strangers.”

And the latter instruction has an additional flaw.

Sometimes, when a child is in trouble, a stranger is precisely the person they need to approach for help.

A bus driver. A shopkeeper. A police officer. A parent accompanying other children.

Teaching children to fear all strangers indiscriminately can make seeking assistance unnecessarily difficult.

The better lesson is to understand behavior, boundaries, and context.

We Protected the Image of Childhood More Than Children Themselves

Perhaps the greatest failure of the “stranger danger” era wasn’t that it warned children about a danger that existed.

It was that it allowed one highly visible, emotionally powerful danger to overshadow other, more prevalent threats.

And in doing so, it encouraged a profoundly misleading understanding of what protecting children actually means.

We became obsessed with controlling where children went, who they encountered, and how far they wandered from home.

But controlling a child’s movements isn’t the same thing as teaching that child to recognize abuse.

And supervising a child isn’t the same thing as ensuring that every supervising adult is trustworthy.

There’s a bitter irony in treating the unfamiliar commuter on a public bus as a potential threat while automatically assuming that anyone invited into the family living room must be safe.

The statistics don’t support that assumption.

They never did.

The greatest lesson we should have learned from the “stranger danger” era is that familiarity is not a guarantee of safety, just as unfamiliarity is not evidence of danger.

Children don’t need to be taught that the world is full of monsters hiding behind unfamiliar faces.

They need to know that dangerous behavior can come from anyone, that their boundaries matter, and that responsible adults will listen when they report something wrong.

And they need the confidence, judgment, and practical skills to navigate the world without living in constant fear of it.

Because teaching children to fear everyone isn’t preparing them for life.

It’s teaching them to be afraid of living.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
