Christmas Greetings and Christian Hostility

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 28, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

An atheist accepts a Christmas greeting. A Christian announces that goodwill can go up your ass. Our pal the Godless Gardener has found the season’s most revealing Christmas message.

December is upon us, and the seasonal grievance department is already open for business.

In a clip featured in the Godless Gardener’s “Happy Holidays Crashout,” a woman makes an announcement: she will be saying “Merry Christmas.” Nobody in the exchange presented has forbidden this. Nobody has confiscated her decorations or demanded that she renounce Jesus at the checkout. Nevertheless, she has prepared a public statement.

People offering “Happy holidays,” she explains, can “shove it up your ass.” Her explanation follows: “this is a Christ-filled home.”

The juxtaposition requires very little editorial assistance. She provides the religious credential and the contempt in the same breath.

Her declaration becomes even more revealing when she explains that she will answer a Hanukkah greeting with thanks and “Merry Christmas,” adding that this is about as diverse as she intends to get.

Reciprocal greetings can be perfectly warm. A Jewish person wishing a Christian a happy Hanukkah and receiving a merry Christmas in return need not be a diplomatic incident. People can share the celebrations that mean something to them. But this woman frames the exchange as the outer boundary of her willingness to acknowledge difference. She wants credit for approaching that boundary without crossing it.

She has turned an opportunity for kindness into a demonstration of how little accommodation she owes anyone else.

The Gardener’s sharpest response is also his simplest: “Happy holidays includes Christmas.”

It does. The greeting leaves room for her celebration and for somebody else’s. Her Christmas has not disappeared. It has acquired neighbors.

That is what makes this particular performance so useful. The inclusive greeting already accommodates her. She responds with hostility anyway. The apparent requirement is that the greeting single out her holiday before she will accept the goodwill behind it.

Christmas must be named. Other people can take a number.

A seasonal greeting becomes a small test of hierarchy: whose celebration receives explicit recognition, whose language sets the terms, and who must absorb the insult when those expectations go unmet. The woman announces her preferred answer before anyone has even addressed her.

There is agency here. She chooses the insult. She chooses to connect it to her Christianity. She chooses to make that connection public.

Treating that performance as an innocent misunderstanding would erase the most obvious fact about it: she understands that people are offering a greeting and has prepared a hostile response. The contempt is deliberate enough to warrant its own announcement.

This is the vitriolic Christianity worth exposing: the version in which declaring your devotion gives you permission to treat other people badly, and their objection becomes further evidence that your faith is under attack. Once that arrangement is accepted, accountability becomes almost impossible. The believer supplies the aggression and claims the wounded status.

The Gardener offers a much more ordinary account of living alongside other people. He is an atheist. When someone wishes him a merry Christmas, he thanks them. He can receive greetings associated with other religions without worrying that courtesy will alter his convictions.

His atheism survives the encounter. Presumably, a Christian faith capable of surviving centuries could endure a cashier using a plural noun.

The difference in the clip is practical. One person can recognize goodwill across a religious boundary. The other announces that goodwill arriving in the wrong words deserves abuse. We can judge those choices without pretending the entire religious identity of either speaker explains everything about them.

Nor do we need to establish who counts as a “real Christian” before holding a Christian accountable. That theological escape hatch is remarkably convenient: the religion receives credit for every act of generosity while its public aggressors are written out of the membership register.

She invokes Christ to explain herself. That makes the use she is making of Christianity a legitimate subject of criticism. Anyone who considers her conduct a betrayal of their faith can say so. Their disagreement does not undo her attempt to recruit that faith as an alibi.

One clip cannot count how many Christians behave this way. It can show the behavior clearly, and it gives us a precise question of responsibility: what happens when religious belonging is offered as an excuse for hostility?

The answer cannot be that everyone else must become more accommodating of the hostility.

Mutual tolerance depends on people accepting one another’s equal right to exist, celebrate, speak, and decline participation. It asks no one to profess a creed they reject. A Christian can say “Merry Christmas.” A Jewish person can say “Happy Hanukkah.” An atheist can enjoy the lights, exchange presents, or ignore the whole production.

Nobody acquires the right to make strangers affirm their religious precedence. And nobody owes an indulgent smile to someone who responds to a friendly greeting with an insult.

This matters because the demand so often travels in one direction. The person issuing the insult expects deference to her convictions; the person receiving it is expected to respect those convictions by swallowing the abuse. The greeting becomes an examination, and only the religious aggressor gets to grade it.

We should refuse that arrangement. Religious freedom protects her ability to practice her faith. Her attempt to dress contempt in sacred language remains open to criticism, ridicule, and rejection.

There is no obligation to romanticize the cruelty because it has a Christmas soundtrack.

The Gardener’s clip also exposes how unnecessary the performance is. She could celebrate Christmas. She could enjoy her home, her faith, and the people she loves. A stranger wishing her happiness need take up no more than a pleasant second of her day.

Instead, she has made room in the celebration for a preemptive insult and invited an audience to admire her resolve.

That is an impoverished way to approach a season supposedly devoted to generosity. Every unfamiliar greeting becomes another chance to demonstrate refusal. Every acknowledgment of someone else becomes a concession to be resisted.

Let her say “Merry Christmas.” Let her decorate every available surface and celebrate with complete religious conviction. The rest of us retain the same freedom to speak, celebrate, and belong.

And when someone tells you to shove your goodwill up your ass, take the statement seriously. She has explained how she intends to treat you. The reference to Christ does not soften it.

Happy holidays.

Christmas is included. Her permission is unnecessary
