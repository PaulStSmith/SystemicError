Startled Trump nominee gets torn apart by unexpected Senate ally

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 19, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A doctrine meets its consequencesThe confirmation machine briefly jamsRejection by voters, rescue by appointment

President Donald Trump’s judicial nominee James P. Sullivan expected help from a Republican Senate racing to confirm Trump’s judges. Instead, at a Wednesday Judiciary Committee hearing, he ran into Sen. John Kennedy of Louisiana, a loyal Trump ally who exposed how quickly Sullivan’s professed constitutional philosophy collapsed under a concrete question: If “originalism” makes the public meaning of the Constitution in 1791 controlling, why would punishments accepted then—such as cutting off an ear—be unconstitutional now?

Sullivan never gave Kennedy a coherent answer. He retreated to Supreme Court precedent, insisted that a district judge could not “grade” the justices’ work, and eventually suggested that the Court only “sometimes” follows originalism. Kennedy’s reply was blunt: “You’re talking in circles.”

A doctrine meets its consequences

Mediaite reported that Sullivan’s hearing had already gone badly when he struggled to recall the subject of the 22nd Amendment, which limits presidents to two elected terms. Kennedy then moved from constitutional trivia to the practical meaning of Sullivan’s judicial ideology.

Kennedy asked whether Sullivan would uphold a congressional statute authorizing the mutilation of people convicted of crimes, noting that cutting off ears and branding people were accepted punishments around the time the Eighth Amendment was adopted. Sullivan initially answered that, as a district judge, he would be bound by Supreme Court precedent.

That answer described the hierarchy of the federal courts but evaded Kennedy’s question. Sullivan had identified himself as an originalist. Kennedy wanted to know whether he would follow that doctrine to its stated conclusion when interpreting the prohibition against “cruel and unusual punishments.”

“Are you an originalist or not?” Kennedy demanded.

Sullivan said he was, while also arguing that originalism should be used to understand the judicial power established by Article III. Kennedy pressed again: If the constitutional meaning fixed in 1791 controls, what makes an ear-cutting statute unconstitutional?

Sullivan again cited Supreme Court precedent. When Kennedy asked whether that meant the Supreme Court itself had rejected originalism, Sullivan said it was not his place to “grade their homework.” He then conceded that the Court “sometimes” does not apply the approach he said he would use. Kennedy caught the contradiction immediately: “It sounds like you just did.”

The confirmation machine briefly jams

Kennedy’s questioning was notable because it came from inside Trump’s own political coalition. Republicans were moving quickly at the time to confirm as many Trump judicial nominees as possible before Democrats took control in January. Nearly one-third of active federal judges were already Trump appointees, according to the reporting.

That campaign made the hearing more than an academic exercise. A Senate majority was using its institutional power to give Trump’s legal movement durable control over federal courts. The officials receiving lifetime appointments would continue shaping constitutional rights long after the electoral coalition that installed them had lost power.

Kennedy’s motives are not established by the exchange. The AlterNet report itself allowed that the confrontation might have been substantive scrutiny or political theater. But the questioning still revealed something the usual confirmation choreography works to obscure: “originalism” is often presented as a neutral restraint on judges until its premises produce an indefensible result. Sullivan could not explain how his declared method treated historical meaning as binding while allowing modern precedent to prohibit punishments once considered acceptable.

The problem was not that a lower-court nominee recognized the authority of Supreme Court decisions. He was required to do so. The problem was that Sullivan marketed a sweeping interpretive creed, then treated precedent as an escape hatch when Kennedy forced him to account for its consequences.

Rejection by voters, rescue by appointment

Trump nominated Sullivan after Texas voters removed him from the Texas Supreme Court in the midterm elections, Mediaite reported. Losing an elected judicial seat did not end Sullivan’s path to power; a president and Senate majority could place him on the federal bench instead.

That sequence captures the institutional stakes more clearly than the hearing’s spectacle. Voters rejected Sullivan for one judgeship, but the federal appointment system offered political allies another route—this time toward a position insulated from future elections.

Kennedy momentarily interrupted that process by demanding an intelligible answer. Whether the Senate ultimately treated Sullivan’s performance as disqualifying was not established in the supplied reporting. What the hearing did establish was narrower and damning enough: when asked to reconcile originalism with the Eighth Amendment’s application to barbaric punishment, Trump’s nominee could repeat the doctrine or invoke precedent, but he could not make the two answers fit.
