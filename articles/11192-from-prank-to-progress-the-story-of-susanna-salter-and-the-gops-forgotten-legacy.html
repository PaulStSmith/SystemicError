From Prank to Progress: The Story of Susanna Salter and the GOP’s Forgotten Legacy

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 26, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

In 1887, Argonia, Kansas, witnessed one of the most bizarre yet consequential episodes in American political history. A group of men, intent on mocking the very idea of women in public life, placed Susanna M. Salter’s name on the mayoral ballot as a prank. What they expected to be a humiliating defeat turned into one of the great ironies of the late nineteenth century: Susanna Salter was elected the first female mayor in the United States. Her victory was not only symbolic, it revealed both the fragility and possibility of democratic progress in a country that, at that time, still broadly denied women political agency.

The "Prank"

Salter’s candidacy was not born from a campaign, a movement, or even her own ambition. On election morning, she reportedly had no idea her name was even on the ballot. It was a political dirty trick, a cynical attempt by local men to discredit the Women’s Christian Temperance Union (WCTU), with which Salter was loosely affiliated. The WCTU, while deeply problematic in its moral authoritarianism, was nonetheless one of the only vehicles through which women could organize politically. By nominating Salter, these men thought they would both humiliate the WCTU and remind the town that politics was a men’s domain.

Instead, they unleashed a backlash. The joke was too transparent, too mean-spirited, and too obviously a smear against a woman who had done nothing to invite it. When Republican Party leaders learned of the stunt, they rallied behind Salter, urging voters to support her not because of her platform—she had none—but because of the principle at stake. On election day, she won by a two-thirds majority, stunning her detractors.

Symbolism Without Suffrage

What makes this victory even more remarkable, and ironic, is that Salter herself represented a constituency denied its own voice. Kansas had just extended women’s suffrage in municipal elections in 1887, and while some women in Argonia could vote, the overwhelming majority of ballots cast for Salter came from men. In other words, the first woman mayor in the United States owed her position largely to male voters disgusted by the prank, rather than to a surge of women’s democratic empowerment. Salter’s victory was both a breakthrough and a reminder of how constrained that breakthrough was.

She did not campaign. She did not stump. She did not even ask to be on the ballot. And yet, by a quirk of circumstance and a backlash against mockery, she took office.

A Competent Mayor

Once elected, Susanna Salter proved herself capable and steady. Her one-year term was marked by administrative competence, fairness, and the complete absence of the kind of “disaster” her detractors predicted. That, in itself, was transformative: the spectacle of a woman in executive office without catastrophe unfolding was enough to shatter a popular prejudice. She didn’t revolutionize Argonia, nor did she ignite a new era of feminist governance. What she did was more subtle: she normalized the presence of a woman in authority.

When her year was up, she did not seek re-election. Instead, she returned to private life, raising her children and living into her nineties, long enough to see the ratification of the Nineteenth Amendment in 1920.

The Role of the Republican Party

One of the most striking aspects of this story, in hindsight, is the role played by the Republican Party. In the late 19th century, the GOP was still the party of Lincoln—the party that had opposed slavery, fought for the Union, and at least nominally embraced the project of Reconstruction and minority rights. Though by 1887 its commitment to racial justice had already withered in practice, it was still a party far more open to democratic experimentation than its Democratic rival. In Argonia, Republican leaders recognized that the prank against Salter was both mean-spirited and politically dangerous. They saw an opportunity to stand against pettiness and for fairness, and they took it.

It is a sobering contrast with today’s Republican Party, which has become a grotesque parody of its former self. The party that once supported expanding suffrage now wages war against voting rights. The party that once defended democracy against rebels and insurrectionists now embraces insurrectionists as its champions. The party that once saw in Susanna Salter an opportunity to defend dignity and inclusion is now the party of book bans, forced pregnancies, and fascist cosplay.

Lessons and Ironies

The election of Susanna Salter reminds us of two key truths. First, progress often arrives sideways—through accidents, ironies, and even pranks that backfire. The men of Argonia set out to humiliate women, and instead handed them a milestone. Second, victories that look symbolic can, in fact, matter deeply. Salter’s tenure as mayor did not dismantle patriarchy, but it undermined one of its most stubborn myths: that women were inherently unfit to govern.

And yet, we cannot romanticize too much. Salter’s association with the WCTU highlights the complexity of progress. The WCTU was not a radical organization for liberation—it was a reactionary moral crusade bent on enforcing temperance, and ultimately became one of the most powerful forces behind Prohibition. Salter’s political symbolism coexisted with her involvement in a movement that, by today’s standards, was paternalistic and repressive. That duality reflects the messiness of history.

But when stacked against the sneering contempt of the men who tried to weaponize her as a joke, her victory still shines. It is the story of prejudice defeated by irony, of misogyny collapsing under its own smugness.

A Far Cry from Today

Looking back, the fact that Republican leaders defended Susanna Salter against mockery shows how far the party has fallen. In 1887, they defended fairness, decency, and the idea that women could govern. In 2025, the GOP is a party that openly mocks women, strips them of reproductive autonomy, and clings to a vision of society where power is hoarded by white men. The irony is almost unbearable: the party that once protected a woman from ridicule now thrives on ridiculing women, minorities, and anyone outside its shrinking cultural fortress.

Susanna Salter’s election was a prank that backfired, but also a glimpse of what democracy could be when prejudice was rejected. Today, the greatest prank is being played on the American people themselves: a Republican Party that claims to defend freedom while dismantling it piece by piece.

Conclusion

In the dusty little town of Argonia, Kansas, in 1887, a group of men thought they would make a fool out of a woman by putting her on the ballot. Instead, they made fools of themselves. Susanna Salter became the first female mayor in the United States, not through her own ambition or a movement’s demand, but through the sheer collapse of a sexist stunt. Her victory is both a cautionary tale and a beacon: progress can come from the unlikeliest of places, but it must be defended fiercely once it arrives.

The story of Susanna Salter is not just about a prank gone wrong. It is about a fleeting moment when fairness outweighed cynicism, when even the Republican Party could recognize decency over mockery. It is a reminder that democracy, when allowed to function, has a way of humbling bigots and elevating those they seek to demean. And it is a warning about what happens when that commitment to democracy rots away, leaving behind only cruelty masquerading as politics.
