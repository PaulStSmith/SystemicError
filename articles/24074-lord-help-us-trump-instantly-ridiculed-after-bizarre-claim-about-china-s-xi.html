‘Lord help us’: Trump instantly ridiculed after bizarre claim about China's Xi

Based on an article by Erik De La Garza

Granite Is Not the Story. Presidential Power Is.

A Spectacle Without Substance

On September 24, 2026, President Donald Trump told reporters that Chinese President Xi Jinping had “great interest in granite” after showing him the White House helipad and ballroom construction. Xi, according to a foreign press pool reporter quoted in the coverage, did not respond or change his expression. The article then devoted most of its attention to ridicule posted on social media.

Power Was in the Room

Trump and Xi were not merely two men discussing stone. They occupied the highest offices of two powerful states. Trump controlled what the White House presented publicly about their meeting; Xi controlled whether to engage with that presentation. Trump chose to emphasize granite. Xi’s silence, as reported, establishes nothing beyond silence.

That distinction matters. The available reporting does not disclose what substantive issues the presidents discussed, what decisions they made, or whether the encounter produced any policy commitments. It therefore cannot support claims about diplomatic success, failure, or motive.

Mockery Replaced Scrutiny

The article’s framing is weak because it substitutes reaction posts for examination of presidential conduct. Former ambassador Michael McFaul questioned whether Xi’s supposed enthusiasm for granite was previously known. Other commentators called Trump a “buffoon,” invoked dementia, or mocked the remark’s relevance. None of that explains what the president did with the institutional power entrusted to him.

An unsupported diagnosis is not accountability. An insult is not analysis. The useful question is why the White House’s public account of a meeting with China’s president centered on construction materials—and what consequential subjects, if any, went unreported.

The President Set the Distraction

This was not confusion created by powerless bystanders. Trump introduced the subject, repeated it, and presented it as evidence of a “great meeting.” He had the platform and authority to identify concrete diplomatic outcomes. According to the article, he instead offered an anecdote about granite.

The press then amplified the triviality on his terms. By cataloguing online disbelief rather than establishing what occurred in the meeting, the coverage turned a failure of public explanation into a personality spectacle. Trump supplied the diversion; the article packaged the backlash.

Government by Content Cycle

The larger pattern is institutional evasion through spectacle. A president can replace an account of statecraft with an odd remark, while political media replace scrutiny with a roundup of jokes. The result is abundant content and almost no public record of consequence.

Granite is politically meaningful only because powerful institutions made it the center of attention. Trump used presidential visibility without providing substantive information about the meeting. The article noticed the absurdity but stopped before examining the power behind it. That is how accountability disappears: not always through secrecy, but through a flood of material too trivial to answer the questions that matter.
