'Hunh?' MS NOW host stumped by Kash Patel answer about Epstein sex trafficking

An answer given by FBI Director Kash Patel to a Republican senator about who Jeffrey Epstein was trafficking teens and young women to, was resurrected and dismantled on MS NOW on Saturday morning.

As part of a “The Weekend” segment on Donald Trump’s Department of Justice, fill-in host Molly Jong-Fast asked the the producers to run a clip from September where Patel was grilled by Sen. John Kennedy (R-LA) when the FBI director said there was no evidence of Epstein trafficking to others, which has since been disproved.

Specifically, Patel told the GOP lawmaker, “There is no credible information. None. If there were, I would bring the case yesterday that he trafficked to other individuals. And the information we have, again is limited.”

Turning to her guest, former U.S. Attorney Barbara McQuade, the baffled Jong-Fast blurted, “Barbara, hunh?”

“Explain to me,” she asked. “I mean, I haven’t seen everything, I haven’t seen the redacted stuff, but I certainly have seen a lot of stuff that that definitely does not say what Kash Patel said under oath.”

“Yes,” the legal analyst agreed. “You know, the word trafficked there seems to be doing a lot of work. It’s hard to know what he means by that. But one of the things that, as you I’m sure know, Molly, that lawyers are trained to listen for is what are the words people are using and what are the words they’re not using. It is often the case that members of Congress are not particularly adept at following up on testimony by asking, ‘What does he mean by that?’”

“Instead, what we saw there is they left alone the word ‘trafficked’ and sort of took it at face value,” she suggested. “Does that mean that other men were not involved, that other men did not abuse young girls? No. It’s a legal term of trafficking which, who knows what he meant by using that term. And so I think that answer is really meaningless because of the failure to probe exactly what he meant by using that verb.”

Our Analysis:

The Art of Evasion: A Closer Look at FBI Director Kash Patel’s Testimony

In a recent episode of MS NOW, a segment resurrected FBI Director Kash Patel’s past comments during a grilling by Sen. John Kennedy (R-LA) over the notorious Jeffrey Epstein case. The conversation, once buried, has resurfaced, bringing with it a cloud of controversy and disbelief. Director Patel’s assertion that there was “no credible information” to suggest Epstein trafficked teens and young women to others has not only been dissected but also dismantled.

Patel’s Statement: A Masterclass in Evasion

Let’s dive into the heart of the matter. Patel, when questioned, stated unequivocally that there was no evidence to support the idea that Epstein trafficked to “other individuals.” This claim, as we now know, is not just questionable; it’s been outright disproved. The assertion of having “no credible information” and the promise of swift action “if there were” comes across as nothing more than a carefully crafted shield, designed to deflect and defer.

The dance around the term “trafficked” is particularly egregious. As former U.S. Attorney Barbara McQuade insightfully pointed out, the focus on this term by Patel and the lack of probing by Congress highlights a significant failure. It’s a stark reminder of the power of language and the ways in which legal terminology can be manipulated to obscure truth.

The Role of Media and Analysis

The analysis provided by McQuade, and the incredulity expressed by fill-in host Molly Jong-Fast, underscores a critical aspect of this discourse: the need for vigilant media scrutiny and public accountability. Jong-Fast’s reaction — a simple “hunh?” — is emblematic of the broader public’s bafflement. It’s a single syllable that encapsulates the disbelief and skepticism that such statements rightly elicit.

McQuade’s commentary further sheds light on the nuances of legal speak and the importance of dissecting every word. The failure to question what Patel meant by “trafficked” reveals a broader issue within our political and legal systems — a reluctance, or perhaps a strategic decision, to not dig deeper when faced with potentially explosive revelations.

Conclusion: The Veil of Vagueness

What this episode reveals is the veil of vagueness that often surrounds legal and political discourse. This isn’t just about the misuse of terms or the evasion of accountability; it’s about the systemic failure to hold powerful individuals and entities to account. Patel’s testimony is a case study in how obfuscation and legal jargon are employed to sidestep truth.

The conversation between Jong-Fast and McQuade, although brief, is a crucial piece of the puzzle. It demonstrates the necessity of a media that questions, probes, and refuses to accept evasive answers at face value. In a world where misinformation can spread unchecked, these moments of clarity and insistence on truth are more valuable than ever.

In the end, what we’re witnessing is a battle for the truth — a battle that requires us to read between the lines, question the status quo, and demand more from those in positions of power. The Epstein case, with all its complexities and shadows, demands nothing less.
