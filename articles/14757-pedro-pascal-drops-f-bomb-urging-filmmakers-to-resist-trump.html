Pedro Pascal drops F-bomb urging filmmakers to resist Trump

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 18, 20254 min read

Chilean US actor Pedro Pascal arrives on September 3, 2022 for the screening of the film “Argentina, 1985” presented in the Venezia 79 competition during the 79th Venice International Film Festival at Lido di Venezia in Venice, Italy. (TIZIANA FABI/AFP

Chilean-American actor Pedro Pascal urged Hollywood to "f--k the people that try to make you scared" on Saturday while admitting it was "scary" to speak out against US President Donald Trump.

Asked about Trump's hardline immigration policies, the "The Last of Us" and "Narcos" star told reporters: "It's very scary for an actor participating in a movie to sort of speak to issues like this."

"I'm an immigrant. My parents are refugees from Chile. We fled a dictatorship, and I was privileged enough to grow up in the US after asylum in Denmark... I stand by those protections," the 50-year-old told a news conference in Cannes.

He stars in new film "Eddington" alongside Joaquin Phoenix, an intense and darkly satirical examination of America's toxic politics set in New Mexico during the Covid pandemic.

Directed by horror specialist Ari Aster, it premiered at the Cannes film festival on Friday.

Echoing a message from Robert De Niro on the opening night of Cannes, Pascal insisted that the film industry needed to find the courage to resist political pressure.

"So keep telling the stories, keep expressing yourself and keep fighting to be who you are," he said. "F--k the people that try to make you scared. And fight back.

"This is the perfect way to do so in telling stories. And don't let them win."

De Niro, who accepted a Cannes lifetime achievement award on Tuesday, urged the audience of A-list directors and actors to resist "America's philistine president".

Aster parodies everyone in his film from gun-loving southern US conservatives to virtue-signalling white anti-racism activists.

Emma Stone ("La La Land" and "Poor Things") plays Phoenix's wife who gets sucked into a world of paedophile-obsessed right-wing conspiracy theorists.

Aster admitted to worrying about America's direction and set out to dramatize it in his film, whose early satire gradually gives way to much darker material.

Asked Friday if America's polarised politics and the breakdown in trust in the media could be setting the country on a path to mass violence, he said, "That is certainly something I'm afraid of.

"It feels like nothing is being done to temper the furies right now," he added

Our Analysis:

Hollywood's Courageous Voices or Echo Chamber?

Pedro Pascal, known for his roles in The Last of Us and Narcos, recently made headlines with his bold statement against those who instill fear, specifically targeting US President Donald Trump's immigration policies. His comments, made during a press conference in Cannes, are emblematic of a larger trend within Hollywood: the industry's increasing willingness to engage in political discourse, often with a sharp critique of the current political climate. Yet, this raises the question: Is Hollywood genuinely pushing the envelope, or merely reinforcing its own echo chamber?

Pascal, a Chilean-American actor with a personal history intertwined with immigration and asylum, brings a unique and authentic perspective to the conversation. His statement, "f--k the people that try to make you scared," underscores a defiance against fear-mongering and political intimidation. However, this boldness begs a deeper analysis of effectiveness and impact.

The Role of Film in Political Discourse

The film Eddington, starring Pascal and Joaquin Phoenix, offers a "darkly satirical examination of America's toxic politics," with direction by Ari Aster. Its setting during the Covid pandemic and the inclusion of themes like gun-loving conservatives and virtue-signalling anti-racism activists indicate a willingness to tackle America's polarized landscape head-on. But one can't help but wonder: Who is listening?

Hollywood's critiques, while sharp and often valid, sometimes suffer from a preaching-to-the-choir syndrome. Aster's portrayal of both sides of the political spectrum could be a double-edged sword. On one hand, it demonstrates an effort to parody the extremes of American politics. On the other, it risks alienating audiences who may see these portrayals as caricatures rather than critiques, thus potentially reinforcing divisions rather than bridging them.

Echoing Echo Chambers

The film industry's call to "keep telling the stories, keep expressing yourself and keep fighting to be who you are" is noble, yet it's imperative to question the reach and reception of these messages. Are these calls to action resonating beyond the walls of Cannes and the silver screen? Or do they simply bounce around within the confines of an already sympathetic audience?

Robert De Niro's comments at Cannes, urging resistance against "America's philistine president," exemplify this conundrum. While rallying a room full of A-list directors and actors against a common adversary may feel empowering, it's crucial to ponder the effectiveness of these statements in enacting real change or swaying public opinion.

The Fear of Polarization and Violence

Aster's admission of fear regarding America's direction and the potential path to mass violence is particularly poignant. It reflects a broader anxiety about the country's deepening divides. However, the role of filmmakers and actors in this scenario is complicated. While they possess the platform to highlight these issues, the challenge lies in crafting narratives that not only critique but also contribute to solutions.

In Conclusion: A Call for Nuanced Narratives

Pedro Pascal's defiance and the thematic boldness of Eddington are commendable. They signify Hollywood's potential role as a catalyst for political and social discourse. However, the true test lies in the ability to foster dialogue, not division. Hollywood must strive for narratives that transcend the echo chamber, reaching those on the fringes of the conversation. Only then can the industry hope to influence the broader societal and political landscape in a meaningful way.
