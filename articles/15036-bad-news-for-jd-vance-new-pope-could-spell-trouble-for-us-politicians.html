That’s according to Commonweal editor-at-large Mollie Wilson O’Reilly, who argued Friday that Pope Leo’s American roots and fluency in English remove longtime language barriers between the Vatican and American elected officials. Unlike his predecessors, she told readers, the new pope is expected to speak directly to American Catholics.

“There will be no opportunity for anyone to soften his remarks with ‘What the Holy Father meant to say…’” Wilson O’Reilly wrote in an MSNBC op-ed. “This is certainly bad news for Catholics like Vice President J.D. Vance, who want to use church teaching to serve authoritarian ends.”

She added: “Perhaps the cardinals liked the idea of an American pope who can condemn human rights abuses and call for restitution and repentance in words that will not need translating.”

The columnist went on to tell readers Friday that the new, native English-speaking pope, “surely recognizes the need to be shrewd in dealing with American politics.”

“If and when he speaks directly to America, it will be because he has something important to say,” she concluded.

The only question left, according to O’Reilly, will be: “ Are we prepared to listen?”

Our Analysis:

The American Pope Phenomenon: A Politician's Conundrum

The recent op-ed published by Mollie Wilson O’Reilly in MSNBC brings to light an intriguing development in the Vatican that's poised to send ripples through the murky waters of U.S. politics. With the election of Pope Leo XIV, the first American pope, the stage is set for a potential upheaval for U.S. politicians, particularly those like Vice President JD Vance, who have tethered their political ideologies closely to Catholic teachings. This isn't just a change in the guard; it's a seismic shift in the way the Vatican communicates with its American flock and, by extension, the American political landscape.

Language Barrier: A Convenient Excuse No More

One of the most striking points O’Reilly makes is about the obliteration of the long-standing language barrier that has, for years, allowed American politicians to manipulate or, at the very least, selectively interpret Vatican statements to align with their political agendas. With Pope Leo XIV, an American fluent in English, there's no escaping the directness of his message. The usual "What the Holy Father meant to say…” excuse is off the table. This is a game-changer, especially for those who have been playing fast and loose with church teachings to justify authoritarian policies.

A Direct Line to American Catholics

Pope Leo XIV's American roots and linguistic capabilities mean he can speak directly to American Catholics without the need for translation or interpretation. This direct communication is a double-edged sword for politicians who have, until now, enjoyed a certain degree of interpretative leeway. The Pope's messages, unfiltered and clear, could serve to galvanize American Catholics in ways unforeseen, particularly on issues where the church's stance is at odds with the policies of these politicians.

Politics, Meet Your Match

The suggestion that Pope Leo XIV's election was favored by the cardinals for his ability to condemn human rights abuses and call for restitution and repentance in unmistakable terms is particularly telling. It suggests a Vatican that's not just looking to lead its flock in spiritual matters but also to wield its influence in shaping moral and ethical standards on a global stage, including the politically charged arena of U.S. politics.

The Shrewdness in Dealing with American Politics

O’Reilly's assertion that Pope Leo XIV “surely recognizes the need to be shrewd in dealing with American politics” hints at a nuanced approach the Vatican might adopt moving forward. This isn't about bludgeoning the U.S. political establishment with moral decrees; it's about strategically leveraging the Pope's unique position to influence discussions on human rights, social justice, and moral governance.

Are We Prepared to Listen?

The closing question of O’Reilly's op-ed is both a challenge and a reflection. It's not just about whether American politicians are ready to heed the Pope's words, but whether American society, in its entirety, is ready to engage with the Vatican's message in a manner that transcends political affiliations and ideological divides.

In summary, Pope Leo XIV's election represents a potential turning point in the relationship between the Vatican and U.S. politics. The directness with which the Pope can now communicate with American Catholics—and by extension, the American political class—poses a challenge to those who have used Catholicism as a cloak for their authoritarian inclinations. As this new chapter unfolds, it will be fascinating to see how politicians like JD Vance navigate the complex interplay of faith and politics in the shadow of a Vatican that's speaking their language, literally and figuratively.
