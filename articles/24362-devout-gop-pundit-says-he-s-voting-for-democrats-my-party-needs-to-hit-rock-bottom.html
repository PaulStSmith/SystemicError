Devout GOP pundit says he's voting for Democrats: My party needs to 'hit rock bottom'

Based on an article by Matthew Chapman

Paulo SantosOctober 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that conservative writer Matt K. Lewis plans to vote for Democrats across the ballot in the November 2026 midterms. Lewis, who previously used protest write-ins rather than support Democratic presidential nominees, argues that Republicans must “hit rock bottom” electorally before the party can reform.

In a Washington Sun column summarized by RawStory, Lewis cited the Iran war, immigration-enforcement violence, media restrictions, election denialism, and Republican dismissiveness toward concerns about AI and data centers. He described Trump’s second term as increasingly chaotic and corrupt and said nearly every elected Republican had enabled the president’s conduct.

Lewis does not claim Never Trump conservatives can decide the election or that Democratic control would remove Trump from office. His narrower case is that a decisive Republican defeat could constrain presidential power and make unconditional loyalty to Trump less politically attractive.

The power is institutional

Lewis’s ballot matters, but elected Republicans hold the power at issue. Members of Congress can investigate the executive branch, restrict funding, reject legislation, contest abusive policies, and impose political costs. According to his account, most have instead chosen accommodation.

That distinction matters because the story is not fundamentally about one pundit’s partisan conversion. It is about officials with constitutional authority declining to use it. Lewis can register dissent; Republican lawmakers have been positioned to turn dissent into institutional resistance.

Complicity is a decision

RawStory frames Lewis as finally reaching his breaking point. The more consequential breaking point never arrived for the officials he indicts. They repeatedly learned that loyalty was safer than independence and acted accordingly.

Lewis points to Republicans quietly distancing themselves from the Iran war and Immigration and Customs Enforcement tactics. Quiet distance, however, is not accountability. It lets lawmakers recognize political danger without accepting responsibility for empowering the administration that produced it.

The redemption narrative misdirects

The language of “rock bottom” turns a political organization into a troubled individual awaiting recovery. That metaphor softens agency. Parties do not stumble into authoritarian habits, election denialism, or institutional cowardice as though afflicted by an illness. Officeholders reward those practices, donors finance them, and voters can either ratify or punish them.

The framing also centers the salvation of the Republican Party rather than the people bearing the consequences of its governance. Immigration-enforcement violence, restrictions on the press, and war are not merely symptoms in a conservative rehabilitation story. They are exercises of state power with human costs.

Elections alter incentives, not character

Lewis’s strategic claim is credible on its own terms: politicians who lose because of obedience may discover independence. But an electoral rout would not automatically produce repentance, and he acknowledges that the party is unlikely to return quickly to its pre-Trump identity.

Defeat can change calculations without changing convictions. Republicans could reject Trump’s policies, blame tactical errors, intensify election denialism, or search for a more disciplined vehicle for the same politics. An opposition majority could impose checks, but it could not substitute for Republican officials choosing to abandon conduct they previously enabled.

The system rewards delayed courage

The larger pattern is a familiar one: influential conservatives tolerate escalating abuses while they can preserve power, then present belated opposition as rebellion once the political cost of loyalty begins to rise. Lewis is at least explicit about voting against the machinery he condemns. Many Republican officials, by his account, remain unwilling to take even that step publicly.

A party does not reach bottom at some objective point. It reaches the point at which defeat becomes more frightening to its officeholders than submission to its leader. Until incentives change, private discomfort and carefully hedged distance are not resistance. They are methods of surviving the consequences of one’s own political choices.
