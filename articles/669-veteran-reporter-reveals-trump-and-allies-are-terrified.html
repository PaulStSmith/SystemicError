Veteran reporter reveals: Trump and allies are terrified

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Trumpism Runs on Fear, Not Confusion

The Story in One Sentence

Zeteo’s Asawin Suebsaeng argues that the second Trump administration is not a repeat of the first but a more brazen, more punitive version of the same project: consolidate presidential power, shield allies, and intimidate enemies while everyone else is told to treat it as mere chaos.

Who Actually Holds Power

The central fact in this story is not that Trump is surrounded by unethical staff. It is that Donald Trump remains the source of direction. Suebsaeng says the administration’s actions are “a direct expression” of what Trump wants: punishment for enemies, expansion of power, and a right-wing authoritarian mission dressed up as governance.

That matters because Washington loves to disperse blame downward. Staffers get named, aides get blamed, and the president gets treated like a bystander to his own government. This framing is convenient for everyone who prefers corruption to have a human-resource department instead of a chain of command.

Misdirection as Political Cover

The soft lie in coverage like this is that scandal is the problem. It is not. Scandal is the smoke. The fire is intent.

The source makes clear that the second Trump White House is operating with a much greater tolerance for scandal than the first, but that is not a bug in the system. It is the system learning. Once institutions absorb enough misconduct without consequences, scandal stops being disqualifying and starts being permission.

That is why the usual language of “controversy” and “questions” is so dishonest here. Deliberate abuse is being laundered into administrative noise. The article’s real warning is that misdirection has become operational: if the public can be kept focused on personalities, it may miss the consolidation of power underneath.

Courts Slow It Down, They Do Not Clean It Up

The source notes three major court cases last week that showed Trump is weaker than his bravado suggests. That is a useful correction, but only a partial one. Courts can delay some pet projects. They do not reverse the political culture that normalizes them.

Suebsaeng’s point is the more important one: legal resistance can buy time, but it cannot substitute for political confrontation. A presidency bent on expansion can lose cases and still keep moving. It only needs enough institutional fatigue, enough partisan cowardice, and enough public exhaustion to keep pressing forward.

Fear Is the Operating System

The source also cuts through one of the more theatrical myths of modern Republican politics: the idea that these people are fearless insurgents. According to Suebsaeng, they are scared of jail, investigations, subpoenas, and what happens when power changes hands.

That is not a side note. It is the operating system.

Preemptive pardons, anxiety over accountability, and the impulse to keep consolidating power all point to the same thing: this is not just ideology, it is self-protection. The more they act like victims of persecution, the more obvious it becomes that they understand the stakes of their own conduct.

Democrats Are Not the Escape Hatch

The source also refuses a comforting liberal fantasy: that a future Democratic House and Senate automatically solves the problem. It does not. If Democrats retake power and then decide the proper response is “let’s just move forward,” they will be helping build the next layer of impunity.

That warning matters because accountability is not a slogan. It is a choice to treat abuse as abuse, and power as power. If Democrats are serious, they will have to do more than inherit the machinery and avoid using it.

The Larger Pattern

What this story reveals is not simply Trump’s appetite for domination. It is the recurring American habit of mistaking visible ugliness for the whole crime. The ugliness is real, but the deeper danger is institutional adaptation: courts that can only slow, parties that hesitate to punish, and a political press that still wants to describe coercion as turbulence.

That is how authoritarian politics advances in plain sight. Not by hiding its aims, but by exhausting the system’s willingness to name them.
