New Trump appeal to Supreme Court banks on idea 'justices don't mind being played': expert

President Donald Trump's administration appealed to the U.S. Supreme Court, requesting that the panel expedite a decision on an emergency application regarding foreign aid funds mandated by Congress.

Writing for his Substack, Georgetown Law School Professor Steve Vladeck said that this is the 23rd emergency request that Trump sent to the High Court in seven months. Vladeck wrote that the Trump team "seems to be structuring at least some of its litigation decisions specifically to take advantage of its expectation that it can receive emergency relief from the Supreme Court."

In this case, he said that the Solicitor General is orchestrating the emergency to justify justices stepping in. Such procedural manipulation is all designed to trick the Court into getting involved, the legal scholar noted.

The administration is "downplaying the fact that the government forfeited the substantive claim on which it claims it is likely to succeed on the merits—by not properly raising it below; and misrepresenting what happened in the lower courts by conveniently leaving out any details that might draw the justices’ (or their clerks’) attention to those first two points."

Vladeck said on BlueSky that this is an example of the Trump administration "apparently banking on the view that the justices don't mind being played."

The issue arose after the Trump administration decided to shut down USAID and stopped delivering U.S.-grown food to those in famine-stricken areas of the world. Refusing to allocate the funds raises both statutory and constitutional challenges (including claims of unlawful "impoundment" of funds), said Vladeck.

A split decision from the D.C. Circuit appellate panel sided with the administration, but critics challenge the case, arguing that the decision was based on issues the government had itself failed to raise on time—violating norms against “sandbagging” in federal litigation.

There's an injunction in place, which puts everything on hold until the entire court process has been completed and formal decisions have been issued.

Vladeck closes by asking if granting the Trump administration's demand, given its questionable grounds, is the Supreme Court being manipulated or supporting the larger Trump agenda of eliminating significant portions of the federal government, even if it affects constitutional norms.

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Strategic Manipulations of the Trump Administration

The Trump administration's latest maneuver—an appeal to the U.S. Supreme Court to expedite a decision on foreign aid funds—is a glaring example of its tactical, albeit controversial, approach to governance and judiciary matters. This move, as dissected by Georgetown Law School Professor Steve Vladeck, is not just another routine legal battle; it's a calculated attempt to exploit the Supreme Court's emergency relief mechanism for political gains. Let's delve into the tactics, implications, and consequences of this strategy.

The Art of Legal Manipulation

The Trump team's approach, sending a whopping 23rd emergency request to the High Court within seven months, is not merely aggressive; it's unprecedented in its audacity. Vladeck's analysis points out a disturbing pattern: the administration is "structuring at least some of its litigation decisions" to secure emergency relief, hoping the Supreme Court will be their de facto legal safety net. This isn't just playing the system; it's attempting to rewrite the rules of engagement with the judiciary.

Orchestrating Emergencies

The Solicitor General's role in creating these so-called emergencies is particularly troubling. By invoking the need for the justices to step in under contrived urgencies, the administration is essentially tricking the Court into involvement. This strategy involves downplaying significant missteps (like forfeiting the substantive claim by not raising it properly in lower courts) and misrepresenting the proceedings of those courts. It's a double whammy of obscuring facts and banking on the justices' (or their clerks') potential oversight or indifference.

The Larger Agenda

The implications of these tactics go beyond mere legal victories or defeats. At its core, this is about the Trump administration's broader agenda to reshape the federal government's role, particularly in foreign aid and humanitarian efforts. The decision to shut down USAID and halt U.S.-grown food deliveries to famine-stricken areas underlines a stark deviation from America's long-standing global humanitarian commitments. Such actions not only raise statutory and constitutional challenges but also question the moral compass guiding these decisions.

The Supreme Court's Role

This brings us to the million-dollar question: Is the Supreme Court being manipulated, or is it complicit in supporting the Trump administration's objectives? Vladeck's concerns about the justices "apparently banking on the view that the justices don't mind being played" illuminate a dangerous possibility—the erosion of judicial independence and integrity. If the High Court is seen as a political tool rather than an impartial arbiter of justice, the consequences could be far-reaching and irreversible.

Conclusion: A Dangerous Precedent

The Trump administration's exploitation of the judiciary's emergency mechanisms sets a dangerous precedent. It's a clear illustration of how legal systems can be manipulated for political ends, potentially undermining the judiciary's role in checking executive power. As the Supreme Court considers this emergency application, the decision will not just impact foreign aid funding; it will signal the Court's stance on being used as a political lever. The integrity of the judiciary, and its role in the balance of power, hangs in the balance.

In conclusion, this episode is a stark reminder of the ongoing battles within the U.S. political and legal landscapes. It's not just about the legal merits of a case; it's about how those merits are presented, manipulated, and ultimately, how they reflect the broader political strategies at play. The Trump administration's tactics, as Vladeck rightly points out, warrant a critical examination of not just the legal system's vulnerabilities but also of the political motives driving these legal battles.
