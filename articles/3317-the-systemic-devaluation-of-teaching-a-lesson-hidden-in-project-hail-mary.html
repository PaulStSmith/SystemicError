The Systemic Devaluation of Teaching — A Lesson Hidden in Project Hail Mary

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 5, 20265 min read

There’s a particular kind of critique that reveals more about the critic than the work itself. It doesn’t just misread a story—it exposes a hierarchy of values so deeply internalized that it feels invisible to the person expressing it.

A recent complaint about Project Hail Mary’s ending—that Dr. Ryland Grace becoming a teacher again is “unsatisfying” or “reductive”—is exactly that kind of critique.

On the surface, it’s framed as a narrative preference. In reality, it’s something else entirely: a quiet, reflexive devaluation of teaching as a meaningful human pursuit.

And that is not a cinematic issue.

That is a systemic one.

The Misreading That Gives the Game Away

The argument goes like this: Grace spends the story rediscovering his identity, rising to the occasion, saving two civilizations—and the conclusion is that he ends up… teaching again. Therefore, the ending diminishes him.

That conclusion only makes sense if you begin from a flawed premise:

Teaching is what you do when you fail at something more important.

That premise is not stated outright, of course. It rarely is. It hides behind words like “unsatisfying,” “anticlimactic,” or “missed opportunity.” But strip away the euphemisms, and the logic is unmistakable.

Grace saved the world. Therefore, he should graduate into something more significant than teaching.

But here’s the problem: the story never agreed with that hierarchy.

Grace Was Always a Teacher

The critique collapses because it misidentifies who Grace is.

He is not a scientist who fell into teaching as a consolation prize. He is a teacher who understands science deeply and communicates it instinctively. The novel—and the story’s entire structure—reinforces this repeatedly.

Every discovery Grace makes follows the same pattern:

  • observe

  • hypothesize

  • test

  • explain

Not just to others—but to himself.

He narrates like an educator constructing a lesson in real time. Even in isolation, even with amnesia, he teaches.

That is not incidental characterization. That is identity.

And crucially, it is that identity—not raw intelligence, not institutional status—that allows him to collaborate with Rocky and ultimately solve the astrophage crisis.

The thing that saves two worlds is not prestige.

It’s pedagogy.

The Hierarchy We Pretend Doesn’t Exist

So why does the ending bother some people?

Because it violates a deeply ingrained cultural ranking of professions.

We don’t like to admit it, but we organize work into tiers:

  1. Elite, high-status roles (scientists, CEOs, innovators)

  2. Functional but secondary roles

  3. “Fallback” roles—teaching often sits here in public perception

Within that framework, the story appears to “downgrade” Grace.

He goes from saving the world—clearly Tier 1—to teaching children—perceived as Tier 3.

Except that ranking is artificial.

And Project Hail Mary quietly dismantles it.

Teaching as Infrastructure, Not Afterthought

The novel makes a point that the critique completely misses: teaching is not an endpoint separate from achievement. It is the mechanism that makes achievement possible.

Grace doesn’t succeed despite being a teacher.

He succeeds because he is one.

His ability to:

  • break down complex systems

  • communicate across radically different contexts

  • build shared understanding with an alien intelligence

is fundamentally pedagogical.

Strip that away, and there is no solution.

The ending doesn’t reduce him to teaching.

It reveals that teaching was the core competency all along.

The Ending Is Not Sentimental — It’s Structural

Another common complaint is that the final scene—Grace teaching Eridian children—feels like a sentimental “full circle.”

But that interpretation only works if you ignore everything required to make that scene possible.

By the time Grace is living on Erid:

  • decades have passed

  • Earth has received his data, decoded it, and deployed a solution

  • the Sun has begun recovering

  • the light of that recovery has crossed interstellar space back to Erid

This is not a quick epilogue. It is the long-term consequence of his choices.

And his continued survival is not trivial. It requires:

  • engineered environments

  • artificial atmosphere and pressure

  • food systems compatible with human biology

In other words, the final image is not symbolic fluff.

It is the visible result of sustained, collaborative, interspecies effort.

Grace is not “back where he started.”

He is where his identity leads when stripped of institutional constraints and placed in its purest form.

The Discomfort With Meaning That Isn’t Spectacular

There’s another layer to the critique, and it’s less flattering.

Modern storytelling—and modern culture more broadly—has an obsession with visible, exceptional achievement. The kind that can be summarized in headlines:

  • Saved the world

  • Discovered X

  • Built Y

Teaching doesn’t fit that mold.

It is slow, repetitive, and largely invisible in its impact. It doesn’t produce singular moments of glory. It produces generations of understanding.

That makes it easy to undervalue.

But Project Hail Mary does something quietly subversive: it places teaching at the center of a story about survival at planetary scale.

And then, at the end, it refuses to abandon that premise.

What the Critique Reveals

The complaint about the ending is not really about pacing or narrative structure. It is a reaction to a violation of expectations.

The expectation is that extraordinary acts must lead to extraordinary status.

The story says otherwise.

It says that the same skill set that saves civilizations is the one used in a classroom.

It says that meaning does not require escalation.

It says that purpose is not something you “graduate” from once you achieve something greater.

And that makes people uncomfortable.

Because it implies something inconvenient:

The work we treat as ordinary may be the most consequential work there is.

The Systemic Error

This is where the discussion stops being about fiction.

The instinct to see teaching as a lesser outcome is not an isolated opinion. It is reinforced by systems that:

  • underpay educators

  • strip resources from schools

  • treat teaching as expendable labor rather than foundational infrastructure

We celebrate breakthroughs but neglect the environments that make them possible.

We glorify innovation while quietly degrading the process that creates innovators.

And then, when a story refuses to follow that script—when it insists that teaching is not a downgrade but a continuation—we call it unsatisfying.

That’s not a storytelling problem.

That’s a values problem.

Conclusion: The Ending Was the Point

Remove the final scene, and you don’t get a more elegant ending.

You get one that quietly aligns with a broken hierarchy:

That saving the world is meaningful.
That teaching is not.

Project Hail Mary rejects that.

It shows a man who saves two civilizations not by transcending who he is, but by fully embodying it.

And when the crisis ends, he doesn’t become something greater.

He remains exactly what made the victory possible.

A teacher.

Sometimes the most radical thing a story can do is refuse to pretend that meaning requires spectacle.
