SYSTEMIC ERROR / COMMENTARY

Jack Smith waving 'proverbial finger' at MAGA could spur 'severe consequences': analyst

Based on an article by Matthew Chapman

Paulo SantosOctober 1, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that former special counsel Jack Smith told a Senate panel on Tuesday that his investigation had developed what he described as proof beyond a reasonable doubt that President Donald Trump committed serious crimes against the United States. Smith said he would make the same prosecutorial decision regardless of a former president’s party. His statement was not a conviction, and the article does not report any new prosecution or legal penalty.

The “severe consequences” in the headline refer to Salon writer Troy Farah’s warning about consequences for Americans if Trump and his allies succeed in recasting investigations of Trump as partisan persecution while Trump’s Justice Department pursues investigations Farah characterized as politically motivated. Farah argued that Smith’s public refusal to accept the MAGA narrative matters as the midterms approach and Trump continues falsely claiming he won the 2020 election.

The hearing also exposed the quality of the Republican counterattack. Senator Eric Schmitt tried to use Smith’s attendance at a basketball game as evidence of illicit coordination with Fulton County prosecutor Fani Willis. According to RawStory, Schmitt cited an Atlanta Hawks game, but Smith had actually attended an Iowa Hawkeyes away game in Maryland.

Power is not evenly distributed

Smith was defending a completed prosecutorial judgment before senators. Trump, by contrast, holds the presidency and controls the executive branch. Republican senators possess subpoena power, a national platform, and the institutional means to turn insinuation into an official spectacle.

That imbalance is the story. Smith can explain what his investigation concluded. Trump and his congressional allies can use state power and legislative theater to discredit the investigation, pressure its participants, and establish retaliation as ordinary governance. Treating this as a feud between equally situated political camps obscures who can actually impose consequences.

The accusation replaces the evidence

Schmitt’s basketball-game gambit was not merely an embarrassing factual error. It showed how the hearing’s attack on Smith operated: begin with the desired allegation, then search for a detail that can be made to resemble support.

The mistake matters because insinuations launched from a Senate dais acquire authority before they acquire evidence. Even after collapse, they can circulate as fragments of a persecution narrative. The correction rarely travels with the accusation, and the official setting lends flimsy material an institutional costume.

Victimhood as governing strategy

Republicans described Trump’s criminal cases as political “witch hunts,” according to RawStory. That framing shifts attention away from the conduct investigators examined and toward Trump’s claimed victimization. It also places prosecutors in a rigged position: defending their work becomes further “proof” that they are partisan actors.

Farah’s argument is strongest here. A president can benefit politically from portraying scrutiny of himself as illegitimate while his own administration directs investigations at adversaries. RawStory presents that contrast sharply, though its broad characterization of those investigations as politically motivated is asserted rather than demonstrated through specific cases in this article.

Accountability is not a taunt

Calling Smith’s testimony a “proverbial finger” to MAGA may be satisfying copy, but it shrinks an institutional conflict into attitude and spectacle. Smith’s relevant act was not defiance for its own sake. It was his willingness to state publicly what his investigation concluded and to defend the standard he says he applied.

The sharper question is whether evidence can survive a political machine organized to make every adverse finding look corrupt. Smith’s declaration carries rhetorical force, but it does not itself create legal accountability. The article’s language of “consequences” risks blurring reputational embarrassment, electoral narrative, and actual institutional sanction.

The system rewards the smear

This episode reveals a durable political method: officeholders use institutional authority to manufacture doubt about institutional findings, then cite that doubt as evidence that the findings lack legitimacy. A failed ambush is still useful if it feeds the larger story that investigators, prosecutors, and political opponents belong to one illicit conspiracy.

The immediate farce was a senator confusing basketball games. The systemic error is more serious: powerful officials can make reckless allegations at low cost while the people they target must answer each one with precision. When fabrication is cheap and correction is laborious, the political advantage belongs to those willing to poison the record faster than anyone can repair it.
