Inside the far-right's secret for winning over working class Americans

The Left Didn’t Lose the Working Class. It Helped Deliver It

The Convenient Myth

The source article makes one useful point and one devastating one. The useful point is that “blue-collar” jobs declined sharply while working-class self-identification did not. The devastating point is that social democratic parties mistook labor-market change for the disappearance of class, then used that mistake to justify governing for markets instead of for workers.

That was never a neutral error. It was a political choice with winners and losers.

Who Had Power

The actors with actual institutional power were not the workers described in the article. It was the party leaderships, governments, employers, and lawmaking institutions that shaped wages, unions, welfare, and economic policy. Labor and social democratic parties had the ballot line, the ministries, and the legitimacy of speaking for labor. They used that position to adopt neoliberal policy while keeping the branding of solidarity.

That is the central fraud here: parties born from class struggle acting as managers of class defeat.

The Decision Was Not Accidental

The article is clear that the leaderships of these parties embraced “market-based neoliberal policies” and reassured themselves that their base would not leave. That was not confusion. It was a bet that class loyalty could be replaced by identity, lifestyle, and managerial competence.

They lost because they stopped offering material defense. Workers were left with higher inequality, weaker unions, and parties that no longer spoke their language. The far right did not create the grievance from nothing. It walked through a door opened by centrist abandonment.

What the Far Right Exploits

The article shows far-right parties gaining ground across Australia, Britain, Germany, the United States, and France. That pattern is not mysterious. Once social democratic parties normalized market rule, they ceded the language of conflict. The right moved in with a simpler lie: blame outsiders, blame institutions, blame cultural elites, but never blame the owners.

That is why the misdirection matters. The problem is often framed as working-class irrationality or democratic volatility. But the deeper story is elite misrule producing political openings for authoritarian nationalism.

The Union Story Is Also A Power Story

The article notes that union decline was driven not by workers rejecting unions, but by hostile employers, governments, and laws. That distinction matters because it identifies agency correctly. Unions did not wither in some weather system of social change. They were squeezed.

And when workers were left exposed, parties that claimed to represent them offered thin language and no durable protection. That is not an accident of communication. It is the predictable result of weakening collective power while pretending class no longer matters.

Class Never Left

The article’s strongest evidence is that working-class identity remained stable even as blue-collar employment fell. People did not stop seeing themselves as working class because clerical, service, or platform work arrived. They stopped being offered institutions willing to defend them.

That is the political pattern the story reveals: when parties abandon labor and dress up capitulation as modernization, class does not disappear. It returns in uglier form, routed through resentment, scapegoating, and reactionary insurgency. The far right thrives not because class is dead, but because too many establishment parties spent years helping bury it.
