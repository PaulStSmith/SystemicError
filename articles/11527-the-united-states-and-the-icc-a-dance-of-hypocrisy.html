The United States and the ICC: A Dance of Hypocrisy

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 18, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

When the International Criminal Court (ICC) issued an arrest warrant for Vladimir Putin in March 2023, headlines erupted worldwide. The charges — war crimes, specifically the unlawful deportation and transfer of Ukrainian children from occupied territories to Russia — were stark, undeniable, and damning. For many, this moment represented a triumph of international law over the brazen impunity of autocrats. Yet within the United States, the warrant exposed a gaping wound of hypocrisy. Washington applauds the ICC when it targets America’s adversaries but refuses, steadfastly and across administrations, to bind itself to the same standard of justice.

This is not a partisan failing. It is a bipartisan doctrine — one rooted in America’s refusal to accept international accountability for its own leaders, generals, and policies. The question is not whether Democrats and Republicans differ on the ICC — they do, and sharply — but whether either party has the courage to face the one thing the United States fears most: the mirror of its own conduct.

The History of American Evasion

The ICC was born in 1998 with the adoption of the Rome Statute, intended to be the world’s first permanent court capable of prosecuting individuals for genocide, war crimes, crimes against humanity, and eventually the crime of aggression. It represented a hard-earned lesson of the 20th century: atrocities thrive where impunity reigns.

Bill Clinton signed the Rome Statute in 2000, but he never sent it to the Senate for ratification. His reasoning was simple: while he supported the principle, he feared that the court could be used against U.S. personnel abroad. George W. Bush went further, taking the unprecedented step of “unsigning” the treaty. His administration not only withdrew but also passed the so-called “Hague Invasion Act,” giving the president authority to use military force to free any American held by the ICC. Barack Obama inched back toward engagement, cooperating with the court in specific cases such as Darfur and Libya, but still refused to ratify. Donald Trump didn’t just oppose the ICC; he launched a direct assault, imposing sanctions on ICC officials investigating alleged U.S. war crimes in Afghanistan. Joe Biden lifted those sanctions, restored diplomatic respect, and cheered the ICC warrant for Putin. But he, too, has refused to submit the Rome Statute to ratification.

This is the circle that no administration, red or blue, has squared. America demands accountability for others while exempting itself.

The Republican Approach: Sabotage

Republicans have made their position explicit: the ICC is illegitimate. From Bush’s unsigning to Trump’s sanctions, the GOP has not just resisted accountability; it has actively tried to cripple the institution itself. The Republican stance is one of open hostility, where international law is treated as a threat to American sovereignty rather than a safeguard of human dignity.

This hostility is not theoretical. Under Trump, the U.S. State Department revoked visas of ICC officials. Sanctions targeted the very judges and prosecutors tasked with investigating war crimes. Imagine the outcry if Russia or China had tried to financially strangle an international court to protect its generals — yet when the United States does it, it is framed as national defense.

Republicans see the ICC not as a flawed institution that could be shaped, but as an enemy to be destroyed. Their solution is brute force: discredit it, defund it, and, if necessary, legislate military options to ensure it never touches an American citizen.

The Democratic Approach: Selective Engagement

Democrats, by contrast, play a subtler game. They do not seek to dismantle the ICC. They are willing to cooperate when the court’s actions align with American interests. Obama assisted in transferring suspects. Biden praised the indictment of Putin. This selective engagement allows Democrats to posture as defenders of international law — but only so long as that law does not turn its gaze on the Pentagon or the Oval Office.

The difference in approach is important. Democrats legitimize the ICC as a tool of U.S. foreign policy, while Republicans delegitimize it outright. Yet the end result is the same: America remains beyond its jurisdiction.

This hypocrisy is not abstract. If the U.S. ratified the Rome Statute, former leaders could face trial: Bush for the invasion of Iraq, Obama for drone strikes that massacred civilians, Trump for enabling Saudi atrocities in Yemen, and Biden for supplying arms used in Gaza. Both parties understand this reality. Neither is willing to accept it.

The Cost of Double Standards

The refusal to join the ICC does not merely shield U.S. leaders; it undermines the very legitimacy of international law. When Washington cheers the arrest warrant for Putin, it rings hollow precisely because everyone knows the U.S. would never submit to the same. This double standard corrodes America’s moral authority. It tells the world that international justice is not universal but conditional, applied only to adversaries.

The damage is not theoretical. Countries in Africa and Asia, long subject to ICC investigations, have pointed to U.S. hypocrisy as proof that the court is a tool of Western power, not a neutral arbiter of law. America’s refusal to ratify the Rome Statute fuels the narrative that the ICC is designed to punish weak states while powerful ones exempt themselves.

Trump and Putin: The Red Carpet of Impunity

Enter Donald Trump, the living embodiment of America’s rot on this issue. For Trump, the ICC’s warrant against Putin is not a deterrent but an opportunity. He can offer the Russian dictator something no other Western leader can: a stage in the United States, safe from arrest. Why? Because America, by refusing to join the ICC, has deliberately made itself a sanctuary for the world’s indicted war criminals — provided they are politically useful.

Trump’s willingness to roll out the red carpet for Putin is not an aberration. It is the logical consequence of decades of bipartisan cowardice. When you refuse to hold yourself accountable, you create the conditions to excuse anyone else.

The Path Not Taken

It did not have to be this way. America could have embraced the ICC as a step toward a world where no leader is above the law. It could have ratified the Rome Statute with reservations to protect due process concerns, while still binding itself to a system that prosecutes the gravest crimes. Instead, it chose self-exemption.

The result is a global order where Washington lectures others about justice but exempts itself, where international law is a weapon of convenience rather than a shared covenant. Until the U.S. joins the ICC, every word of condemnation it utters about Putin will carry the stench of hypocrisy.

Conclusion: A Nation Afraid of the Mirror

The ICC’s warrant against Putin is a historic moment. But it is also a mirror held up to the United States. Republicans, with their sabotage, and Democrats, with their selective engagement, both recoil from what they see. Because to join the ICC is to admit that American presidents, too, can be criminals. It is to admit that the rules we demand of others apply equally to us.

That is the circle America has never squared. And until it does, every condemnation of Putin is tainted, every appeal to international law is compromised, and every proclamation of moral leadership is hollow. The true indictment is not just of Putin, but of a United States that fears the very justice it claims to champion.
