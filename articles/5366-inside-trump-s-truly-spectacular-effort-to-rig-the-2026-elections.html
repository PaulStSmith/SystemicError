Inside Trump's 'truly spectacular' effort to rig the 2026 elections

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 14, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:Trump’s Alleged Election Meddling Machinations: A Closer Look

U.S. President Donald Trump speaks to the press about deploying federal law enforcement agents in Washington to bolster the local police presence, in the Press Briefing Room at the White House, in Washington D.C., U.S., August 11, 2025. REUTERS/Annabelle Gordon

A pair of experts warned this week that President Donald Trump is clearly telegraphing his intention to meddle in the 2026 midterm elections.

Stephen Richer, former recorder of Maricopa County, Arizona, said during an interview with The Atlantic published Wednesday that he’s grown worried that “something truly spectacular is going to happen in which our 2026 midterm elections are not administered like past elections have been.”

When asked to flesh out how Trump could potentially rig the upcoming elections, Richer said it was unlikely that he would deploy US Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) agents to polling places across the country, if for no other reason than he lacks the manpower to accomplish such an operation.

However, Richer did express concern about the president’s ability to muddy waters in tight races and put pressure on his Republican allies to refuse to seat Democratic winners when he is claiming there are disputes about the results.

“Where I think President Trump is most potent is still in the post-election procedures,” he explained, “still in sowing doubt in the minds of enough Americans that they don’t think the elections are legitimate and, therefore... the Congress doesn’t have to seat its new members. That’s certainly a popular theory that’s floating about: that Speaker Mike Johnson (R-La.), the outgoing speaker, will choose not to seat the new members, because they’re in allegedly disputed elections.”

Richer argued that California could be particularly vulnerable to this, since the state infamously takes so long to finish tallying its votes.

In a New York Times editorial published Thursday, Sean Morales-Doyle, director of the Brennan Center for Justice’s voting rights and elections program, argued that Trump’s “campaign to rig our elections is well underway,” and he pointed to the president’s mass pardon last year of rioters who violently stormed the US Capitol on January 6, 2021 as the beginning of his election subversion campaign.

“We have every reason to expect more actions like these in the coming months,” wrote Morales-Doyle. “A few weeks ago, Mr. Trump reiterated his threats to prosecute election officials who ran the 2020 election. Just days later, FBI agents seized ballots and election records from 2020 in Fulton County, Georgia.”

However, Morales-Doyle also said there was reason to believe that the American system can withstand the president’s assault on its election integrity, and he gave a nod toward several efforts across the country to fight back, including states resisting Trump’s demands to hand over their voter rolls and Democrats refusing to let new voter suppression legislation pass through Congress.

“We are already seeing how effective people can be in pushing back,” he concluded, “whether on the streets of Minneapolis or at town halls hosted by their representatives in Congress. It will be incumbent on all of us—election officials, advocates, state law enforcement, and voters—to see the administration’s efforts for what they are and to fight back.”

Our Analysis:

Trump’s Alleged Election Meddling Machinations: A Closer Look

In a stark warning that reads more like a plot out of a dystopian novel rather than the expected proceedings of a democratic nation, experts have sounded the alarm on former President Donald Trump’s purported intentions to interfere with the 2026 midterm elections. The allegations, as discussed in recent interviews and editorials, paint a picture of a democracy teetering on the brink, with Trump at the center, orchestrating chaos.

Stephen Richer, a former recorder from Maricopa County, Arizona, expressed his apprehensions to The Atlantic, suggesting that we could witness an election unlike any before. The fear isn’t rooted in the fantastical deployment of ICE agents at polling stations—a scenario Richer dismisses due to logistical impracticality—but in a more insidious form of manipulation: casting doubt.

The Power of Doubt: Trump’s real threat, according to Richer, lies in his ability to erode trust in the electoral process. By challenging the legitimacy of election results and pressuring Republican allies to obstruct the seating of Democratic victors, Trump could wield significant influence without direct intervention. This strategy of sowing discord post-election is especially concerning given the outgoing Speaker Mike Johnson’s purported willingness to entertain such disputes.

California’s Vulnerability: The spotlight on California underscores a broader issue—the weaponization of procedural delays. The state’s protracted vote counting becomes a double-edged sword, potentially serving as fodder for those wishing to question the election’s integrity.

Sean Morales-Doyle of the Brennan Center for Justice echoes these concerns in a New York Times editorial, framing Trump’s election-related maneuvers, including mass pardons for Capitol rioters, as the beginning of an extensive campaign to subvert the democratic process. Morales-Doyle warns of Trump’s continued threats against election officials and the ominous seizure of ballots in Fulton County, Georgia, by FBI agents.

However, Morales-Doyle also offers a glimmer of hope, highlighting resistance efforts across the nation. From state officials refusing to acquiesce to Trump’s demands for voter rolls to Democrats blocking voter suppression laws, there is evidence of a robust defense being mounted against these potential threats.

The Bottom Line: The narrative that unfolds from these assertions is not merely one of political intrigue but a chilling forecast of the potential breakdown of democratic norms. It’s a story of a former president allegedly leveraging the machinery of doubt and procedural manipulation to challenge the very foundation of electoral legitimacy.

Yet, amidst the dire warnings, there is an underlying message of resilience. Morales-Doyle’s call to arms, urging election officials, advocates, law enforcement, and voters to recognize and combat these efforts, is a testament to the strength of democratic institutions and the collective will of the people.

In sum, while the specter of election meddling looms large, the response from various quarters of American society suggests a readiness to confront and counter these challenges head-on. Democracy, after all, thrives not just through the integrity of its elections but through the vigilance of its guardians.
