'God is angry': Internet bemused as Mother Nature comes for Trump's Mount Rushmore speech

Based on an article by Daniel Hampton

Paulo SantosJuly 5, 20262 min read

Mount Rushmore Meets the Weather, and the Neglect Shows

Stagecraft First

The source is simple enough: Trump flew to South Dakota for a July 4 and America-250 stop at Mount Rushmore, then a severe thunderstorm warning forced the event into chaos. Fox News had to interrupt its own coverage and tell viewers to get inside. The internet did what it always does when Trump’s pageantry gets embarrassed by reality: it mocked him.

Power Was Not in the Sky

The storm is the headline gimmick, not the political story. The actual power sits with the president, the agencies he starves, and the media apparatus that turns every Trump appearance into a live-production event. Weather did not choose the setting. Trump did. Fox amplified it. And the federal government is the one that controls the budgets and institutions attached to a place like Mount Rushmore.

The Budget Story Hiding in Plain Sight

Dan Koh’s jab matters because it points at the material contradiction the joke culture skips over. The source notes Trump wants to cut the National Park Service budget by 40%, while Mount Rushmore already has a $57 million repair backlog. That is not a punchline. It is a governing decision: celebrate the monument, underfund the institution, then act surprised when the place is vulnerable and the machinery around it is brittle.

Weather as a Convenient Distraction

The online reaction tried to turn the storm into cosmic commentary, even “God is angry” theater. That framing is weak. It turns a political arrangement into superstition and lets the human agents off the hook. The storm did not create the damage. The damage was already there in the budget cuts, the backlog, and the decision to use a national landmark as a backdrop for patriotic branding while hollowing out the public capacity that keeps such sites functioning.

Fox as Amplifier, Not Witness

Fox’s on-air scramble says something important about the media role in this ecosystem. It is not simply covering Trump; it is part of the production line that normalizes spectacle as governance. The moment the weather interrupts the show, the network is forced to acknowledge that reality exists outside the set. That interruption is useful precisely because it reveals how much of the coverage depends on insulating Trump from consequence.

What This Actually Shows

The larger pattern is familiar: authoritarian spectacle wrapped in patriotic symbolism, paired with institutional neglect underneath. Trump gets the stage, the monument, the fireworks, and the media oxygen. Public agencies get budget cuts. The public gets a performance built on decay. The storm did not expose divine judgment. It exposed a political system that prefers costumes to maintenance, myth to stewardship, and blame-shifting to responsibility.
