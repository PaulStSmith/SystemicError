Charlie Kirk statues would be mandated on every campus with red state's new legislation

Objects are placed near an image, at a prayer vigil for Charlie Kirk, a U.S. right-wing activist and commentator, and ally of U.S. President Donald Trump, who was killed on September 10, in Boston, Massachusetts, U.S., September 18, 2025. REUTERS/Lauren Owens Lambert

One Republican state lawmaker in Oklahoma now wants to require every public university in the state to erect statues to slain far-right activist Charlie Kirk.

On Friday, Jeffrey Sachs — an assistant professor at Nova Scotia's Acadia University — called attention to a bill by Oklahoma state senators Shane Jett (R) and Dana Prieto (R) that would mandate every public university in the Sooner State build a "Charlie Kirk Memorial Plaza." The bill, which has been filed as Senate Bill 1187 and does not yet have a formal name, would force universities to set aside a portion of real estate in a visible public location — like the quad, the student union or main entrance pathways — "to maximize public awareness and utilization."

Senate Bill 1187 would also make it a requirement for schools to erect a statue of both Kirk and his family, and to include a plaque honoring Kirk as "a voice of a generation, modern civil rights leader, vocal Christian, martyr for truth and faith, and free speech advocate."

"Square or plaza plans shall include a statue of Charlie Kirk sitting at a table with an empty seat across from him or a statue of Charlie Kirk and his wife standing and holding their children in their arms as a central element of the square or plaza design. The statue design and size shall be approved by the Legislature as part of the overall design review and approval process," the bill reads.

Universities would be authorized to accept donations to fund the cost of the monuments "from the National Endowment for the Arts and any private, corporate, or public source to fund the development, maintenance, and enhancement of the squares or plazas and any associated statues." It also stipulated that the state legislature may appropriate tax dollars as a "donation matching funds" as a way of showing "broad-based support for the squares or plazas."

Additionally, the bill makes it clear that any "student, employee or faculty member" who vandalizes or defaces statues of Kirk on their campuses could face steep punishment, including immediate termination of their enrollment or employment. And the legislation even includes an emergency declaration it deems "necessary for the preservation of the public peace, health or safety," in order to immediately have the legislation go into effect.

In July, WalletHub found Oklahoma ranked 50th out of 51 states (when including Washington D.C.) in public education. Only New Mexico ranked further behind the Sooner State. Criteria used to determine rankings included "test scores, graduation rates, school safety and bullying rates."

Our Analysis:

Mandatory Monuments to Martyrdom? Oklahoma's Far-Right Folly

In a move that reads like a satirical headline straight out of The Onion, Oklahoma state senators Shane Jett (R) and Dana Prieto (R) have launched an audacious, eyebrow-raising proposal. Their brainchild, Senate Bill 1187, demands every public university in Oklahoma erect statues and dedicate plazas to the far-right activist Charlie Kirk. This isn't just any ordinary legislative suggestion; it's a masterclass in politicizing public spaces under the guise of honoring a "modern civil rights leader, vocal Christian, martyr for truth and faith, and free speech advocate."

Let's dissect the absurdity piece by piece, shall we?

The Proposal: A Monument to Myopia

The bill insists on the creation of a "Charlie Kirk Memorial Plaza" at every public university, complete with statues of Kirk engaging in various familial and discussion-based poses. The specificity is almost comedic—down to the design of Kirk sitting at a table with an empty seat across from him. It's as if the senators are vying for an award in the 'Most Absurdly Specific Legislative Requirement' category.

Funding Fantasies and Punitive Measures

Funding for these monuments to Kirk can come from donations, including those from the National Endowment for the Arts—a suggestion that might raise a few eyebrows, considering the source. Should the state legislature feel particularly generous, they could also match these donations with taxpayer dollars, showcasing a "broad-based support" that, frankly, seems more imagined than real.

Moreover, the bill includes draconian measures against anyone who dares to vandalize or deface these statues. The punishment? Immediate termination of enrollment or employment. It's an authoritarian cherry on top of an already overbearing legislative sundae.

Emergency Declaration: A Misplaced Priority

Perhaps the most bewildering aspect is the emergency declaration attached to the bill, claiming it's "necessary for the preservation of the public peace, health or safety." This hyperbolic statement reveals the misplaced priorities of its sponsors. In a state that ranks 50th out of 51 in public education, according to a WalletHub study, one would think the emergency in need of addressing would be the abysmal state of its educational system, not the lack of Charlie Kirk statues.

A Voice for Whom?

The bill lauds Kirk as "a voice of a generation," a title that many would contest. Kirk's polarizing views and the far-right ideologies he champions are hardly representative of an entire generation's voice. Labeling him a "modern civil rights leader" is not just a stretch—it's an affront to the very concept of civil rights, twisting the term beyond recognition.

Conclusion: A Monumental Misstep

Senate Bill 1187 is not just a proposal; it's a symptom of a larger problem. It reflects a troubling trend toward idolizing divisive figures and enforcing a singular narrative within public institutions. The insistence on memorializing a figure like Charlie Kirk in such a grandiose manner speaks volumes about the priorities of its proponents. Instead of erecting statues, Oklahoma's legislators should focus on uplifting its education system from the depths of its dismal rankings. The real emergency isn't a lack of monuments; it's the state's failing commitment to its students' future.

In the end, Senate Bill 1187 serves as a stark reminder of the systemic errors plaguing our political landscape—a landscape where the erection of statues can be prioritized over the education of a generation. One cannot help but wonder: when will the real priorities stand up?
