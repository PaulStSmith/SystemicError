Donald Trump has awakened a sleeping giant

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump threatened to halt his administration’s work for Alaska if the state votes Democratic, while attacking Republican Sen. Susan Murkowski. The report presents the remark as evidence that Trump and his allies fear losing the Senate, although it does not provide the date or full context of his statement.

Democratic strategist Simon Rosenberg told The New Republic’s Greg Sargent that worsening economic reports, conflict in the Middle East, internal White House turmoil and declining polls had left Trump’s operation panicked before the election. Rosenberg also cited sharply increased Latino participation in early voting compared with the 2022 midterms, including increases ranging from 15 percent in Arizona to 84 percent in Virginia.

The “sleeping giant” is Latino voters: Rosenberg argued that Trump and Stephen Miller’s treatment of Hispanic communities and Latin America helped drive their greater participation. The figures establish an increase in early-vote share, not why individuals voted or which candidates they supported.

Power is making the threat

The central political fact is not Trump’s anxiety. It is a president telling a state that federal attention may depend on its electoral obedience.

A president controls executive agencies and enormous discretionary power. Alaska’s voters do not possess comparable leverage over federal administration. Framing the episode mainly as a “freakout” risks reducing an abuse of institutional power to campaign psychology. Whatever Trump’s emotional state, the reported statement describes government assistance as something he can condition on partisan submission.

The voters are not the aggressors

AlterNet’s account says Trump has “manufactured his own election downfall,” but the reporting does not establish an electoral downfall. It describes unfavorable polling, economic trouble and early-voting patterns before ballots have been fully counted.

That distinction matters. Latino voters are not an electoral force Trump accidentally activated through some mysterious tactical error. Rosenberg’s argument is that an administration used its power in ways that disproportionately harmed or antagonized Hispanic families and communities, and that participation rose afterward. The conduct belongs to the administration; the political response belongs to the voters.

Panic is not the explanation

The article leans heavily on Rosenberg’s characterization of Trump as erratic, physically diminished and surrounded by chaos. Those observations may enliven campaign commentary, but they are weaker than the institutional evidence.

A president threatening retaliation against a state does not become less serious because advisers appear panicked. Indeed, concentrating on appearance, mood and campaign dysfunction can obscure the operative choice: Trump reportedly used the authority of his office as electoral leverage. Personal spectacle becomes a distraction from accountable action.

The same problem appears in the discussion of Trump’s $1 million offer for evidence of voter fraud. The report treats it as another sign of instability without establishing what rules governed the offer or what evidence, if any, followed. The defensible point is narrower: Trump publicly promoted fraud claims while seeking information to substantiate them.

Turnout is evidence, not a verdict

Rosenberg’s state-by-state figures describe Latino early-vote share relative to the 2022 midterms. They do not prove that all additional voters oppose Trump, that immigration policy caused the increase or that Democrats will win those states. High participation can alter an election without validating every strategist’s forecast about it.

Still, the breadth of the reported increases is politically significant. A government may possess enforcement agencies, federal money and the presidential platform, but targeted communities retain agency through organization and voting. That is not equivalent power. It is one of the few available checks on power.

The larger pattern

The systemic error is to cover coercive politics as personality drama. Trump’s reported threat to Alaska is consequential because presidential authority stands behind it, not because it reveals that he is nervous.

The broader pattern is straightforward: leaders exercise state power against disfavored groups, then commentators portray those groups’ political response as a surprising demographic awakening. Nothing “woke” these voters in the abstract. If Rosenberg’s interpretation is correct, identifiable policies and officials produced foreseeable resistance. The people holding power made choices; the people subjected to those choices began showing up in greater numbers.
