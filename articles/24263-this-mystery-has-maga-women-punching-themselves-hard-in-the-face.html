This mystery has MAGA women punching themselves hard in the face

Based on an article by Tara Dublin

Paulo SantosSeptember 30, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory’s column argues that the supposed “mystery” is why some women embrace MAGA politics and “trad wife” ideology despite the restrictions those movements may impose on women. But the piece never establishes an answer. Its author points to conservative religious upbringing, patriarchal expectations and group conformity, while openly acknowledging that she does not understand these women’s choices.

The column contrasts politically vocal liberal Gen X women with younger conservative influencers. Its main example is Julia Kate, identified only by that name, who reportedly argued in a Jubilee Media debate that women should marry young and have at least 10 children. RawStory provides no broader evidence showing how common those views are among Republican women or trad-wife influencers.

Power is not an aesthetic

The article’s strongest underlying point is obscured by its fixation on generational personas. Gray hair, tattoos, prairie dresses and social-media cadence do not determine who holds institutional power. Legislatures, courts, executives, parties and well-funded political organizations do.

Women who promote submission or prolific childbearing may help market a political program, but they do not necessarily control it. The consequential decisions are made by officials who write laws, judges who interpret them, donors who finance campaigns and party organizations that turn religious doctrine into public policy. Treating an influencer as the central antagonist mistakes a salesperson for the governing apparatus.

The punching metaphor misdirects blame

Describing Republican women as punching themselves in the face reduces politics to individual irrationality. Some voters may support policies that ultimately constrain them, but that observation does not explain how political power manufactures consent, rewards conformity or narrows the choices available.

It also shifts attention downward. Mocking women for accepting subordination is easier than examining the institutions that benefit from their compliance. The women repeating the message have agency and deserve scrutiny. But the men and organizations capable of converting that message into law possess greater power and therefore greater responsibility.

Speculation is not an explanation

RawStory attributes conservative women’s views largely to Christian patriarchy and “cult-like” socialization. That may be the author’s interpretation, but the article supplies no interviews, research or evidence establishing why the women in question hold their beliefs. It does not examine material incentives, community status, media ecosystems, partisan identity or the authority structures surrounding them.

That evidentiary gap matters. Declaring that conservative women cannot think for themselves reproduces a familiar paternalism: women’s political choices count as authentic when they are approved and as brainwashing when they are not. A serious critique can condemn an ideology of female submission without erasing the agency of women who promote it.

Ragebait is not reporting

The column identifies Jubilee Media as a producer of ragebait, then adopts the same economy of attention. Julia Kate’s voice, facial expression and clothing receive more scrutiny than any institution capable of enforcing the politics she advocates. The spectacle supplies a villain who is easy to ridicule and difficult to place in any meaningful hierarchy of power.

RawStory’s framing is therefore weaker than its subject. It gestures toward reproductive autonomy and political equality, but offers no specific policy decision, legislative action or measurable consequence. The result is cultural combat presented as structural analysis.

The system prefers a caricature

The larger pattern is not simply that some women support a movement hostile to feminist goals. It is that political media repeatedly substitutes conspicuous minor figures for the officials, funders and institutions with actual authority. A viral influencer becomes the face of a system she did not build and may not control.

That substitution protects power. Readers are invited to despise a woman on camera while the machinery of governance recedes from view. The useful question is not why a stranger online appears eager to surrender her autonomy. It is who gains enforceable authority from that message, who supplies the money and law behind it, and who bears the consequences when private doctrine becomes public rule.
