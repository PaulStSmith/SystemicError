Trump-backed governor in the hot seat after vowing support for free press

Based on an article by Joshua Haiar, South Dakota Searchlight

Larry Rhoden’s Press-Freedom Promise Has a Presidential Exception

The Ban Is the Story

President Donald Trump announced that CNN, Politico and MS Now would be barred from the White House, accused them of reporting “fake news,” and later defended the decision while South Dakota Gov. Larry Rhoden stood beside him in the Oval Office. The three organizations subsequently sued to restore their access. Asked for his reaction, Rhoden repeatedly retreated to one sentence: “The president can make his own decisions.”

Power Does Not Need Rhoden’s Permission

Trump held the institutional power at issue. His administration controlled White House access; the excluded journalists did not. Treating the ban as merely a personal “decision” strips away the relevant fact: this was government power being used against particular news organizations.

Nobody asked Rhoden whether Trump was capable of making decisions. South Dakota Searchlight asked whether Rhoden—who had publicly promised to protect press freedom—approved of this one. His answer avoided the substance while granting the exercise of power the deference it required.

That is not neutrality. It is political insulation.

Transparency, Conditional on Good Behavior

Rhoden’s praise for South Dakota journalists was revealing. He said they had earned his trust, shown him respect and “reciprocated” his administration’s commitment to openness. His spokesperson denied that access would depend on journalists living up to the governor’s trust.

The denial matters, but so does the governor’s framing. A free press is not a rewards program for respectful coverage. Investigative reporting routinely creates hostility because its function is to scrutinize officials, not maintain a mutually affirming relationship with them.

Rhoden understood that distinction in 2025, when he wrote that “tough, investigative reporting” could produce needed reforms and promised the most transparent administration in South Dakota history. Faced with a powerful ally excluding disfavored outlets, that principle abruptly became local, personal and conveniently nontransferable.

A Crowd of Reporters Does Not Erase Exclusion

Rhoden called it “ironic” that Trump was criticized over free speech while taking questions from dozens of journalists. That argument substitutes volume for access.

Allowing many reporters into a room does not negate the deliberate exclusion of three organizations. It demonstrates selection: some outlets retained access while others lost it after the president branded their reporting fake. When Searchlight pressed this obvious point, Rhoden’s office again replied that the president could make his own decisions.

The framing misdirects attention from the official imposing the restriction to the existence of journalists he still tolerated. By that logic, selective retaliation disappears whenever enough unaffected people remain nearby.

The Price of Proximity

Rhoden had come to discuss a Medicaid drug-pricing program. His office said three identified drugs could save South Dakota approximately $750,000 annually, against a state Medicaid budget of about $2 billion, including roughly $715 million from the state. That policy announcement gave the governor a reason to value presidential access—and a reason not to confront the man providing it.

The same Oval Office appearance ended with Trump joking about putting his face on Mount Rushmore and Rhoden replying with a joke about the Crazy Horse Memorial. The exchange does not prove Rhoden’s motives. It does show the political atmosphere: deference, laughter and cultivated rapport surrounding an exercise of federal power against disfavored media organizations.

Principles With an Escape Clause

The larger pattern is institutional cowardice disguised as jurisdictional modesty. Officials advertise transparency when the promise is cheap, then declare themselves spectators when an ally tests the principle.

Rhoden did not order the White House ban. He did something politically useful to the official who did: he declined to judge it, diluted it with a misleading reference to other reporters and treated executive discretion as a sufficient answer to a question about executive abuse.

A commitment that applies only to friendly journalists, local circumstances or moments without political cost is not a governing principle. It is branding—and power learns quickly which watchdogs will bark only when permission is granted.
