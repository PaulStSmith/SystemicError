'Time for him to go': Insiders reveal top Trump adviser is done with Pete Hegseth

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 26, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

U.S. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth reacts during a press conference to discuss the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA)’s “National Farm Security Action Plan,” outside the USDA in Washington, D.C., U.S., July 8, 2025. REUTERS/Umit Bektas

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth is in trouble again over his alleged careless handling of classified material, and sources close to the White House said patience has worn thin.

Hegseth canceled two previously scheduled visits to military bases in Georgia to attend a White House meeting Thursday after The Washington Post reported he sent classified information over the non-secure Signal app. Sources close to the administration told NOTUS the latest revelations could be the last straw for the embattled cabinet official.

"A source close to the White House described one of President Donald Trump’s top aides as being 'p---ed off' about Hegseth’s time leading the Defense Department and feeling like it was 'time for him to go,'" the website reported. "A second source close to the White House confirmed the characterization of the aide’s stance."

The two sources told NOTUS the defense secretary was wearing out his welcome by repeatedly surprising the White House, such as his recent decision to pause a munitions shipment to Ukraine, and some congressional Republicans feel that his actions deserved consequences.

“I don’t think it’s partisan, but I do think it’s challenging for one branch to tell another branch that we are going to hold them accountable,” said Sen. Mike Rounds (R-SD), a senior member of the Senate Armed Services Committee. “The entity that has to hold a secretary of defense accountable specifically is his boss, and that’s the president.”

Rep. Don Bacon (R-IL) was the only Republican on the House Armed Services Committee to vote in favor of a measure recently calling for disciplinary action for revealing classified information, as Hegseth has been accused of doing.

“It was common sense that was [classified as] secret,” said Bacon, who retired from the U.S. Air Force as a brigadier general. “They’re stating the obvious. I’m glad that they made it official.”

The White House is still publicly standing by Hegseth, but his support in Congress isn't completely stable.

“I get Bacon’s point, but I’m not politicizing anything that happened,” said Rep. Rich McCormick (R-GA), a former Marine helicopter pilot and Navy commander. “I’m saying, live and learn. If you don’t learn, then go on. If it happens again, then we’ve got to start talking about it. People make mistakes; the mistakes we repeat are the ones that matter.”

Our Analysis:

Hegseth's Classified Missteps: A Symptom of Systemic Recklessness

In a striking display of what can only be described as gross negligence, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth finds himself embroiled in yet another scandal, this time over his alleged mishandling of classified information. This incident, as reported by The Washington Post and further elaborated by sources via NOTUS, is not merely a lapse in judgment but a glaring testament to the systemic issues plaguing the current administration's approach to national security.

Carelessness in the Digital Age

It is both astonishing and profoundly disturbing that Hegseth, a cabinet official charged with the defense of our nation, is accused of sending classified information over the non-secure Signal app. This act of carelessness not only undermines the very essence of national security protocols but also blatantly disregards the risks posed by cyber espionage. In the digital age, where information is as potent a weapon as any arsenal, Hegseth's alleged actions represent a fundamental failure to protect the nation's most sensitive data.

The White House's Waning Patience

The reaction from within the White House itself speaks volumes. Descriptions of top aides being "p---ed off" and the sentiment that "it was time for him to go" reflect a broader understanding of the gravity of Hegseth's alleged misconduct. Yet, despite the apparent frustration and the cancellation of Hegseth's scheduled visits to military bases in Georgia, the White House publicly continues to stand by him. This juxtaposition of internal exasperation with public support underscores a troubling pattern of incoherence and indecision at the highest levels of government.

Congressional Discontent and the Question of Accountability

The narrative of discontent is not confined to the executive branch. Congressional reactions, particularly among Republicans, signal a growing concern over Hegseth's actions and the broader implications for accountability. Rep. Don Bacon's lone vote in favor of disciplinary action for Hegseth's revelation of classified information is telling. It underscores a common-sense approach to national security—one that seems to be in short supply.

Senator Mike Rounds' commentary on the difficulty of one branch holding another accountable is a veiled critique of the current state of checks and balances. It is a reminder that the principle of accountability seems to have lost its weight, with the executive branch acting with apparent impunity.

A Culture of Repeated Mistakes

Rep. Rich McCormick's statement, "People make mistakes; the mistakes we repeat are the ones that matter," encapsulates the crux of the issue. Hegseth's alleged mishandling of classified information is not an isolated incident but a symptom of a broader culture of recklessness and repeated mistakes. This pattern of behavior, if left unchecked, poses a significant threat not only to national security but to the very foundations of democratic accountability.

Conclusion

The Hegseth scandal is more than a story of individual failings; it is a stark illustration of systemic error. It lays bare a troubling disregard for the principles of security, accountability, and prudent governance. As the situation unfolds, one must wonder: How many more breaches will be tolerated before decisive action is taken? The answer to this question will speak volumes about the current administration's commitment to national security and the rule of law.
