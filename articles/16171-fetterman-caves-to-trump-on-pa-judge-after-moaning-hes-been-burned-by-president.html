Fetterman caves to Trump on PA judge after moaning he’s been burned by president

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 7, 20262 min read

The Real Story Is Trump’s Court Capture, Not Fetterman’s Surrender

Context

Sen. John Fetterman waived his right to block a Trump judicial nominee in Pennsylvania, becoming the first Senate Democrat in Trump’s second term to hand over a blue slip for one of the president’s picks. Because Pennsylvania’s other senator is Republican David McCormick, Fetterman was the only real institutional obstacle in the state. He removed it.

Who Actually Has Power

The power in this story sits with Trump, who gets to nominate lifetime judges, and with the Senate process that still gives individual senators leverage over home-state nominees. Fetterman is not the center of power here; he is the last visible checkpoint. Once he signs off, the machinery moves. The nominee, Antonio Pozos, benefits from that decision. Trump gets a cleaner path to shape the federal bench.

What Fetterman Enabled

This was not some neutral procedural gesture. It was an enabling act. The blue slip exists precisely because lifetime judicial appointments are serious and because senators can slow or block nominees who will outlast presidents and reshape law long after the headlines disappear. Fetterman chose not to use that leverage. He gave Trump what critics wanted Democrats to deny: easy movement for a district court nominee in a Democratic state.

The Misleading Frame

The article spends a lot of energy on the drama around Fetterman, his critics, and the liberal backlash. That is politically useful misdirection. It turns a power question into a personality fight. The real issue is not whether Fetterman annoys progressives. The issue is that Trump is advancing judicial nominees while the opposition treats one of its few remaining tools as optional etiquette.

What This Reveals

Demand Justice is right about one thing: these are not normal times. But the deeper point is not simply that Trump is aggressive. It is that institutions that were supposed to constrain presidential power are now so weakened, politicized, or timid that the fight over a lifetime judgeship gets reduced to whether a single senator wants to be the exception. That is how capture works in plain sight: not only through the executive’s force, but through the opposition’s decision to stop resisting at the point where resistance still matters.
