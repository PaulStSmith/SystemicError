Trump official’s chilling threat revealed in court docs

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 26, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Trump administration used control over billions of dollars in federal grants to pressure Intel into surrendering roughly 10 percent of the company to the government, according to court records described by WIRED and reported by AlterNet. The threat came from Commerce Department lawyer David Shapiro, who told Intel’s chief financial officer in August 2025 not to mistake the demand for a negotiation and warned that rejecting it would bring consequences he could not predict. Intel’s board approved the deal.

That is the “chilling threat” in the court documents: accept the administration’s terms, or face unspecified retaliation from a government with enormous influence over Intel’s financing, customers and regulatory environment. The ambiguity was part of its force.

Federal grants became leverage for a government stake

Under the agreement, Intel would receive $8.9 billion in federal grants it had already secured, largely through the CHIPS Act. In return, the government would obtain about 10 percent of Intel’s stock without paying separately for the shares.

Notes from Shapiro’s call indicate that President Donald Trump intended to announce the deal that Friday. A summary prepared for Intel’s board records Shapiro acknowledging that directors could refuse, while adding that rejection would have consequences he could not predict. He also urged the board to consider the value of having the administration on Intel’s side, including in the company’s relationships with customers.

That formulation paired an inducement with a warning: cooperation could purchase presidential favor, while refusal could invite an undefined response. The administration held the institutional power. Intel’s directors possessed the formal authority to vote, but they were making that decision under pressure from officials controlling the release of federal money and invoking the president’s influence over the company’s commercial interests.

Board minutes show that directors considered the arrangement less than ideal. They accepted it anyway.

Trump described the extraction as effortless

Trump later advertised precisely the feature that makes the deal troubling. He wrote on social media that the United States had “paid nothing for these Shares,” which he valued at approximately $11 billion. Discussing the stake afterward, he said: “I should’ve asked for more. It was so easy.”

Before the agreement, Trump had publicly demanded the resignation of Intel CEO Lip-Bu Tan over Tan’s connections to China. Tan met Trump in the Oval Office on August 11 and promised to divest his China-based holdings. The sequence described in the reporting placed the company and its chief executive under direct presidential pressure before Intel surrendered a major equity stake.

A shareholder lawsuit brought by Richard Paisner alleges that Intel’s board “caved to the government’s extortion.” Scott Lincicome, a vice president at the libertarian Cato Institute, called the transaction “straight-up coercion.” Those are their characterizations, not established judicial findings. But they track the language attributed to Shapiro: this was supposedly not a negotiation, the president’s announcement was already being prepared, and resistance would carry unknowable consequences.

Intel disputes that account. Its attorneys argue in a motion to dismiss that genuine negotiations occurred and improved the terms for the company, including the immediate release of its grants. That defense may explain why directors accepted the arrangement. It does not erase the reported warning that refusal could provoke consequences or the administration’s use of money Intel had already been awarded as leverage for an ownership stake.

Industrial policy turned into presidential patronage

The CHIPS Act grants were meant to support semiconductor production. In this transaction, the administration reportedly converted their release into leverage for acquiring corporate equity—and presented presidential favor as an asset Intel should price into its decision.

That is not merely aggressive economic policy. It replaces predictable administration of federal programs with a patronage logic in which companies are encouraged to calculate the value of being “on the side” of the president. The foreseeable consequence is a market shaped not only by statutes, grants and regulations, but by executives’ willingness to accommodate demands delivered with warnings of unspecified retaliation.

The same pattern reportedly extended to Nvidia. Trump announced that he had “convinced” Nvidia’s board to reinstate Jensen Huang as CEO after the board chair had said days earlier that it would not do so. The New York Times reported that Nvidia reached an agreement with the administration on a Sunday night following days of pressure from senior Trump officials. Paisner is separately seeking Nvidia’s internal records concerning an agreement to give the government a share of its China chip sales.

The available reporting does not establish the merits of Paisner’s lawsuits or prove that either company’s directors violated their duties. It does show an administration using concentrated federal power to intervene in corporate governance, financing and sales—and a president publicly boasting about how little resistance he encountered.

Intel’s board could technically say no. Shapiro’s reported message explained why that freedom might be largely ceremonial: the people demanding the stake also claimed the power to make refusal hurt.
