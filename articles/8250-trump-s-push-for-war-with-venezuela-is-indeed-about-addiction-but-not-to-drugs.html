Trump's push for war with Venezuela is indeed about addiction — but not to drugs

President Donald Trump’s saber-rattling about potential military action in Venezuela is indeed about drugs, but not cocaine. It is about a far more dangerous drug that former President George W. Bush admitted (in his 2006 State of the Union address) the US is addicted to.

Oil.

Venezuela has the largest proven oil reserves in the world — 300 billion barrels — even larger than reserves in Saudi Arabia.

Mr. Trump and his oil industry friends may imagine that by deposing President Nicolás Maduro and installing a friendly government there, the US would have unlimited access to this huge oil reserve, which is five times larger than the proven reserves in the US.

Never mind the fact that for any hope of future climate stability, most of this oil needs to stay right where it is: in the ground.

We’ve seen this tragic play before. The Bush administration justified its disastrous 2003 invasion of Iraq with the pretext that Iraq had weapons of mass destruction which, as it turned out, it didn’t.

As US Central Command commander General John Abizaid admitted about the Iraq War at the time: “Of course it’s about oil, it’s very much about oil, and we can’t really deny that.”

The invasion killed tens of thousands of people, mostly civilians, and destabilized the broader Middle East region for years.

And now here we go again. A similar pretext — this time “drug interdiction” — is being used to justify a potential US invasion and regime change in Venezuela. But this is not about stopping the flow of dangerous drugs, it is about actually increasing the flow of the dangerous drug some pushers want to keep us all hooked on.

Oil.

As Colombian President Gustavo Petro recently stated on the US-Venezuela threat: “Oil is at the heart of the matter.”

Instead of admitting their addiction, the damage it causes, and committing to recovery, hardcore junkies are always desperate for more supply. It seems Mr. Trump and his oil industry friends are the most dangerous narco-traffickers we need to worry about.

Our Analysis:

The Opiate of the American Regime: Oil Addiction and Imperial Maneuvers

In an audacious display of historical amnesia and imperial hubris, the saber-rattling over Venezuela by President Donald Trump’s administration starkly underscores a dangerous addiction. Not to cocaine, as some might suspect given the region’s notorious reputation, but to a far more insidious and globally destructive substance: oil.

The Veiled Pretexts of Imperial Ambitions

The pretext of “drug interdiction” as justification for potential military action and regime change in Venezuela is not just disingenuous; it is a brazenly transparent guise for the true motive—oil acquisition. Richard Steiner’s pointed analysis in the article reveals a chilling parallel to the disastrous 2003 invasion of Iraq, where the Bush administration’s false claims of weapons of mass destruction masked a covetous eye on Iraq’s oil reserves. The tragic cost of that venture was immense, measured not only in the destabilization of an entire region but in the tens of thousands of civilian lives lost.

It seems the lessons of the past are lost on Trump and his coterie of oil industry magnates. Venezuela, home to the world’s largest proven oil reserves, has become the latest target in the ongoing saga of America’s oil dependency. This dependency is so severe that former President George W. Bush candidly admitted to the nation’s oil addiction in his 2006 State of the Union address. Yet, instead of seeking rehabilitation, the current administration appears hell-bent on securing its next fix.

The Environmental Catastrophe in Waiting

The pursuit of Venezuelan oil is not only morally bankrupt; it is ecologically catastrophic. In an era where the clarion call for climate action has never been louder, the notion of tapping into Venezuela’s vast oil reserves is anathema to any hope of future climate stability. The scientific consensus is clear: the majority of existing fossil fuel reserves, including oil, must remain unexploited if we are to avert the worst impacts of climate change. And yet, the insatiable thirst for oil seems to trump ecological sanity.

The Real Narco-Traffickers

Steiner’s analysis compellingly frames Trump and his oil industry allies as the most dangerous narco-traffickers of our time. This analogy is as scathing as it is apt. The havoc wreaked by oil addiction—war, environmental degradation, and the perpetuation of authoritarian regimes—far surpasses the damage inflicted by the drugs Trump ostensibly aims to combat through intervention in Venezuela.

Colombian President Gustavo Petro’s assertion that “Oil is at the heart of the matter” in the US’s posture towards Venezuela is a sobering reminder of the true stakes involved. It’s not democracy, human rights, or drug interdiction at play here; it’s the relentless pursuit of oil.

Conclusion: A Call for a New Direction

The article lays bare the cyclical folly of America’s oil addiction and its catastrophic consequences, both human and environmental. The scenario unfolding around Venezuela is not just a geopolitical chess game; it is a stark illustration of a superpower unable to break free from the chains of its fossil fuel dependency, even as the planet teeters on the brink of climate disaster.

As we reflect on the dire implications of this addiction, the question becomes: When will America finally admit its problem and commit to recovery? The first step in overcoming addiction is acknowledging it. It’s high time for the U.S. to confront its oil dependency and embark on a genuine path toward energy sustainability and climate responsibility. Only then can we hope to avert the continuation of these tragic, oil-fueled interventions that have cost far too many lives and threaten the future of our planet.
