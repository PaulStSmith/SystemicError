The Dog Foghorn of American Reaction

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 4, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A dog whistle is subtle. A dog foghorn is not.

That is where today’s Republican Party increasingly lives on race: not in coded hints so delicate they require interpretation, but in blaring signals that only pretend to be deniable. The words change. The target changes. The costume changes. But the message remains painfully legible: some people belong inside the circle of American protection, and others exist to be feared, controlled, exploited, or erased.

This is the real function of the modern GOP’s war on “wokeness,” “DEI,” “critical race theory,” “open borders,” and “heritage.” These slogans are not separate arguments. They are chapters in the same story. They tell white Americans that equality is humiliation, that history is persecution, that immigrants are invaders, and that any attempt to name racism is itself the true racism.

The party’s obsession with Confederate memory makes the game especially obvious.

Before understanding the Confederacy’s connection to slavery and the racial order of the South, the fixation can seem almost incoherent. Why would any country celebrate people who took up arms against it? By the most basic civic definition, Confederate leaders were not patriots. They were traitors. They rejected the United States, launched a war against it, and did so to preserve human bondage.

That is why the “heritage” defense collapses so quickly. If this were simply about loyalty to America, Confederate nostalgia would be impossible. No one proposes putting Benedict Arnold on a pedestal as a founding father. No one insists that honoring him is necessary to understand the Revolution. We all understand the difference between remembering a historical figure and celebrating betrayal.

The difference is that Confederate memory was never really about neutral history. It was about racial power.

The Lost Cause turned defeat into nobility, slavery into “states’ rights,” and rebellion into “heritage.” It transformed men who fought to preserve slavery into tragic gentlemen defending a way of life. That “way of life” was the ownership, torture, rape, sale, and forced labor of Black human beings. Strip away the romance and the cause is not complicated. The Confederacy was a slaveholders’ republic.

So when modern politicians defend Confederate monuments, or restore statues of enslavers under the banner of patriotism, they are not making a consistent argument about American history. They are choosing which betrayals count, which crimes can be forgiven, and whose suffering can be erased.

That same moral logic points toward the future.

The frightening part is not that a Republican lawmaker has openly introduced a bill restoring slavery. The frightening part is that the groundwork is being laid in public: divide people into “real Americans” and disposable outsiders, strip the outsiders of sympathy, describe their labor as something owed, and then pretend exploitation is merely policy.

Once a movement convinces itself that undocumented immigrants are invaders, criminals, parasites, or less deserving of rights, the next step is not hard to imagine. Forced labor can be dressed up as “repayment.” Abuse can be called “deterrence.” Bondage can be rebranded as “work requirements.” The language will be sanitized. It always is.

America already carries a constitutional wound in the Thirteenth Amendment’s exception for punishment after conviction. Slavery and involuntary servitude are prohibited, “except as a punishment for crime.” That exception has long been used to justify coerced prison labor. The danger is that a politics built on racial hierarchy will keep expanding the category of people deemed punishable, removable, rightless, and exploitable.

First prisoners. Then migrants. Then anyone the state decides is outside the circle of protection.

This is how reactionary politics works. It rarely begins by announcing its final destination. It begins by training the public to accept categories of human beings who deserve less. Less due process. Less safety. Less dignity. Less pay. Less freedom. Eventually, “less” becomes “none.”

That is why the Confederate fixation matters. It is not just nostalgia. It is rehearsal. It teaches people to admire a society organized around racial domination while pretending they are merely honoring ancestors. It teaches people to separate “history” from accountability. It teaches people that slavery can be softened, renamed, aestheticized, and defended if the victims are pushed far enough out of frame.

The GOP’s dog foghorn is not subtle anymore. It tells us who counts. It tells us who does not. It tells us which past they want restored and which future they are willing to build.

And if we wait for them to say the word “slavery” before taking the danger seriously, we will have missed the entire method.
