Jimmy Kimmel fallout: WSJ takes sides in Trump war that blew up in his face

The FCC Is the Weapon, and Kimmel Is the Decoy

The Real Power Is Not in the Monologue

This story is not about a comedian’s tone or a network’s programming choices. It is about a president using the Federal Communications Commission as leverage against a broadcaster, while ABC and Disney are forced to defend themselves against regulatory pressure and license threats. That is the actual power structure here: the administration has the state; the network has a legal problem; Kimmel is the pretext.

How the Pressure Works

According to the source, ABC sued the FCC over “coercing and retaliating,” and says the government launched pretextual investigations and threatened broadcast licenses. That is not casual political sparring. It is state power reaching into editorial decisions. When the FCC can chill booking decisions and shape who gets airtime, the issue is no longer taste or partisanship. It is coercion dressed up as regulation.

The Blame Is Being Pushed Downward

The Trump operation keeps trying to make Kimmel the villain: first by falsely claiming he disparaged Charlie Kirk after the assassination, then by recasting his jokes as dangerous rhetoric, then by turning Melania Trump into a conveyor belt for the same accusation. This is classic misdirection. A president and his regulators are acting; a TV host is being used as the excuse. The point is not to rebut comedy. The point is to normalize government punishment for disfavored speech.

ABC’s Retreat Shows the System’s Weakness

ABC initially took Kimmel off the air, then reversed course after mass protest. That sequence matters. It shows that even large institutions are often willing to fold first and explain later when a hostile administration turns up the pressure. The public pushback mattered because corporate caution almost always moves faster than constitutional principle. Disney was not discovering courage; it was responding to a backlash strong enough to make surrender expensive.

Even the Wall Street Journal Can See the Mob Logic

When the conservative editorial board starts describing Brendan Carr as a “mob-boss,” the problem is not ambiguity. It is that the coercion is so blatant it becomes hard to deny even for friendly observers. Carr once warned against weaponizing the FCC under Democrats, and now serves as proof that the same tactics are only condemned when they are used by the other side. The hypocrisy is the method. The state gets used when it is useful, and denounced when it is not.

The Pattern Is Authoritarian by Design

This is not a story about hurt feelings or edgy jokes. It is a familiar authoritarian pattern: invent an offense, assign moral panic to the target, apply regulatory pressure, and force institutions to police themselves before the government has to do the dirty work openly. The danger is not that one comedian gets bullied. The danger is that broadcasters learn the lesson the administration wants them to learn: compliance is safer than editorial independence.
