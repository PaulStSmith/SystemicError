From Satire to Statecraft: When Crime Becomes Policy

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 26, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Once upon a time, a YouTube short skit floated across the algorithmic tide, where a disillusioned young man, fed up with the American job market’s cruelty, declares: “I just do one crime a day.” What followed was a comically absurd—but disturbingly plausible—monologue of petty and not-so-petty criminal activity. Check fraud, embezzlement, arson, jaywalking (“for the love of the game”) — all laid out with the breezy confidence of someone who’d cracked the code. And the kicker? Even if caught, he shrugs, prison still feeds you, houses you, gives you healthcare. The audience chuckled. Online comments flooded in. Relatable. Hilarious. But the subtext lingered: if the system fails you long enough, crime starts to look like strategy.

We laughed. Because it was satire.

But satire only works when it’s rooted in truth. And this week, truth punched through the veil of comedy like a fist through drywall.

In a damning segment from MSNBC’s The Beat with Ari Melber, the farce of “crime doesn’t pay” was obliterated with surgical legal analysis and brutal fact. Donald J. Trump, the sitting President of the United States—a man already adjudicated as a rapist and now a convicted felon—sent his lawyers to the Supreme Court to argue, essentially, that he can ignore the Constitution. That he, through executive order, can override the Fourteenth Amendment and redefine citizenship. That his Department of Justice doesn’t always have to obey the courts. That rulings are just... suggestions. It wasn’t a legal argument. It was an audition for autocracy.

And you dare to mock the skit?

Let’s be very clear: this isn’t just about a morally bankrupt president. This is about a systematic normalization of lawlessness at the highest echelons of power. Trump’s lawyers, with stone-faced audacity, told the highest court in the land that the administration reserves the right to ignore legal precedent. They’re not just moving the goalposts; they’re bulldozing the field. This is not constitutional interpretation—it’s constitutional vandalism.

Justice Kagan’s incredulous response—“You’re losing. Why take this to us?”—isn’t just a rhetorical question. It’s an alarm bell. One echoed even by Trump appointees like Gorsuch and Barrett, who pressed their own side on the sheer absurdity of the case. The look of discomfort on their faces wasn’t judicial restraint—it was intellectual recoil. And let’s not forget, the very lawyer defending this constitutional defilement is the same one who once argued that a president might be immune even if he ordered political assassinations on American soil. Yes, that argument was made with a straight face.

At this point, if you’re still treating the first skit as fiction, you’re not paying attention. If anything, it underestimated just how broken the system is.

What we are witnessing is a two-tiered legal system so grotesquely warped that it makes petty crooks look principled by comparison. A guy defrauding checks to pay rent is called a criminal. A president who commits fraud, incites insurrection, and tries to nullify citizenship is called “Mr. President.” One gets a cell; the other gets a motorcade.

And when the institutions charged with safeguarding democracy debate whether they can trust the DOJ to obey court orders? That’s not constitutional crisis — that’s collapse. That’s the final stage of democratic decay, when the referees admit they can’t enforce the rules anymore.

A professor in the segment says it flat out: this administration breaks the law until it’s stopped. That’s gangster governance. That’s mafioso policy-making. It’s not even clever corruption. It’s brazen, public, arrogant criminality packaged in legalese and sold with a patriotic bow. And the legal community, for all its robes and rituals, is struggling to hold the line against a tidal wave of institutional contempt.

So yes, crime does pay. If you have money, power, lawyers, and the audacity to redefine the rules. The only difference between the skit’s check fraudster and the president is scale and audacity. One breaks the law because he’s hungry. The other breaks the law because he can. One gets locked up for shoplifting. The other gets standing ovations at political rallies.

We’re not living in satire anymore. We’re living in its sequel — dystopia. One where the punchlines are real and the crimes are televised. The absurd has become ordinary. The immoral, institutionalized. The law, selectively enforced.

And this time, the punchline is redacted, footnoted, and rubber-stamped by the very people meant to stop it.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
