The Sidewalk Paradox: Public Infrastructure, Private Responsibility, and the Bureaucratic Art of Passing the Buck

It's a sidewalk in São Paulo, Brazil.

Or, more accurately, it's a collection of concrete slabs, improvised ramps, abrupt changes in elevation, exposed tree roots, and questionable paving decisions masquerading as a sidewalk.

Some sections are tiled. Others are bare concrete. One section looks like it has been cultivating its own ecosystem.

There are steps where there shouldn't be steps, slopes where there should be level ground, and obstacles that make walking an exercise in concentration.

Now imagine navigating this mess in a wheelchair.

Or with a walker.

Or pushing a stroller.

Or simply being elderly and having the audacity to leave your house.

Welcome to modern urban accessibility, where governments proudly proclaim everyone's right to mobility while leaving the actual implementation to whoever owns the nearest piece of real estate.

And before American readers dismiss this as another example of Brazilian infrastructure problems, there's something they should know.

The United States does much the same thing.

The Public Sidewalk That Somehow Became Your Problem

In São Paulo, Municipal Law 15,442/2011 generally requires property owners to construct, maintain, and repair the sidewalks bordering their properties.

The city establishes technical standards, accessibility requirements, and penalties for noncompliance.

But the practical responsibility for maintaining much of this public infrastructure falls on private individuals.

Think about that arrangement.

The sidewalk is public. Everyone has the right to use it. Its primary purpose is to allow people to move safely through the city.

Yet its condition depends on the financial resources, competence, willingness, and maintenance priorities of individual property owners.

One homeowner installs attractive tiles.

The neighbor pours concrete.

Another raises the sidewalk to accommodate a garage entrance.

Someone else ignores a growing crack for fifteen years.

And the municipality expects these independently maintained fragments to magically become a coherent pedestrian transportation network.

That's not urban planning. That's wishful thinking with a municipal ordinance attached.

America: Different Language, Same Absurdity

Consider New York City.

Under Section 19-152 of its Administrative Code, property owners are generally responsible for installing, repairing, and maintaining the sidewalks adjoining their properties.

The New York City Department of Transportation can inspect sidewalks and issue violations. In certain circumstances, the city can arrange repairs and bill the property owner.

There are exceptions, including special arrangements for some damage caused by city-owned trees.

But the underlying principle is familiar: public pedestrian infrastructure, private maintenance responsibility.

And New York is hardly the only American jurisdiction that uses some version of this arrangement.

The details differ from city to city, but the basic model appears across the country.

Meanwhile, American governments have accessibility obligations under the Americans with Disabilities Act.

Which produces a remarkable contradiction.

The government is responsible for ensuring accessibility, but it can leave significant portions of the physical infrastructure needed to achieve that accessibility in private hands.

The legal obligations don't simply disappear because a city delegates maintenance. But the fragmentation makes consistent implementation considerably more difficult.

It's a system practically designed to generate inconsistent results.

Imagine If We Did This to Roads

Here's a thought experiment.

Imagine your city announced that, starting tomorrow, every property owner would be responsible for maintaining the portion of the roadway directly in front of their property.

You would have to pay for the asphalt.

Your neighbor could choose concrete.

The person across the street might prefer cobblestones.

Someone with a little extra money could build a beautifully finished section.

Someone struggling financially might leave a pothole untouched for years.

And the fellow with the oversized pickup truck might decide that raising the road surface by six inches would make entering his driveway more convenient.

The municipality would establish technical standards, of course.

It would publish manuals.

It would threaten fines.

It might even launch a public awareness campaign explaining that roads should be smooth and safe.

But actually maintaining the road?

That's your problem.

The result would be a transportation network consisting of thousands of disconnected construction projects, each maintained according to the resources and priorities of a different individual.

We would immediately recognize the absurdity.

Yet when the infrastructure serves pedestrians instead of automobiles, we somehow accept the same logic.

Why is the roadway treated as a unified transportation system while the sidewalk is treated as a collection of private chores?

Accessibility Doesn't Work One Property at a Time

Accessibility has a fundamental characteristic that this model ignores.

It requires continuity.

A wheelchair-accessible sidewalk that ends at a six-inch step isn't an accessible route.

A beautifully paved stretch followed by broken concrete isn't an accessible route.

A compliant ramp leading directly into a tree trunk isn't an accessible route.

And a pedestrian network where 95% of the route is perfectly accessible but the remaining 5% is impassable may be functionally useless to someone who cannot cross those barriers.

Accessibility isn't something you achieve by counting how many individual property owners have complied with the rules.

It's something you achieve by ensuring that a person can travel from one location to another without encountering an obstacle that makes the journey impossible.

That requires coordinated planning, consistent engineering, maintenance schedules, and accountability across property boundaries.

In other words, it requires exactly the kind of centralized infrastructure management that municipalities already perform for roads.

The sidewalk is not merely decorative concrete outside someone's house.

It is part of the city's transportation network.

The Convenient Economics of Passing the Bill

Of course, there's a financial reason municipalities find this arrangement attractive.

Sidewalks cost money.

Construction costs money.

Repairs cost money.

Tree-root damage costs money.

Accessibility improvements cost money.

And shifting those costs onto adjacent property owners reduces the amount municipalities must pay directly from their own budgets.

From a narrow accounting perspective, it's convenient.

But transferring a cost doesn't eliminate it.

It merely changes who receives the bill.

Worse, it creates a system where public infrastructure quality can depend on private wealth.

A prosperous homeowner may afford proper reconstruction.

A struggling family may postpone repairs.

An absentee landlord may do the bare minimum.

And the pedestrian who needs to use the sidewalk has absolutely no control over any of these decisions.

The elderly woman with a walker doesn't care whether the adjacent property owner is financially comfortable.

The wheelchair user doesn't care whether the landlord is waiting for a contractor.

The parent pushing a stroller doesn't care whose responsibility it is to fix the broken pavement.

They need a safe, continuous route.

And the public authority responsible for managing the city's transportation system should be accountable for providing one.

But What About Property Owners Who Cause Damage?

There's an obvious objection.

What if a property owner damages the sidewalk?

What if someone illegally modifies it to accommodate a driveway, allows construction equipment to destroy the pavement, or installs an obstruction?

Should taxpayers pay for that?

Of course not.

There is a perfectly reasonable distinction between responsibility for maintaining public infrastructure and liability for damaging it.

The municipality should own the responsibility for designing, constructing, inspecting, and maintaining the pedestrian network.

If a private individual or company damages that infrastructure, the municipality should have the authority to require restoration or recover the costs.

The same principle can apply to utility companies excavating sidewalks to install pipes, cables, or other equipment.

This isn't complicated.

We already understand that someone who damages public property can be required to pay for the damage without becoming responsible for maintaining that property forever.

Why should sidewalks be different?

A Better Model: Treat Sidewalks Like Streets

The alternative doesn't require revolutionary technology or some elaborate new administrative philosophy.

It requires governments to treat pedestrian infrastructure as actual infrastructure.

Municipalities should assume responsibility for the design, construction, reconstruction, and maintenance of public sidewalks.

They should establish consistent standards for surfaces, gradients, drainage, tree placement, and accessibility.

They should inspect pedestrian routes as connected networks rather than isolated property frontages.

They should prioritize repairs based on safety, accessibility, pedestrian traffic, and the needs of vulnerable populations.

And they should fund this work through transparent public budgets, just as they fund road maintenance.

That doesn't mean every sidewalk in every city can be rebuilt overnight.

It means the government takes responsibility for a coherent, long-term program instead of expecting thousands of independent property owners to somehow produce a unified result.

Nor does it necessarily mean abolishing every form of local cost recovery. Cities can debate how infrastructure is financed.

But financing a public service and fragmenting responsibility for delivering that service are two very different things.

The municipality should be accountable for the outcome, regardless of how the work is funded.

The Real Test of a City's Accessibility

Politicians love announcing accessibility initiatives.

New ramps.

New regulations.

New public awareness campaigns.

New penalties.

New slogans about inclusive cities.

But there's a much simpler test.

Put someone in a wheelchair at one end of a residential block.

Ask them to reach the other end without entering the roadway, requiring assistance, or encountering an impassable obstacle.

Then repeat the experiment across the neighborhood.

That tells you far more about a city's actual commitment to accessibility than a hundred pages of regulations.

Because accessibility isn't a legal declaration.

It's a physical reality.

And if the infrastructure doesn't allow people to move safely, the existence of a law promising that they should be able to do so is cold comfort.

The Systemic Error

The photograph that started this discussion isn't remarkable because it shows an unusually bad sidewalk.

It's remarkable because the scene is so ordinary.

Millions of people encounter variations of this problem every day.

We've become so accustomed to broken, uneven, disconnected pedestrian infrastructure that we barely question why it exists.

We blame careless homeowners.

We blame poor workmanship.

We blame insufficient enforcement.

And sometimes those criticisms are justified.

But they miss the structural problem.

We have organized the maintenance of a continuous public transportation network as thousands of disconnected private obligations.

Then we act surprised when the resulting infrastructure is disconnected.

The problem isn't simply that people fail to follow the rules.

It's that the rules themselves create a fragmented system for delivering something that must function as a unified whole.

A sidewalk shouldn't become unusable because you've crossed an invisible property boundary.

A person's ability to move safely through a city shouldn't depend on whether the next homeowner has enough money for concrete.

And governments shouldn't be able to congratulate themselves for passing accessibility legislation while leaving the practical delivery of accessibility to a patchwork of private actors.

We would never accept that arrangement for a major roadway.

We shouldn't accept it for the people walking beside one.

Public infrastructure deserves public accountability.

And a sidewalk is public infrastructure.

It's time we started treating it that way.
