One single Trump decision is the direct cause of your shrinking paycheck

Based on an article by Dean Baker

Paulo SantosOctober 21, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet attributes the renewed squeeze on US paychecks to President Donald Trump’s decision to attack Iran at the end of February. The outlet reports that Iran retaliated by blocking oil shipments through the Strait of Hormuz, driving up energy, transportation, and fertilizer costs while wage growth slowed.

According to the article, year-over-year wholesale inflation reached 6.3 percent in September, while wage growth fell from 4.0 percent in 2023 and 2024 to 3.0 percent in the latest data. AlterNet argues that consumer prices are therefore likely to keep rising—not necessarily by 6.3 percent—as businesses pass along some of their higher costs.

The article also says inflation had already been pushed upward by purchases made in anticipation of Trump’s tariffs. Its central claim, however, is that the attack on Iran transformed that pressure into a broader inflationary shock, causing prices to outpace wages and reducing workers’ purchasing power.

The decision belongs to the president

The institutional power here sits with the president who chose military escalation. Consumers did not raise the price of oil. Workers did not obstruct a major shipping route. Businesses stocking imported goods ahead of tariffs were responding to policy, not creating it.

AlterNet says Trump and Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu wanted the attack, while also arguing that its stated nuclear rationale was unsound. That judgment is the outlet’s. The concrete political point does not require speculation about motive: Trump authorized a military action, Iran retaliated, and the resulting oil disruption spread costs through an economy dependent on fuel-intensive production and transportation.

Inflation is being used to hide causation

Republican messaging benefits when inflation is treated as an atmospheric condition—something that simply descends on a country without identifiable decisions behind it. That framing erases agency. The reported sequence is not mysterious: anticipated tariffs altered purchasing and prices; military escalation disrupted oil supplies; higher energy costs moved into wholesale prices.

The comparison with the Biden-era inflation surge matters because it exposes a deliberate collapse of unlike events into one political slogan. AlterNet describes the earlier spike as part of a worldwide pandemic reopening, when production struggled to meet rapidly returning demand. It reports that US inflation peaked at 9.0 percent in June 2022, fell to 3.1 percent a year later, and reached 2.6 percent by the November 2024 election. Whatever criticism Biden’s recovery package warrants, that chronology does not absolve Trump of responsibility for later policies that reversed the decline.

Workers absorb the damage

A paycheck can rise in dollars and still shrink in value. If wages grow by 3.0 percent while the prices workers pay accelerate faster, real purchasing power falls. That is the mechanism behind the headline—not a literal reduction in every worker’s nominal pay.

The distributional consequence is political, not incidental. AlterNet reports that wage growth is not matching the new inflation and argues that higher prices are feeding profits rather than compensating labor. Workers bear the loss through groceries, fuel, and transported goods, while firms with pricing power can protect margins and asset owners remain better insulated.

Fertilizer costs extend the damage beyond the immediate energy shock. The article says Gulf countries account for substantial fertilizer production and warns that the larger effect on crops may arrive the following year. A military decision can therefore keep extracting money from households long after the first headlines disappear.

Risk is socialized, power is not

The administration held the authority to impose tariffs and initiate military action. Households received neither a vote on those decisions nor protection from their economic fallout. Yet they are expected to manage the consequences privately: drive less, buy less, accept smaller real wages, and treat deteriorating living standards as personal budgeting failures.

That is the larger pattern. Political leaders exercise concentrated power, donors and profitable firms are positioned to capture gains, and the public is handed diffuse costs disguised as an economic puzzle. The misdirection works only if policy choices are stripped of authorship.

The system protects the decision-maker

This story is ultimately about how executive power converts geopolitical choices into domestic hardship without requiring the decision-maker to bear the price. The attack may have occurred abroad, but the reported consequences arrive through every supply chain that uses oil, diesel, or fertilizer.

Calling the result “inflation” is economically accurate but politically incomplete. Inflation names the symptom. The relevant cause, according to AlterNet’s account, was a chain of identifiable decisions made by officials with the power to choose differently. When responsibility is obscured, workers are blamed for failing to endure costs imposed from above—and those who created the shock remain free to market themselves as its cure.
