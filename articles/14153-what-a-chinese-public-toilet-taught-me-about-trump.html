What a Chinese public toilet taught me about Trump

By Paulo Santos

I went to law school at night because I had a day job working for, believe it or not, a Republican governor. Gov. Robert D. Orr walked the talk, and I respected him.

Orr was such a big believer in spreading democracy through global commerce, he forged an early, formal trade relationship with China. Indiana’s partnership with Zhejiang Province promoted economic growth between the two regions, and the trade relationships it nurtured continue to this day.

Although I didn’t know it at the time, Gov. Orr was also a dinosaur. He was one of the last principled Republicans to walk the halls of government.

I have missed real Republicans since their tragic mass extinction, and mourn their disappearance. I sometimes leaf through All my friends are dead, a book about the last dinosaur, in their memory.

What I learned in China

I left Indiana to finish law school at the East China University of Political Science and Law in Shanghai. Studying the Chinese Commercial Code paid off, in that I could fake it enough to get a job later, but the most valuable lessons I learned came from outside the classroom.

First, I learned that communists in China placed more value on public surveillance than on public health. This was the 1990s, when Chinese people were overly plentiful, so it’s possible Deng Xiaoping didn’t much care if his people died young, fewer mouths to feed and all.

The government’s dismissive attitude toward — and its failure to fund — public health manifested most clearly in China’s lack of plumbing. Their public toilets were nasty affairs, consisting of holes carved in wooden planks that hovered over a long, shared, communal trough that smelled like the color of necrosis.

The telling contrast with these non-funded public toilets was this: Xiaoping didn’t care enough about public health to modernize China’s plumbing, but he cared mightily, some might say obsessively, about what Chinese citizens read in the privacy of their homes.

As foreign students, we’d been told to bring inexpensive gifts for new friends we’d meet. Chinese youth loved Americans back then, so they wanted gifts reflecting Americana: Levis 501s, packs of Marlboros, CDs they couldn’t yet play.

More than any of these things, however, the young Chinese we met wanted western magazines. Sports, fashion, or cars, the subject didn’t matter, Chinese students wanted to study them to improve their English, and to glance at life in the west.

But a few days after these exchanges started, we found out that every magazine and newspaper we gave them was confiscated by the government. As soon as we dispersed at night, authorities crawled out of the woodwork to take each and every magazine, every brochure, every piece of paper we shared. Not one Chinese student was allowed to look at them because their Communist government did not want them to know what the world looked like outside China.

Cocky law students that we were, we asked our new friends how many government spies were watching us at any given moment, and how many descended on them every night to confiscate the Rolling Stones we gave them. We calculated the costs nationwide, assuming starvation wages, and deduced that Xiaoping’s government was spending three times what it would have cost to modernize China’s plumbing, just to pay spies whose only job, whose only purpose, was to keep Chinese citizens uninformed.

It was a lesson in autocracy I will never forget.

Today we see similar upside down priorities parading in the Trump administration. In executive orders that would make Xi Jinping proud, Trump hints at siccing the military on US citizens, and announces that federal agents will now scour the social media pages of every student coming into the country. We don’t have spies snatching magazines, but Fox News keeps 45% of the country uninformed while Trump tries to put other networks out of business.

In his Big Beautiful Bill, Trump wants to spend a trillion dollars on the military while gutting medical research, education, and healthcare. Trump wants to let an alleged alcoholic who can’t shut up about “lethality” manage a trillion-dollar defense budget. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and Trump keep flexing empty-brain machismo, dreaming of 19th-century conquests despite Trump stressing isolationism. What’s the purpose of a trillion-dollar military when we’re shirking our national security alliances, including in Ukraine? Gold trinkets in the sky and ego-stroke parades are expensive, but they’re not that expensive.

Venom of the red scorpion

The second lesson I learned in China wasn’t about plumbing or spying. It was about science.

The university had wide, white marble stairs that curved around to the first floor, where the base opened to the front desk. I was walking down the stairs when I almost stepped on a blood red scorpion. I don’t mean russet or sepia toned, I mean blood red, like a crayon in a primary color box. I was spooked by its aggressively curled tail, so I motioned the guy at the front desk, and within nano-seconds, a least 20 scientists were positioned in a circle around the creature, two and three lab coats deep.

I later learned that scorpion venom is one of the most rare and precious materials in the world. As a medical compound, Stanford University’s Richard Zare said it would “cost $39 million to produce a gallon of it.” Biotech scientists have only recently developed applications using the venom for pain management and pain blocking, immunosuppressive therapies, and drugs to treat glioma, leukemia, human neuroblastoma, brain tumor, melanoma, prostate cancer, and breast cancer.

My take away was that scientists in China are serious people. They immediately recognized the medicinal value of the scorpion, as well as the research gift that had landed in their laps. Again, the contrasts with Trump’s America sting.

University scientists in China are making people healthier. Research scientists in the US are looking for a job. In China, science is revered. In Trump’s America, science is silenced.

Just this week, Chinese scientists enabled paralyzed patients to walk again using a world-first brain-spinal implant. Just this week in the US, advanced clinical trials on everything from cancer to dementia were defunded.

While Trump is trying to ruin universities and end scientific research, letting China pull ahead, he’s expanding the use of the military to “enforce the law” and quadrupling federal resources for prisons and detention-related facilities. Just like the authoritarians in China, instead of taking care of the nation’s health, Trump is expending resources to surveil us.

But if a red scorpion appeared on the steps of the Capitol, Trump would stomp on it, brag about how brave he was, and blame Joe Biden if it stung him.

I never saw a scorpion as metaphor, till now.

    Our Analysis:

    The Dichotomy of Governance: A Tale of Two Ideologies

    The article presented offers a stark and vivid contrast between the political and ideological landscapes of China in the 1990s and the United States under the Trump administration. The author, Sabrina Haake, draws upon her personal experiences and observations to critique the prioritization of governmental controls over public welfare and scientific advancement. However, while the narrative is compelling, it's essential to dissect the underlying themes and assertions with a critical eye, grounded in fact and devoid of unwarranted generalizations.

    The Chinese Conundrum: Surveillance Over Sanitation

    Haake's recounting of her time in China provides a grim depiction of a government prioritizing surveillance and information control over basic public health needs, such as modern plumbing. This portrayal taps into broader criticisms of autocratic regimes — their tendency to suppress freedoms for the sake of stability and control. The juxtaposition of the Chinese government's investment in a vast network of spies against its negligence towards public sanitation is both shocking and telling. It serves as a potent reminder of the skewed priorities that can emerge when a state values control over the well-being of its citizens.

    However, it's crucial to recognize the evolution of China since the 1990s. While the country remains under the tight grip of the Communist Party, economic and infrastructural developments have significantly improved the quality of life for many Chinese citizens. Thus, while Haake's experiences offer valuable insights into China's past, they may not fully represent the complexities of its present.

    Trump's America: Echoes of Autocracy

    The transition from discussing China to critiquing the Trump administration is where Haake's argument becomes particularly scathing. She draws parallels between the Chinese government's tactics and Trump's policies, suggesting that the latter aspires to the same level of control and suppression. The comparison is bold—equating executive orders hinting at military deployment against U.S. citizens and the surveillance of students' social media to China's autocratic governance.

    While the critique of the Trump administration's prioritization of military spending over health, education, and scientific research is well-founded and grounded in observable policy decisions, the comparison to an authoritarian regime should not be made lightly. The assertion that Trump's actions could make "Xi Jinping proud" is a sharp commentary on the perceived erosion of democratic norms. Yet, it's essential to maintain a clear distinction between the operational mechanisms of a democratic nation, where checks and balances still function, and an autocratic state, where dissent is systematically suppressed.

    The Red Scorpion Metaphor

    Haake's encounter with the red scorpion and the subsequent scientific interest it sparked in China serves as a powerful metaphor for the differing attitudes towards science in China and Trump's America. The reverence for scientific discovery and its potential societal benefits contrasts sharply with the depiction of a U.S. administration that seemingly silences and sidelines scientific expertise and research. This metaphor effectively highlights the importance of fostering a culture that values and invests in science as a means to advance public health and welfare.

    Conclusion

    Sabrina Haake offers a compelling narrative that underscores the dangers of prioritizing governmental control and militarization over public health and scientific advancement. While her comparisons between China and the Trump administration are provocative, they also risk oversimplification. The reality is often more nuanced, and the challenges of governance and policy-making in any context are complex.

    In a world where the balance between security and freedom, control and welfare, is perpetually in flux, Haake's article serves as a catalyst for reflection on the values and priorities that should guide our leaders. However, as we engage with such comparisons, it's vital to remain meticulous and discerning, ensuring that our critiques are as grounded in reality as they are sharp.
