Trump's surgeon general pick scrambles to blame Dem senator as she's accused of corruption

Casey Means, nominated to serve as the next U.S. Surgeon General, testifies before a Senate Health, Education, Labor, and Pensions Committee confirmation hearing on Capitol Hill in Washington, D.C. on Feb. 25, 2026. REUTERS/Kylie Cooper

President Donald Trump’s nominee for surgeon general, Casey Means, claimed that a Democratic lawmaker’s staff had manipulated data concerning her ties to wellness products she has endorsed and her financial connections to those companies.

Sen. Chris Murphy (D-CT) accused Means of not disclosing her endorsed social media posts — and she criticized his team in response.

“Dr. Means, are you familiar with [Federal Trade Commission] policy that requires those who are recommending products online to disclose their financial connection to those companies?” Murphy asked.

“In particular, the document from the FTC says this, ‘If you endorse a product through social media, your endorsement message should make it obvious when you have a relationship with the brand.’ Are you familiar with that?”

Means replied, “Certainly.”

Murphy mentioned the pending complaint “regarding your failure to adhere to those guidelines, that basically makes the contention and this committee has verified the data that underlies their complaint, that you routinely violated this policy and that in fact, in the majority of your posts for many of the products you recommend, you did not transparently reveal your financial connection.”

“That’s false,” Means said.

Murphy brought up Means’ connection to WeNatal, a prenatal vitamin company, which Means’ filings show she received compensation for in spring 2024.

“And yet in September of 2024, you posted a video saying you ‘had no financial relationship to the company — just a big fan,’” Murphy said. And then in October, you said, ‘not sponsored, just love these.’ But in fact you have documentation before this committee that showed, when you said those things, you had a financial relationship. You had already started receiving money from that company.”

Means denied receiving partnership fees for the product promotion.

“It’s incorrect and it’s a false representation,” Means said. “And just to be very clear, I’ve spent the last several months working with the Office of Government and Ethics to be fully compliant with this process. I take it very seriously.”

Murphy listed other companies Means has had reported ties to, including Genova Diagnostics, Daily Harvest and Zen Basil Seeds, and claimed she had not consistently disclosed her financial connections to the companies.

“This seems systemic. It seems that in the majority of instances, in which you were, as a medical professional recommending a product, you were hiding the fact that you had a financial partnership,” Murphy added. “You seem to be in regular, willful violation of the FTC rules. That is concerning as someone who agrees with Sen. Cassidy that our focus has to be on restoring trust in the medical profession and yet, over and over again, you seem to be at scale, recommending products without telling your followers.”

Murphy also pointed out that Means has a large following — 200,000 newsletter subscribers and nearly 1 million Instagram followers.

“It sounds like you have a lot to say about this issue and I would be very interested to see how your staff looked at this data,” Means said. “I have a strong feeling that the way in which they gathered this data is done intentionally to create these claims that you’re making.”

Murphy responded, saying he had the backup information to the claims. But Means pushed back again on his remarks.

“I don’t think that’s true,” Means said. “And if it inadvertently has happened, I would rectify that immediately. However, I would be interested to see how your staff gathered this data... I take conflict of interests very seriously.

Our Analysis:

Dissecting the Surgeon General Nominee’s Denials: A Case of Systemic Deception?

In a recent Senate hearing, President Donald Trump’s nominee for surgeon general, Casey Means, found herself embroiled in a contentious exchange with Sen. Chris Murphy (D-CT) over allegations of misleading endorsements and undisclosed financial ties. This episode not only sheds light on Means’ credibility but also casts a long shadow over the ethical landscape of influencer marketing in the medical field.

Murphy’s Accusations: Unveiling a Pattern of Deception

Senator Murphy’s interrogation of Means was not just a probing; it was a surgical dissection of a pattern of behavior that seems to systematically breach Federal Trade Commission (FTC) guidelines. The FTC mandates crystal clear transparency about any financial relationships when endorsing products on social media. Means, however, appeared to weave a narrative of innocence and ignorance that crumbles under scrutiny.

Casey Means’ Counter-Claims: A Deflection or Denial?

Means’ responses to the accusations were a cocktail of denial, deflection, and the all-too-familiar promise of immediate rectification “if” any inadvertent mistakes were made. The use of the word “if” is particularly telling—it introduces doubt where there should be accountability. It’s not about if the guidelines were violated; the issue at hand is the systemic failure to adhere to established ethical standards.

The WeNatal Contradiction: A Financial Faux Pas?

The mention of WeNatal, a prenatal vitamin company, from which Means received compensation, yet publicly denied any financial ties, is particularly egregious. This isn’t just a minor oversight; it’s a glaring contradiction that questions the very foundation of Means’ ethical compass. To claim to be “just a big fan” of a product while pocketing money from the same is not just misleading—it’s deceitful.

The Bigger Picture: Restoring Trust in the Medical Profession

Senator Murphy’s concern about restoring trust in the medical profession is not just rhetoric; it’s a clarion call for integrity. When medical professionals, who wield influence over public health decisions, engage in undisclosed paid endorsements, they erode the bedrock of trust and credibility that the profession stands on. Means’ alleged “willful violation” of FTC rules is not a trivial matter; it’s a symptom of a larger problem of transparency and ethics in the intersection of medicine and marketing.

Means’ Defense: A Questionable Stand

Means’ defense, claiming a rigorous compliance process with the Office of Government and Ethics, rings hollow in the face of documented discrepancies. Her attempt to question the methodology of Murphy’s staff in gathering data is a classic diversion tactic. When caught in the headlights of accountability, deflecting scrutiny onto the process rather than addressing the substance of the accusations is a well-worn path of those with something to hide.

Conclusion: A Systemic Error in Judgment

The exchange between Senator Murphy and Casey Means is a microcosm of a larger issue at hand—the need for unwavering ethical standards in the endorsement of health-related products by medical professionals. Means’ alleged actions and her responses to the accusations reveal a troubling disregard for the transparency required by both the FTC and the ethical standards of the medical profession. This incident should not just fade into the next news cycle; it should prompt a serious reevaluation of how medical endorsements are regulated and enforced. The health of the public, and the trust they place in medical professionals, demands nothing less.
