Gloves off: Warning Trump’s second term is about to get way more tumultuous

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 31, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power is in the White HouseRepublicans want distance, not restraintUnpredictability is a misleading frameElections as temporary disciplineThe system rewards evasion

What happened

The warning is that President Donald Trump may intensify tariffs and military pressure on Iran after the midterm elections. AlterNet, summarizing Politico, reports that Republican officials, industry representatives, congressional aides, and current and former officials expect a more volatile second half of Trump’s term, including potentially higher tariffs.

Politico reported that Trump wanted tariffs restored near levels preceding a February Supreme Court ruling that invalidated many of them. It also reported that new trade measures were being prepared, the Pentagon was sending additional forces to the Middle East, and Trump had suggested further action involving Iran.

A White House official called discussion of future tariff rates “baseless speculation” and said announcements would come from the administration. According to Politico, the White House did not answer separate questions about its post-midterm Iran plans. The reporting therefore establishes preparations and expectations—not a confirmed post-election decision.

The power is in the White House

Trump holds the decisive institutional power described here. His administration can initiate trade measures, direct foreign policy, and deploy military resources, subject to legal and congressional constraints. Consumers, border-state businesses, and Republican candidates can absorb the consequences, but they do not control those decisions.

That distinction matters because the story’s anonymous voices describe turmoil as though it were approaching like weather. It is not weather. Any new duties would be imposed by the administration. Any further military action would result from choices made by officials empowered to order it.

Republicans want distance, not restraint

The reported Republican response is political self-preservation. Candidates in competitive races, including in Michigan, sought distance from tariffs affecting trade with Canada. One Michigan Republican said the tariffs were “hammering us,” while a House Republican argued that Midwestern candidates might win despite them.

But distancing rhetoric is not institutional opposition. The article provides no evidence that these Republicans successfully constrained Trump’s tariff policy or the reported military buildup. Their objection, as presented, is largely that the policies could cost Republicans elections—not that imposing broad costs by executive choice is itself an abuse of power.

Unpredictability is a misleading frame

Calling the prospective agenda “tumultuous” or “unpredictable” obscures what the reporting actually describes: identifiable officials considering identifiable actions with foreseeable economic effects. Higher tariffs can raise costs for importers and consumers. Escalation involving Iran can push oil and gasoline prices upward and expose people to far graver consequences than electoral discomfort.

The precise scale of those effects remains uncertain, and the article does not establish that every price increase it mentions resulted solely from Trump’s decisions. But uncertainty about magnitude is not confusion about agency. The administration would own the choice to escalate.

Elections as temporary discipline

The most revealing allegation is that electoral pressure moderated Trump before the midterms and that its removal could free him to act more aggressively afterward. Politico’s sources did not describe a president reconsidering policy after evidence of public harm. They described one potentially delaying or softening action to protect congressional majorities.

That interpretation remains a forecast from unnamed and interested sources, not a proven account of Trump’s motives. Still, it exposes the governing culture surrounding him: Republican and industry insiders apparently regard public accountability as a temporary tactical constraint rather than a reason to change course.

The system rewards evasion

The larger pattern is executive power paired with partisan cowardice. The White House retains room to act, congressional Republicans complain when consequences reach their districts, and anonymous insiders translate deliberate policy choices into warnings about volatility.

That arrangement lets powerful actors evade responsibility at every stage. The administration can dismiss advance reporting as speculation. Republican candidates can posture as bystanders. Industry officials can predict damage without attaching their names. Then consumers and communities are left to experience political decisions as an inexplicable crisis instead of the foreseeable product of who held power, what they chose, and who declined to stop them.
