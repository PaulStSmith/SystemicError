'You've bitten me!' Trump's closest ally gets warning that he's about to be burned

British Prime Minister Keir Starmer needs to take heed of Donald Trump’s favorite poem, he was warned Thursday.

“The Snake,” which tells of a half-frozen reptile that is helped by a “tender-hearted woman” before it betrays her with a vicious bite — has been frequently recited by the U.S. president, who appears to consider it a truth about human interactions, wrote Guardian opinion writer Aditya Chakrabortty.

And, as Trump rambled over his grievances for 55 minutes at the United Nations Tuesday, the danger was clearly messaged to Starmer, she wrote.

“Watching his extraordinary speech this week at the UN, I thought again of The Snake,” she wrote.

“It is the perfect depiction of the dynamic between Trump and the British establishment – a relationship where Trump is the vicious serpent.

“Last week, he was Keir Starmer’s most honored guest, enjoying a banquet at Windsor Castle and wooed by King Charles as ‘the closest of kin.’ One return flight later, Trump tore into his host – ‘I hope the prime minister’s listening’ – and lumped the UK among those Old World s---holes ‘going to hell.’”

Starmer, backed up by Trump, has frequently bragged about their close relationship — and friendship, even inviting Trump for an unprecedented second state visit to the UK last week.

A recently announced US-UK tech deal, touted as “record-breaking” by Downing Street, represents another manifestation of this serpentine dynamic, she wrote. Cecilia Rikap, an AI expert at University College London, described the emerging data centers as “American military bases on British soil.”

Britain’s previous Deputy Prime Minister Nick Clegg gave a similar warning, saying that the UK risks becoming a “vassal state” and underscoring the poem’s underlying message, Chakrabortty wrote. The massive technology investments are not merely financial transactions, but strategic infiltrations.

“If Labour [Party] members and other voters knew what had actually been signed in their names they would be angered, rather than heartened,” she wrote.

“Far closer to the truth is to imagine a snake in skin of stars and stripes, wrapping itself ever tighter around your land, your data, your water supply and your electricity pylons – all the while claiming it’s for your own good.”

Chakrabortty concluded with another reference to The Snake.

“These rhymes are the very essence of Trumpism: never let outsiders get too close, or you’ll be repaid in venom,” she wrote.

“No hugs, just extraction. It’s how he sees business, politics and, naturally, immigration.”

Our Analysis:

Unpacking the Allegorical Caution: “The Snake” and UK-US Dynamics

Aditya Chakrabortty’s Guardian opinion piece deftly interweaves Donald Trump’s favored allegory, “The Snake,” into a piercing critique of British Prime Minister Keir Starmer’s cozying up to the former U.S. President. The article is less an exposition on Trump’s rhetorical motifs than it is a stark warning to the UK establishment, with Starmer at its helm, to reconsider its diplomatic dalliances and economic entanglements with Trump’s America. Here’s why Chakrabortty’s argument holds water, or rather, venom.

The Allegory’s Essence: At the heart of Chakrabortty’s piece is the poem “The Snake,” which Trump has paradoxically embraced as a cautionary tale against benevolence towards the unfamiliar or foreign. The poem’s narrative arc—of a woman punished by the very creature she saved—serves as a potent metaphor for the geopolitical dynamics between the UK and Trump’s America. The writer casts Trump as the “vicious serpent,” a depiction rich with implications, given his track record of transactional relationships and abrupt policy reversals.

Starmer’s Misjudged Hospitality: The crux of the analysis points to a glaring misstep by Keir Starmer—his extension of an “unprecedented second state visit” to Trump. This, coupled with claims of a close relationship, is portrayed as naiveté at best, and at worst, a hazardous gamble with the nation’s dignity and autonomy. The immediate aftermath of this hospitality, as noted, was Trump’s public disparagement of the UK, an act emblematic of the serpent’s bite.

The US-UK Tech Deal: A Trojan Horse?: The so-called “record-breaking” tech deal between the US and UK is scrutinized not for its economic merits but for its strategic implications. Chakrabortty leans on expert opinion to paint this agreement as less of a partnership and more of an “infiltration” by American interests into the UK’s sovereign resources. The analogy of American data centers to “military bases on British soil” is stark and serves to highlight the potential loss of control over critical national infrastructure.

The Vassal State Warning: Echoing former Deputy Prime Minister Nick Clegg’s caution, the article suggests that the UK is at risk of becoming a “vassal state” to the US. This is a powerful assertion, suggesting that the UK’s sovereignty and decision-making capabilities could be significantly compromised under the guise of strategic partnerships and investments.

Chakrabortty’s Final Verdict: The closing of the article ties back to “The Snake” allegory, framing Trump’s worldview as one of extraction and opportunism, devoid of genuine camaraderie or cooperation. This is a scathing indictment of not just Trump’s approach to international relations but also a critique of the UK’s foreign policy posture under Starmer’s leadership.

In conclusion, Aditya Chakrabortty’s article serves as a compelling critique wrapped in allegory, urging a reevaluation of the UK’s diplomatic and economic ties with Trump’s America. It’s a cautionary tale that warns of the dangers of mistaking transient gestures of friendship for enduring alliances, especially when dealing with figures known for their capriciousness. The lesson here is clear: when dealing with serpents, one must always beware of the bite.
