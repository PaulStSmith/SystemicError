'Suck up': Elizabeth Warren comes out swinging as former allies court billionaires

By Paulo Santos

U.S. Sen. Elizabeth Warren speaking with attendees at the Clark County Democratic Party’s 2020 Kick Off to Caucus Gala at the Tropicana Las Vegas in Las Vegas, Nevada. (Photo credit: Gage Skidmore)

Zohran Mamdani crushed the Democratic establishment to become NYC's mayoral nominee, winning the primary by focusing on the affordability crisis faced by many New York residents. But his grassroots victory has sent shockwaves through the billionaire class — and two so-called Democrats are busy sucking up to them instead of listening to the people, Sen. Elizabeth Warren (D-MA) blasted on Monday.

Billionaire investor Bill Ackman called on former Democratic governor Andrew Cuomo—now an independent opponent of Mamdani's—to step aside from the New York City mayoral race and allow incumbent Eric Adams—also now running as in independent—to take on democratic socialist Mamdani in November.

The hedge fund mogul has reportedly promised to spend "hundreds of millions of dollars" to defeat the working-class champion, Warren wrote.

"You don’t have to look very hard to see just how out of touch with working people and the mainstream values of the Democratic Party Adams and Cuomo have become," she wrote in a Rolling Stone column.

"Adams withdrew from the primary early on, and Cuomo felt so entitled to the nomination that he barely bothered to campaign. But they’ve both gotten the memo from their wealthy backers to step it up."

Why are Wall Street titans so terrified, she asked? Because Mamdani threatens their stranglehold on the city. While Adams switched to independent in April a few months after federal corruption charges were dismissed, and Cuomo is pursuing a political comeback after he resigned as governor in 2021 amid sexual harassment allegations, Mamdani represents something they can't buy: authentic populist rage, Warren wrote.

The 33-year-old democratic socialist has outlined radical plans to build 200,000 affordable housing units, implement rent control, and provide universal childcare. His promise to tax the ultra-rich and giant corporations to fund these initiatives has the billionaire donor class in full panic mode.

Real estate developers such as RXR Realty, Two Trees and Vornado ponied up checks in the $150,000 to $250,000 range to stop Mamdani during the primary. Cuomo's donors include Laurie Tisch, a billionaire philanthropist among others flooding money into the race.

The message is crystal clear: when working families finally get a champion willing to take on corporate power, the billionaires unleash their war chests, Warren wrote.

"Mamdani has charged ahead with plans to make New York more affordable, and he’s showing how to pay for it by taxing the ultra-rich and giant corporations," the senator wrote.

"That may not make him popular with the richest New Yorkers, but he’s willing to let Adams and Cuomo suck up to those guys. Mamdani is fighting to make New York work for working people."

Our Analysis:

Mamdani's Victory: A Beacon of Hope or a Threat to the Billionaire Class?

In a political climate that often feels as if it's crafted by the highest bidder, Zohran Mamdani's triumph in the New York City mayoral primary isn't just a win; it's a seismic event that's rattling the golden cages of the billionaire class. The narrative unfolding is one of a classic power struggle: the people versus the plutocrats. Senator Elizabeth Warren's scathing critique in a Rolling Stone column of so-called Democrats pandering to the wealthy, while a true progressive like Mamdani pushes for radical change, encapsulates this battle perfectly.

The Billionaires' Panic

Bill Ackman's rallying cry for former Governor Andrew Cuomo to bow out of the mayoral race to consolidate efforts against Mamdani is telling. The hedge fund mogul's readiness to pour "hundreds of millions of dollars" to thwart Mamdani underscores a panicked elite. Why such fear? Because Mamdani represents the antithesis of their interests: a politician who dares to dream of a city that caters to its working-class citizens rather than its gilded residents.

The Adams-Cuomo Spectacle

Eric Adams and Andrew Cuomo's roles in this saga are particularly illuminating. Both, having distanced themselves from the Democratic primary, signal a disturbing trend of political figures sidelining democratic processes in favor of backdoor machinations and elite endorsements. Adams' and Cuomo's apparent indifference to grassroots campaigning, as Warren points out, speaks volumes about their disconnect from the average New Yorker's daily struggles.

A Populist Rage They Can't Buy

At the heart of this tumult is Mamdani's audacious policy propositions: 200,000 affordable housing units, rent control, and universal childcare, financed by taxing the ultra-rich and corporate behemoths. In a city where the affordability crisis is not just a talking point but a lived reality for millions, these are not radical ideas but necessary interventions.

The Donors' Dilemma

The response from the billionaire donor class to Mamdani's primary victory is as predictable as it is disheartening. Real estate titans and billionaire philanthropists opening their coffers to stop a candidate focused on the working class is a stark illustration of how deeply money has infiltrated our political system. The message is unmistakable: the ultra-wealthy will spare no expense to maintain their grip on power.

Conclusion: A Fight for New York's Soul

As Mamdani gears up for the general election, his campaign is more than a political contest; it's a fight for the soul of New York City. With the billionaire class rallying against him, the stakes could not be higher. But perhaps, in this David versus Goliath battle, Goliath has reason to fear. Mamdani's vision for New York—a city that prioritizes its people over its penthouses—is a call to arms for anyone who believes in the power of democracy over dollars. As Warren aptly notes, while Mamdani may not curry favor with New York's richest, he's proving that the heart of the city beats not in its luxury lofts, but in the homes of its working people.
