But this freakout is even more embarrassing given that a task force had been working to improve BLS data accuracy, and the Trump administration disbanded it, conservative National Review writer Dominic Pino explained in a lengthy thread posted to X.

"Mad about jobs report inaccuracy?" wrote Pino. "One reason it has gotten harder in recent years is declining survey response rates. There was an unpaid advisory group of statistical experts that was working on solving that problem at its last meeting... before Howard Lutnick disbanded it."

This group, known as the Federal Economic Statistics Advisory Committee, "was established in 1999 to advise the Bureau of Labor Statistics, Bureau of Economic Analysis, and the Census Bureau, on technical methodology issues," and consisted of 15 unpaid volunteer experts from think tanks and academic institutions.

The committee held two in-person meetings each year, the last of which occurred Dec. 13, 2024, where they talked about how to improve survey response rates, wrote Pino. Falling rates on the BLS establishment survey, which asks employers how many people they employ and is used for the jobs report, has been a "major impediment to achieving greater accuracy," he said, as the response rate has plunged since the COVID-19 pandemic and no one knows how to reverse it.

"FESAC members at the December meeting heard from officials at statistical agencies in the U.K., Canada, and Germany who had all tried different things to improve response rates, with varying levels of success," wrote Pino. "In other words, this advisory group was doing exactly what one should want the government to do to improve its service to the public: Asking questions about what works based on experience and trying to formulate a better process."

Lutnick, he noted, ended the program in February, stating that "the purposes for which FESAC was established has been fulfilled" and provided no further reason.

"Was FESAC going to completely solve the long-running problem of lower response rates? No. But having it is better than not having it, and at little to no cost to the taxpayer, Lutnick did not need to disband it," Pino concluded. "Now, the Trump administration is mad about the exact thing FESAC was working on during its final meeting."

Our Analysis:

Trump's BLS Director Firing: A Masterclass in Irony and Ineptitude

In an audacious display of what can only be described as political theater at its most absurd, President Donald Trump has once again showcased his unparalleled ability to deflect responsibility and blame. Reacting to a disastrous jobs report, Trump took the drastic step of firing the director of the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), hurling baseless accusations that the data was fabricated by so-called anti-MAGA forces. This act of scapegoating, however, plunges into the depths of irony when considering the Trump administration's own role in undermining the accuracy of the very data he now maligns.

Dominic Pino, a writer for the conservative outlet National Review, uncovers this hypocrisy in a detailed exposition. Pino points to the disbanding of the Federal Economic Statistics Advisory Committee (FESAC) by Trump's administration as a critical self-inflicted wound. FESAC, an unpaid advisory group consisting of 15 volunteer experts, was instrumental in advising on technical methodology issues to enhance the accuracy of economic data. Their last meeting, held on December 13, 2024, focused on addressing the declining survey response rates—a major impediment to accurate economic reporting.

Yet, in a move that reeks of shortsightedness, Howard Lutnick, presumably at the behest of the administration, disbanded FESAC in February, claiming its purposes had been fulfilled. This claim is laughably disingenuous, considering the plummeting response rates since the COVID-19 pandemic—an issue FESAC was actively seeking to ameliorate.

The implications of such a decision are twofold. Firstly, it strips away any pretense of the Trump administration's commitment to factual accuracy and economic transparency. Secondly, and perhaps more damningly, it illustrates a profound misunderstanding of governance and public service. The advisory group was a cost-effective means of leveraging expert knowledge to improve governmental functions—a no-brainer in terms of public policy.

Pino rightly lambasts the administration for its actions, highlighting the absurdity of being "mad about the exact thing FESAC was working on during its final meeting." This situation is a textbook example of political self-sabotage, where the administration's actions directly undermine their own interests and those of the public.

In firing the BLS director and blaming phantom adversaries for the unfavorable jobs report, Trump has not only shown a petulant unwillingness to confront reality but also a staggering incompetence in stewarding the nation's economic data infrastructure. The dismantling of FESAC—an entity working to improve data accuracy, at little to no taxpayer cost—underscores a governance philosophy marred by myopia and a detachment from the principles of effective public administration.

In summary, the Trump administration's reaction to the jobs report and the preceding undermining of the BLS's accuracy is a stark illustration of its broader failures. It's a saga of missed opportunities, willful ignorance, and an abdication of responsibility. One cannot help but marvel at the administration's capacity for self-defeat, orchestrated with such brazen disregard for the consequences of its actions.
