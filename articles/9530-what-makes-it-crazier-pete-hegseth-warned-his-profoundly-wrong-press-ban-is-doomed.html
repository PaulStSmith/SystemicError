'What makes it crazier': Pete Hegseth warned his 'profoundly wrong' press ban is doomed

By Paulo Santos

Donald Trump and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth attend a cabinet meeting. REUTERS/Jonathan Ernst

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s plan to keep reporters out of the Pentagon, unless media organizations agree to an excessively stringent set of rules that would restrict what they can report on, is doomed to fail.

That is according to long-time Washington D.C. observer John Heilemann who told a “Morning Joe” panel that the end effect of the ban that goes into effect on Wednesday will only heighten scrutiny of what the embattled Donald Trump appointee tries to pull off behind closed doors.

With even Hegseth’s previous employer, conservative Fox News, balking at the move and releasing a statement saying, “The policy is without precedent and threatens core journalistic protections,” Heilemann predicted the new rules — should they survive the firestorm they have set off — will blow up in the Pentagon chief’s face.

With MSNBC host Joe Scarborough calling Hegseth’s plan “boneheaded,” and pointing out, “I’ve never heard of anything like this before in American history,” Heilemann contributed, “But here’s what makes it even crazier, is it’s it’s going to be futile. It will not work because anybody who covers, whether it’s the White House or the Defense Department, knows the reporters are not going to stop digging for scoops and not going to stop holding the Pentagon’s feet to the fire.”

“It’s not going to stop calling sources and looking for the $900 toilet seat, or looking for the Pentagon Papers,” he pointed out. “They’re just going to do it from outside the building. And every reporter who covers it is going to be like, you know, ‘This is a pain in the a--. I can’t go to the Pentagon briefing anymore, and I can’t sit with my sources in the Pentagon cafeteria, but I’m still going to talk to him. I’m going to talk to him off campus. I’m going to talk to him on chat. I’m going to talk to him on the phone. I’m going to talk to him on email.”

“It’s not going to work!” he asserted. “Apart from the fact that it’s profoundly wrong, it’s not going to have anything like the effect that Pete Hegseth thinks it’s going to have.”

“There’s another part of this too,” Scarborough interjected. “And that is Pete Hegseth is trying to stop the president of the United States from getting information that may not reflect well on him. How many times have we read throughout history the president of the United States reading the newspaper wherever he is and saying, ‘Wait. What’s going on at the Pentagon? What’s going on at Treasury? What’s going on?’”

Our Analysis:

Analyzing Hegseth’s Pentagon Press Ban: A Futile and Dangerous Endeavor

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s recent attempt to shut the press out of the Pentagon is not just a blatant affront to the principles of transparency and freedom of the press; it is a misguided strategy doomed to backfire. According to John Heilemann, a veteran observer of the Washington D.C. scene, this move, set to take effect on Wednesday, will only amplify the scrutiny on Hegseth, an already controversial figure appointed by Donald Trump.

The Unprecedented Nature of the Ban

What strikes most in this scenario is the unprecedented nature of the rules Hegseth is attempting to impose. When conservative stalwart Fox News—Hegseth’s former employer—publicly denounces the policy as a threat to “core journalistic protections,” it’s a clear sign that Hegseth has crossed a line that few have dared to approach. MSNBC host Joe Scarborough’s characterization of the plan as “boneheaded” and unparalleled in American history only adds to the chorus of disbelief and disapproval surrounding this decision.

Futile Efforts and Inevitable Outcomes

Heilemann’s insights into the futility of Hegseth’s plan are particularly telling. The notion that reporters, those perennial seekers of truth, would simply cease their investigations into the Pentagon’s workings is laughably naive. As Heilemann rightly points out, journalists will continue to dig, to probe, and to expose—regardless of whether they can physically access the Pentagon’s briefing rooms or cafeterias. The barriers Hegseth seeks to erect will only serve as minor inconveniences; the work of journalism will find a way around them.

Moreover, the assertion that this move could somehow prevent the President of the United States from obtaining crucial information about his own administration is both alarming and absurd. It paints a picture of a Defense Secretary not only at odds with the principles of a free press but also intent on creating a bubble of ignorance around the country’s highest office.

The Core of the Problem

At its heart, Hegseth’s plan is more than just a strategic misstep; it is a profoundly wrong attempt to manipulate the flow of information and to shield the Pentagon from scrutiny. This approach betrays a fundamental misunderstanding of the role of the press in a democratic society. The press serves not just as a conduit of information from the government to the public but as a crucial check on power.

The backlash from media organizations, commentators, and the public at large suggests that Hegseth’s plan will not only fail to achieve its intended effects but will also likely erode trust in his leadership and the transparency of the Pentagon under his watch. The attempt to curtail press freedom in such a manner is not only an affront to democratic principles but a clear indicator of a leadership style that is both authoritarian and ill-conceived.

In sum, Hegseth’s plan to restrict Pentagon access to the press is a misguided endeavor that will likely exacerbate the challenges he faces rather than mitigate them. It stands as a testament to the importance of vigilance in defending press freedoms against those who would seek to undermine them for their own ends.
