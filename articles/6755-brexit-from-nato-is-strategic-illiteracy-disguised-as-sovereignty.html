“Brexit from NATO” Is Strategic Illiteracy Disguised as Sovereignty

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJanuary 12, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The headline alone tells you everything you need to know: “It’s time for a Brexit from NATO.” Four words, each more unserious than the last. Not because alliances are sacred or immutable—they are neither—but because this argument mistakes petulance for policy and collapse for courage.

Britain already ran the Brexit experiment once. It was sold as national renewal, sovereign rebirth, and liberation from foreign constraint. What it delivered was diminished leverage, weaker trade power, and a country more dependent—not less—on the whims of larger actors. Proposing a second Brexit, this time from the single most stabilising security architecture in Europe, isn’t bold. It’s reckless repetition.

Trump Is the Problem — Not NATO

Let’s dispense with the central sleight of hand. NATO did not suddenly become obsolete. It is being actively undermined by the behaviour of its most powerful member, the United States, under Donald Trump.

Trump’s rhetoric about Greenland—an autonomous territory of Denmark—has been grotesque, irresponsible, and deliberately destabilising. Threatening to “acquire” allied territory by pressure or force is not alliance politics; it is gangster logic. But recognising that fact does not lead to the conclusion that NATO itself is the disease. It leads to the opposite conclusion: that alliances exist precisely to restrain the worst impulses of powerful states.

Walking away from NATO does not punish Washington. It rewards it. Inside the alliance, the United States is constrained by treaty obligations, norms, consultation mechanisms, and allied resistance. Outside the alliance, Britain would face the U.S. alone—without cover, without leverage, without backup. Fragmentation doesn’t weaken empires. It frees them.

The “Russia Is Weak” Fallacy

A recurring claim in this line of argument is that Russia poses no meaningful threat to Europe—that it can barely sustain its war in Ukraine, let alone threaten the Atlantic world. This is dangerously shallow thinking.

Power is not binary. Russia does not need to march tanks to the Channel to be destabilising. It exerts influence through energy coercion, cyber operations, political corruption, intelligence penetration, disinformation, and the deliberate cultivation of chaos. Calling this “hybrid warfare” as a substitute for real war misses the point entirely. It is real war—adapted to modern conditions.

NATO was never just about stopping armoured divisions. It was about deterrence, signalling, and collective response. The moment allies start declaring threats “over,” adversaries don’t retreat—they advance.

Brexit Already Answered This Question

The most damning part of the “Brexit from NATO” proposal is that we don’t have to speculate about how it would go. Britain already tested the underlying theory.

Brexit did not increase British sovereignty in any meaningful sense. It reduced Britain’s ability to shape outcomes. Outside the EU, Britain did not become freer; it became more exposed. Trade negotiations became asymmetric. Diplomatic clout shrank. Washington did not treat Britain as an equal partner—it treated it as a pliable one.

Repeating this logic in defence policy would be catastrophic. Security is not an area where you get to “learn by doing.” The margin for error is measured in deterrence failures, miscalculations, and human lives.

Sovereignty Is Capacity, Not Posture

Much ink is spilled invoking Palmerston and “eternal interests.” Fine. But Palmerston operated in a world where power was exercised unilaterally and empires acted alone. That world is gone.

Modern sovereignty is not about standing alone. It is about having the capacity to shape outcomes. That capacity is pooled, institutional, and networked. Middle powers do not gain influence by isolating themselves; they gain it by embedding themselves in systems they can shape.

Leaving NATO would not make Britain independent. It would make Britain marginal.

If NATO Is in Crisis, the Answer Is Reform — Not Flight

Yes, NATO faces a legitimacy crisis if its strongest member behaves like a predator rather than a guarantor. That crisis demands response. But the response is not to tear down the architecture—it is to rebalance it.

The real task is to strengthen NATO’s European pillar, reduce unilateral U.S. leverage within the alliance, and make alliance norms enforceable rather than optional. The goal should be to constrain Washington, not indulge its worst instincts by giving it fewer rules to break.

Quitting is not resistance. It is abdication.

“You Can’t Fire Me, I Quit” Is Not Foreign Policy

At its core, the call for a Brexit from NATO is emotional rather than analytical. It is the geopolitics of wounded pride. Trump behaves like a bully, therefore we should storm out and declare independence. That is not strategy; it is sulking elevated to doctrine.

History is not kind to states that confuse indignation with leverage. NATO is imperfect, strained, and under assault—but it remains the single most effective mechanism for preventing great-power conflict in Europe. Destroying it voluntarily would not be an act of sovereignty. It would be an act of strategic illiteracy.

The lesson of the past decade is brutally clear: walking away from shared institutions does not restore control. It forfeits it.

A second Brexit—this time from NATO—would not make Britain safer, freer, or more respected. It would make Britain smaller, weaker, and alone.
