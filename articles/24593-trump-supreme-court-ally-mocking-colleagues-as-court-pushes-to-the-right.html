Trump Supreme Court ally 'mocking' colleagues as court pushes to the right

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 10, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power is judicialAlito is making choicesPersonality is the distractionThe succession fightThe larger project

What happened

AlterNet, citing Washington Post reporting, identifies Supreme Court Justice Samuel Alito as the Trump ally whose opinions have become “mocking” and contentious. Harvard law professor Richard Lazarus said Alito has moved further right in his votes, rhetoric, and judicial posture as Donald Trump’s second-term agenda has reached the Court.

The Post reported that Alito supported the Trump administration in every major case it had brought during the term described, including dissents when the Court blocked sweeping tariffs, a proposed restriction on birthright citizenship, and an attempt to federalize mail-in voting. Alito said he intended to remain on the Court through the term, rejecting retirement speculation.

The power is judicial

Trump proposes policy, but Alito holds a different kind of power: a lifetime seat capable of converting an administration’s demands into constitutional doctrine. His importance is not that his language resembles Trump’s. It is that his votes can help make Trump’s agenda legally durable.

That distinction matters. A justice is not merely another combatant in partisan debate. Supreme Court decisions bind lower courts, constrain elected governments, and can outlast the coalition that produced them. Alito’s institutional authority—not his temperament—is the central fact.

Alito is making choices

The article describes an ideological transformation, but “evolution” can blur agency. According to the reporting, Alito repeatedly chose the administration’s position, adopted a more combative voice, and developed a method for producing aggressively conservative opinions that could still attract five votes.

Former clerk J. Joel Alicea’s account is especially revealing: Alito can assemble majorities without making the resulting opinions feel like political compromises to his conservative colleagues. That is coalition management from the bench. The movement rightward is not something happening around Alito; it is a project he is helping direct.

Personality is the distraction

Calling Alito “mocking” supplies the headline hook, but judicial sarcasm is not the primary injury. The consequential question is what legal authority his arguments give the executive branch and what protections they remove from people subject to government power.

The focus on grievance, culture-war rhetoric, and a supposedly disappearing older America helps explain Alito’s public voice. It does not explain away his conduct. Treating the shift as a matter of personality risks shrinking an institutional campaign into a story about one justice becoming crankier with age.

The succession fight

Retirement speculation exposes how openly the Court is treated as an instrument of partisan succession. Trump had already appointed three justices, and an Alito vacancy could permit a fourth appointment. The reported possibility of Democratic control of the Senate intensified pressure around whether Alito might leave during a Republican-controlled lame-duck period.

Alito’s refusal to retire preserves his own power while denying Trump an immediate replacement opportunity. Either outcome revolves around the same structure: unelected justices can time lifetime appointments around partisan control, while the public lives under the resulting doctrine for decades.

The larger project

This is not simply a conservative Court drifting further right. The reporting depicts an experienced justice using lifetime tenure, ideological discipline, and internal persuasion to move law toward the Trump-aligned wing of the Republican Party—even when a majority rejects the administration’s most expansive claims.

The systemic error is to cover that project chiefly as tone, personality, or interpersonal tension. Alito’s sharp prose may advertise the politics, but five votes operationalize them. Power lies in turning factional objectives into governing law, and accountability disappears when coverage mistakes that exercise of power for judicial mood.
