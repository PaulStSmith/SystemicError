'Painful to watch': Hundreds of Todd Blanche's ex-colleagues unite to condemn confirmation

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

Paulo SantosAugust 19, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Blanche Is Not Being Judged on Merit. He Is Being Tested for Loyalty.

The Source of the Alarm

Former federal prosecutors Mimi Rocah and Perry A. Carbone have joined more than 1,200 Justice Department alumni in opposing Todd Blanche’s confirmation. Their objection is simple: Blanche has behaved like a political instrument, not an attorney general. The issue is not a personnel dispute. It is a warning that the department is being asked to bless conduct that bends law enforcement around Donald Trump’s interests.

Who Holds the Power

The key fact here is not that a few ex-prosecutors are upset. The actual power sits with Trump, who gets to decide whether the Justice Department remains a public institution or becomes a personal enforcement arm. Blanche is the vehicle. Senate confirmation is the legitimizing ritual. And the harm alleged by the former prosecutors is not abstract: firings of FBI agents, the Maxwell interview, the Comey indictment, and the Proud Boys conviction dismissals all point in the same direction, toward a department used to punish enemies and protect allies.

What the Objection Really Says

Rocah and Carbone are careful to frame their criticism as institutional, not partisan. That distinction matters because it exposes the real collapse: the Justice Department is being made to serve power first and law second. They are not objecting to a different law-enforcement philosophy. They are objecting to a commander’s logic, where outcomes are selected to satisfy a president and the machinery of justice is forced to follow.

That is the central fact the Blanche hearing is meant to wash away.

The Misdirection Is the Point

The article’s deeper value is that it shows how corruption is laundered through procedure. A Senate hearing makes the process look orderly. A nomination makes the choice look normal. A long line of former DOJ officials makes the objection look like professional grumbling. But the conduct described here is not confusion or overreach in the ordinary sense. It is the deliberate conversion of prosecutorial authority into political control.

Calling that anything softer is just propaganda for the people doing it.

The Pattern Beneath the Nomination

This is not about one man’s temperament or résumé. It is about whether the Justice Department can still plausibly claim independence while being staffed by loyalists who have already shown whose interests they serve. Once that standard collapses, the institution stops functioning as a check and starts functioning as a shield.

That is the pattern: not law enforcement broken by accident, but law enforcement repurposed on purpose. The damage is not only in the cases mentioned. It is in the message sent to every prosecutor, agent, defendant, and witness afterward: the department exists for power, and only secondarily for law.
