'Completely failed': GOP strategist drops truth bomb about Trump and Black voters

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosAugust 20, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The SetupWho Actually Held PowerThe Same Old TransactionDemocrats’ Real ProblemWhat the Framing HidesThe Pattern

Black Men Were Not the Story. Power Was.

The Setup

On CNN, Republican strategist Shermichael Singleton said Trump and the GOP had failed Black men after winning more of their support in 2024, while Democratic strategist James Carville warned that Black voters will not tolerate being ignored. The segment treats this like a debate about outreach. It is really about who gets votes, who gets office, and who cashes the check once power has been delivered.

Who Actually Held Power

The answer is not subtle: Trump and the Republican Party held the institutional power in the source story. They won the election, returned to the White House, and now control the agenda they were supposed to translate into results. Singleton’s own critique matters because it concedes the central fact of governance: Republicans asked for Black support and then failed to convert it into legislative or material benefit.

That is the real measure. Not the performance on cable. Not the rhetoric. Delivery.

The Same Old Transaction

Republicans did what they always do when they want to broaden their coalition: they approached Black voters as a target market, not a constituency with claims on policy. Singleton’s complaint that the party was “not supporting Black male candidates” and should be backing “legislation that empower Black men” is an admission that the effort was hollow from the start. They wanted the votes, the optics, and the claim of diversity. They did not build the governing relationship.

That is not a messaging failure. It is a governing choice.

Democrats’ Real Problem

Carville’s warning is less wrong than it is revealing. Black voters are not an inherited asset. The Democratic Party cannot treat loyalty as automatic and then act shocked when turnout and trust wobble. But there is a difference between complacency inside the democratic coalition and the predatory politics of the right. Democrats can and should be forced to earn Black support through material accountability, not ritualized gratitude and empty symbolism.

What Democrats cannot afford is the fantasy that Black voters are a permanently captive audience. The coalition survives only if it delivers.

What the Framing Hides

The article frames this as a partisan conversation about who is “failing” Black men. That is too soft. The deeper issue is how Black political power gets converted into somebody else’s leverage. Republicans solicit it as a stunt, then govern elsewhere. Democrats are told to protect it, but too often reduce that obligation to messaging discipline instead of real redistribution of power.

That is the misdirection in the piece: it treats Black voters as a constituency to be reassured, rather than as a political force entitled to outcomes.

The Pattern

The larger pattern is constant. American parties keep talking about Black voters when they need them and forgetting them when they have them. Republicans are the most openly cynical about this, because their coalition is built around extraction, grievance, and opportunism. Democrats are not the same problem, but they are not exempt from blame either. They lose trust when they substitute respect language for action and assume historic loyalty will cover for present-day neglect.

Black voters are not a moral symbol for either party. They are a political force with memory. Any party that wants their support has to prove, in office, that it understands the difference.
