SYSTEMIC ERROR / COMMENTARY

Scott Colom has already won

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosNovember 12, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Scott Colom had not won Mississippi’s U.S. Senate race when AlterNet published this account. He led Republican incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith by nearly 2,600 votes as of Monday morning, but officials in the state’s 82 counties were still reviewing eligible absentee and provisional ballots ahead of Friday’s certification deadline.

Hyde-Smith had effectively declared victory on election night despite the incomplete count, then said her campaign would monitor the remaining ballots and “protect the integrity of the process.” Colom, who could become Mississippi’s first Black U.S. senator since 1881, closed his campaign by invoking the sacrifice behind Black voting rights at Medgar Evers’s Jackson home.

The headline’s claim is therefore moral, not electoral: Robert Reich argues that Colom had already achieved a historic victory by making the result competitive in a state where white supremacy once excluded most Black citizens from voting through law, violence, and official complicity.

The count is the authority

The institutions with immediate power were Mississippi’s county election offices. Their officials—not either campaign, not social-media declarations, and not a columnist’s preferred historical meaning—were responsible for determining which outstanding ballots qualified and certifying the totals.

That distinction matters because democracy is not secured by declaring the right candidate symbolically victorious. It depends on administering the count accurately, allowing voters to resolve identification problems within the prescribed period, and refusing to let partisan pressure substitute for procedure.

The incumbent’s convenient posture

Hyde-Smith tried to occupy both positions available to a candidate protecting her own power: presumptive winner when the early margin appeared favorable, guardian of an unfinished process once Colom moved ahead. Her language about “integrity” did not establish misconduct. It did, however, place suspicion around ordinary ballot counting without any irregularity described in the report.

That rhetorical maneuver has a clear political use. Votes not yet counted become objects of distrust precisely when they may alter the result. Administrative procedure is recast as a threat requiring partisan vigilance. The campaign gains room to challenge an unfavorable outcome while presenting its self-interest as public protection.

History is not a substitute for power

Reich grounds Colom’s showing in Mississippi’s history of organized racial domination: Black citizens were excluded from the electorate, voting-rights workers were murdered, and state institutions declined to prosecute their killers for murder. Deputy Sheriff Cecil Ray Price did not merely belong to a hateful culture; according to the account, he used public authority to detain James Chaney, Andrew Goodman, and Michael Schwerner before delivering them to fellow Klansmen.

That is the crucial lesson. White supremacy survived through institutional power—police authority, one-party control, discriminatory rules, all-white juries, and official refusal—not simply through private prejudice. The weaker framing would turn this into a sentimental story about Mississippi overcoming its past. The stronger one asks who controls the machinery now and whether every lawful ballot receives equal treatment.

Symbolism can conceal the unfinished fight

Calling Colom, Mississippi, and America winners before the count ended risks converting political struggle into national redemption. Colom’s competitiveness was historically significant. It was not the same thing as winning office, controlling a Senate seat, or ensuring that the institutions administering the election would remain insulated from partisan intimidation.

The sacrifices of Chaney, Goodman, Schwerner, Evers, and generations of Black Mississippians should not become decorative proof that the system ultimately corrects itself. The reporting itself shows otherwise: progress came because people confronted institutions built to deny them power, often while those institutions protected their attackers.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is the American habit of celebrating access to power as though it were possession of power. A Black candidate approaching victory in Mississippi carries enormous historical weight, but representation becomes governing authority only after ballots are counted, results are certified, and office is won.

Colom’s campaign demonstrated what organized voters could make possible. Hyde-Smith’s shifting posture demonstrated how incumbency responds when entitlement meets uncertainty. The decisive question was not whether history had produced an inspiring symbol. It was whether Mississippi’s institutions would count lawful votes, certify the actual winner, and deny either campaign the power to turn preference into fact.
