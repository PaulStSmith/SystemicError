A floating bucket of filth is the clear signal of Trump's incompetence

Based on an article by Jason Miciak

Paulo SantosAugust 20, 20263 min read

A Billion-Dollar Military, Run Like a Negligent Pantry

Power Was Not Absent

The basic facts in the source are simple: the USS Abraham Lincoln has been kept deployed far beyond a normal rotation, and sailors are dealing with shortages, broken plumbing, and rising mental health crises. The article’s target is Peter Hegseth, with Donald Trump ultimately at the top of the chain. That’s the correct target. This is not a mystery of logistics. It is a failure of command.

The Decision Makers Own the Damage

The people with actual institutional power are not the sailors, not the families, and not the exhausted middle layers asked to absorb the collapse. It is Hegseth at Defense and Trump as commander in chief. If the Navy knew the carrier would need relief, supplies, repairs, and medical attention, then someone in authority chose not to make those adjustments in time, or chose to treat them as optional. Either way, the burden falls on the people who made the deployment, not the people trapped inside it.

The Senate also bears responsibility for confirming a man the source itself describes as grotesquely unfit. When the warning signs are that obvious and the vote still goes through, the institution is not checking power. It is laundering it.

Negligence Disguised as Security

The article is right to mock the Pentagon’s “operational security” excuse. That language gets used to conceal incompetence, not protect national security. Telling the public when a replacement carrier arrives does not hand Iran a secret weapon. It reveals that the Navy cannot or will not solve a basic readiness problem in plain view.

This is the standard tactic of military bureaucracy under strain: turn a solvable failure into a classified fog. Once the problem is wrapped in official secrecy, the people responsible can pretend the issue is complexity rather than abandonment.

Human Harm Is Not A Side Effect

The source keeps returning to the same point because it is the point: sailors are breaking down. One tried to jump overboard. Others were stopped in time. Families are frightened. Meals are being rationed to absurdity, and the ship’s conditions are degrading. That is not “stress.” That is institutional cruelty.

The article’s strongest claim is also the most important one: mental illness is not moral failure. If the Navy knows crew members are reaching the edge and still refuses to relieve them, repair the ship, and restore basic conditions, then the problem is not individual weakness. It is command indifference.

A Pattern of Disposable Service

What this story exposes is a governing model that praises sacrifice while refusing maintenance. The same state that can spend immense sums on weapons and force projection suddenly acts helpless when asked to feed, repair, and relieve the people actually carrying out its orders. That contradiction is not accidental. It is the hierarchy speaking clearly.

Trump and Hegseth want the image of military strength without the discipline of care. That is how institutions rot: not in open collapse, but in a steady decision to treat human beings as interchangeable hardware.

The Real Verdict

The blame in this story does not belong to confused subordinates or faceless “conditions.” It belongs to the officials who had authority, received warning, and still allowed the situation to degrade. The article’s anger is justified, but its deeper value is simpler than its rhetoric: it shows how authoritarian politics and managerial incompetence converge. Power takes credit for command, then disappears when the command produces suffering.

That is the pattern. Centralize authority, strip out accountability, and let the rank and file absorb the damage. When leaders do that to sailors, they will do it anywhere they can get away with it.
