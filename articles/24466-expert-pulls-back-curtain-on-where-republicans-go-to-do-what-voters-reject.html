Expert pulls back curtain on 'where Republicans go to do what voters reject'

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

The venue Elie Mystal identifies as “where Republicans go to do what voters reject” is the Supreme Court. AlterNet reports that Mystal, writing in The Nation on Monday, argued that Republican-aligned litigants use the Court to pursue outcomes they could not secure through elections, including challenges involving the National Voter Registration Act, Title IX, gun restrictions, protections for runaways, and fossil-fuel regulation.

Those cases had not been decided in the reporting supplied. Mystal was forecasting how the Court’s conservative majority would rule during the coming term, warning that it could invalidate popular laws despite record-low public approval measured by Gallup. He argued that Congress could respond through ordinary legislation by changing the Court’s size, reducing its funding, or limiting its jurisdiction.

Power without an election

The institutional power belongs first to the justices who choose cases, interpret federal law and can nullify decisions made by elected governments. Lifetime tenure insulates them from the voters affected by those decisions. In the cases Mystal describes, Republican-aligned plaintiffs provide the vehicles; the conservative majority holds the authority to deliver the result.

That arrangement converts judicial review into a political bypass. Losing at the ballot box does not necessarily end a campaign against a law. It can redirect the campaign toward a forum where public approval is irrelevant and five justices can override legislative majorities, state policy or local voters.

The architects are not bystanders

The Court did not acquire its current ideological direction by accident. Republican political power placed the conservative majority in position, and aligned litigants now ask that majority to produce outcomes unavailable through ordinary democratic persuasion. The reporting supports describing this as a coordinated institutional strategy, even though it does not establish how the justices will decide the pending cases.

The crucial distinction is between prediction and action. Mystal expects the majority to dismantle protections and restrictions he considers popular; AlterNet does not report that those rulings have already occurred. But the decision to bring these disputes before the Court is itself political agency, not neutral weather.

The convenient blame shift

Mystal says voters must pressure Washington, but that formulation risks placing responsibility on the people with the least direct control. Citizens did not grant themselves jurisdiction, select the cases or cast judicial votes. Elected officials constructed the present Court and retain powers that Mystal says could constrain it.

His sharper point is that the political branches “will never buck” the Court on their own. That is institutional cowardice, not public confusion. If lawmakers possess legislative remedies and refuse to use them, the failure belongs to those lawmakers. Voters can apply pressure; they cannot introduce a bill, schedule a vote or enact jurisdictional limits themselves.

Minority rule by legal process

The larger pattern is minority governance laundering itself through judicial procedure. Policies that cannot command electoral support are repackaged as legal claims and delivered to officials who never face reelection. The language of doctrine can make the result appear detached from politics even when the dispute was selected precisely because ordinary politics produced the wrong answer for its sponsors.

The Court’s insulation is therefore not merely a constitutional feature. It is a political resource. Republican-aligned interests can use that insulation to seek durable victories while elected allies avoid openly voting for the consequences.

The system working as arranged

Mystal’s proposed remedies expose the real structure of responsibility. Congress has tools, the justices have authority, litigants choose their cases, and voters bear the consequences. Treating the conflict as a generalized loss of faith in the Court obscures who can act and who is declining to do so.

The systemic error is not that an unpopular institution has become mysteriously powerful. It is that political actors can transfer contested policy choices to an unaccountable tribunal, accept the resulting benefits and then describe the outcome as law operating above politics. The robe does not erase the power struggle. It conceals who won it.
