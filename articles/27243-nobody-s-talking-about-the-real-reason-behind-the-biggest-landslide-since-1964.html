Nobody's talking about the real reason behind the biggest landslide since 1964

Based on an article by John Stoehr

Paulo SantosNovember 13, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet argues that the decisive force behind the reported 2026 Democratic landslide was a broad rejection of authoritarian governance—not any single dispute over war, tariffs, or impeachment. The outlet cites a 13.5-point Democratic lead in the national House vote after more than 116 million votes were counted and a reported gain of 545 state-legislative seats.

The article attributes that coalition to voters reacting to different exercises of institutional power: Hispanic voters to ICE enforcement, Black voters to the Supreme Court’s weakening of the Voting Rights Act, religious liberals to the administration’s use of Christianity, and the No Kings movement to perceived lawlessness generally.

It also reports that prominent Democrats increasingly refused customary gestures of reconciliation. Governor-elect Amy Acton rejected contact with defeated opponent Vivek Ramaswamy after his campaign used her estranged mother in an advertisement; Hakeem Jeffries called Stephen Miller “a stone-cold racist”; and Representative Stephen Lynch answered a reported White House warning against congressional investigations with, “come at me, bro.”

Power was the issue

The narrowest interpretation of an election is often the safest one for those already holding power. Prices can be adjusted. Tariffs can be revised. Wars can be wound down. But an electorate rejecting the political use of police power, judicial power, racial hierarchy, and executive impunity is rejecting the governing arrangement itself.

That is the article’s strongest argument. The reported coalition was not united by one policy preference. Its members encountered the same administration through different institutions: immigration enforcement, the courts, religious rhetoric, and executive authority. Treating those reactions as unrelated “issues” strips the power relationship out of each one.

The institutional balance is not obscure. The president commands the executive branch. ICE exercises coercive federal authority. The Supreme Court determines the reach of voting protections. Congressional leaders decide whether oversight becomes accountability or ceremony. Voters can punish those institutions electorally, but they do not wield their machinery.

The moderation trap

The strategist quoted by AlterNet compares polling on Iran, tariffs, and impeachment as though Democrats must select one instruction from a menu. That framing converts a sweeping electoral result into tactical advice designed to limit what the winners may do with it.

Impeachment polling does not settle whether Congress should investigate alleged misconduct. Opposition to war or tariffs does not mean voters consented to executive lawlessness everywhere else. Different majorities can reject several abuses at once. The insistence on finding one permissible explanation is not analytical discipline; it is political containment.

The misdirection is aimed downward. Instead of scrutinizing the officials who exercised state power, the argument warns Democrats against responding too forcefully. Responsibility shifts from those who created the conflict to those expected to manage it politely.

Civility for the aggressor

The reported episodes involving Acton, Jeffries, and Lynch expose how “conciliation” can operate as an asymmetric demand. Ramaswamy’s campaign reportedly deployed Acton’s estranged mother despite the history AlterNet describes: Acton said her stepfather raped her when she was 12 and her mother accused her of lying. Asking Acton to restore normal relations afterward would make her conduct—not the campaign’s decision—the test of political decency.

The same structure appears in Jonathan Karl’s questions about whether Jeffries would contact Trump. According to AlterNet, Trump had not called to congratulate Jeffries, yet the burden of reconciliation was placed on Jeffries. The person denied the courtesy was invited to supply it.

Civility becomes institutional cowardice when it requires the target of an abuse to repair the appearance of normal government. It protects the aggressor from consequence while marketing the weaker party’s restraint as statesmanship.

Accountability is not polarization

AlterNet reports that the White House warned congressional Democrats that investigating the administration could itself be criminal. If accurately characterized, that is not ordinary partisan friction. It is an attempt to place executive intimidation between Congress and its oversight power.

The actors and choices are concrete: the administration issued the warning; congressional Democrats must decide whether to retreat; and the press must decide whether to describe the conflict as a struggle over constitutional authority or flatten it into another round of partisan hostility.

Calling racism racist or treating threats against oversight as intimidation does not create the underlying conflict. The conflict was created by the conduct. “Polarization” is frequently the label applied after powerful officials cross a line and their opponents decline to pretend otherwise.

The system rewards selective blindness

The larger pattern is an elite preference for stability of appearance over accountability in practice. Republican escalation is treated as an enduring condition to be accommodated; Democratic resistance is treated as the disruptive choice. That arrangement grants one side agency and assigns the other side responsibility for everyone’s comfort.

AlterNet’s interpretation of the reported landslide is therefore less about Democratic messaging than about whether electoral victory changes institutional behavior. Winning seats matters only if those officials use their authority: investigate executive conduct, defend voting rights, check coercive agencies, and refuse rituals that erase responsibility.

A political system does not correct itself because the injured parties remain gracious. It changes when abuses stop producing compliance and begin producing enforceable consequences.
