This MAGA lackey is drowning in cash — and Trump haters are to blame

Based on an article by Ray Richmond

Paulo SantosJuly 8, 20263 min read

Bezos Did Not “Hedge.” He Chose Power.

The Story in Brief

The source is about Jeff Bezos’s turn from public Trump adversary to eager Trump ally: blocking a Post endorsement, donating to the inauguration, taking front-row seats, benefiting from Blue Origin contracts, and approving a $75 million Melania documentary deal through Amazon MGM Studios. It also folds in the narrator’s consumer guilt about using Amazon, which is the wrong lens for the real problem.

Follow the Power, Not the Vibes

The relevant fact is not that Bezos feels awkward about Trump. It is that he controls a newspaper, a platform, a space company, and a media studio, and he is using that power to accommodate a president who rewards obedience. Trump holds executive authority. Bezos holds corporate leverage. The relationship is not personal drama; it is patronage with better lighting.

The source gets one thing right by accident: the president and the billionaire are not equals. Trump can steer contracts, pressure agencies, and set the tone of state power. Bezos can decide whether his paper resists or retreats, whether his companies court favor or accept conflict. He chose retreat, then called it prudence.

The Post Was the First Surrender

Blocking The Washington Post from endorsing a presidential candidate was not a neutral editorial reset. It was an owner overriding newsroom judgment to avoid irritating an aspiring autocrat. That matters because it shows where the real veto power sits. The staff took the reputational damage. Bezos kept the asset. Trump got the signal.

The article correctly notes the fallout: subscriber cancellations, internal chaos, and a paper pushed toward safer, market-friendly language. That is not an accidental drift. It is what happens when a major paper becomes a property rather than an institution. The owner’s political anxiety starts editing the public record.

Contracts Are the Real Currency

The Blue Origin detail is the most revealing part of the entire story. Trump is reportedly fast-tracking contracts and is determined to help Bezos’s space company reach the moon. That is not a quirky billionaire friendship. It is the state bending toward private advantage, and the private actor smiling along because he understands the arrangement.

Likewise, the Melania documentary deal is not some innocent coincidence of celebrity content. It is a multimillion-dollar favor involving the president’s family and a company owned by the man who now praises Trump’s “discipline.” The source is right to smell quid pro quo. It is just too polite about what that smell means.

Consumer Guilt Is a Red Herring

The weakest part of the source is the detour into personal Amazon guilt. Buying batteries from Amazon is not the moral center of this story. That framing shrinks a political problem into a lifestyle dilemma, which is exactly the kind of misdirection billionaire power depends on. The issue is not whether a consumer can live flawlessly under monopoly capitalism. The issue is that one man’s corporate empire now intersects with state power, media influence, and public contracts.

The narrator asks whether using Amazon endorses its politics. That question is too small. The real question is how a company so embedded in daily life gets turned into a vehicle for elite appeasement, while ordinary people are encouraged to treat the whole thing as a personal purity puzzle.

The Pattern Is Oligarchic Submission

Bezos is not an outlier. He is a case study in how wealthy institutions behave when the political climate turns openly authoritarian: they do not defend democratic norms unless it is cheap, and often not even then. They hedge, flatter, donate, retreat, and call it realism. The press gets softened. The contracts keep flowing. The billionaires keep their access.

That is the systemic error here. The danger is not just Trump’s crude autocracy. It is the class of powerful people who recognize it, profit from it, and help normalize it while telling everyone else to worry about their shopping habits.
