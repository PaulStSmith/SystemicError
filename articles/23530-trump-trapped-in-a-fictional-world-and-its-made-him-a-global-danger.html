Trump 'trapped in a fictional world' — and it’s made him a global danger

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

The Fiction Is Not the Failure. The Permission Structure Is.

A Memorial Recast as Personal Myth

At an event honoring first responders near the 25th anniversary of September 11, Donald Trump turned the occasion into another story about Donald Trump. He claimed he brought a construction crew downtown after the attacks and described firefighters carrying him away from a supposedly endangered building. When fact-checkers noted the absence of supporting evidence, he insisted the account was “exactly accurate,” while also clarifying that he arrived only after authorities permitted access.

The Presidency Has the Power

Trump’s disputed recollections matter because he is not merely an aging raconteur inflating his place in history. He is the president of the United States, with command authority, emergency powers, control of the executive branch and an enormous propaganda apparatus built around his statements.

The central political fact is therefore not that Trump tells self-glorifying stories. That pattern is old. It is that institutions continue treating his detachment from verifiable reality as a spectacle to be checked, diagnosed and then absorbed into the news cycle.

A falsehood from a private citizen is a falsehood. A falsehood from a president can become an instruction, a pretext or an official version of events.

The Diagnosis Displaces the Decision

Thomas Edsall’s framing places heavy emphasis on whether Trump is “trapped in a fictional world” and on medical specialists’ concerns about his judgment and impulse control. Those concerns are consequential, especially when attached to presidential power. But diagnosis can also misdirect attention.

The political problem does not depend on proving what Trump privately believes. He publicly asserted an unsupported heroic role, dismissed scrutiny and attempted to place himself at the emotional center of a national trauma. Whether that conduct reflects delusion, calculation or some combination cannot be confirmed from the episode. Its function is clearer: it demands that evidence yield to the leader’s self-mythology.

Calling this instability without examining who accommodates it risks turning organized political permission into one man’s pathology.

The Enablers Are Not Confused

The specialists’ statement entered into the congressional record describes reckless threats, fixation on enemies, advocacy of lethal force and the use of state institutions against opponents. Yet Edsall concludes that removal will not occur unless Trump commits some act so damaging that expulsion becomes unavoidable.

That is not institutional helplessness. It is institutional choice.

Officials with constitutional authority are deciding that the threshold for intervention should remain somewhere beyond the conduct already documented. Party allies, executive officials and legislators who preserve that arrangement are not bystanders to presidential power. They are its operating system. Their restraint toward Trump becomes permission for Trump to exercise less restraint toward everyone else.

Fact-Checking Cannot Contain State Power

The coverage describes fact-checks “pouring in,” as though accumulated corrections might restrain a president whose political method depends on exhausting the distinction between memory, fantasy and propaganda.

They will not.

Trump’s false 2001 boast that one of his buildings had become downtown Manhattan’s tallest already revealed the underlying instinct: catastrophe becomes raw material for status. The newer story follows the same structure. First responders become supporting characters. The destroyed city becomes scenery. The president becomes the hero.

The harm is not confined to historical accuracy. A political system that repeatedly negotiates with invented reality trains officials, media institutions and supporters to debate the leader’s narrative instead of confronting his use of power.

The System Waits for the Catastrophe It Enables

The larger pattern is institutional cowardice disguised as prudence. Trump supplies the fiction, but others decide how much authority it will carry. Legislators decline to act. Allies normalize the conduct. Coverage personalizes the danger as a question of mental condition. Each institution narrows its own responsibility until accountability appears to belong to nobody.

Waiting for an “inescapable necessity” is itself a policy: preserve the powerful actor, discount the warnings and transfer the risk to everyone subject to his decisions.

The systemic error is not failure to recognize the pattern. The reporting shows that the pattern is widely recognized. The error is maintaining a political order in which recognition carries no obligation—and in which institutions will acknowledge escalating danger only after they have helped make its consequences irreversible.
