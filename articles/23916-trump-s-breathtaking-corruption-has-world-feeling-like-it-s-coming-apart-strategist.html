Trump's 'breathtaking' corruption has world feeling like it's 'coming apart': strategist

Failure Is Policy When Power Refuses Correction

A Presidency of Compounding Crises

Donald Trump enters this week’s United Nations gathering with wars involving Ukraine and Iran, stubborn inflation at home, and rapidly advancing artificial intelligence all testing American leadership. Political strategist Simon Rosenberg’s central charge is that the administration has failed to meet these challenges—and that its refusal to correct course has magnified the damage.

Power Is Not Missing

“Absence” is too forgiving a description. The presidency still possesses enormous institutional power: executive authority, diplomatic reach, regulatory influence, and the ability to set national priorities. What appears absent is responsible stewardship of that power.

The resulting disorder cannot be blamed on some generalized breakdown in “the world.” Trump leads the administration making—or refusing to make—the relevant decisions. His enablers help sustain those choices by declining to impose consequences or force correction. The failures Rosenberg identifies therefore belong to identifiable actors, not to history, complexity, or bad luck alone.

Deregulation With a Family Interest

The sharpest conflict in the story concerns artificial intelligence. Trump has resisted calls to regulate the technology while his family has reportedly made massive investments in AI companies. That combination raises an obvious question of self-dealing.

The reporting cited here does not establish the answer. But the political problem exists even without proof of a corrupt bargain: presidential opposition to regulation can increase the value and freedom of an industry in which the president’s family has substantial financial exposure. Treating that merely as an optics problem would obscure the institutional reality. Public power and private interest are operating in the same field, under rules the president can influence.

“Coming Apart” Hides the Hands at Work

The image of a world “coming apart” captures the atmosphere while blurring agency. Things do not simply come apart. Governments escalate wars, abandon diplomatic leverage, reject safeguards, tolerate conflicts of interest, and allow economic pressures to persist without an effective response.

Political coverage often converts decisions into weather. Crises “mount.” Confidence “erodes.” Disorder “spreads.” Such language makes powerful officials sound like bystanders observing conditions they helped shape. In this case, Rosenberg ultimately names Trump and his enablers, but the broader framing still risks turning political responsibility into ambient chaos.

Enablers Make Failure Durable

A reckless leader matters. An institutional network willing to protect him matters more.

Rosenberg’s emphasis on Trump’s enablers points toward the larger structure: consequential failure survives when allies refuse to acknowledge harm, accept responsibility, or change direction. Their role is not secondary. They convert one official’s impulses into government policy and make correction politically costly.

Institutional cowardice rarely announces itself as surrender. It appears as loyalty, message discipline, strategic silence, or endless insistence that the next crisis—not the last decision—is the real problem. The practical result is the same: power remains insulated while consequences travel outward.

The System Protects Decisions, Not Outcomes

The deeper pattern is not simple incompetence. It is an arrangement in which leaders can mishandle international conflict, resist oversight in an industry tied to family investments, and preside over domestic strain while responsibility dissolves into descriptions of a troubled age.

That is how deliberate choices become “confusion” and accountable officials become spectators. The world may feel as though it is coming apart, but the political system is functioning well enough to protect those holding power. What it is failing to protect are the people living with their decisions.
