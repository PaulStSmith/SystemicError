Trump spoils his own 'sweet spot' for being tough on China

The Guardrail Is Presidential Whim

Power Sits in the White House

The Trump administration has the institutional authority to restrict advanced chip exports, regulate technology companies, and negotiate with China. Donald Trump also claims “tremendous criminal and regulatory power” over AI firms. The central problem, then, is not a lack of tools. It is how the president chooses to use them.

Trump reversed established export controls to permit Nvidia to sell H200 processors to China, despite warnings that the chips could accelerate Chinese AI development. That was a presidential policy decision—not an unfortunate gap in an otherwise coherent strategy.

“Tough on China” Meets a Profitable Exception

Trump’s China posture depends heavily on the performance of aggression. Yet when toughness carried costs for a powerful American technology company, the administration weakened a concrete restriction.

Skanda Amarnath attributes that incoherence to lobbying, including pressure from Chinese interests. The source does not establish the precise influence behind Trump’s decision, so that claim remains Amarnath’s interpretation. But the visible result is clear: a policy advertised as strategic containment yielded when commercial access was at stake.

The rhetoric stayed confrontational. The material restriction did not.

Regulation for Enemies, Discretion for the President

Trump argues that domestic AI safeguards would help China while simultaneously allowing advanced American processors into the Chinese market. These positions are not reconciled by shouting “WHOEVER WINS AI, WINS.” They expose a governing model in which policy consistency matters less than preserving presidential discretion.

Trump’s proposed guardrail is himself. He couples that demand for personal authority with threats against “Treasonists, Traitors, and Leakers” and boasts of criminal power over companies. That is not a regulatory framework. It is an assertion that firms should operate under the threat of selective punishment from one political executive.

The Article Mistakes Agency for Incompetence

Calling this approach merely “incompetent” is too forgiving. Incompetence suggests officials attempted a coherent policy and failed to execute it. Here, Trump made the decision to loosen export controls while rejecting binding domestic restraints and claiming personal supremacy over the sector.

The contradiction may be incoherent as strategy, but it is coherent as power: rules remain negotiable, executives seek favor, critics face denunciation, and the president reserves the right to intervene without limiting himself.

That distinction matters. Confusion obscures responsibility. Trump did not stumble into weaker controls; he authorized them.

A Treaty Cannot Repair Rule by Whim

Sam Altman’s suggestion that Trump and Xi Jinping might earn a Nobel Peace Prize for signing a one-page AI standards agreement reduces enforcement to ceremony. Amarnath is right to focus attention on who would administer such a deal.

An agreement is only as credible as the institutions applying it. If export rules can be reversed by the same president who dismisses ordinary guardrails, then a compact built around his discretion offers pageantry without durable constraint. The Supreme Court’s expansion of presidential regulatory authority, as Amarnath notes, raises the stakes further: more power has been concentrated in the official openly insisting that his judgment should substitute for rules.

The System Rewards Personal Rule

This story is larger than one chip sale or one belligerent social-media post. It shows how economic nationalism can become political camouflage. China supplies the enemy image; technology companies supply the lobbying pressure; presidential discretion determines which rules survive.

The systemic failure is not that Trump lacks sufficient authority to govern AI policy. It is that authority is being detached from stable standards and converted into leverage. When restrictions bend for favored interests, regulation becomes a threat aimed at disobedient companies, and national security becomes a slogan used to defend contradictory decisions, policy stops functioning as policy. It becomes personal rule with an industrial strategy attached.
