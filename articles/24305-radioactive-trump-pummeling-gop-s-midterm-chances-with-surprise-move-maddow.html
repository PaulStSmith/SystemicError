'Radioactive' Trump pummeling GOP's midterm chances with surprise move: Maddow

Based on an article by Robert Davis

Paulo SantosOctober 1, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Rachel Maddow argued on NBC’s “Late Night” that Donald Trump’s unpopularity is damaging Republicans’ midterm prospects because GOP candidates remain publicly aligned with him instead of distancing themselves. RawStory, citing The Daily Beast’s account of the Tuesday-night interview, reported that Maddow described Trump as “radioactive” and said his approval rating was in the low 30 percent range.

Maddow attributed part of that political weakness to Trump’s effort to accelerate deportations involving people in the country both legally and illegally. She said the policy had broken trust with Latino voters who shifted heavily toward Trump in the 2024 election. The article supplies no polling methodology, specific midterm races, candidate statements, or evidence that Trump expressly ordered Republicans not to criticize him.

Power runs through Trump

Trump holds the institutional power in this story: the presidency, control over executive immigration policy, and enormous influence inside the Republican Party. Republican candidates may possess individual agency, but their apparent reluctance to separate themselves from him reflects the party structure they helped build.

That distinction matters. Trump’s political dominance is not an inexplicable weather system descending on helpless candidates. Republican officials repeatedly chose alignment as the price of standing within the party. Now that alignment constrains their ability to present themselves as independent when presidential approval becomes an electoral liability.

Loyalty is a choice

RawStory’s framing turns an absence into a “surprise move”: Trump apparently has not given Republicans permission to criticize him. But the report does not establish that he issued any directive at all. Maddow’s point is an inference about his political character and the GOP’s behavior, not a documented campaign order.

The more revealing fact is that Republican candidates seemingly require such permission. Politicians seeking power are responsible for the positions they endorse and the coalitions they maintain. Describing them as trapped by Trump risks laundering strategic submission into mere campaign difficulty.

The policy has consequences

The deportation push is not simply another item depressing a president’s approval rating. According to Maddow, it reaches people in the country legally as well as illegally and has damaged trust among Latino voters who moved toward Trump in 2024. That is a policy consequence before it is an electoral one.

Reducing those voters to a bloc that may punish Republicans also narrows the story. Executive power determines who faces removal, insecurity, or disrupted legal expectations. Midterm backlash, if it materializes, would be a consequence of those decisions—not the primary harm.

Polling is not a verdict

The article leans heavily on dramatic language while providing little supporting detail. “Historically unprecedented hate” is Maddow’s characterization; RawStory offers no underlying survey, comparison period, or evidence establishing that claim. A low approval rating can signal grave political weakness, but it does not by itself prove why voters disapprove or predict control of Congress.

The headline likewise claims Trump is “pummeling” Republican chances, yet the article identifies no race in which his conduct changed the result or any forecast measuring the damage. The argument may be plausible, but the reporting presented here does not demonstrate it.

The party built its own constraint

The larger pattern is a party surrendering independent judgment to a dominant leader and then discovering that personal loyalty creates collective exposure. Trump exercises presidential and partisan power; Republican officeholders decide whether to reinforce it. Neither side is merely confused about the arrangement.

The sharpest political lesson is therefore not that an unpopular president failed to rescue his candidates. It is that those candidates accepted dependence on him when it offered power. If that dependence now carries an electoral cost, it belongs to the officials who sustained the system as well as the president at its center.
