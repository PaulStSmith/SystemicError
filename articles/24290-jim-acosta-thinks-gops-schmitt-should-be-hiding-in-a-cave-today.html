Jim Acosta thinks  GOP’s Schmitt should be 'hiding in a cave today'

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 1, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe senator had the powerThe humiliation is secondaryThe bubble has gatekeepersSpectacle displaced consequenceThe system rewards the rake

What happened

Jim Acosta said Republican Sen. Eric Schmitt of Missouri should be “hiding in a cave” after Schmitt’s conspiracy theory involving former special counsel Jack Smith and Atlanta reportedly collapsed during a televised exchange on Tuesday. AlterNet does not explain what Schmitt alleged or what evidence disproved it, so the precise substance of that failure cannot be established from this account.

Acosta called the episode an extraordinary political blunder and accused conservative media figures of trying to rehabilitate Schmitt’s claim afterward. He argued that the spectacle distracted from Smith’s presentation concerning Donald Trump’s efforts to overturn the 2020 election. The article attributes to Smith the position that Trump committed prosecutable crimes; it does not provide Smith’s evidence or detail the proceeding in which he spoke.

The senator had the power

Schmitt was not a confused bystander wandering through an information silo. He was a United States senator using national television to advance a prepared accusation. That office supplies authority, attention, staff and a presumption of relevance unavailable to the ordinary people later described as “trapped” inside partisan media.

The immediate failure therefore belongs first to Schmitt. Whatever role conservative outlets played afterward, he chose to deploy the claim. AlterNet’s lack of detail prevents judging its full content, but not identifying the institutional imbalance: an elected official manufactured the spectacle, while the audience was treated as the explanation for it.

The humiliation is secondary

Acosta’s mockery is understandable, but embarrassment is a weak form of accountability. A senator can endure ridicule, retreat into friendly media and continue exercising exactly the same power the next morning. Calling the episode an “epic fail” risks converting political misconduct into entertainment with a short shelf life.

The more consequential question is why a demonstrably failed argument carries so little professional cost. Schmitt’s allies reportedly tried to recast it as reasonable within 24 hours. That is not merely an audience misunderstanding something. It is an organized political-media apparatus insulating a powerful participant from correction.

The bubble has gatekeepers

“Information bubble” can become evasive language when it makes propaganda sound atmospheric—something that simply forms around people. AlterNet’s account names actors who allegedly sustained the distortion: Schmitt, conservative influencers and programs such as The Charlie Kirk Show. They possess agency. They decide what to repeat, omit or invert.

The weaker actors are the consumers whose confusion is emphasized. Blaming their entrapment obscures the people with platforms and incentives who stock the silo. The audience may accept falsehoods, but officeholders and media operators give those falsehoods institutional reach and political utility.

Spectacle displaced consequence

Acosta’s strongest point was that Schmitt’s performance overshadowed Smith’s claims about Trump’s attempt to overturn the 2020 election and the failure to hold him criminally accountable. Yet even that argument requires precision. The supplied report says Smith believed Trump could have been convicted but for Supreme Court intervention; it does not establish that Trump otherwise would now be imprisoned.

The distinction matters because accountability did not disappear through abstraction. Prosecutors made choices, courts issued rulings and political institutions produced an outcome in which the underlying allegations never reached a completed federal trial. Schmitt’s televised failure was memorable partly because it offered a comic substitute for confronting that institutional result.

The system rewards the rake

This episode reveals a durable pattern: powerful officials can launch weak accusations, allied media can repair the narrative, and broader coverage can fixate on the resulting humiliation instead of the machinery that made it useful. The false claim gets airtime, its collapse becomes content, and the officeholder retains power.

The joke is therefore not the corrective. It is another stage of the same spectacle. A political system that substitutes viral embarrassment for material accountability does not punish propaganda; it turns propaganda’s failure into the next profitable distraction.
