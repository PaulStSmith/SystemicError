Honeymoon over as Hegseth's handpicked 'reporter' turns on him

By Paulo Santos

FILE PHOTO: U.S. Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth attends a cabinet meeting at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., April 10, 2025. REUTERS/Nathan Howard/File Photo

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth is discovering that he can change the players, but he can’t change the game as one of the more prominent conservative “reporters” he has allowed to cover the Pentagon is already giving him grief less than a week in.

The embattled Donald Trump appointee effectively purged the mainstream media from covering the Pentagon if they didn’t agree to highly restrictive rules.

In their place, the Pentagon has allowed in what the Guardian has labeled, “mouthpieces for Trump,” which includes, “dozens of rightwing media figures and organizations who agreed to the strict rules, including Laura Loomer, a Trump confidante who has described herself as a ‘proud Islamophobe’; LindellTV, an online streaming channel founded by Mike Lindell, a conspiracy theorist and CEO of MyPillow; and Matt Gaetz, a disgraced former congressman turned host at One America News Network.”

With Hegseth facing a firestorm accompanied by accusations of war crimes from lawmakers on both sides of the aisle, the honeymoon with Loomer — who has a history of urging Trump to fire aides and government officials she feels are “disloyal“ — looks to be on shaky ground.

On Monday, as the debate rages about drug interdiction and how far the Pentagon can legally go, Loomer weighed in on X, writing, “What’s really interesting about the strikes in Venezuela is the fact that only 8% of the drug supply that comes into the US is coming from Venezuela. Most of it is cocaine. I have no sympathy for narcoterrorists being killed. It’s just worth noting that over 90% of the drug supply coming into the US is coming in from Mexico, especially the fentanyl.”

“Fentanyl isn’t being manufactured in Venezuela,” she asserted. “Given that 90% of the drugs coming into the US that are killing Americans are coming from Mexico, when can we expect to see air strikes on the Mexican cartels in Mexico? Or what about China, since China produces the chemicals that are used to make fentanyl in Mexico?”

Needling the Pentagon chief, she baited him with, “It makes a lot of sense to start with neutralizing the Mexican cartels since that’s where most of the damage is coming from. It makes me wonder why this hasn’t been done… surely the @DeptofWar knows where all of the Mexican drug lords live.”

Our Analysis:

The Hegseth Honeymoon: A Critical Analysis

In a move that could only be described as the Pentagon’s flirtation with authoritarian media practices, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s decision to purge mainstream media in favor of sycophantic, right-wing mouthpieces is an alarming development that deserves nothing short of fierce scrutiny. This strategy, as reported, not only undermines the very essence of journalistic freedom but also dangerously skews the narrative toward a singular, partisan viewpoint—a tactic eerily reminiscent of authoritarian regimes, not a democratic republic.

The Unholy Alliance

Labeling the new media ensemble as “mouthpieces for Trump” might seem like a harsh indictment, yet when the cast includes figures like Laura Loomer, a self-proclaimed “proud Islamophobe,” and entities such as LindellTV, founded by conspiracy theorist Mike Lindell, it becomes a statement of fact rather than hyperbole. The inclusion of Matt Gaetz, a figure whose reputation is marred by controversy, only serves to underline the partisan hackery that now seems to be the new norm at the Pentagon’s press briefings.

The Loomer Factor

Laura Loomer’s swift disenchantment with Hegseth, despite her initial endorsement of his purging act, is a testament to the inherent volatility of aligning with extremist voices. Her criticisms, laced with accusations and insinuations, challenge the Pentagon’s strategy on drug interdiction, pointing out the somewhat myopic focus on Venezuela despite the larger threats emanating from Mexico and China. While her approach is undoubtedly provocative, it inadvertently highlights the potential shortsightedness of the Pentagon’s current stance on narcotics.

A Dangerous Game

Hegseth’s dalliance with such radical voices raises profound questions about the integrity of defense communications and the potential for misinformation. By allowing only those who agree to “highly restrictive rules” to cover the Pentagon, Hegseth is not just changing the players; he’s attempting to alter the very game of how military operations are reported and perceived by the public. This strategy, far from ensuring a more favorable coverage, might actually exacerbate the Pentagon’s challenges, embedding a narrative that lacks the balance and scrutiny essential for democratic discourse.

Conclusion: Beyond the Echo Chamber

The situation unfolding under Hegseth’s watch is more than just a change in media access policies; it’s a stark deviation from the principles of transparency and accountability that are the bedrock of democratic governance. While Loomer’s grievances might seem like mere internal squabbles within the conservative echo chamber, they underscore a larger, more troubling trend of empowering extremist viewpoints at the expense of factual, unbiased reporting. This move not only undermines the public’s trust in military leadership but also poses a grave threat to the very fabric of democratic society.

In the final analysis, Hegseth’s strategy of media manipulation serves as a cautionary tale of what happens when the lines between partisan advocacy and objective reporting are not just blurred but obliterated. The Pentagon, and indeed all branches of government, must be held to a higher standard—one that values truth, encourages diverse viewpoints, and fosters an informed citizenry. Anything less is a disservice to the principles upon which the United States was founded.
