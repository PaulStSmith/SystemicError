Florida House Republicans ignore DeSantis — again

The Florida House on Wednesday approved in a near-unanimous vote this year’s condominium bill, which Gov. Ron DeSantis has criticized as part of his feud with the chamber’s leadership.

With a little more than a week until the scheduled end of the session, House lawmakers approved Miami Republican Rep. Vicki Lopez’s bill, HB 913, which would allow condominium associations to open lines of credit instead of raising cash from owners to fund building repairs.

The legislation attempts to address escalating association fees following last year’s deadline for condominiums to complete studies detailing how much each needs to save to pay for roof and structural maintenance. Lawmakers passed legislation, requiring those studies and milestone inspections 30 years after the buildings’ construction, after the 2021 collapse in Surfside of a 12-story condo that killed 98 people.

“I did not come to the Florida House to become a housing expert, but here we find ourselves,” Lopez said Wednesday. “So, this affectionately known as the condo queen is prepared to continue to do the work to make sure that we have listened. I’m telling you, this is landmark legislation that finally does provide relief, the financial relief that all of our condo owners deserve.”

The vote followed criticism from DeSantis, who in press conferences said that language allowing condo owners to vote electronically on matters brought by their associations would incite fraud. The version the House approved Wednesday requires 25% of condo owners who can vote to ask the association to hold an electronic voting option, which Lopez said is needed because a lot of owners live outside of the state or country.

“The House condo bill seems to be something that favors developers and puts the interest of developers over the interests of Florida residents,” DeSantis said during an April 10 press conference at the City of Sweetwater Community Center.

The only two votes against the bill came from DeSantis allies: Republican Reps. Michael Caruso of Delray Beach and Kiyan Michael of Jacksonville.

Multiple Democrats praised Lopez on the House floor, saying she took the time during the summer to meet with their constituents about the rising condominium fees burdening elderly people on fixed incomes.

The Senate is set to take up its proposal, from Fleming Island Republican Sen. Jenifer Bradley, SB 1742, on Thursday. There are still some key differences between the two proposals. For example, Bradley’s bill would allow condominium associations to invest their funds for repairs and to pause their contributions for reserve funds for repairs for up to two years after the milestone inspection.

However, one provision that intensified disagreement between the sponsors early on is no longer a part of the House bill. Lopez originally wanted to prohibit Citizens Property Insurance Corp., the state’s property insurer of last resort, from issuing or renewing policies for condominium buildings that haven’t completed their building inspections.

“We want to make sure that associations comply, but I’m also very concerned about making sure that they don’t compound their troubles by not being able to secure insurance,” Bradley told Florida Phoenix in March.

Hardening pilot program

House lawmakers also voted unanimously to extend a pilot program for grants for condominiums to take on projects that would make buildings more resilient against hurricanes. HB 393, co-sponsored by Lopez and Parkland Democratic Rep. Christine Hunschofsky, lowers the requirement of 100% approval from condo owners to 75% for the association to apply for a grant.

However, the House is not adding additional funds for the pilot other than the $30 million appropriated last year, of which $29.6 million remains unspent because of the timeline of the application process.

The Senate bill, SB 592, has passed all of its committee stops but it has not been scheduled for a floor vote.

Our Analysis:

A Closer Look at Florida's Condominium Bill: A Balancing Act or Developer's Delight?

In an almost unanimous decision that could only be described as a legislative rarity, the Florida House has passed a bill that has stirred the pot in more ways than one. HB 913, spearheaded by Miami Republican Rep. Vicki Lopez, seeks to revolutionize how condominium associations handle their finances for building repairs. But is this revolution for the better, or does it mask a more developer-friendly agenda beneath its surface? Let's dissect.

The Bill's Core: Financial "Relief" or a Financial Fiasco?

At the heart of HB 913 lies a provision that would allow condominium associations to open lines of credit rather than amassing funds directly from owners for necessary repairs. This comes on the heels of previous legislation, sparked by the tragic 2021 Surfside condo collapse that claimed 98 lives, mandating detailed studies and milestone inspections for condominiums 30 years post-construction. Rep. Lopez, dubbing herself the condo queen, touts this as "landmark legislation" aimed at providing financial relief to condo owners. However, one must ponder if the relief promised is a short-term facade for a potentially long-term financial quagmire.

Governor DeSantis' Skepticism: Political Theater or Genuine Concern?

Governor Ron DeSantis, in his characteristic bluntness, has not shied away from criticizing the bill, specifically targeting the provision that would allow electronic voting on association matters, citing fears of fraud. His skepticism extends to a broader accusation: that the bill "favors developers and puts the interest of developers over the interests of Florida residents." This from a governor known for his ferocious feuds with the legislature's leadership, yet when DeSantis waves a red flag, it might be wise to at least consider the bull that's coming.

Democratic Praise: A Bipartisan Mirage?

In an unusual twist, multiple Democrats have lauded Lopez for her efforts, particularly highlighting the perceived alleviation of rising condominium fees on the elderly and those on fixed incomes. Yet, one has to wonder if this bipartisan praise is a mirage, obscuring the potential long-term ramifications of enabling associations to easily accrue debt through lines of credit.

Senate's Take: A Divergent Path?

The Senate, not to be outdone, has its version of the bill, SB 1742, which introduces its own nuances like allowing associations to invest funds for repairs and temporarily pause contributions to reserve funds post-inspection. The divergence between the two chambers underscores the legislative tug-of-war and the complexity of finding a middle ground that genuinely serves residents' interests without empowering developers unduly.

The Insurance Conundrum and the Hardening Pilot Program

A particularly contentious issue was Lopez's initial proposal to restrict Citizens Property Insurance Corp. from covering condos that failed to complete their inspections—a proposal that has since been dropped. This move, while seemingly in favor of compliance, flirted dangerously with leaving many in a precarious uninsured state, highlighting the bill's teetering between enforcement and endangerment.

Furthermore, the unanimous vote to extend a pilot program for hurricane resilience grants, while commendable, is marred by the reality that no additional funds have been allocated beyond the initial $30 million. It paints a picture of legislative goodwill constrained by fiscal conservatism, leaving one to question the program's potential impact.

Conclusion: A Precarious Balancing Act

As Florida's legislature continues its dance, the condominium bill stands as a testament to the intricate balancing act of addressing public safety, financial stability, and developmental interests. While proponents celebrate its potential for financial relief, the shadows of skepticism cast by figures like DeSantis, coupled with the potential for increased developer influence, loom large.

In the end, one must ask: Is HB 913 a well-intentioned attempt at reform or a Trojan horse for developer interests, wrapped in the guise of consumer relief? As always, the devil is in the details—or in this case, the lines of credit.
