'Hard to be optimistic': Expert flags 'cynical' reasons Trump ordered Iran strikes

By Paulo Santos

U.S. President Donald Trump sits at his desk, behind a hat that reads “America is back” at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., February 3, 2026. REUTERS/Evelyn Hockstein

A legal expert flagged a “cynical” reason why President Donald Trump ordered the U.S. to coordinate strikes against Iran on early Saturday morning.

Jack Goldsmith, a law professor at Harvard University, argued in a new Substack essay on Saturday that Trump’s decision to strike Iran alongside Israel came down to a handful of factors. One of the most important is that “there are no effective legal limitations within the executive branch” that would prevent Trump from ordering the strikes. At the same time, Congress has largely ceded its authority to hold Trump accountable for such actions, he argued.

“I’m praying for U.S. troops and for everyone involved, and hoping for the best,” Goldsmith wrote. “But it is hard to be optimistic given the terrible U.S. record with violent military disruptions and regime changes in and around the Middle East in my lifetime. Maybe this time will be different.”

The U.S. and Israel coordinated attacks against multiple sites in Iran on early Saturday morning. Israel struck multiple sites where Iran’s military and political leadership were located, an operation that killed Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, the dictator who has ruled the country since 1989. The U.S. struck Iran’s nuclear and ballistic missile facilities.

Goldsmith also noted that the debates about Trump’s legal authority are largely “empty.” Instead, those debates serve to deflect attention away from the people who refused to restrain Trump.

“My point is that the rhetoric of legal constraint, and debates about the legality of presidential uses of force, are empty,” he continued. “And they deflect attention from Congress’s constitutional responsibility to exercise its political judgment and the political powers that the framers undoubtedly gave it to question, to hold to account, and (should it so choose) to constrain presidential uses of force.”

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Cynicism Behind Trump’s Strike Order

In an era where political maneuvers often walk a tightrope between legal authority and moral ambiguity, President Donald Trump’s decision to coordinate strikes against Iran alongside Israel has sparked a notable analysis from Jack Goldsmith, a Harvard Law professor. Goldsmith’s insights, laid out in a recent Substack essay, merit a deeper dive, not just for the immediate implications of the strikes themselves, but for the broader commentary on the state of American executive power and congressional oversight.

Unfettered Executive Power?

At the heart of Goldsmith’s argument is a stark observation: “there are no effective legal limitations within the executive branch” to prevent a president from ordering military strikes. This isn’t a novel critique in the context of U.S. presidential powers, but coming in the wake of targeted strikes in Iran, it underscores a chilling reality. The decision to engage militarily, particularly in a region as volatile as the Middle East, without tangible checks and balances, is not just risky—it’s recklessly unconstrained.

Congressional Abdication

Goldsmith doesn’t stop at the executive branch. He points a damning finger at Congress, accusing it of ceding its authority to hold the president accountable. This isn’t merely a failure; it’s a systemic abdication of responsibility. In the dance of democracy, Congress is supposed to lead—or at least, choose the music. Instead, it has allowed the executive branch to DJ, unchecked, potentially playing tunes that could lead to catastrophic consequences.

The Mirage of Legal Debates

Perhaps most strikingly, Goldsmith calls out the “empty” nature of debates around the president’s legal authority to use force. This isn’t just academic musing; it’s a critical insight into how discussions of legality often serve to distract from the real issue: the lack of political will to exercise checks and balances. By focusing on whether the president can use force, we sidestep the more pressing question of whether he should—and who gets to make that decision.

The Price of Inaction

What’s at stake here isn’t just a geopolitical chess game. It’s the lives of U.S. troops, Iranian citizens, and the stability of an already tumultuous region. Goldsmith’s hope for a different outcome this time is a thin veil over a grim historical pattern of U.S. military interventions in the Middle East. His prayer for the troops and all involved is a sobering reminder of the human cost behind strategic calculations.

Conclusion: A Call for Restraint and Responsibility

Goldsmith’s analysis isn’t just a critique; it’s a clarion call for a return to a more restrained and responsible exercise of power. The strikes in Iran, ordered without apparent regard for legal constraints or congressional oversight, exemplify a broader issue: an executive branch that operates with impunity and a legislative branch that seems all too willing to look the other way.

The real cynicism here isn’t just in the decision to strike; it’s in the systemic erosion of the checks and balances that are supposed to guard against unilateral executive action. As readers and citizens, we’re left to ponder a critical question: if the mechanisms designed to prevent such unchecked actions are effectively sidelined, what’s left to stop a cycle of conflict and retaliation that no one—not even the president—can predict the end of?
