Something’s changed about the way Marco Rubio talks about his future

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 21, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

From chosen heir to undeclared contestantThe midterms changed the calculationLoyalty with an expiration date

Marco Rubio’s posture toward 2028 has shifted from waiting for Donald Trump to clear his path to considering whether remaining in Trump’s administration could damage it. Bloomberg reported Tuesday that Rubio and his advisers were weighing his departure after Republicans’ midterm wipeout, while CNN reported Thursday that Trump may delay endorsing a successor rather than immediately anointing one. Rubio has made no decision, and the State Department denied that he was considering resignation. But the change is unmistakable: the secretary of State is no longer being described only as a loyal aspirant seeking Trump’s blessing. He is also being described as a politician calculating the cost of staying.

From chosen heir to undeclared contestant

For much of the year, the emerging Republican succession contest revolved around two men already inside the administration: Rubio and Vice President JD Vance.

In March, The Wall Street Journal reported that Trump had been privately soliciting assessments of their strengths and weaknesses from advisers, donors and friends. Trump was not merely observing a future primary. He was using his control over the MAGA coalition to pit two ambitious subordinates against one another while deciding who might inherit it.

That arrangement exposed where the operative power lay. Rubio’s prospects did not principally depend on assembling an independent constituency or offering a distinct governing program. CNN reported that many of his allies believed he would run only if Trump endorsed him and effectively removed serious competition. The prospective candidate’s first electorate was one man.

Trump encouraged that dependency. CNN’s Adam Cancryn, Kylie Atwood, Kristen Holmes and Jennifer Hansler reported that he repeatedly asked allies whether they preferred Vance or Rubio, while publicly suggesting the two should share a ticket. The exercise kept both men competing for favor without giving either certainty.

Rubio appeared to gain ground by June, when a conservative publication reported that Vance’s standing with Trump had declined after the vice president criticized the administration’s decision to declare war on Iran. In that telling, proximity to the nomination depended partly on avoiding disagreement with the president—even on a decision as consequential as war.

The midterms changed the calculation

Republicans’ Election Day losses altered the incentives. Trump had previously been preoccupied with selecting an heir; CNN now reports that, in the aftermath of the midterms, he has signaled that he may withhold an endorsement until later in the 2028 primary.

That leaves Rubio without the guarantee his allies reportedly considered essential. It also changes the value of his Cabinet service. Holding two powerful posts—secretary of State and national security adviser—gives Rubio status, access and a claim to governing experience. It also ties him directly to an administration whose political standing has just suffered a major rebuke.

Bloomberg’s Alan Wickham reported that Rubio had spoken with White House officials about stepping down and had been asked to remain. According to people familiar with the internal deliberations, Rubio and his advisers were reviewing his options, but he had neither decided to leave nor established a timetable. The State Department disputed the central claim, denying that Rubio was thinking about resigning.

The disagreement matters. A reported internal discussion is not a resignation, and it does not establish Rubio’s motive. But the political logic is plain enough: departure has become an option worth discussing precisely when association with Trump may carry greater electoral liability and Trump’s endorsement has become less certain.

Loyalty with an expiration date

Rubio’s predicament is a product of the power structure he accepted. Trump has been able to treat the next Republican nomination as a succession he controls, testing Vance and Rubio against each other while reserving the right to delay his choice. Their ambitions remain subordinate to his timetable.

Now that Republicans have suffered an electoral defeat, Rubio faces a familiar problem for politicians who build their futures around personal loyalty to a dominant leader: they must eventually decide whether to share responsibility for the leader’s failures or attempt a separation that itself looks opportunistic.

Nothing in the reporting establishes that Rubio will resign, run in 2028 or openly distance himself from Trump. What has changed is the reported nature of his calculation. Earlier accounts cast him as a contender waiting for Trump to hand him an unobstructed route to the nomination. The latest reporting casts him as an official considering the exits while Trump, after an electoral wipeout, declines to guarantee anyone the inheritance.
