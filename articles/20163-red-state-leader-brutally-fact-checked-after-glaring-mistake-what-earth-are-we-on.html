Red state leader brutally fact checked after glaring mistake: 'What Earth are we on?'

Based on an article by Bennito L. Kelty

Power Lies Better Than It Reads

The People in Charge

Texas Lt. Gov. Dan Patrick was not making a stray comment from the sidelines. He was standing behind Trump, speaking from the posture of state power, while the Texas Board of Education approved a required reading list that included Bible passages. That is the real story: elected officials with institutional authority are normalizing a religious agenda through state machinery, then pretending the constitutional architecture is optional.

This is not confusion. It is governance by assertion.

The Claim Was the Move

Patrick’s line that “Separation of Church and state is not in the Constitution” is not an innocent mistake waiting for a civics lesson. It is a political signal. The claim reframes a settled constitutional constraint as if it were a liberal myth, which gives officials room to smuggle sectarian preferences into public institutions while claiming they are merely correcting the record.

The Constitution does contain the relevant protection. The First Amendment starts with a ban on laws respecting an establishment of religion. Patrick’s wording tries to move the debate away from that text and into a fake technicality. That is the trick: deny the phrase, erase the principle.

The Article’s Weak Spot

The reporting does its job by including immediate corrections, but the larger frame still risks treating this as a dumb fact-check circus. That is too soft. The issue is not whether a politician can recite the slogan accurately. The issue is that public officials are using state power to advance a religious program and then laundering it through ignorance theater.

If this were just a verbal slip, it would deserve a shrug. Instead, it came the same day the Texas Board of Education approved Bible passages for required reading. The statement and the policy fit together.

Who Gets Blamed

The predictable online pile-on lands on the claim itself, and that is useful, but it can also hide the decision-makers. The weak actors in this story are the citizens and commentators forced into real-time correction duty. The strong actors are the ones making institutional choices: Texas officials, the education board, and the political coalition that wants public schools to reflect a preferred theology.

That matters because misdirection is built into the power structure. Officials say something provably false, critics rush to explain the basics, and the underlying policy advances anyway.

What This Pattern Is

This is not merely anti-constitutional rhetoric. It is a familiar form of conservative statecraft: use bad-faith legal claims to make authoritarian or sectarian policy sound like common sense. First deny the principle. Then test the institutions. Then make the violation look normal enough that opposition sounds eccentric.

The Bible-in-school move is not about literacy or heritage. It is about control over public education and the moral framing of state authority. Once that is understood, Patrick’s statement reads less like a mistake than like a license.

The Real Separation

The real divide in this story is not between church and state as an abstract slogan. It is between institutions that still have to justify themselves and politicians who believe repetition can overpower the Constitution. Patrick and the Texas Board of Education are not confusing the law. They are trying to move past it.

That is the pattern: not ignorance, but deliberate erosion dressed up as correction.
