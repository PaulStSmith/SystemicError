The Mirage of Justice: Brazil, the U.S., and the Empty Promise of Rehabilitation

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 2, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Brazil: The Humanitarian VeneerThe United States: The Iron Fist of CrueltyThe Pro Side: Why Leniency Looks RationalThe Con Side: Why It’s a Cruel Joke in PracticeThe Real Solution: Rehabilitation That Isn’t Fiction

Justice, we’re told, wears a blindfold. But when it comes to how nations punish their criminals, she’s not only peeking—she’s drunk and staggering between extremes. Brazil and the United States are prime examples of this grotesque dance: one drapes itself in lofty constitutional guarantees while running medieval dungeons, the other slaps people with absurd sentences that outlive their grandchildren while calling it justice. Neither system works, and both are prime monuments to hypocrisy.

Brazil: The Humanitarian Veneer

Brazilian law is clear: no life sentences, no perpetual punishments. The Constitution of 1988, forged after decades of dictatorship, declares that even the worst criminal must retain dignity. The Penal Code caps sentences at 30 years (now 40 after the 2019 “Pacote Anticrime” reforms). On paper, Brazil rejects vengeance, embracing rehabilitation and reintegration.

But step inside the prisons and the illusion collapses. Overcrowded, violent, dominated by organized crime factions, Brazilian prisons are no more about rehabilitation than a slaughterhouse is about animal welfare. The state doesn’t rehabilitate—it abandons. Guards maintain the perimeter; gangs maintain order. Sanitation is nonexistent, diseases fester, massacres erupt. Education and work programs exist mostly in theory. For most inmates, “rehabilitation” means surviving until the day the law’s automatic clock spits them back out into society.

And then comes the fury of families like that of Lorenzo Eduardo Terra Avelino, a 19-year-old brutally murdered by someone he trusted. His father is leading a petition to abolish progressão de regime, the mechanism that lets convicts move from closed to semi-open to open regimes long before their full term expires. To victims’ families, it’s a betrayal: a killer sentenced to 25 years might walk out after 10. The state shrugs, calling it rehabilitation. But in truth, no rehabilitation happened—just calendar math and bureaucratic ritual.

The United States: The Iron Fist of Cruelty

On the other side of the hemisphere sits the U.S., the world’s jailer-in-chief. Here, rehabilitation is barely even mouthed as a platitude anymore. The American way is raw incapacitation: lock them up, throw away the key, and then add another hundred years just to be sure. Life without parole is common. “Three strikes” laws give shoplifters decades behind bars. Even juveniles have faced de facto life terms. Sentences of 200, 400, even 1,000 years are doled out like candy—judicial performance art masquerading as justice.

And yet, for all this cruelty, the American system also has its own “progression”—custody levels and good-time credits. Inmates can work their way down from maximum security to medium, low, or even camp. But here’s the catch: Supermax. ADX Florence is the tomb where the state buries the “worst of the worst,” often in permanent solitary confinement. Unlike in Brazil, there’s no legal ceiling, no guaranteed release, no constitutional promise that your punishment must end. The system pretends it is rational and procedural, but in reality, it’s arbitrary cruelty stacked on arbitrary cruelty.

The Pro Side: Why Leniency Looks Rational

There’s a compelling argument in favor of the Brazilian model—if it were real. Societies should not treat human beings as disposable. Caps on sentences prevent the grotesque inflation of punishment seen in the U.S. Progressão de regime makes sense if paired with actual rehabilitation: education, therapy, skills training, monitored reintegration. A system that releases offenders back into society better than they entered is not just humane; it’s practical. Locking people away for life wastes lives, money, and potential.

In theory, Brazil’s model is civilized. It rejects the sadistic spectacle of life without parole, acknowledges that even violent offenders may one day be fit for society again, and avoids the American disease of mass incarceration as social policy.

The Con Side: Why It’s a Cruel Joke in Practice

But Brazil doesn’t practice what it preaches. Its prisons are crime factories, not correctional facilities. When a murderer walks out after a decade in hell, he isn’t “rehabilitated”—he’s traumatized, hardened, and more deeply bound to organized crime. Victims’ families see early release not as justice but as a second injury. The lofty rhetoric of rehabilitation is a cruel joke when the system provides no such thing.

In the U.S., at least there’s no pretense. The cruelty is upfront. You get 200 years because the state wants you to rot, period. Brazil lies to itself, and to its citizens, pretending it is more enlightened while presiding over medieval chaos.

The Real Solution: Rehabilitation That Isn’t Fiction

The tragedy is that the Brazilian model could be the better one—if it weren’t sabotaged by neglect and corruption. True rehabilitation requires investment: schools inside prisons, vocational training, drug treatment, psychological counseling, real reintegration programs. Without these, progressão is nothing but a mathematical exercise, freeing time bombs into society.

The U.S. needs to learn mercy; Brazil needs to learn honesty. One system clings to vengeance, the other to illusions. Both betray their people. Justice isn’t blind—she’s been blinded, gagged, and turned into a stage prop for political theater.

Until rehabilitation becomes more than a legal fiction, Brazil’s humanitarian veneer is a mask hiding a nightmare. And until the U.S. learns that cruelty is not the same as justice, its prisons will remain monuments to a society addicted to punishment over progress.

Verdict: Neither Brazil nor the United States has found the balance. One pretends to rehabilitate while doing nothing; the other openly embraces cruelty and calls it order. If either truly cared about justice, they’d build systems that reduce crime by actually changing people. Until then, victims will continue to feel betrayed, inmates will continue to be dehumanized, and society will continue to pay the price for lies dressed up as law.
