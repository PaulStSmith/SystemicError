Reporter corrects Trump prosecutor mid-press conference on crumbling cases

The State Calls It Violence After It Builds the Trap

Power Is Not With the Defendants

This story is not about a swarm of shadowy militants suddenly overwhelming the state. The actual power sits with the federal government: the U.S. Attorney’s Office, Homeland Security Investigations, and the Trump White House that set the machinery in motion through National Security Presidential Memorandum 7. That is where the authority to define the threat, select the targets, and turn political conflict into felony exposure lives.

What the Reporting Says

A federal prosecutor in Minnesota announced indictments against 15 people tied to Direct Action Minnesota on charges including conspiracy, stalking, and assault on a federal officer. Reporters immediately punctured the performance: the office’s own track record on related § 111 cases includes dismissals, non-prosecution agreements, and even a judge calling one HSI affidavit “a fake affidavit” because the agent lacked personal knowledge. Rosen’s response was not evidence but confidence: “You watch how this case plays out.”

The Real Misdirection

The misdirection is obvious. The weak are being cast as the primary problem while the institutional actors responsible for building the case get treated as neutral referees. That is the oldest trick in political law enforcement: recast contested protest, surveillance, and prosecutorial overreach as public safety, then act insulted when reporters bring receipts.

The source article does not show a clean, airtight law-enforcement triumph. It shows a prosecutor trying to narrate certainty in front of a record that already undermines him.

Evidence as Theater

The task force branding matters. “Joint Task Force Vanguard” sounds like command authority because it is meant to sound like command authority. It dresses a political campaign in the language of national security and lets the government present itself as reacting to disorder it helped define. When prosecutors are forced to defend a case by denying dismissals that are already on the public record, the problem is not confusion. The problem is institutional habit: assert first, correct never, and let the defendants absorb the stigma.

The Brennan Center’s warning that these prosecutions risk criminalizing constitutionally protected protest is not a rhetorical flourish. It is the plain implication of using anti-extremism branding to stretch ordinary dissent into a federal violence frame.

The Pattern

This is how modern state power launders its own aggression. A president issues a directive. A task force translates that directive into prosecutions. A U.S. attorney sells the result as order. Reporters are then expected to treat the defendants as the main actors, even when the more consequential choices were made by offices with badges, subpoenas, and the authority to ruin lives.

The larger pattern is not just overreach. It is selective moral inversion: the state claims the language of protection while using its coercive machinery to criminalize opposition and protect its own narrative from scrutiny.
