Trump wants to take over Latin America and 'brand it as his own': WaPo columnist

Reacting to Donald Trump’s administration’s late night announcement of two more unprovoked attacks on alleged drug boats that followed the president renewed threats against Venezuela, longtime Washington Post columnist David Ignatius claimed he sees a pattern of conquest and domination emanating from the White House.

During an appearance on MS NOW’s “Morning Joe,” Ignatius, an expert on foreign affairs, claimed the president appears dead set on taking over Latin America.

Speaking with the hosts, he glibly called it a “branding exercise” for Trump who loves nothing more than seeing his name on everything he touches.

“This Congress, as you say, seems determined to give up its Article One powers to declare war,” he said of Trump’s saber-rattling. “Nobody really has the guts to stand up and insist on a clarity about what this mission is.”

“My own perspective as an observer is that this is, in a way, a branding exercise,” he added. “Donald Trump wants to brand Latin America as his own. This is going to be Trump Latin America and Venezuela is the first test case. He wants hegemony, he wants to have us rule in our hemisphere unquestioned. It’s the biggest difference, really, in his foreign policy is this focus on Latin America. And I think that he is gradually tightening the squeeze on [President Nicolás] Maduro and Venezuela, finding new reasons each week.”

“For now, several months, it’s been these drug smuggling boats,” he noted. “But, as we’ve noted, they’re not smuggling the drugs that are really doing damage to the United States. Then it was seizing oil tankers that had been sanctioned under previous reasons but were available targets. And now the president, as we sometimes say, is jawboning he’s threatening Maduro with further additional strikes. And this not so veiled suggestion that what he really wants is for him out of Venezuela.”

Our Analysis:

Analysis of Ignatius’ Commentary on Trump’s Actions in Latin America

The Alleged “Branding Exercise”

David Ignatius, a venerable Washington Post columnist known for his insights into foreign affairs, has thrown a scorching spotlight on President Trump’s actions towards Latin America, particularly Venezuela. Ignatius characterizes Trump’s aggressive posture as nothing short of a “branding exercise” aimed at stamping Latin America with the indelible mark of Trump’s ownership. The audacity to frame geopolitical strategy as akin to branding real estate or casinos is not only brazen but chilling in its implications.

Congress’ Abdication of Power

Ignatius doesn’t pull his punches when he critiques Congress for its apparent lack of backbone. The constitutional power to declare war lies squarely with Congress, yet Ignatius points out a disturbing trend of inaction and acquiescence towards Trump’s military bravado. This isn’t just a failure of legislative duty; it’s a wholesale surrender of checks and balances, allowing Trump to inch closer to the role of a unilateral warlord than a president restrained by governance.

The Drug Boats Distraction

The pretext of combating drug smuggling boats serves as a thin veil for Trump’s broader ambitions in Venezuela, according to Ignatius. It’s a classic move—fabricate a menace that plays well with public sentiment, then ride the wave of approval for aggressive foreign intervention. However, Ignatius astutely observes that these boats are not even linked to the drugs “really doing damage to the United States,” exposing the operation as possibly more of a theatrical display than a genuine security measure.

The Quest for Hegemony

Trump’s fixation on Venezuela, as Ignatius argues, is a test case for establishing a Trump-branded hegemony in Latin America. This pursuit of dominance is portrayed not just as a policy decision but as a personal crusade for Trump, where foreign nations are mere properties awaiting the Trump stamp. Such a worldview is not only myopic but perilous, risking long-term stability for short-term gains and ego boosts.

Conclusion: The Dangerous Game of Personal Ambitions

Ignatius’ analysis, if accurate, paints a picture of a presidency dangerously intertwined with the personal ambitions of Donald Trump. The pursuit of hegemony in Latin America, disguised under the guise of combating drug trafficking and imposing sanctions, could be seen as an extension of Trump’s brand rather than a coherent foreign policy strategy. This “branding exercise” risks destabilizing an already volatile region and undermining the United States’ standing on the global stage.

Congress’ silent compliance in this endeavor only adds fuel to the fire, signaling a disturbing departure from the constitutional mandate that seeks to prevent such unilateral exercises of power. The implications of Ignatius’ observations are grave, not just for Latin America but for the international order and the very fabric of American democracy. This is a classic tale of power unchecked and principles forsaken, with consequences that could reverberate far beyond Trump’s tenure.
