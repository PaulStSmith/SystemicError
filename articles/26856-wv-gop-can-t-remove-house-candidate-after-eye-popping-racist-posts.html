WV GOP can't remove House candidate after eye-popping racist posts

Based on an article by Amelia Ferrell Knisely, West Virginia Watch

Paulo SantosOctober 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Chris Clagett, the Republican nominee for West Virginia’s 91st House District, remained on the ballot after confirming he wrote racist social-media posts targeting Black women and Muslims. State law prevented the West Virginia Republican Party from replacing him at that stage even if he resigned. Clagett faced Democrat David Michaels in the heavily Republican Berkeley County district, with early voting already underway and Election Day set for November 3.

Clagett defended one post by invoking “context” and then repeated its premise, saying the Black women shown were behaving as he had described. State GOP chair Josh Holstein condemned the language, while House Speaker Roger Hanshaw said Clagett could be excluded from legislative committees if elected. Clagett had run unopposed in the Republican primary.

The party’s power came earlier

The headline’s “can’t” is legally accurate but politically incomplete. The party could not make a late ballot substitution. That narrow restriction does not erase the institutional process that delivered Clagett the nomination without primary opposition in a district where the Republican label carries substantial electoral power.

Nothing in AlterNet’s account establishes when party leaders learned about the posts or whether they could have recruited an opponent before the primary. But the result is plain: Republican voters were offered no internal contest, and the party discovered its supposed helplessness only after its nominee had become difficult to dislodge.

Condemnation without displacement

Holstein’s statement separates Clagett’s language from Republican “values,” but separation by press release is not separation by power. Clagett still held the party’s ballot line. In a heavily Republican district, that institutional advantage mattered more than a carefully worded rebuke.

Hanshaw’s proposed response exposes the contradiction. If Clagett’s conduct warranted making him legislatively irrelevant, then voters were effectively being asked to elect a representative whom House leadership already considered unfit for ordinary influence. Committee exclusion could contain Clagett inside the chamber, but it would also leave constituents with diminished representation.

“Context” is not an answer

Clagett’s defense did not identify missing facts that materially changed his words. According to the reporting, he invoked context and then reaffirmed the racial generalization behind the post. That is not clarification; it is an attempt to move scrutiny from what he chose to say toward whether critics interpreted it politely enough.

The broader misdirection works similarly. Treating this chiefly as a procedural puzzle—whether Republicans could remove him—shifts attention from political selection to ballot mechanics. The law did not write the posts, leave Clagett unopposed in May, or make the district heavily Republican. People and institutions produced those conditions.

A district governed by damage control

The 91st District had already cycled through extraordinary controversy. Republican delegate-elect Joseph de Soto was arrested after allegedly making terrorist threats against lawmakers; House Republicans later vacated the seat rather than expelling him, and the resulting appointment remained tied to a legal dispute over party control. His appointed successor, Ian Masters, denied responsibility for an antisemitic comment attributed to a private Instagram account using his name.

Those episodes are not interchangeable, and allegations must remain allegations. Together, however, they show a political system repeatedly forced into cleanup after candidate selection has already failed. The recurring institutional response is containment: vacate a seat, appoint a replacement, threaten committee isolation, issue a condemnation.

The systemic error

A dominant party can become less accountable precisely because its nomination is often the decisive election. When meaningful competition disappears at the primary stage, scrutiny arrives late and voters inherit a choice shaped by institutional neglect.

The lasting consequence is larger than one candidate’s posts. Party leaders retain control of the chamber and its committees; Clagett retains the ballot advantage; constituents risk receiving either a representative steeped in racist contempt or one deliberately stripped of influence. The institution protects its machinery first, then presents the resulting damage as an unfortunate problem of timing.
