Trump's dumb misfire shot a gaping hole in his billion-dollar grift

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosOctober 5, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power sits in WashingtonThe public pays either wayScarcity is the misdirectionA backlash can accelerate the transitionThe system protects ownership, not consumers

What happened

RawStory argues that the “misfire” was a war-driven energy shock that pushed consumers and governments away from oil, undermining the fossil-fuel interests Donald Trump had promised to serve. The article says oil prices had recently eased as Iran allowed tankers through the Strait of Hormuz, while an International Energy Agency report found global oil demand had fallen 2.5 percent that year.

The reported result was faster adoption of alternatives: the IEA expected electric vehicles to reach 29 percent of global car sales that year, compared with 5.8 percent in the United States. RawStory attributes much of that shift to higher fuel prices, though the article does not establish how much was caused by the war rather than policy, technology costs, or existing market trends.

The headline’s “billion-dollar grift” refers to a reported 2024 Mar-a-Lago meeting where Trump asked oil executives for $1 billion while promising to dismantle climate rules. The article says he subsequently eliminated wind, solar, and EV incentives. It does not report that the industry actually provided $1 billion, so the transaction implied by “grift” remains an argument, not an established payment.

The power sits in Washington

Individual drivers do not determine whether the United States builds charging networks, finances rooftop solar, prices carbon pollution, or preserves clean-energy subsidies. Presidents, Congress, regulators, utilities, and financial institutions do.

RawStory identifies the Republican Party and the fossil-fuel industry as the central political alliance. Its concrete charge is that Trump offered regulatory relief while seeking extraordinary financial support from oil executives. That is the relevant power relationship: an officeholder controlling federal policy soliciting money from businesses that stand to profit from those decisions.

The public pays either way

The article’s strongest distinction is between price increases imposed by crisis and those created through taxation. A war-related oil shock sends additional revenue toward producers and petroleum-exporting states. A tax can return revenue to households or finance transit, electric vehicles, and distributed energy.

RawStory cites an International Monetary Fund estimate of $7 trillion annually in worldwide fossil-fuel costs and subsidies, including damage shifted away from producers. Its political point is straightforward: cheap fuel is not truly cheap when illness, climate damage, and military costs are charged to everyone else.

That transfer is policy, not weather. The federal gasoline tax cited by the article has remained at 18.4 cents per gallon since 1993. Keeping visible prices low while socializing less-visible costs protects incumbent industries and disguises who receives the subsidy.

Scarcity is the misdirection

The article’s rooftop-solar example exposes another manufactured limit. Germany used favorable loans, government-backed risk, and feed-in tariffs to spread ownership beyond households able to pay upfront. RawStory reports that more than a million German homes now combine solar panels with battery storage and that solar supplies roughly 14 percent of the country’s electricity.

By contrast, treating household electrification as a boutique consumer choice shifts responsibility downward. Families are told to shop their way out of an energy system designed by lawmakers, utilities, lenders, and corporate incumbents. Affordability is then presented as a personal constraint instead of the predictable result of financing and subsidy decisions.

The article is weaker when it leans on the author’s own well-subsidized home as proof of a national solution. One household demonstrates that incentives can work; it does not establish every cost, grid constraint, or distributional effect of scaling the model.

A backlash can accelerate the transition

RawStory presents the energy shock as an unintended consequence of Trump’s policies: an administration aligned with fossil-fuel producers helped make petroleum dependence more expensive, strengthening the economic case for electrification elsewhere.

That interpretation is plausible within the figures presented, but the causal claim outruns the evidence supplied. Sales percentages and declining oil demand show movement, not a complete explanation for it. The article should distinguish more carefully between an accelerator and a sole cause.

Even with that limitation, the broader pattern is clear. Governments can protect entrenched energy wealth, but they cannot guarantee permanent dependence when the costs of that dependence become immediate. Policy can delay a transition, concentrate its benefits, and leave poorer households trapped with higher bills. It cannot make fossil-fuel volatility disappear.

The system protects ownership, not consumers

This is not fundamentally a story about Trump accidentally teaching the world a lesson. It is about institutions deciding who owns energy infrastructure, who finances the transition, and who absorbs the damage while it is delayed.

The American failure described by RawStory is not a shortage of technology. It is a distribution of political power that rewards fossil-fuel producers, restricts public investment, and leaves clean-energy access dependent on income and geography. When crisis finally changes behavior, the same structure makes ordinary people pay first—and allows the officials who preserved that vulnerability to claim credit when prices retreat.
