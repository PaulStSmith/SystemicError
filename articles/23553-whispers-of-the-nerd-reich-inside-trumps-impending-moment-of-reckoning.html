Silicon Valley Is Building the Machinery of Authoritarianism

Domination Is the Product

Palantir’s recruitment language is unusually honest: “we build to dominate.” The company supplies data systems to intelligence agencies, police, militaries and Immigration and Customs Enforcement. Meanwhile, wealthy technologists including Peter Thiel, Elon Musk, Marc Andreessen and Balaji Srinivasan have promoted political visions that subordinate democracy to private power, technological control or elite “exit.” The relevant story is not that eccentric billionaires hold disturbing opinions. It is that they possess the money, infrastructure and government access to turn those opinions into operating systems.

Follow the Institutional Power

The people with meaningful agency here are not migrants, online extremists or vaguely defined “everyday people.” They are corporate executives, investors, elected officials and state agencies. Venture capital finances the companies. Executives determine what gets built. Governments award contracts and decide where the technology is deployed. Police, military and immigration authorities convert software into surveillance, targeting and coercion.

Palantir’s work with ICE illustrates the chain clearly. A private company provides the technical capacity; a federal agency supplies legal authority and armed enforcement; political leaders set the policy. Technology does not independently choose whom the state tracks or removes. It makes choices already made by powerful institutions faster, broader and less visible.

“Apocalypse” Is an Alibi

Naomi Klein and Astra Taylor describe an elite politics that treats social and ecological breakdown not as a problem to repair, but as a transition to exploit. That distinction matters. Housing costs, inequality and environmental collapse are presented as evidence that shared institutions have failed beyond recovery. The proposed answer is then privatized survival: bunkers, proprietary infrastructure, fortified borders and insulated enclaves for those rich enough to purchase escape.

This is not fatalism in the passive sense. It is a political alibi. Declaring the world unsalvageable releases elites from any obligation to maintain it. If collapse is inevitable, hoarding looks prudent, abandonment becomes visionary and cruelty can be marketed as realism.

The “Big Man” Lens Lets Institutions Hide

Both books discussed in the source reportedly concentrate on notorious personalities. That makes for vivid journalism, but it risks reducing organized power to a gallery of villains. Peter Thiel matters because wealth, companies, political donations and receptive officials amplify his ideas—not because he possesses some singular ideological contagion. Remove one billionaire and the procurement systems, investment incentives, surveillance markets and executive powers remain.

The source article recognizes this weakness but partly reproduces it, racing through personalities while asking how authoritarian ideas reach ordinary people. That question is useful only if it does not redirect responsibility downward. The audience consuming propaganda did not build the platforms, tune the algorithms, finance political campaigns or sign deportation contracts. Concentrating on how “normal folks” become radicalized can obscure who designed the channels, profits from engagement and commands the state machinery awaiting those recruits.

Technology Launders Political Decisions

Calling these systems “AI,” “data-driven intelligence” or algorithms gives political choices the appearance of technical necessity. Classification becomes calculation. Surveillance becomes optimization. A deportation apparatus becomes a software deployment. The language removes the official who authorized the policy, the executive who sold the capability and the institution that applies force.

That laundering function is central to technofascism. The technology does not merely circulate bigotry; it operationalizes hierarchy. It can sort populations, identify targets, coordinate enforcement and manufacture a sense that the resulting harm emerged from neutral systems rather than deliberate policy. “Rationality” is the interface. Domination is the purpose beneath it.

Authoritarianism With a Terms-of-Service Agreement

The larger pattern is not a mysterious collision between futuristic technology and ancient prejudice. Concentrated private wealth is merging with public coercive power. Political myths supply enemies; digital platforms distribute those myths; contractors build the tools; state agencies impose the consequences. Each participant can then disclaim responsibility by pointing elsewhere—to the algorithm, the market, the law, the user or the supposed emergency.

That is why the slogan “we build to dominate” deserves to be taken literally. The danger does not begin when Silicon Valley’s most theatrical reactionaries acquire uniforms and banners. It begins when their infrastructure becomes ordinary administration: another contract, another platform, another automated decision whose victims are visible but whose authors pretend to be nowhere.
