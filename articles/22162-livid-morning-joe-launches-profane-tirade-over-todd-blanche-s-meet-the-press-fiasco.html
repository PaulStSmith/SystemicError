Livid Morning Joe launches profane tirade over Todd Blanche's Meet the Press fiasco

Pardons for the Mob, Cover From the State

The Real Power

The source is simple enough: Todd Blanche, Trump’s attorney general, was asked to defend the president’s pardons of Jan. 6 rioters and chose not to defend them on principle so much as to launder them as presidential prerogative. Joe Scarborough erupted over the consequences. The exchange matters because it exposes who actually holds the leverage: Trump made the decision, Blanche normalized it, and the Justice Department’s top lawyer is now functioning as a spokesman for impunity.

The Defense Is the Crime

Blanche’s answer was not confusion. It was institutional submission. When an attorney general shrugs at pardons for people who attacked police while trying to overturn an election, that is the state signaling which violence it will absorb and which it will excuse. The office meant to represent law has instead been reduced to a shield for presidential favoritism.

Scarborough Gets the Outrage Right

Scarborough’s central point is correct: pardoning people who beat cops tells the next set of thugs that there is no penalty. That is not abstract rhetoric; it is how political violence gets priced into the system. Trump is not merely forgiving old offenses. He is advertising future permission, and Blanche is helping broadcast the ad.

What the Framing Misses

The weakness in the segment is that it still treats this as a “law and order” dispute rather than what it is: a loyalty test inside an authoritarian political project. The issue is not whether cops feel supported. The issue is that the federal government is being used to retroactively sanctify an attack on democratic transfer of power. Calling that “controversial” softens the reality that it is deliberate political protection for anti-democratic actors.

The Pattern Beneath It

Trump rewards the people who act for him, punishes institutions that restrain him, and pressures officials like Blanche to translate abuse into procedure. That is the pattern. It is not chaos. It is a working arrangement: violence from below, cover from above, and legal fig leaves in the middle. Every time a high official says “that’s okay,” the message is larger than one pardon. It is that the state will bend before it will discipline its own insurgents.
