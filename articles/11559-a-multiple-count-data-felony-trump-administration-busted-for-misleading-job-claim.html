‘A multiple-count data felony:’ Trump administration busted for 'misleading' job claim

By Paulo Santos

Trump administration officials have touted new data they say shows employment among native-born Americans has surged; economists, however, argue the administration’s interpretation is tantamount to a “multiple-count data felony,” and that the numbers simply don’t add up.

Last week, President Donald Trump fired the Bureau of Labor Statistics commissioner after the agency published an abysmal jobs report, and in their stead, appointed E.J. Antoni to head the agency, who earlier this month touted numbers that allegedly show employment among native-born Americans were up two million over the past year, and employment among foreign-born workers fell by around 237,000.

“These charts and commentaries are a multiple-count data felony,” wrote Jed Kolko, a senior fellow at the Peterson Institute.

“The statistical agencies explicitly warn that these data are not suitable for sizing and trending the foreign-born and native-born populations. In fact, the apparent boom in native-born employment is just a statistical artifact, arising from arcane rules about how the data are constructed and population levels are determined.”

Several economists concurred with Kolko’s assessment, according to a Saturday report from The Washington Post, who cite several issues with the way the Trump administration is counting the numbers.

“If there’s a sudden drop in immigration, or if fewer foreign-born residents respond to the survey, then, by design, the number of native-born workers would almost certainly go up,” Kolko told The Washington Post. “The way the calculation is set up, it’s not like you can lower the population of foreign-born workers without raising the population of native-born.”

In essence, employment data for native- and foreign-born workers is based on the U.S. Census, conducted every ten years, and is adjusted dynamically in between census counts.

“As an extreme illustration, Kolko said that if the entire foreign-born population vanished at the end of July, this dataset would show the population of native-born residents skyrocketing by tens of millions of people,” wrote The Washington Post’s Abha Bhattarai.

Other factors economists pointed to that contributed to the job data being misleading include that fact that, outside of the U.S. Census count, much of the data is based on self-reporting, which would likely be skewed in recent months due to fears among migrants.

“If households that previously reported themselves as foreign born are now reporting themselves as native born, that means comparing statistics based on the two classifications are not comparable over time,” wroteUBS economistsJonathan Pingle and Alan Detmeister.

Our Analysis:

The Great Employment Data Debacle: A Tale of Misinterpretation and Misdirection

In a recent spectacle that can only be described as a farcical manipulation of numbers, Trump administration officials have been caught red-handed, attempting to spin the employment data to fit a narrative that blatantly distorts the truth. The administration's claim that employment among native-born Americans has surged is not only misleading but borders on statistical heresy.

The Misinterpreted Data Fiasco

E.J. Antoni, the newly appointed head of the Bureau of Labor Statistics—following the unceremonious firing of the previous commissioner over an "abysmal jobs report"—presented numbers claiming a two million increase in employment for native-born Americans, while foreign-born worker employment supposedly fell by around 237,000. This narrative, as tempting as it is for the administration to tout as a victory, is a classic case of misinterpretation so egregious it's been dubbed a "multiple-count data felony" by economists.

Economists Call Foul

Jed Kolko, a senior fellow at the Peterson Institute, alongside several other economists, has raised the alarm, highlighting the misuse of employment data. The administration's interpretation is, in essence, a manipulation of statistical methodologies that result in a grossly inaccurate representation of the labor market. The core of the issue lies in the dynamic adjustments made to the U.S. Census data, which, when misrepresented, can lead to wildly inaccurate conclusions.

The Washington Post report sheds light on the fact that any sudden changes in immigration or survey responses can skew the data significantly. The calculation method used by the Trump administration essentially ensures that a decrease in foreign-born workers artificially inflates the number of native-born workers employed—a statistical mirage that misleadingly suggests an employment boom among native-born Americans.

A Matter of Misleading Methodology

The employment data for native- and foreign-born workers, dependent on the decennial U.S. Census and adjusted in between, is not immune to manipulation. As Kolko points out, if the foreign-born population were to suddenly decrease, the dataset would misleadingly show a dramatic increase in native-born employment. This is not just a statistical anomaly but a deliberate misreading of demographic data to serve a political agenda.

Additionally, the reliance on self-reporting for these statistics introduces another layer of unreliability. In a climate of increased fear among migrants, the likelihood of skewed reporting is high, further distorting the employment landscape. UBS economists Jonathan Pingle and Alan Detmeister underscore the incompatibility of comparing these classifications over time, given the shifting sands of self-reported demographic data.

Conclusion: A Statistical Artifact, Not an Employment Boom

What we are witnessing is not a testament to policy success but a glaring example of data manipulation to paint a rosier picture of the employment situation for native-born Americans than reality warrants. The administration's narrative is not only misleading but dangerously close to a statistical felony, bending the numbers to serve a predefined narrative rather than presenting an honest assessment of the labor market.

In times where accuracy and transparency are of the utmost importance, the Trump administration's handling of employment data serves as a cautionary tale of how easily facts can be twisted for political gain. It's a stark reminder that in the realm of data interpretation, vigilance and skepticism are valuable allies in discerning the truth from statistical manipulation.
