Trump’s new 2028 favorite is the son ‘he always wanted’

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosNovember 25, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A successor without independenceThe family model of party controlLoyalty as the qualification

Stephen Miller is the “son” in question—not literally, but as the political heir Jonathan V. Last argues Donald Trump would prefer over Vice President JD Vance or Secretary of State Marco Rubio. After Trump floated Miller as a possible 2028 presidential candidate at a recent press conference, Last wrote in The Bulwark that the chief of staff offers what Trump’s would-be successors do not: no independent constituency, no separate political machine and no plausible center of power outside Trump himself.

The remark does not establish that Miller will run or that Trump has selected him. It does, however, expose the governing logic behind Trump’s apparent lack of interest in anointing the Republican figures already maneuvering for 2028. The succession Trump could most comfortably tolerate is one that does not actually require him to surrender control.

A successor without independence

AlterNet reports that Trump suggested, in a half-joking manner, that Miller would be a stronger 2028 candidate than Vance or Rubio. Last interpreted Miller’s appearance at the same press conference as an effective audition for Trump’s endorsement.

That assessment rests on three conditions Last believes Trump would impose on any successor: Trump must remain the campaign’s central figure, retain control of the Republican Party and trust the nominee completely. Miller, whose political identity has been built around Trump and Trumpism, could satisfy all three.

Unlike Vance and Rubio, Miller has not constructed an obvious presidential brand distinct from the president he serves. Last argued that a Miller campaign would consequently be staffed by Trump’s people and organized much like another Trump campaign. Miller would not inherit the movement so much as provide a legally eligible vehicle for its founder to continue directing it.

That is the political significance of Trump’s suggestion. A normal succession transfers authority. This model preserves the existing hierarchy by elevating a subordinate whose principal qualification is that he has nowhere else to stand.

The family model of party control

Last’s “son Trump always wanted” formulation is deliberately caustic, but it identifies the dynastic structure beneath the speculation. In his telling, Trump’s ideal arrangement would be to hand the nomination to one of his children, ensuring that the family’s interests, the party apparatus and the presidential campaign remained fused.

Last dismissed that prospect with personal assessments of Ivanka, Donald Jr. and Eric Trump, while noting that Barron Trump is too young. Barron, now 20 according to the report, could not meet the Constitution’s presidential age requirement until the 2044 election. AlterNet adds that Tiffany Trump, whom Last did not discuss, would turn 35 by Inauguration Day and therefore could be eligible in 2028.

But constitutional eligibility is not the same as political viability, and Last’s argument does not identify any Trump child preparing a credible campaign. Miller matters in this analysis because he could perform the function of a dynastic heir without belonging to the family: absolute alignment without an independent claim to the inheritance.

Loyalty as the qualification

Last described Vance and Rubio as ambitious “shapeshifters” whose loyalty ultimately belongs to themselves. That is his characterization, not an established fact. Both men have their own careers, constituencies and reasons to seek power, however faithfully they currently serve Trump. In the political order Last describes, that ordinary presidential ambition becomes a liability because it creates the possibility of autonomy.

Miller’s advantage is the opposite. Last called him Trumpism’s “truest true-believer” and portrayed Trump’s confidence in him as total. He concluded that Miller is “worshipful, aligned, smart, and ruthless”—the qualities behind the metaphorical son favored over both biological heirs and conventional successors.

The framing risks turning a consequential concentration of power into palace gossip. The relevant issue is not whether Trump feels paternal affection toward Miller or privately dislikes one of his children. It is that the reported 2028 calculation prizes personal submission over an independent mandate. Vance and Rubio would seek to become president in their own right. Miller’s supposed appeal is that his candidacy could keep Trump functionally at the center.

Trump’s comment may have been only a provocation, and Last’s column remains analysis rather than confirmation of a campaign plan. Yet the choice of Miller as the joke’s answer is revealing. Even in jest, Trump bypassed the vice president and secretary of state to elevate the aide whose lack of political independence could make him the safest custodian of Trump’s control.
