​Trump made a telling reveal as he quietly stopped toying with this illegal plan: analyst

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 12, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

FILE PHOTO: U.S. President Donald Trump and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth attend a cabinet meeting at the White House, in Washington, D.C., U.S., October 9, 2025. REUTERS/Evelyn Hockstein/File Photo

President Donald Trump has long entertained a particularly absurd and illegal idea. But in recent months, it appears far less commonly in his speeches, wrote columnist Jamelle Bouie of The New York Times — and it might show he is aware of his crashing popularity and weakening hold over his own party.

For starters, Bouie wrote, Trump’s second term never had to go like this. There was a path for him to be a relatively popular, or at least relatively drama-free, president, and it could have made him more dangerous.

“There is an alternate universe in which Donald Trump is the popular, successful president of his imagination,” wrote Bouie. “In this world, Trump has a clear view of the political landscape. He knows he won a narrow victory, not a landslide. He knows that his key voters — the ones who put him over the top, as opposed to his core voters — elected him to lower the cost of living and turn the page back to where it was before the pandemic.” This version of Trump would have been far more cautious, pursuing targeted stimulus, a more modest tariff plan centered on national security, and truly just deporting the “worst of the worst.”

This, he argued, might have allowed Trump to gradually build real public support for an American autocracy. Instead, Trump went all out, pushing for absolute power and unpopular far-right fantasies, stacking his Cabinet with walking controversies, and generally making himself even less popular than he was in his first term.

This, Bouie wrote, could be why Trump no longer talks as frequently about trying to run for a third term — something that was constitutionally prohibited in the first place, but that Trump used to love toying with.

“Perhaps he still intends to,” he wrote. “Or perhaps he has enough self-awareness to know that he is not the triumphant leader of his imagination. That he is, instead, a lame duck whose White House is in disarray and whose actions have plunged the world into chaos. He thought he might remake the country in his own image. Instead, he’s likely to leave it like one of his casinos: broke, broken and in desperate need of new management.”

“If impeachment weren’t a dead letter, then we could remove him and end his misrule,” Bouie concluded. “As it is, we have nearly three more years to live through. It’s an open question whether we survive it intact.”

Our Analysis:

Trump’s Delusional Autocracy: A Failed Gamble

In a recent op-ed for The New York Times, columnist Jamelle Bouie dissects the tumultuous presidency of Donald Trump, suggesting that the man who once entertained the absurd and illegal notion of a third term is now a shadow of his former self, grappling with dwindling popularity and a loosening grip on his party. Bouie’s analysis, while not groundbreaking, reiterates a critical perspective on Trump’s tenure, highlighting a path not taken—a path that might have led to a more insidious form of autocracy had Trump played his cards with a semblance of strategic foresight.

Trump’s Alternate Reality

Imagine, if you will, a parallel universe where Trump is the popular, successful president of his own delusion. According to Bouie, in this universe, Trump acknowledges his narrow victory and focuses on the pragmatic concerns of his key voters rather than pandering to his base with far-right fantasies. This version of Trump could have implemented targeted stimulus, a modest tariff plan, and a focused deportation policy, potentially laying the groundwork for a stealthier push towards autocracy.

However, back in our universe, Trump chose the path of absolute power, stacking his Cabinet with controversies and pushing for policies that only served to diminish his popularity further. This strategy, or lack thereof, reveals not only a misunderstanding of his political mandate but also a profound overestimation of his charisma and influence.

The Third Term Fantasy

Bouie points out that Trump’s once frequent musings about running for a third term have become less common, suggesting a possible, albeit reluctant, acceptance of his waning influence. This idea of a third term, while constitutionally prohibited, was emblematic of Trump’s flirtation with autocracy and his desire to test the limits of his power. The fact that this rhetoric has simmered down could indeed signal an acknowledgment of his lame duck status, though it’s equally plausible that Trump is merely adjusting his strategy to navigate the current political landscape.

The Reality of Trump’s Legacy

Bouie’s conclusion—that Trump might leave the country “broke, broken and in desperate need of new management” akin to one of his failed casinos—hits hard. It encapsulates the fear and frustration of those who have watched Trump’s chaotic governance with growing alarm. Yet, Bouie’s invocation of impeachment as a missed opportunity to end Trump’s “misrule” feels like a nod to a bygone era, especially considering the political realities that made impeachment a non-starter for much of Trump’s presidency.

Surviving Trump’s America

The speculation that we have to “live through” nearly three more years under Trump’s leadership and the open question of whether America can survive it intact is a stark reminder of the enduring impact of his presidency. Whether one views Trump’s tenure as a cautionary tale of unchecked ambition or a missed opportunity for a more cunning autocracy, the implications are deeply unsettling.

In essence, Bouie’s analysis offers a scathing critique of Trump’s failed gamble for autocracy, grounded in a reality where strategic missteps and a lack of self-awareness not only undermined his authoritarian fantasies but also left the country reeling. While Bouie’s perspective is not new, it reinforces the necessity of vigilance and accountability in the face of political hubris. The Trump presidency, with all its chaos and controversy, serves as a systemic error in America’s democratic experiment—a bug that the system must learn to guard against in future iterations.
