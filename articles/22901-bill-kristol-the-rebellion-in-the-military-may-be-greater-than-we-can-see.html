Bill Kristol: The 'rebellion in the military may be greater than we can see'

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Hegseth Is Not a Symptom. He Is the Instrument.

The Source, Briefly

The reporting here says Army Secretary Daniel Driscoll resigned after months of conflict with Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth over Army direction, readiness, modernization, and the removal or sidelining of senior officers. Driscoll reportedly warned Trump that experienced leadership was being stripped out in ways that were harming the Army. Conservative figures on the Bulwark podcast treated the leaks and resignations as evidence of rising institutional alarm.

Power Sits With Trump and Hegseth

This story is not about confusion inside the Pentagon. It is about a deliberate political project being carried out by the people who hold the levers. Hegseth is not drifting into military politics by accident. He is using his office to fire generals, sideline senior officers, and reshape the institution around loyalty and spectacle. Trump is not outside that chain of command. The source makes clear that Driscoll warned him directly.

That is the actual center of gravity. Not the leaks. Not the pundit chatter. The decision-makers are Trump and Hegseth, and the consequence is an Army being bent away from competence and toward ideological obedience.

The Real Story Is the Purge

The strongest fact in the reporting is also the least disguised: Hegseth has been removing or marginalizing experienced leadership. That is not administrative housekeeping. It is a purge. When senior officers are pushed out because they interfere with the secretary’s “direction,” the institution is being made safer for political control and less safe for everyone else.

Driscoll’s warning about readiness matters because it cuts through the usual euphemism. The issue is not hurt feelings among insiders. It is that military transformation cannot survive a leadership structure stripped for loyalty theater. The source describes a civilian leadership that is treating the Army like a factional prize.

Misdirection Works by Calling Harm a Rift

The article’s framing keeps drifting toward personality conflict, leaks, and whether there will be a “literal rebellion.” That language softens what is happening. It turns a power grab into a procedural drama. It invites readers to watch for insider unrest instead of the original offense: the civilian leadership itself is politicizing the military.

That misdirection matters. Public debate gets redirected toward whether military elites are “bothered enough” to talk, rather than whether the people in charge are abusing their authority. The institution is supposed to serve the constitutional order, not the ego and electoral needs of a president and a defense secretary.

The People Most Alarmed Are Still Speaking Like Insiders

Bill Kristol’s comments in the source are revealing for reasons he probably did not intend. He treats the problem as if the main question is whether Trump can keep using the military effectively in the next two years. That is the wrong frame. The issue is not whether Trump can manage his instrument cleanly. It is that he is trying to use the military as an instrument at all.

Even the talk of finding a more “palatable” replacement for Hegseth misses the point. A different face on the same project does not make the project less corrupt. If the job is to fire generals, intimidate institutions, and politicize command, the problem is not one man’s personality. The problem is the mission.

This Is the Pattern

The pattern is straightforward: purge expertise, reward loyalty, normalize decay, then act surprised when the institution weakens. That is how authoritarian politics eats state capacity. It does not always arrive with tanks. Sometimes it arrives through personnel decisions, chest-thumping rhetoric, and the quiet removal of anyone who might resist the leader’s demands.

The most telling detail in the source is not the speculation about leaks. It is that experienced leaders are reportedly being pushed out while allies in the national security world try to decide how much damage they can tolerate before speaking. That is what institutional cowardice looks like in real time: people recognize the danger only after the damage is already in motion.
