Panic as Pentagon quietly moves to expand Trump's 'nuclear options'

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosOctober 3, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower sits inside the reviewAn option is not an orderThe spectacle of reactionSecrecy expands presidential power

What happened

RawStory reports that Elbridge Colby, the Pentagon’s under secretary for policy, told the Senate Armed Services Committee during a closed Sept. 17 hearing that the Defense Department was conducting a classified review of U.S. nuclear-employment guidance “with the goal of increasing options available to the president.” The president is Donald Trump. The report does not establish that Trump ordered the review, approved a new policy, or decided to use nuclear weapons.

Colby said nuclear weapons would be considered only in “extreme circumstances” and attributed the review to a changing global nuclear landscape. Independent journalist Ken Klippenstein argued that expanding presidential options is especially dangerous after what he described as the administration’s war with Iran earlier in 2026. RawStory cites alarmed reactions from journalist Mehdi Hasan, historian Alexander Howlett and professor Ambereen Dadabhoy, but reports no completed policy change or operational decision.

Power sits inside the review

The decisive actor is not the academics reacting online. It is the executive branch’s national-security apparatus, which writes classified guidance governing the circumstances under which a president may consider nuclear use. Colby’s description matters because “more options” is not an abstract debating exercise. It means officials are examining how to widen the menu presented to the one office empowered to authorize nuclear employment.

The Pentagon can package that expansion as technical planning. Classification keeps the underlying scenarios, thresholds and proposed changes beyond meaningful public scrutiny. The result is a profound imbalance: officials deliberate over civilization-scale violence inside secure rooms while the public receives a phrase from closed testimony.

An option is not an order

RawStory’s framing risks outrunning its evidence. The article establishes a classified review intended to increase presidential options. It does not establish that new guidance has been adopted, that nuclear deployment against Iran is under consideration, or that the administration believes an atomic strike would deliver victory. Howlett criticized that idea as a myth, but the report does not show that Pentagon officials advanced it.

That distinction does not make the review harmless. It identifies the danger accurately. Nuclear policy can become more permissive before any launch order exists, through revisions that make previously unthinkable choices appear administratively available. Inflating what has already happened only gives officials an easy rebuttal while obscuring the consequential work they acknowledged doing.

The spectacle of reaction

The headline’s “panic” relocates attention from state power to public emotion. Hasan’s fury and the academics’ alarm are reactions; they neither created the review nor control its outcome. Making those reactions the spectacle can reduce a substantive policy disclosure to another episode of partisan outrage.

Klippenstein also accused mainstream outlets of ignoring the testimony while concentrating on an FBI data breach. RawStory supplies his criticism but no systematic evidence establishing the scope or motive of that alleged neglect. Still, the contrast identifies a familiar media incentive: institutional drama with immediate victims is easier to package than classified policy machinery whose consequences are uncertain but potentially irreversible.

Secrecy expands presidential power

The larger pattern is executive authority growing through obscure procedure. A closed hearing, a classified review and the antiseptic language of “options” allow officials to alter the boundaries of catastrophic force without first defending the details in public.

The immediate fact is limited but severe: the Pentagon acknowledged work aimed at increasing the nuclear choices available to Trump. Whether those choices become policy remains unreported. That uncertainty is not a reason for melodrama, but it is no excuse for complacency. When institutions conceal the substance and coverage centers the people expressing alarm, power disappears from the frame precisely while it is being enlarged.
