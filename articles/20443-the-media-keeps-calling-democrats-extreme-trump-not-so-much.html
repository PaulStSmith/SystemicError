The media keeps calling Democrats extreme. Trump? Not so much

Based on an article by D. Earl Stephens

The Party of the Strongman Wants to Redefine the Rules Again

The Story Beneath the Story

The source piece is arguing over labels, but the actual story is simpler: Donald Trump and the Republican machinery around him are not waiting for voters to decide the outcome on fair terms. They are using federal power, state election rules, redistricting, voting restrictions, and retaliation against election workers to shape the battlefield before ballots are cast. That is not confusion. It is a deliberate attempt to control the conditions of democracy while pretending to defend it.

Who Holds Power

The key fact is institutional power. Trump sits in the White House. Republicans control the levers that matter for election manipulation: executive authority, state governments, judicial cover, and a party apparatus willing to normalize sabotage. The source lists the tactics plainly enough: nationalizing elections, tightening voting restrictions, mid-decade redistricting, cutting election security, undermining trust in results, and punishing people who resisted election denialism. That is not fringe behavior. That is the governing project.

The point is not merely that Trump is loud. It is that he has allies positioned to convert loudness into policy, procedure, and punishment.

How Misdirection Works

The media’s favorite trick is to describe obvious anti-democratic conduct as if it were just another round of aggressive campaigning. “Tip the scales” is a polite phrase for stealing control of the rules. The soft language matters because it launders intent. If you call arson “dry heat,” you help the arsonist.

The source piece is right to attack euphemism, but its own value lies in exposing how often the press and political class hide behind sterile verbs and managerial nouns. “Restrict,” “question,” “undermine,” “tighten” all sound procedural. What they actually describe is a power grab designed to make some votes count less and some officials easier to intimidate.

The Real Radicalism

There is a useful inversion here. The source spends a lot of energy on the word “extreme,” because the right has spent years pinning it on Democrats while shielding itself from the label. That criticism lands because the Republican record is not ideological disagreement in a normal democratic sense. It is a sustained campaign to treat opposition as illegitimate, to punish institutional independence, and to make elections safer for the party than for the public.

Republicans benefit when “extreme” means anything a Democrat supports, from voting access to reproductive freedom to public health. That linguistic scam is part of the political operation. If the public can be trained to hear a demand for equal participation as radical but hear voter suppression as routine, then the damage is already half done.

The Press Still Undersells the Crime

The source is furious that the New York Times framed Trump’s effort as an attempt to “tip the scales.” That complaint is justified. Trump is not merely nudging the system. He is trying to rig it through the tools of state power and fear. The paper’s caution may read as neutrality, but neutrality is exactly how establishment journalism often disguises asymmetry. It describes one side as controversial and the other as procedural, even when one side is openly trying to narrow the electorate and intimidate dissent.

This is where the political stakes get sharper. When the press adopts language that flattens coercion into strategy, it helps the stronger actor. Trump does not need praise. He needs normalization. He gets it when elite institutions speak as if assault on election legitimacy were just another line item in the campaign playbook.

The Democratic Problem Is Not Excess, It Is Restraint

The source is not wrong that Democrats are routinely saddled with language meant to imply danger, chaos, or radicalism. The deeper problem is that Democrats often accept that frame instead of attacking it. They still speak as though the threat is a dispute over tone when the actual fight is over whether elections will remain meaningfully competitive.

Democrats do not need to mimic Trump’s authoritarian style. They need to stop treating democratic defense as a branding problem. The public should hear, plainly, that one party is trying to govern within elections and the other is trying to rewrite the rules around them. Any analysis that fails to name that distinction is not moderation. It is disarmament.

The Pattern

This story is bigger than one president or one midterm cycle. It reveals a recurring pattern in American politics: elites who feel entitled to power do not always abandon democracy outright. Often they hollow it out first, then call the wreckage “election integrity” and the resistance “extreme.” They weaponize language, institutions, and media caution to make authoritarian tactics look like hardball politics.

That is the real lesson here. Democracy is not being tested by abstract polarization. It is being tested by a party and a president who understand that if they can control the rules, they may not need to win the argument at all.
