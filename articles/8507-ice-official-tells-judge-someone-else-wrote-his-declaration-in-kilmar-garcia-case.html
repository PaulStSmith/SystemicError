ICE official tells judge someone else wrote his declaration in Kilmar García case

Kilmar Armando Ábrego García’s ongoing case was back in court on Thursday as Judge Paula Xinis questioned U.S. Customs and Border Protection and Immigration and Customs Enforcement officials.

It was revealed that top immigration official John Cantú didn’t draft the sworn statements submitted to the court for the case. The statement was previously sealed, Lawfare’s Roger Parloff wrote in a BlueSky thread.

Ábrego was sent to an El Salvador prison, despite a judge’s order not to send him to the country. Initially, the administration sought to deport him to various African countries such as Uganda, Eswatini and now, Liberia.

Ábrego sought to go to Costa Rica, but Cantú wrote, and the government has alleged, that the country would not accept him.

Legal analyst Adam Klasfeld, of “All Rise News,” reported on X, “A senior ICE official just admitted in an evidentiary hearing in Kilmar Abrego Garcia’s case that someone else drafted his declaration in the case and he didn’t know what certain words meant.”

Parloff, who was live-posting about the court hearing, cited Judge Xinis saying that the witness had zero information about the content of his declaration.

“No shade on you Mr. Cantú,” Xinis said, according to Parloff. “You’ve been very candid with the court.”

Ábrego’s attorney, Sascha Rand, asked who might have more information “about the sum and substance of your declaration that we could go talk to?”

Cantú responded, saying, “No, sir. As far as I know, I’m the only one he talked to,” Parloff relayed.

Our Analysis:

The Farce of Accountability in Immigration Enforcement

In a courtroom drama that reads more like a Kafkaesque novella than a procedural hearing, the ongoing case of Kilmar Armando Ábrego García has once again illuminated the Kafkaesque labyrinth of U.S. immigration policy and the startling disconnection between those who draft policies and those who enforce them. Judge Paula Xinis’ questioning of U.S. Customs and Border Protection and Immigration and Customs Enforcement officials has peeled back another layer of the bureaucratic incompetence plaguing these agencies.

Ghostwritten Declarations and Admissions of Ignorance

At the heart of this circus is John Cantú, a top immigration official whose involvement in Ábrego’s case has raised more questions than answers. It was disclosed that Cantú did not pen the sworn statements submitted to the court in this case, a revelation that should shock no one familiar with the machinations of bureaucratic red tape but is nonetheless appalling. This bombshell, initially sealed and later exposed by Roger Parloff of Lawfare on a BlueSky thread, casts a long shadow over the veracity and integrity of the proceedings.

Ábrego’s saga, involving a judge’s order blatantly ignored and a scattergun approach to deporting him to any African country that might accept him, is a stark illustration of the ad-hoc and desperate measures often employed by immigration enforcement. The attempt to deport Ábrego to Uganda, Eswatini, and Liberia, before finally settling on the unattainable goal of Costa Rica, is not merely a failure of diplomacy but a damning indictment of the system’s cruelty and absurdity.

Adam Klasfeld’s report from “All Rise News” underscores a particularly damning admission: a senior ICE official acknowledged in an evidentiary hearing that someone else drafted his declaration in the case, and he was unaware of the meanings of certain terms within it. This confession, laid bare in court, is a microcosm of the systemic failures within ICE and CBP, where accountability is as elusive as a coherent policy directive.

Judge Xinis’ Understated Rebuke

Judge Xinis’ interaction with Cantú, as reported by Parloff, was a masterclass in judicial restraint and understated rebuke. “No shade on you Mr. Cantú,” Xinis remarked, a comment dripping with the subtext of the absurdity of the situation. Cantú’s candidness, while perhaps commendable for its honesty, is a damning self-indictment and an indictment of the system that put him in such a position.

The farcical nature of the proceedings was further highlighted when Ábrego’s attorney, Sascha Rand, sought to identify who might possess actual knowledge about the content of Cantú’s declaration. Cantú’s response, as relayed by Parloff, that he was the only one his interlocutor talked to, encapsulates the isolation and confusion endemic within these agencies.

Conclusion: A System in Disarray

The case of Kilmar Armando Ábrego García is not an isolated incident but a symptom of a system in disarray. It reveals a bureaucracy so entangled in its own processes that the left hand is unaware of what the right is doing. The ramifications of such systemic dysfunction are not limited to the halls of courtrooms but are felt most acutely by those like Ábrego, whose lives hang in the balance.

In the final analysis, this episode is a damning testament to the state of immigration enforcement in the United States. It lays bare a system characterized by a lack of accountability, transparency, and, most critically, humanity. As the gears of this bureaucratic machine grind on, one is left to wonder how many more cases like Ábrego’s are out there, obscured by the shadows of incompetence and indifference.
