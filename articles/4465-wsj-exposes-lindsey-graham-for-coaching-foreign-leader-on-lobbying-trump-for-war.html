WSJ exposes Lindsey Graham for 'coaching' foreign leader on lobbying Trump for war

By Paulo Santos

U.S. Senator Lindsey Graham of South Carolina speaking at the Iowa Republican Party’s 2015 Lincoln Dinner at the Iowa Events Center in Des Moines, Iowa. (Gage Skidmore/Flickr)

Republican U.S. Senator Lindsey Graham of South Carolina has been exposed by the Wall Street Journal for “coaching” a foreign leader on how to influence Donald Trump.

The WSJ ahead of the weekend published a story called, “Lindsey Graham’s Quest to Sell Trump on Striking Iran.” In that piece, there is a nugget about the senator engaging in a campaign to help Netanyahu to persuade Trump to launch an Iran war.

“To help make the case on Iran, Graham traveled several times to Israel in recent weeks, meeting with members of the country’s intelligence agency,” the Journal reported Friday.

Graham is quoted in the article as saying, “They’ll tell me things our own government won’t tell me.”

The report further states, “He spoke with Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, coaching him on how to lobby the president for action. Netanyahu showed the president intelligence that persuaded Trump to go ahead, Graham said.”

That line stood out to White House columnist Niall Stanage, who flagged it on X.

“Just so we’re all clear — a new WSJ story has a U.S. senator (Lindsey Graham) meeting a foreign intelligence agency and ‘coaching’ that foreign nation’s prime minister (Benjamin Netanyahu) on how to push Trump into bombing Iran,” he stated Saturday.

Popular podcaster Mel also chimed in, “The WSJ has an article out today describing how Lindsey Graham plays toddler memory games with Trump and coaches Netanyahu on the things to say in order to get Trump do some more war.”

Lindsey Graham: The Puppeteer Behind the Curtain?

In a revelation that reads more like a script from a political thriller than the day-to-day operations of U.S. foreign policy, Republican U.S. Senator Lindsey Graham of South Carolina has been spotlighted by the Wall Street Journal for his dubious role in “coaching” a foreign leader on how to manipulate then-President Donald Trump. The headline alone, “Lindsey Graham’s Quest to Sell Trump on Striking Iran,” should send shivers down the spine of any American concerned with the integrity of their nation’s foreign policy and the shadowy maneuvers that underpin it.

Graham’s actions, as reported, venture beyond the pale of typical senatorial duties, diving headlong into the murky waters of international manipulation. By engaging in a concerted effort to sway Trump towards a military confrontation with Iran, Graham not only oversteps his bounds but also wades into a dangerous game of geopolitical chess, with potentially catastrophic consequences.

“They’ll tell me things our own government won’t tell me,” Graham is quoted, a statement that should raise eyebrows if not outright alarm. This admission, candid and chilling, speaks volumes about the lengths to which Graham was willing to go in his quest. It begs the question: what does it say about a senator who seeks and values intelligence from foreign entities over that of his own country?

The article further outlines Graham’s strategy, detailing his multiple trips to Israel, where he met with members of the country’s intelligence agency and Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu himself. Coaching Netanyahu on how to lobby Trump is an act that blurs the lines between diplomacy and outright manipulation, transforming foreign policy into a game of influence and persuasion.

Niall Stanage and Mel, a popular podcaster, both express their incredulity and concern over these revelations. Stanage’s commentary, “Just so we’re all clear — a new WSJ story has a U.S. senator meeting a foreign intelligence agency and ‘coaching’ that foreign nation’s prime minister on how to push Trump into bombing Iran,” succinctly encapsulates the gravity and audacity of Graham’s actions. Mel’s remark, likening Graham’s tactics to “toddler memory games” with Trump, while humorous, underscores the manipulative and demeaning nature of these political maneuvers.

This incident illuminates the shadowy underbelly of international relations and the lengths some U.S. politicians will go to influence the highest levels of power. It raises significant questions about the transparency, integrity, and autonomy of U.S. foreign policy decisions. One must ponder, if these are the lengths Graham is willing to go to in order to wage war, what other backroom deals and shadowy coaching sessions have influenced the course of international politics?

In conclusion, Graham’s actions, as reported by the Wall Street Journal, are not just a breach of ethical standards but a stark reminder of the complex web of influences and manipulations that drive the decisions leading nations to the brink of war. It’s a scenario that demands not just scrutiny but a reevaluation of the roles and boundaries of those entrusted with shaping our world’s future. The implications of Graham’s “coaching” go far beyond mere political maneuvering; they touch on the very essence of democracy, sovereignty, and the ethical conduct expected of those in power.
