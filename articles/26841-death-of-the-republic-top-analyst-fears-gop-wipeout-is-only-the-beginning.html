'Death of the Republic': Top analyst fears GOP wipeout is only the beginning

Based on an article by Roger Chesley, Virginia Mercury

Paulo SantosOctober 27, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that University of Virginia political analyst Larry Sabato forecast major Republican losses in the November 3 midterms: Democrats winning at least 224 House seats and the Senate reaching at least a 50-50 split. His Crystal Ball rated Republican Rep. Jen Kiggans’ Virginia Beach seat as leaning Democratic and Rep. Rob Wittman’s race against Shannon Taylor as a toss-up. These are projections, not results.

Sabato’s larger fear was that electoral defeat would not settle the country’s political crisis. He said it was possible to trace how the republic could decline and die, citing the collapse of meaningful discussion about the public good. AlterNet connects that warning to President Donald Trump’s attacks on election administration, pressure for mid-decade Republican redistricting and threats of prosecution against local officials over voter rolls.

The article also attributes Republican vulnerability to public dissatisfaction with Trump administration policies, including tariffs, cuts to Medicaid and food assistance, immigration enforcement and the reported economic effects of war with Iran. It notes that Virginia officials had sued to block armed federal agents from polling places and that the Supreme Court had rejected an administration effort involving Postal Service screening of mail ballots.

Power is not evenly distributed

The decisive actors here are not voters being “nasty” to one another. They are the president, his administration, Congress, state legislatures, federal agencies and courts. They possess the authority to draw districts, reduce benefits, deploy officers, administer elections and determine which executive actions survive legal review.

Republicans held the congressional majorities described by AlterNet and approved the reported cuts. Trump demanded new maps from Republican-controlled states and used the presidency to pressure election officials. Democratic-controlled states answered with partisan mapmaking of their own, including an effort in Virginia that voters narrowly approved before the state Supreme Court overturned it. The institutions differ in reach, but the operative choice was the same: manipulate district lines for partisan advantage.

Civility is a decoy

Sabato’s concern about public nastiness may be sincere, but it is a poor diagnosis of institutional decay. Rude political speech does not redraw a congressional district, cut Medicaid or place armed federal personnel near polling locations. Officials with legal authority do those things.

Framing the crisis as a collective loss of civility spreads responsibility across the population and obscures the hierarchy of power. The relevant distinction is not between polite and impolite Americans. It is between people arguing about government and officials using government to entrench themselves, punish vulnerable groups or raise the cost of administering an election.

Defeat does not erase the machinery

The article’s strongest point is buried beneath its electoral forecast: a Republican wipeout, if it occurs, would be a consequence rather than a cure. Seats can change hands while the incentives that produced partisan redistricting, attacks on voting systems and retaliatory threats remain intact.

Trump’s claims about election security, as characterized by AlterNet, also serve a practical function regardless of whether they persuade a majority. They prepare supporters to treat an unfavorable outcome as illegitimate. That converts ordinary electoral accountability into another pretext for institutional pressure. The Supreme Court’s intervention and Arlington County’s lawsuit show other institutions resisting, but resistance after escalation is not evidence that the system is healthy.

Cruelty has a constituency in government

AlterNet describes policies whose burdens fall primarily on people with less institutional leverage: low-income recipients of Medicaid and food assistance, immigrants facing detention or deportation, and even very young children in immigration proceedings. Calling the resulting anger a “national funk” sanitizes the relationship between policy and suffering.

The reported harm was not produced by national mood alone. Congress authorized benefit cuts. Executive agencies conducted enforcement. The administration pursued tariffs and war policy. Whatever electoral judgment follows, the chain of agency should remain visible: powerful officials made choices, and people with fewer means absorbed the consequences.

The systemic error

American political coverage often treats elections as both diagnosis and treatment: unpopular governance produces a wave, the wave changes control, and the system corrects itself. This story contains evidence against that comforting sequence. The same actors anticipating punishment can use public power to alter districts, intimidate administrators and discredit the vote before it is counted.

The larger pattern is institutional self-protection disguised as political conflict. When coverage emphasizes polarization, incivility or generalized despair, it turns exercises of state power into a shared cultural failure. The sharper account identifies who controlled the machinery, what they chose to do with it and who was forced to bear the cost.
