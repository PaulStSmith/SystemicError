Trump just ripped up the rulebook — and left Canada with only one choice

Based on an article by The Conversation

Trump’s Tariffs Are Not a Negotiation Problem. They Are a Power Problem.

The Source Tries to Clean Up a Coercion Story

The reporting starts with a simple fact: Canada had a trade deal in force, Donald Trump imposed new tariffs anyway, and when Canadian negotiators say Washington added fresh conditions at the end of talks, Ottawa walked away rather than swallow the terms. That is the useful core of the story. Everything else is the article trying to turn a political shakedown into a classroom exercise about “trust.”

Who Actually Holds the Leverage

The power is not evenly distributed, and the source knows it. The United States is Canada’s main trading partner. Trump controls the tariff threat. Canada does not control the larger market it depends on. That is the whole game.

So when the article asks why enter an agreement with someone who may go back on their word, it is posing the wrong question in a polite accent. The real question is why a smaller country should treat a dominant power’s commitments as binding when that power can revise the terms whenever it wants and suffer little immediate cost. This is not a misunderstanding between equals. It is dependency being converted into leverage.

Bad Faith Is Not a Personality Trait Here

The source leans hard on the language of unreliability, credibility, and trust. That framing softens what is happening. Trump’s administration did not merely “seem” inconsistent. According to the article, it imposed new tariffs even while CUSMA was already in force, then presented additional conditions at the last minute. That is not confusion. That is deliberate pressure.

The article’s negotiation jargon treats the problem as if it were a broken norm among adults at the table. But the point of the table is that one side can kick it over. The harm is not accidental. It is structural and intentional: create instability, force the other side to absorb the uncertainty, and make capitulation look like pragmatism.

The Real Lesson Is Not Better Trust

The piece gestures toward game theory, BATNA, staged commitments, and consequences for breach. Those tools are not wrong, but they are too bloodless for the political reality they describe. They become a kind of managerial camouflage if you ignore the underlying imbalance.

“Reduce dependence” is the only serious sentence in the whole thing. Everything else is a technical workaround for a relationship that is already corrupted by asymmetry. If the stronger party can break agreements at low cost, then the weaker party’s best defense is not faith in process. It is reducing exposure, narrowing vulnerability, and refusing to make concessions that lock in dependency.

The article hints at this, then retreats into neutral negotiation theory as if the problem were a generic one about durable contracts. It is not. It is about power that does not want to be constrained.

Canada Cannot Negotiate Its Way Out of Vulnerability

The source’s three tactics all boil down to a grim truth: when you cannot compel compliance, you manage risk by limiting what the stronger party can extract from you. Stage agreements. Tie future benefits to present behavior. Make breach costly enough that even an unreliable partner has to calculate.

That is not an ethics lesson. It is a survival manual.

And it exposes the weakness in the article’s framing. The issue is not that Trump is uniquely rude or that trust has evaporated in the abstract. The issue is that Canada is exposed to a United States political order where the executive can weaponize trade policy against a neighboring democracy and then keep negotiating as if nothing happened. That is what dominance looks like when it is normalized as “uncertainty.”

The Broader Pattern

This story is not really about one tariff fight. It is about the political fantasy that interdependence is automatically mutual. It is not. Interdependence without countervailing power becomes a trap. The stronger side gets to redefine the terms, and the weaker side is told to be creative, patient, and credible.

That is the pattern across modern authoritarian politics: break the rules, recast the damage as a negotiation issue, and make the weaker party spend its energy on adaptation instead of retaliation. The system rewards whoever can impose consequences and punishes whoever is expected to “honor commitments.”

If Canada wants a serious strategy, it should take the article’s one honest premise and discard the rest: reduce dependence on a partner whose word cannot be treated as stable. Everything else is just etiquette for asymmetry.
