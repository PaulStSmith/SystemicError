GOP senators are plotting Hegseth's doom — but it's really Trump they're coming for

Based on an article by Jason Miciak

Paulo SantosOctober 1, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower sits above the proxyThe evidence does not support the headlineAccountability delayed is protectionThe system rewards disposable loyalists

What happened

RawStory columnist Jason Miciak argues that Republican senators’ complaints about Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth are an indirect challenge to President Donald Trump. But the column does not establish that senators are “plotting” Hegseth’s removal or using him as a proxy to target Trump. It offers two critical Senate quotations—one from an unnamed source—and interprets their tone as evidence of a broader revolt.

The reported objections concern the handling of the Iran war, the removal of officers from promotion lists, the firing of generals and admirals, and Hegseth’s request for a $1.5 trillion Pentagon budget plus $88 billion connected to the war and additional weapons. The unnamed source said senators feared the war’s apparently haphazard execution would damage Republican candidates in the fall elections.

Miciak also reports that Hegseth faced eight prospective articles of impeachment associated with Rep. Thomas Massie, while Trump continued to support him. The column predicts that electoral losses, military casualties or renewed escalation could turn Republican frustration into action, but it provides no named senator pledging to remove Hegseth and no evidence that conviction—or a campaign against Trump—was underway.

Power sits above the proxy

Hegseth commands the Defense Department, but Trump holds the greater institutional power described here. He selected Hegseth, continues to back him and, under the column’s account, controls whether the administration intensifies the war. Senators control confirmation, appropriations, oversight and any eventual impeachment trial. House members control whether impeachment advances.

That division matters because Hegseth is not an autonomous accident. If his personnel decisions, spending demands and management of the war reflect administration policy, then concentrating blame on the secretary can obscure presidential responsibility. Republican lawmakers also possess tools to resist. Complaining privately while continuing to fund, defend or tolerate the policy is not resistance; it is participation with reservations.

The evidence does not support the headline

The column’s strongest factual material shows Republican unease with Hegseth’s conduct and its electoral cost. Its central claim—that senators are really “coming for” Trump—is an inference built largely from tone, analogy and hypothetical future events.

That gap should not be disguised as inside knowledge. The unnamed source explicitly discussed the war and the midterms, not a plan to confront Trump. The other quoted senator objected to personnel removals. Neither quotation establishes an organized effort against Hegseth, much less a staged assault on the president.

The framing converts ordinary congressional self-preservation into a dramatic conspiracy. A simpler reading fits the evidence better: senators fear that visible disorder, higher prices and an unpopular war could cost them power. That may eventually produce a break with the administration. RawStory had not shown that it already had.

Accountability delayed is protection

The senators’ reported concern arrived after Hegseth’s confirmation and after the administration had begun removing senior officers. If lawmakers considered him unqualified or regarded those decisions as reckless, they were not powerless observers. The Senate had an opportunity to deny him authority, and Congress retained leverage through money and oversight.

Their quoted anxiety centers partly on electoral consequences. That exposes the governing incentive: tolerate dangerous conduct while it remains politically affordable, then discover institutional standards when the polling turns. The weaker actors—the dismissed officers, deployed service members and civilians paying the economic price—bear consequences before senators decide whether accountability is convenient.

The system rewards disposable loyalists

Hegseth may function as a shield, but not necessarily in the elaborate way the column proposes. Presidents benefit when failure can be assigned to a subordinate, while legislators benefit when they can condemn that subordinate without confronting the leader or policies they enabled.

That is the larger pattern: concentrated executive power paired with conditional legislative courage. Congress delegates, confirms and funds; officials execute; lawmakers complain anonymously when the damage threatens their seats. The public is then offered a personnel drama instead of an accounting of decisions.

Whether Hegseth falls is less important than whether the policies, spending and chains of responsibility change. Removing one loyalist while preserving the machinery that empowered him would not be a reckoning. It would be the system protecting itself by sacrificing its most convenient proxy.
