GOP senator silenced as he sneers at Jack Smith: 'You make me want to throw up'

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

Paulo SantosSeptember 30, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that Sen. Dick Durbin cut off Sen. John Kennedy during a Tuesday hearing after Kennedy repeatedly interrupted former special prosecutor Jack Smith and told him, “You make me want to throw up in my mouth.”

Kennedy alleged that President Biden or White House staff had directed Attorney General Merrick Garland to prosecute Donald Trump. Smith said he had no knowledge of that and tried to explain his role, but Kennedy interrupted him with questions about the tooth fairy and Easter Bunny. Durbin intervened, calling the hearing constitutionally and historically important and insisting that Smith be allowed to finish his answers.

Power behind the performance

Kennedy was not a powerless critic confronting an official who could ignore him. He was a senator using the authority and visibility of a congressional hearing to accuse, interrupt, and demean a witness.

Durbin also held institutional power within the proceeding, and he exercised it by stopping the barrage and enforcing the basic condition required for testimony: the witness must be permitted to answer. That intervention matters because hearing rules are meaningless when those empowered to enforce them choose spectacle over evidence.

An allegation without support

Kennedy’s central insinuation was that Biden or his staff ordered Garland to prosecute Trump. The reporting provides no evidence for that assertion, and Smith explicitly said he did not know whether it was true.

Instead of developing the claim through documents, testimony, or a coherent chain of responsibility, Kennedy smothered Smith’s response with ridicule. The childish questions were not evidence. They were a device for making an unsupported premise feel established by preventing the witness from contesting it in complete sentences.

Contempt as misdirection

The sneer at Smith supplied the emotional conclusion that Kennedy’s questioning had failed to prove. Disgust replaced substantiation; interruption replaced examination.

This is how a hearing can be converted from an instrument of oversight into a propaganda stage. The senator’s conduct becomes the event, while the unsupported allegation lingers beneath it. RawStory’s framing emphasizes that Kennedy was “silenced,” but Durbin did not suppress an evidentiary argument described in the report. He stopped a powerful official from denying a witness the ability to answer.

The institutional test

Durbin’s intervention was necessary, but it also exposed how little separates formal accountability from televised abuse: one presiding official’s willingness to enforce procedure.

The larger pattern is congressional power used to manufacture suspicion rather than establish facts. An accusation is announced, the answer is obstructed, and humiliation fills the resulting void. When institutions tolerate that method, hearings cease to clarify responsibility. They become machinery for laundering insinuation into political belief.
