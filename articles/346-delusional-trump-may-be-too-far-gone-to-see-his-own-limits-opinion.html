'Delusional' Trump may be too far gone to see his own limits: Opinion

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosJune 22, 20263 min read

Trump’s “No Limits” Boast Is Not a Psyche Problem. It Is a Power Problem.

What Happened

CNN spent a Saturday morning doing what the cable class does best: turning Trump’s latest self-exalting claim into a debate over whether he is sincere, delusional, or merely “messaging.” The prompt was his claim in an Axios interview that there are now “no limits” to his power since he started a war with Iran. The panel treated the statement as evidence of personality. It is better understood as evidence of an office that still gives one man too much room to perform domination.

Power Has a Holder

The answer to who holds actual institutional power is not mysterious. It is the president of the United States, backed by the military, the executive branch, and a political system built to absorb abuse while calling it procedure. Trump’s boast only works because the office is real power, not theater. He is not inventing authority from nothing; he is advertising how far he believes the office can be stretched before anyone stops him.

That distinction matters. The story is not “Trump has big feelings.” The story is that a man with real institutional leverage is narrating war and state power as proof of personal invincibility.

The Decision and the Cover

If Trump is making these claims, he made the choice to make them. If others around him shrug, amplify, or normalize them, they enable the outcome. The source text shows a familiar pattern: Trump says something authoritarian, and the public conversation immediately shifts to whether he “really believes” it.

That is already a concession. It relocates the problem from action to temperament. It asks viewers to monitor a politician’s head instead of the machinery around him. It treats a claim of boundless power as a psychological riddle rather than a political warning.

The Misdirection

This is where the media framing gets weak. Pamela Brown’s comparison to Hitler and Stalin, and the panel’s speculation about delusion, do not clarify the underlying abuse of power. They sensationalize it. They encourage the audience to stare at Trump’s ego while ignoring the institutional fact that he can use war, office, and media attention to normalize the language of unchecked rule.

The softer version of this misdirection is the panel’s hope that he is only “messaging.” That word sanitizes the act. Messaging is what campaigns do. Claims of unlimited power after launching a war are not messaging in any useful civic sense. They are a public test of what abuse can be spoken aloud without consequence.

Cruelty as Confidence

The deeper pattern is authoritarian performance disguised as self-confidence. Trump does not merely enjoy power; he presents domination as a moral metric. In the source, Jonah Goldberg says Trump measures everything by power, not by distinctions between good and bad. That is the right diagnosis, even if it stays too close to personality. The political meaning is harsher: once power becomes the only standard, cruelty stops looking like a failure and starts looking like competence.

That is why these claims matter even when they sound absurd. They are not random noise. They are bids to reset the terms of public tolerance. The more the audience is invited to debate whether he “believes his own press,” the less attention is paid to what he is telling everyone he thinks he can get away with.

The System Behind the Show

The real scandal is not that Trump boasts like a despot. It is that American political media keeps translating despotism into a personality segment. The office grants the power, the political ecosystem protects the performance, and the press repeatedly turns structural abuse into a conversation about tone, mood, or mental state.

That is the system in plain view: not one man’s ego, but an institutionally protected habit of mistaking naked power for commentary about it.
