'Screwed up': 3-time Trump voter hammers president as policies cripple his farm

House Agriculture Chair G.T. Thompson (R-PA) is planning to introduce legislation he says will ease a major shortage in workers that's followed Trump's immigration crackdown, but farmers in the Pennsylvania Republican’s district told Politico they need help right now.

“The whole thing is screwed up,” said three-time Trump voter John Painter, who operates an organic dairy farm in Westfield. “We need people to do the jobs Americans are too spoiled to do.”

The U.S. agricultural workforce fell by 155,000 — about 7 percent — between March and July, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, and the Pew Research Center found that total immigrant labor fell by 750,000 from January through July, leaving farmers with crop surpluses made worse by shrinking export markets.

“People don’t understand that if we don’t get more labor, our cows don’t get milked and our crops don’t get picked,” said dairy farmer Tim Wood, a member of the Pennsylvania Farm Bureau board of directors.

Farmers often turn to undocumented laborers to cut through red tape and higher costs related to the H-2A program that covers seasonal workers, but Thompson's legislation would allow employers to hire for year-round operations like dairy farms.

“It’s only going to last as long as this administration does, and that’s why we have to codify this,” Thompson said. “Because what farmers need [is] certainty.”

Painter hired a Mexican couple who worked for him for two years until the husband was pulled over in a traffic stop in New York, and was eventually deported after more than a year in detention. His wife and children followed him out of the country.

“I understand that he was here illegally, but I also understand that he’s human,” Painter said. “They want the American dream, and they want to work.”

Painter eventually hired another couple from Guatemala, but the Trump supporter admitted that he's “very disappointed” in the president's immigration policy in his second term.

“If they’re here working and paying their taxes,” Painter said, “they are not the troublemakers that we should be focusing on getting rid of. ... It’s not right, what they’re doing. All of us, if we look back in history, including the president, we have somebody that came to this country for the American dream.”

Our Analysis:

The Paradox of Trump's Immigration Policies and the Agricultural Sector's Plight

In a stark revelation of the unintended consequences of President Donald Trump's stringent immigration policies, farmers across the United States, particularly those in the heart of Pennsylvania, are voicing their frustrations over a burgeoning labor crisis. The policies, which were presumably designed to curb illegal immigration and protect American jobs, have instead precipitated a substantial labor shortage in the agricultural sector, highlighting a critical oversight in the administration's approach.

Labor Crisis in Numbers

According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the U.S. agricultural workforce saw a decline of 155,000 workers, approximately 7 percent, between March and July. The Pew Research Center adds another layer of context to this crisis, pointing out that the total immigrant labor force fell by a staggering 750,000 during the same period. These figures are not merely statistics; they represent the backbone of the agricultural industry, an essential pillar of the American economy, now crumbling under the weight of ill-conceived policies.

A Cry for Help

Farmers, such as John Painter, a three-time Trump voter and organic dairy farm operator, and Tim Wood, a dairy farmer and member of the Pennsylvania Farm Bureau board of directors, are on the front lines of this crisis. Their testimonials shed light on a harsh reality: the jobs that are vital for the sustenance of their farms and, by extension, the nation's food supply, are being left unfilled. The sentiment, "We need people to do the jobs Americans are too spoiled to do," as expressed by Painter, cuts to the heart of the matter, revealing a disconnect between policy and practicality.

Legislative Band-Aids

In response to the outcry, House Agriculture Chair G.T. Thompson (R-PA) is proposing legislation intended to alleviate the worker shortage. The proposed bill, which seeks to enable employers to hire for year-round operations, including dairy farms, under the H-2A program, is a step in the right direction. However, it is but a temporary solution tethered to the whims of political tides. Thompson himself acknowledges this, stating, "It’s only going to last as long as this administration does, and that’s why we have to codify this."

The Human Cost

Beyond the economic implications, there lies a human cost to these policies. Painter's account of hiring a Mexican couple, only for the husband to be deported following a traffic stop, personifies the turmoil and disruption inflicted upon lives in pursuit of the "American dream." These are not faceless numbers; they are individuals and families seeking better opportunities, much like the ancestors of many Americans, including, ironically, those who enact and support these policies.

A Disillusioned Base

Perhaps most telling is the disillusionment voiced by those like Painter, who supported Trump but now find themselves at odds with the repercussions of his policies. "If they’re here working and paying their taxes," Painter argues, "they are not the troublemakers that we should be focusing on getting rid of." This sentiment underscores a broader issue within the Trump administration's policy framework: a failure to differentiate between the economic necessities of the nation and the populist rhetoric that undermines them.

Conclusion

The labor crisis in the agricultural sector is a glaring example of policy backfiring, where the intended security measures have instead jeopardized the livelihoods of American farmers and the stability of the nation's food supply. It is a stark reminder that policies crafted in the vacuum of ideology, without regard for their practical implications, can have devastating effects on the very fabric of American society. As the nation grapples with these challenges, one can only hope for a course correction that aligns more closely with the realities of the American dream, not just for its citizens but for those who aspire to contribute to its legacy.
