The GOP faces a reckoning for letting MAGA take the reins

Trump’s Inflation Is Policy, Not Misfortune

The Damage Has an Author

Donald Trump promised to lower prices. Instead, the policies described in the reporting—mass deportations, new taxes and war with Iran—are raising the cost of energy, food and ordinary commerce. Diesel has already broken price records, gasoline is following, and inflation stands at 3.4 percent.

That is enough context. The central fact is agency: Trump is not merely presiding over an economic shock. His administration is making choices that intensify it.

The Carter Comparison Hides More Than It Reveals

Jimmy Carter inherited oil shocks he could not command. Trump is implicated in producing the disruption now consuming his presidency. Treating both men as casualties of inflation blurs the distinction between political vulnerability and political responsibility.

The analogy also flatters Trump by casting him as another president overwhelmed by history. According to the source, his administration pursued the war, deportation program and taxes driving prices upward. Those are exercises of institutional power, not weather events.

Carter’s failure was an inability to master a crisis. Trump’s failure is using presidential power to deepen one.

War Costs Disguised as Necessity

Trump’s stated reasons for attacking Iran have shifted, while the most repeated justification—preventing an Iranian nuclear bomb—is challenged by the reporting’s assertion that Iran was not close to producing one. That instability matters. A government that cannot maintain a coherent rationale for war is not explaining policy; it is manufacturing permission after the decision.

The consequences are tangible. Iran is using the Strait of Hormuz as leverage, with an adviser to its supreme leader tying its reopening to the removal of Trump and Benjamin Netanyahu from power. American consumers are paying through fuel and food prices for a conflict they were never given a consistent reason to accept.

Calling this inflation alone launders the chain of responsibility. The administration chose escalation. Escalation threatened a crucial energy route. The resulting price shock moved through the economy. The market did not invent that sequence.

Blame Is the Only Policy Left

Trump campaigned on the fiction that a president could lower prices immediately. Now that his own decisions are pushing them higher, he is asking voters to accept the pain without offering a stable account of what it purchases.

That is not confusion. It is political misdirection: promise control when seeking power, disclaim control after exercising it, then portray the consequences as unavoidable sacrifice. Even some of Trump’s strongest supporters are reportedly pleading for an end to the war and cheaper diesel. The material cost has become too obvious for partisan messaging to conceal.

The polling reflects that collapse. Trump’s inflation ratings are deeply negative, while Democrats are suddenly competitive in Texas contests that Republicans normally treat as secure. Polls are snapshots, not destinies. But these figures indicate that the administration’s preferred scapegoats are failing to absorb the blame.

The Reagan Order Reaches Its Logical End

The larger political pattern extends beyond Trump. For decades, conservative governments described public assistance as reckless spending while directing generous state support toward the wealthy. “Supply-side economics” supplied the respectable vocabulary; upward redistribution supplied the result.

MAGA did not abandon that system. It stripped away its euphemisms. The same political project that burdens working people with bureaucratic suspicion now expects them to absorb higher prices created by presidential adventurism. Public power remains expansive when serving wealth, coercion and war. It becomes helpless only when ordinary people need relief.

If voters come to understand that arrangement as extraction rather than economics, Trump’s inflation crisis could discredit more than one administration. It could expose the governing bargain constructed since Reagan: concentrated benefits at the top, dispersed pain below, and an elaborate propaganda apparatus insisting that no one in power chose any of it.

Impunity Eventually Appears on the Receipt

The important historical parallel is not that Trump resembles Carter. It is that economic crises can destroy the stories political orders tell about themselves.

Trumpism promised command, prosperity and national strength. The reporting instead depicts deliberate choices producing scarcity, higher prices and foreign leverage over American economic life. The people wielding state power created the conditions; consumers received the bill.

Systems built on impunity rarely collapse because their leaders discover restraint. They weaken when consequences become too immediate to misdirect—and when the public can finally see whose signature is on the policy.
