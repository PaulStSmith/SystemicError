Prison sentence for secretive pro-Trump mega-church leader raises concern

The Church That Bought Access Now Wants Sympathy

A Court in Seoul, Not a Culture War

On Monday, a Seoul court sentenced Family Federation for World Peace and Unification leader Han Hak-ja to two years in prison for political bribery, illegal political contributions, and related misconduct. Prosecutors had asked for 13 years. The result was a major reduction, but the center of gravity is still obvious: this is not a story about religious persecution. It is a story about political money, elite access, and the collapse of the alibi.

The Real Power Was Never Spiritual

Han Hak-ja is not operating as a lonely faith leader under siege. The source describes a long-running political machine with 50-year ties to the Republican Party, a record of defending Nixon during Watergate, and recent efforts to win favor inside Trump’s orbit. That is institutional power, not innocence. The church’s claim to be a persecuted Christian body is being used as a shield for a far-right network that has treated donations, conferences, and political proximity as a business model.

Bribery Was the Method

The most important facts in the reporting are blunt. Han was convicted of bribing former first lady Kim Keon Hee and another conservative politician. Investigators also found more than $26 million hidden in a safe in Han’s bedroom, while Han, her chief of staff, and a former deputy were accused of moving over $7 million through false accounting between 2017 and 2025. Even where the court acquitted her on the embezzlement charge, the broader picture does not soften. It sharpens it. The institution was not merely influential. It was allegedly laundering money into politics and calling the result a moral mission.

Trumpism Is the Open Secret

The church’s appeal to the Trump administration on the grounds of “Christian persecution” is classic misdirection. The source makes clear that the relationship rests on something much less pious: long-standing financial arrangements and lavish church largesse for senior figures in Trump’s circle, including Trump himself. That is the actual exchange. The church offers money and access. Republicans offer proximity and legitimization. When the arrangement becomes inconvenient, the language shifts to faith, grievance, and anti-communist nostalgia.

South Korea Is Not the Main Point

Trump’s recent praise of Kim Jong Un and his Truth Social attack on South Korea for refusing to join his war against Iran have complicated the alliance the church once relied on. That matters, but not because it reveals a subtle policy dispute. It shows how fragile these patronage networks are when they are built around personality, donor access, and ideological theater instead of public accountability. Seoul’s conservative Christian right may be dismayed. That is not a defense of democracy. It is a complaint from a faction that expected its investment to keep paying.

The Pattern Is Corruption Dressed as Faith

This is the recurring logic of authoritarian politics: money buys influence, influence buys legitimacy, and legitimacy is then repackaged as moral persecution when the account books are exposed. The church’s shrinking power does not make it harmless; it makes it more desperate. The Republican network around it is not being tricked. It is participating. The scandal is not that a fringe religious empire drifted into politics. The scandal is that U.S. conservatism has been willing for decades to rent itself out to whatever coalition can fund its ambitions, flatter its resentments, and launder its power in the language of Christianity.
