Trump's tantrum just crossed a line even Nixon feared: analyst

By Paulo Santos

President Donald Trump's tantrum over the weak jobs numbers and termination of the Bureau of Labor Statistics director is in many ways a repeat of history, as former President Richard Nixon similarly became paranoid that federal data was being cooked to sabotage him. But there's a big difference in how far each president was willing to go, at least in public, to twist the civil service to their will, historian Tim Naftali wrote for The Atlantic.

Trump's move against BLS Commissioner Erika McEntarfer — which he justified by baselessly claiming that Democratic operatives fraudulently manufactured the numbers to make him look bad — has raised massive alarms among experts who fear it could undermine the government's entire ability to produce reliable economic data used for all manner of policymaking.

Nixon was in a similar state of mind more than 50 years ago, wrote Naftali.

"In July of 1971, the president was infuriated that an unnamed official at the Bureau of Labor Statistics had seemed to downplay the administration’s progress on reducing unemployment while briefing reporters," he wrote. "His suspicions fell on Harold Goldstein, the longtime civil servant and BLS official in charge of the jobs numbers, who had attracted his ire for other comments earlier in the year. Nixon ordered his political counselor, Charles Colson, to investigate. If it had been Goldstein, he said, 'he’s got to be fired.'"

Nixon went on to privately rage to his staff that the government was "full of Jews" who were "disloyal" to America.

And yet, wrote Naftali, Nixon had a few lines that he wasn't willing to cross, that Trump is.

"Unlike Trump, who lashed out publicly against McEntarfer, Nixon was afraid to own his bad behavior," wrote Naftali. "He did not force out his BLS commissioner in 1971, instead waiting for the chance to accept his resignation two years later. Not wanting his hands to be dirty — as defined by the presidential norms of his era — Nixon constrained himself to abuse power only indirectly. He had no desire to risk public disapproval by firing bureaucrats for specious and explosive reasons."

Nixon's chief of staff, Bob Haldeman, was instrumental in restraining him from going that far. But today, "Neither Trump’s Cabinet members nor his White House staff are willing to serve as a check on presidential bad behavior," wrote Naftali. "And so last week, Trump did what not even Nixon had dared, becoming the first president ever to fire his BLS commissioner."

Our Analysis:

Trump's Assault on the Bureau of Labor Statistics: A Dangerous Precedent

In an era where the lines between political bravado and authoritarian tendencies blur with alarming regularity, President Donald Trump's recent outburst and subsequent firing of Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) Commissioner Erika McEntarfer marks a chilling escalation in presidential overreach. Drawing parallels with former President Richard Nixon—a figure synonymous with political paranoia and scandal—the comparison laid out by historian Tim Naftali in The Atlantic reveals much about the degradation of presidential norms and the increasing threat to democratic institutions.

Trump's Unprecedented Move

Trump's decision to terminate McEntarfer, allegedly due to baseless claims that Democratic operatives manipulated jobs numbers to tarnish his presidency, is not just an act of political retribution; it's a direct assault on the integrity of the United States' economic data infrastructure. This infrastructure is not only critical for policymaking but also for maintaining public trust in the government's ability to operate beyond the reach of partisan manipulation.

The boldness of Trump's actions cannot be overstated. Unlike Nixon, who, despite his deep-seated paranoia and anti-Semitism, hesitated to publicly purge the civil service of perceived enemies, Trump has shown no such restraint. His willingness to openly challenge and dismantle the norms that have safeguarded the independence of the civil service signals a dangerous departure from past presidential behavior.

Nixon's Reluctance vs. Trump's Recklessness

Nixon, for all his flaws, operated within a framework that still recognized certain limits. His decision not to publicly fire his BLS commissioner, as Naftali notes, stemmed from a desire to avoid direct public backlash. This restraint was partially due to the presence of figures like Chief of Staff Bob Haldeman, who, despite their complicity in other abuses of power, served as a buffer against the most damaging impulses of the presidency.

Fast forward to the present, and the absence of such restraining influences around Trump has become painfully evident. The lack of opposition from Trump's Cabinet or White House staff to his actions against McEntarfer underlines a broader erosion of checks and balances within the executive branch. It's a scenario where the president's worst instincts are not only unchallenged but often enabled and amplified.

The Implications

The ramifications of Trump's actions extend far beyond the immediate political fallout. By undermining the credibility of the BLS, Trump is attacking the very foundation of evidence-based policymaking. In a world increasingly governed by data, the ability to trust that data as impartial and accurate is paramount. Once this trust is eroded, the path is open not just for mismanagement and inefficiency but for the manipulation of public perception and the erosion of democratic governance.

Moreover, Trump's willingness to flout norms and engage in such public confrontations with the civil service sets a dangerous precedent for future administrations. It signals a shift towards a more autocratic style of governance, where loyalty to the president outweighs expertise, integrity, and the public good.

In conclusion, Trump's firing of McEntarfer should not be viewed in isolation but as part of a broader assault on democratic institutions and norms. It's a stark reminder of the fragility of these systems and the constant vigilance required to protect them. As we reflect on the lessons of history, the comparison to Nixon's era serves not just as a cautionary tale but as a call to action to defend the principles that underpin democratic governance.
