Trump menaces in overnight finger-pointing frenzy: 'Liars must be held accountable'

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

Paulo SantosAugust 19, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump Spent $1.7 Billion on a Spectacle and Wants to Punish the Receipt

Power Is the Story

The reporting here is simple enough: a Center for American Progress analyst, Chandler Hall, said National Guard deployments to city streets showed no measurable effect on violent crime, and Trump answered not with evidence, but with threats. He is now talking about suing the think tank and its donors after presiding over a deployment that reportedly cost almost $1.7 billion.

That is the whole hierarchy in miniature. Trump holds the office. Trump authorized or embraced the deployment. Trump spent public money on the performance. And Trump is now trying to turn the aftermath into a legal and political hunt for the people who documented the failure.

The Report Is Not the Offense

The report did not create the boondoggle. It described it.

Trump’s response tells you everything about the function of the deployment: it was never just about crime. It was about staging force, broadcasting domination, and demanding gratitude for theater. When the data does not flatter the performance, the data becomes the enemy.

That is a familiar authoritarian move. Harms are reframed as misunderstandings. Evidence becomes “scam” language. Accountability gets recast as sabotage.

The Donor List Is the Real Threat Vector

Trump’s Truth Social post is not a rebuttal. It is intimidation by registry. He names donors, foreign institutions, corporations, and foundations as if the mere fact of funding a policy research group is a crime. That is not concern about transparency. It is a pressure campaign against independent criticism.

The point is to widen the blast radius. If the analyst can be dismissed, fine. If the institute can be smeared, better. If donors can be frightened into silence, ideal. The message is crude: fund research that contradicts power and power may come for you.

Crime Talk as Cover for Waste

Trump also claims “crime is way down since I took Office,” while using the threat of crime to justify militarized street politics. That contradiction is the center of the story. The public is asked to accept extraordinary spending, extraordinary deployments, and extraordinary executive posturing on the basis of fear, while the reported evidence says the deployments did not change violent crime in the cities studied.

So the issue is not whether Trump believes the rhetoric. He does not need to. The rhetoric is the instrument. The money goes out. The optics are harvested. The failure is then blamed on anyone who says the numbers out loud.

What This Pattern Reveals

This is not a policy dispute. It is a governing method: spend first, intimidate later, and attack the institutions that measure the result. The more useless the spectacle, the louder the effort to punish witnesses.

That is the real political meaning of the story. A president who can deploy force without delivering public safety will reach for lawsuits, donor shaming, and propaganda to conceal the gap. The danger is not just the wasted $1.7 billion. It is the normalization of a state that treats accountability as hostility and evidence as treason.
