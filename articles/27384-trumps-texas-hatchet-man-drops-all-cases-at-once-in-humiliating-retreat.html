Trump’s Texas hatchet-man drops all cases at once in 'humiliating' retreat

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 21, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The state walks away after imposing the costsA culture-war docket collapses at onceTexas supplied the lawyers; weaker targets paid the price

Ken Paxton’s final weeks as Texas attorney general have become an exercise in destroying the evidence of his office’s political priorities—not by concealing records, but by abandoning the cases that made those priorities unmistakable. After losing his bid for the U.S. Senate and his post in a Democratic sweep, Paxton abruptly withdrew a string of lawsuits targeting immigrant aid organizations, transgender athletes, a Muslim civil rights group and others. Some dismissals permanently bar the state from reviving similar claims.

The retreat matters because these were not incidental disputes. Paxton used the authority and public funding of the attorney general’s office to turn conservative grievances into state litigation, often against organizations with far fewer resources than Texas. Now, before Democrat Nathan Johnson can inherit the cases and decide their fate, Paxton is disposing of them himself.

The state walks away after imposing the costs

Among the defendants was Annunciation House, an El Paso shelter for migrants led by Ruben Garcia. Paxton’s office accused the organization of harboring undocumented immigrants and participating in human smuggling. According to the Houston Chronicle, the state dropped the suit without explanation.

Garcia described the withdrawal as an immense relief, but the state’s exit does not restore what the litigation consumed. He said Annunciation House lost money to legal fees and lost donors while defending itself against Texas. State power extracted those costs even though the case never reached a judgment validating Paxton’s allegations.

“The case was very much alive,” Garcia said. “I can’t help but believe that this was an acknowledgement of the writing on the wall.”

Paxton’s office also abandoned a case against FIEL, a Houston-area immigrant advocacy group. The Annunciation House and FIEL cases were dismissed with prejudice, meaning the attorney general’s office surrendered its ability to bring similar charges against the organizations later. That choice goes beyond a temporary change in litigation strategy. It closes the door Paxton’s own office had insisted needed to remain open.

Former Maine Attorney General James Tierney, a Democrat, offered the blunt interpretation: “The lawsuits have no merit, and he knows it.” That is Tierney’s assessment, not a judicial finding. But the unexplained withdrawals give Paxton’s office responsibility for answering the obvious question it has so far left to others: If these cases justified deploying the state’s lawyers and money, why abandon them now?

A culture-war docket collapses at once

The immigrant organizations were not isolated beneficiaries of Paxton’s retreat. His office also stopped pursuing a lawsuit against the NCAA over transgender athletes and, according to the Chronicle, is no longer pursuing a case against the Council on American-Islamic Relations. It withdrew support for a MAGA-aligned doctor reprimanded for prescribing ivermectin during the COVID-19 pandemic.

The cases shared a political function even where their legal claims differed. Each placed the Texas attorney general’s office inside a national conservative conflict—immigration, transgender participation in sports, Muslim civil rights or pandemic-era medical backlash. Paxton had made that alignment a defining feature of his tenure, using the state’s institutional weight against targets already marked as enemies by the Trump movement.

Former Washington Attorney General Rob McKenna, a Republican, told the Chronicle that an outgoing attorney general abandoning cases on this scale was extraordinary. “It’s not at all normal,” he said. “I can’t think of another example of an AG doing that.”

That unusual concentration of dismissals makes the timing politically revealing, even if Paxton has not supplied a public explanation. Nathan Johnson is preparing to take control of the office and has said he will end its service as what he described as a publicly financed law firm for MAGA interests. Paxton’s withdrawals deny the incoming attorney general the chance to terminate the cases himself—and to expose, through that process, which arguments the new administration considers indefensible.

“My guess is they don’t want to grant me the privilege of terminating their bad work,” Johnson told Texas Monthly. “They don’t want me taking credit.”

It is an inference, but one grounded in the transfer of power unfolding around the dismissals. Paxton is not merely leaving office. He is clearing contested litigation from the desk before his successor can examine and repudiate it.

Texas supplied the lawyers; weaker targets paid the price

Under Paxton, Texas became an important launch site for conservative legal campaigns. His office defended the state’s severe abortion restrictions and an immigration law authorizing local police to arrest migrants, an authority traditionally exercised by the federal government. The U.S. Supreme Court allowed both measures to take effect.

That record shows why describing Paxton as merely a partisan provocateur understates his power. He controlled a state legal apparatus capable of burdening organizations with expensive litigation, defending coercive laws and advancing cases through conservative courts toward a Supreme Court often receptive to his arguments. His targets had to respond whether or not the cases ultimately survived. Annunciation House’s lost donations and legal expenses were not rhetorical fallout; they were material consequences imposed by the state.

The dismissals do not prove that every abandoned claim was knowingly frivolous when filed. They do, however, expose the asymmetry built into this kind of government litigation. Paxton’s office could initiate headline-generating confrontations at taxpayer expense. The organizations he targeted had to divert their own money and labor to survive them. When the political conditions changed, the state could simply walk away.

Johnson has called the arrangement corrupt, arguing that MAGA treated Texas as “their own private law firm” while the public paid the bill. His description identifies the central abuse alleged here: not that an attorney general pursued policies consistent with his politics, but that the office’s coercive and financial power was harnessed to a national movement’s chosen enemies.

Paxton’s final act does not erase that record. It sharpens it. Annunciation House and FIEL are protected from a renewed attack on similar grounds, but neither recovers what the state forced it to spend. Paxton leaves before his successor can fire him from cases that, at the decisive moment, Paxton was unwilling to defend himself.
