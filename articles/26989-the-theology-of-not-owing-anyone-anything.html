The Theology of Not Owing Anyone Anything

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 2, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Every obscene system needs a moral vocabulary. It needs phrases that make cruelty sound principled.

One of the most revealing is the response that appears whenever someone says food, housing, or healthcare should be treated as a human right: “No one should be entitled to the labor of another.”

At first, it sounds serious. It borrows the language of freedom, autonomy, and consent. But in practice, it is almost never used to defend workers from exploitation. It is not usually aimed at the landlord, the shareholder, the insurance executive, the inherited fortune, or the billionaire whose wealth exists because millions of people’s labor was converted into private ownership.

Instead, it is aimed downward.

It is used to tell the hungry that feeding them would be tyranny. It is used to tell the sick that treating them would be theft. It is used to tell the poor that survival itself is an unreasonable demand on society.

But food as a right does not mean enslaving a farmer. Healthcare as a right does not mean enslaving a doctor. Public education as a right does not mean enslaving a teacher. It means organizing a society so that essential human needs are not left to charity, market luck, or private mercy. It means paying people, taxing wealth, building institutions, and deciding that no child should starve because someone found a clever sentence on the internet.

The phrase also hides a spectacular hypocrisy. Modern capitalism is already built on people being entitled to the labor of others. That is what profit is. That is what rent is. That is what interest is. That is what shareholder value is. The owner is entitled to the worker’s surplus. The landlord is entitled to the tenant’s wages. The insurer is entitled to deny care while collecting premiums. The billionaire is entitled to appreciation created by public infrastructure, state contracts, workers, consumers, courts, roads, schools, subsidies, and government research.

Apparently, the moral emergency begins only when the entitlement flows toward keeping people alive.

No one says, “Why is Musk entitled to the labor of Tesla workers?” No one says, “Why are shareholders entitled to the labor of nurses?” No one says, “Why is a landlord entitled to half a worker’s paycheck because his name is on a deed?” The objection is strangely selective. It appears precisely at the moment when society might owe something to the vulnerable.

That selectivity is the tell.

The real principle is not “no one should be entitled to the labor of another.” The real principle is: the powerful may extract, but the desperate may not claim.

And that is not freedom. That is hierarchy with better branding.

A humane society begins from a different premise: people are not born owing proof of usefulness before they are allowed to eat. Children do not have to justify calories. The disabled do not have to win a productivity contest to deserve medicine. The elderly do not become disposable because they no longer generate profit. Human beings are not invoices waiting to be approved.

Rights are not magic. They require labor, institutions, taxation, and coordination. So does every society. The question is never whether labor will be organized. The question is for whose benefit.

If we can organize labor to build yachts, data centers, private rockets, luxury towers, weapons systems, and algorithmic advertising markets, then we can organize labor to feed people. The obstacle is not capacity. It is moral permission.

And that is what the defenders of this obscenity are really protecting: not liberty, but permission. Permission to look at preventable suffering and call it discipline. Permission to confuse abandonment with responsibility. Permission to imagine themselves as temporarily inconvenienced millionaires rather than members of a society that could break under the weight of its own indifference.

The French aristocracy had this too. Not the same words, but the same structure of thought: the people below were always asking for too much, failing to understand economics, failing to appreciate order, failing to respect the natural arrangement of things. The poor were not starving because the system was cruel. They were starving because reality was hard, because reform was complicated, because privilege was necessary, because the people who benefited from the arrangement had developed a philosophy proving they deserved to benefit.

That is how ruling ideologies work. They do not say, “Let them suffer.” They say, “Be realistic.”

And then they are shocked when the suffering stop asking politely.
