What Were Once Trains… Have Become Planes

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 7, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The image is stark, deliberately provocative, and impossible to ignore. In the upper panel, Jewish families—men, women, children—are being herded at gunpoint by uniformed Nazis into cattle cars marked “JUDEN.” This chilling depiction is not just historical reference; it is a visual shorthand for genocide, for state-sanctioned erasure, for the cold, bureaucratic efficiency of evil. It evokes the Holocaust in all its horror: not just the violence, but the process—the systemized machinery of oppression that turned human beings into problems to be solved, units to be transported, obstacles to be eliminated.

In the lower panel, that same mechanism has been transposed into a modern setting. Instead of cattle cars, we see a passenger plane. Instead of the word “JUDEN,” we see “EL SALVADOR.” Instead of Nazi uniforms, the agents wear tactical gear emblazoned with “ICE.” The detainees—brown-skinned, anonymous, burdened—are marched single file up the loading ramp. The uniformed men do not shout; they don’t need to. Their authority is implicit, and unquestioned. The visual symmetry between past and present is undeniable.

The caption ties both scenes together with brutal clarity: “What were once trains… have become planes.”

This is not a subtle political cartoon. It is not meant to tiptoe around the edges of moral discomfort. It kicks the door in, dares the viewer to look away, and asks: how different are we, really? The answer, unsettling as it is, may be: not enough.

For those who recoil at the comparison—invoking “Godwin’s Law” or labeling it “alarmist hyperbole”—consider what underlies that discomfort. It isn’t just historical indignation; it’s the disquieting realization that the mechanisms of atrocity have not vanished. They have simply evolved. They wear different uniforms, speak a different bureaucratic dialect, and move with the same detached precision.

The image doesn’t accuse the present of being the past. Rather, it warns that we are treading parallel tracks.

The power of this imagery lies in the emotional continuity it evokes—continuity between the bureaucratic management of genocide and the procedural coldness of modern deportation. It’s not about moral equivalence; it’s about method. About how institutions depersonalize human lives into categories: refugee, criminal, deportee, undesired. It is about the banality of state cruelty wrapped in the language of law and order.

We like to believe that history is a linear journey toward enlightenment. But in truth, it is circular. Each generation is tested not just by new challenges, but by familiar temptations: scapegoating, nationalism, racial supremacy, legalistic cruelty masquerading as civic duty. When children are ripped from their parents and locked in cages, when refugee claims are systematically ignored or fast-tracked into rejection, when deportations are executed without oversight or accountability, the state is not failing—it is functioning exactly as designed.

ICE deportation flights often happen in the dead of night, out of public view, with minimal transparency. Deportees are shackled, disoriented, and sometimes returned to the very violence they fled. And yet, the public is told this is normal. This is the system working. This is what law enforcement looks like. But history warns us that such normalization is the first step toward moral rot.

Uniformed agents executing orders under the banner of legality—this is not unique to the Third Reich. It is a feature of every authoritarian regime that has ever existed. It is not the armband that matters—it is the impunity. It is the obedience. It is the dulling of conscience in the service of a process.

We must resist the seduction of sanitization. Planes may seem more humane than cattle cars. Deportation centers may not look like concentration camps. But the trauma inflicted is not a function of aesthetics. The loss—the disintegration of families, the exile from one’s community, the fear of returning to violence—is real, visceral, and generational.

This image poses a challenge not only to our policies but to our moral memory. Are we truly committed to “never again,” or only when it’s politically convenient? Do we apply historical lessons universally, or do we twist them into selective outrage? If we allow cruelty to masquerade as patriotism, and bureaucracy to shield conscience, we are not protecting our society—we are degrading it.

The line between national security and human rights abuse is perilously thin. In times of fear, that line tends to vanish. And when it does, we don’t notice the tipping point until we’re standing on the wrong side of history.

So yes, this image is uncomfortable. It should be. It forces us to confront the things we would rather keep buried: our indifference, our complicity, our failure to heed the lessons of history.

Because if we’re not horrified by what is being done in our name—if we do not act, speak, vote, and resist—then we have not just boarded the plane.

We have arrived at the station.

And we are wearing the uniform.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.

Written and published by Paulo Santos for Systemic Error.
