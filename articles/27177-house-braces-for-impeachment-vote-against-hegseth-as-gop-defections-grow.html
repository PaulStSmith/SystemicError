House braces for impeachment vote against Hegseth as GOP defections grow

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosNovember 10, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower made the warThe convenient sacrificeCongress arrives latePropaganda cannot repair capacityThe system protects the policy

What happened

AlterNet reports that House Republican leaders expected enough GOP defections to impeach Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth over his leadership of the Pentagon and the war in Iran. Republican Rep. Thomas Massie planned to introduce an impeachment resolution and force a vote that week unless Hegseth resigned; House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries said he would support it.

The report says Hegseth released a video promising to “finish what we have started” as senior military leaders warned that the Iran operation was unsustainable. The Wall Street Journal reportedly described mounting strain on military families and a maintenance backlog that could take years to clear, while Navy chief Adm. Daryl Caudle said the service could not sustain its current support level.

AlterNet does not establish that Hegseth had resigned, been impeached, or lost the expected vote. It presents his removal as an approaching possibility, with author Robert Reich arguing that the Trump administration could use Hegseth as a convenient scapegoat for a wider policy failure.

Power made the war

Hegseth holds immense institutional power as defense secretary, but he does not possess the presidency’s authority to initiate and direct national policy. If the administration entered and prolonged a costly war, responsibility extends beyond the Pentagon official selling it on camera. The president owns the policy; senior civilian officials execute it; Congress decides whether to restrain or sustain it.

That hierarchy matters because Reich’s account risks making Hegseth’s manifest inadequacy the whole explanation. An unqualified loyalist can worsen a disaster, purge expertise and punish dissent. He cannot absolve the people who selected him, empowered him and accepted loyalty as a substitute for judgment.

The convenient sacrifice

Removing Hegseth could be justified on the conduct described: chaos inside the Pentagon, attacks on officers, fixation on culture-war grievances and dismissiveness toward warnings about military capacity. But accountability becomes misdirection when it stops with the most expendable official.

Reich explicitly identifies the political utility of firing him: the administration could declare a reset, alter its presentation and leave the underlying decision-makers untouched. That would not be institutional correction. It would be damage control through personnel theater.

Congress arrives late

Massie’s threatened resolution and the reported Republican defections show that Congress can act when the political cost of inaction becomes high enough. They also expose how long lawmakers can tolerate dysfunction while executive policy remains useful to their coalition.

Impeachment is a constitutional instrument, not a substitute for sustained oversight. A vote against one Cabinet secretary does not answer who authorized the war’s course, who funded it, who ignored military constraints or why congressional resistance emerged only after the operation became politically toxic.

Propaganda cannot repair capacity

Hegseth’s reported answer to disclosures about military strain was to condemn the media for revealing classified information. That response redirects attention from the substance of the warnings to the fact that the public learned about them.

His triumphalist claims perform the same function. Declarations that America is winning “decisively” do not repair equipment, relieve service members or create strategic coherence. They turn measurable institutional limits into a messaging problem and treat scrutiny as disloyalty.

The system protects the policy

The larger pattern is familiar: leaders centralize power, elevate loyalists, marginalize expertise and then personalize failure when consequences become undeniable. The subordinate may deserve removal, but his removal also protects the structure that made him useful.

If Hegseth falls while the officials who empowered him retain control of the war and its narrative, the system will have punished incompetence without confronting authorization. That is not full accountability. It is the political management of blame.
