'Few options remain' for Trump as advisor says he’s 'just trying to hang on'

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosAugust 28, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Sanctions as Stagecraft

Power Still Sits With Washington

The reporting is clear about where the leverage is: Trump’s White House controls sanctions, Treasury enforcement, and the public story around Iran. Scott Bessent’s evasiveness on whether China would be pressed to join sanctions matters because it shows the policy is not some external constraint. It is a choice. If the administration does not use the instruments it controls, then “maximum pressure” is not strategy. It is branding.

The Old Failure, Repackaged

Trump is recycling the same sanctions-and-isolation approach that already failed to force Iran to abandon its nuclear program. The source notes that the intelligence community had expected financial strain to break Tehran quickly, and that did not happen. That is not a minor forecasting error. It is evidence that economic punishment has limits when the target can absorb pain, reroute trade, and wait out U.S. politics.

The Real Goal Is Time

The most revealing line in the reporting is the one about Republicans trying to “hang on until the midterms.” That is the operating system here. The point is not resolution. It is delay. Reduce the conflict’s visibility, hope gas prices ease, stop talking about the war, and try to preserve Republican electoral prospects. Foreign policy is being managed like a communications problem, not a security challenge.

Misdirection as Policy

This is where the framing gets cynical. The White House can talk about sanctions all it wants, but if it refuses to confront Iran’s biggest buyers, the effort becomes performative. Meanwhile, the affordability crisis is treated as an annoyance to be managed until it can be hidden behind another headline. The administration is not solving the economic burden it helped intensify. It is trying to outlast the public’s memory of it.

Victory Theater, Then Cuba

The reporting also shows the deeper political habit: declare victory the moment the optics become useful. If Trump can get Iran to open the Strait of Hormuz, he can pretend the mission is complete and move on to the next target, with Cuba already waiting in the queue. That is not statecraft. It is serial escalation followed by self-congratulation. The pattern is not competence interrupted by bad luck. It is a governing style built around spectacle, interruption, and the constant search for a new enemy.

The Pattern

This story is not about sanctions in the abstract. It is about a ruling coalition using coercion to buy time, then asking the public to mistake delay for control. That is how authoritarian politics works in practice: not always through clean declarations, but through exhausted institutions, evasive officials, and a permanent campaign that treats war, prices, and diplomacy as interchangeable tools of electoral survival.

It’s the design.
