'Trump is our bully': Why some Americans can't quit the president

Based on an article by Chauncey DeVega

Paulo SantosOctober 22, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Some Americans remain loyal to President Donald Trump despite policies that hurt their communities because, sociologist Arlie Hochschild told AlterNet, he has tied their economic losses and wounded pride to a story of betrayal, scapegoats and promised retribution. Voters she interviewed acknowledged that Trump lies and bullies but described him as “our bully”—a powerful figure they believed would fight for people otherwise ignored.

AlterNet reports that, less than two weeks before the midterm elections, Trump’s approval stood at 31 percent in a Reuters/Ipsos poll and that analysts expected Republicans could lose the House and possibly the Senate. Hochschild said enthusiasm had faded in eastern Kentucky, but she observed depressive withdrawal rather than a decisive political realignment.

She attributed that endurance to decades of lost coal employment, opioid addiction, low wages and collapsing civic institutions. She also said Trump had not restored coal jobs or substantially addressed the opioid crisis, while Republican safety-net cuts had inflicted particular damage on food-insecure Appalachian communities.

The people with the power

The decisive actors are not hungry families in West Virginia or unemployed miners in Kentucky. Trump holds the presidency. Republican lawmakers exercised legislative power. Their administration and congressional majority chose the policies Hochschild describes as cutting food assistance and shifting resources upward.

Yet the story devotes most of its attention to why injured voters remain attached to the politician presiding over that injury. That is a legitimate question, but it can quietly relocate responsibility: policy becomes a puzzle of voter psychology rather than an exercise of governing power.

Residents may have helped elect these officials, but casting a ballot is not equivalent to drafting legislation, controlling agencies or allocating federal resources. Political responsibility is shared unevenly because institutional power is distributed unevenly.

Pain converted into permission

Hochschild’s “emotional capture” describes a recognizable political transaction. Trump acknowledges real humiliation, supplies weaker targets to blame and presents himself as the instrument of revenge. Economic decline caused by disappearing industries and institutional abandonment is redirected toward immigrants, journalists and government workers.

The pet-eating falsehood she cites illustrates the mechanism. An outrageous claim attracts condemnation; Trump then recasts that condemnation as persecution shared with his supporters. The lie becomes secondary to the emotional bond produced by defending him. Accountability is repackaged as elite contempt.

This is propaganda built from genuine injury. The hardship need not be invented when its cause can be falsified and its anger pointed sideways.

“Our bully” is not confusion

Calling Trump “our bully” suggests that at least some supporters understand his conduct and accept it as useful. Their allegiance is not simply a failure to receive correct information. It can also be a decision that domination is tolerable when the dominant figure appears to target disliked outsiders.

That does not make impoverished voters the authors of every policy imposed on them. It does mean hardship cannot automatically be treated as political innocence. People can be manipulated and still possess agency; they can recognize a lie and continue rewarding the liar.

Hochschild’s account also identifies a practical failure by Democrats. In a region where they have become culturally marginal, sporadic appeals cannot substitute for durable organization, credible economic policy and institutions that remain after an election ends.

Division pays upward

The central misdirection is lateral. Rural and urban workers, poor and middle-class people, and voters with different levels of formal education are encouraged to treat one another as the cause of decline. Meanwhile, according to Hochschild, money and resources move from poorer people toward richer ones.

Her account of a Kentucky discussion over food assistance shows what breaks through that diversion: wages of eight or nine dollars an hour, caregiving obligations, unreliable cars, fuel costs and the absence of sustaining jobs. These are material constraints, not cultural riddles. They expose the fraud in demanding work while tolerating an economy that does not supply viable work.

The system behind the loyalty

The larger pattern is not that rural voters inexplicably prefer suffering. It is that concentrated political power can exploit abandonment it did not repair, direct resentment toward weaker groups and then use the resulting loyalty to deepen material deprivation.

Trump’s strength in this account rests on more than personal magnetism. It depends on social isolation, collapsed local institutions, weak unions, an absent opposition and a political culture that substitutes recognition for provision. The bully remains valuable because the system offers many people humiliation, then lets the most powerful man in it pretend he is merely avenging them.
