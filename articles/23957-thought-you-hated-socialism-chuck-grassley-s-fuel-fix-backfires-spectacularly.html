'Thought you hated socialism?' Chuck Grassley's fuel fix backfires spectacularly

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosSeptember 22, 20263 min read

Grassley Wants to Discount the Damage He Helped Enable

The Bill Arrives at the Pump

Reporting links roughly $97 billion in additional fuel costs paid by Americans to the Trump administration’s war against Iran. With diesel averaging a reported $6.51 per gallon on Monday—described as an all-time high—Sen. Chuck Grassley proposed an emergency export embargo, then suggested oil companies accept smaller margins when selling diesel to farmers and truckers.

That is the necessary context. The political story is not that Grassley has discovered an eccentric form of socialism. It is that a senator who supported the war is now searching for a politically convenient way to suppress one of its domestic costs.

Power Made the Crisis

The relevant institutional power sits with the administration that launched the war and the lawmakers who supported it. Farmers did not set foreign policy. Truckers did not choose military escalation. Consumers buying fuel did not design the conditions behind the reported price surge.

Grassley nevertheless framed the problem downstream. His answer was not to challenge the war policy identified in the reporting as the source of higher fuel costs. It was first to restrict diesel exports and then to pressure producers to charge selected domestic constituencies less.

Those measures may affect prices, but they leave the originating decision conveniently outside the frame.

“Big Oil” Becomes the Substitute Culprit

Grassley said “Big Oil” rejected his embargo proposal, though the reporting provides no response from the companies or detail about who communicated that position. He then argued that oil companies need not earn the same profit from American farmers and truckers that they earn from foreign sales.

Oil companies possess enormous economic leverage, and their pricing deserves scrutiny. But Grassley’s pivot also performs a useful act of misdirection: it transfers attention from officials who chose and supported the war to companies managing the market consequences.

The companies may be exercising pricing power. The article does not establish that they engineered the record diesel price or artificially inflated it. What it does establish is that Grassley supported the war and, once its reported costs became politically painful, began demanding sacrifice from another powerful actor.

“America First” Means Favored Constituencies First

Grassley attached “AMERICA FIRST” to his demand for reduced margins. Yet his proposal was narrower than the slogan. He named farmers and truckers—groups with obvious economic importance and political resonance—rather than offering a general account of who should absorb the costs of war.

That selectivity exposes the governing logic. The policy that generated the burden remains intact; politically valuable constituencies are offered insulation from it. The cost must go somewhere else: exporters, producers, other consumers, or the broader economy.

This is not a coherent commitment to free markets or state intervention. It is constituency management after a policy failure.

The Socialism Taunt Misses the Larger Fraud

Critics mocked Grassley for embracing something resembling the price and export controls that Republicans routinely condemn as socialism. The hypocrisy is real, but ideological name-calling is the smaller story. Capitalist governments have used price controls before, as the reporting notes, and the label alone says little about whether a particular intervention is workable or justified.

The sharper question is why government intervention becomes acceptable only after leaders have helped create an intolerable cost. Grassley’s reversal suggests that “free market” language is conditional: profits and market prices are treated as sacred until they threaten favored industries, voters, or incumbents.

The doctrine bends. Political responsibility does not.

War Policy Without Political Accountability

Macroeconomist Philip Pilkington argued that Grassley bore responsibility because he supported the war; journalist Ryan Grim supplied the more direct alternative: “Could also end the war?” The reporting does not establish that ending the conflict would immediately restore fuel prices, but the question correctly returns attention to the decision that Grassley’s proposals evade.

This is the larger pattern: officials authorize disruptive policy, defend it at the level of patriotic abstraction, and then distance themselves from its material consequences. When the damage reaches voters, they stage a rebellion against prices, corporations, or market mechanisms—as though those consequences appeared without political authors.

Grassley is not powerless before this crisis. He is a United States senator who supported the policy the reporting identifies as its cause. His attempt to recast himself as the defender of farmers and truckers is not accountability. It is the political system offering a discount on harm while refusing to reconsider who ordered it.
