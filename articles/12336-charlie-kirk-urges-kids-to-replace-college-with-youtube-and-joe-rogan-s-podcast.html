Charlie Kirk urges kids to replace college with YouTube and Joe Rogan's podcast

MAGA influencer Charlie Kirk argued that young people should skip college because they could get "the same wisdom and the same teaching" by watching YouTube videos and listening to Joe Rogan's podcast.

While hosting Fox & Friends on Sunday, Rachel Campos-Duffy noted that Kirk had been able to quote Aristotle, despite not attending college.

"So what is it that kids who don't go to college, how are they going to get that kind of, what did you do to get that kind of education?" she wondered.

"You could be autodidactic, which is to be self-taught," Kirk replied before promoting his business relationship with Hillsdale College, a Christian school offering free online courses.

"You can also get the same wisdom and the same teaching free and accessible online, listening to podcasts, listening to long YouTube form videos," he continued. "In fact, I believe that if you listen to Joe Rogan's podcast with some of the historians, if you listen to some of the deep dives that some of these top thinkers do, you're going to actually learn a lot more about Roman history."

"You're going to learn a lot more about Western civilization than if otherwise, if you were stuck in some sort of sociology class at University of Massachusetts, Boston."

Our Analysis:

Charlie Kirk's Misguided Educational Advice: A Critical Analysis

In an era where misinformation can spread faster than legitimate knowledge, Charlie Kirk's recent comments on education and self-learning via platforms like YouTube and Joe Rogan's podcast are not just misguided, they are dangerously oversimplified. While promoting an autodidactic approach to learning on Fox & Friends, Kirk undermines the value of formal education with a broad stroke that deserves a sharp critique.

The Autodidact Appeal

Kirk's assertion that young people can gain "the same wisdom and the same teaching" through YouTube videos and podcasts as they would in a college classroom is a glaring oversimplification of education. The appeal of being self-taught, or autodidactic, is not new. However, Kirk's framing of this approach as a one-size-fits-all solution to the challenges and costs of higher education is both naive and dangerous.

The Misrepresentation of Higher Education

By contrasting the supposed value of listening to Joe Rogan's podcast with attending a "sociology class at University of Massachusetts, Boston," Kirk not only misrepresents the depth and breadth of higher education but also undervalues the importance of structured, academic exploration of complex subjects. The suggestion that a podcast could offer more insight into Roman history or Western civilization than a university course is a profound misunderstanding of the value that structured education provides in developing critical thinking, analytical skills, and a comprehensive understanding of nuanced topics.

The Dangers of Self-Selected Echo Chambers

One of the most significant dangers of Kirk's advice is the encouragement of educational echo chambers. YouTube videos and podcasts often cater to specific viewpoints, potentially reinforcing existing biases and limiting exposure to a diverse range of perspectives. Formal education, particularly in the humanities and social sciences, challenges students to confront and engage with differing viewpoints, fostering a more rounded and critical understanding of the world.

The Value of Accreditation and Peer Review

Furthermore, Kirk's suggestion overlooks the importance of accreditation and peer review in education. University courses are designed, reviewed, and taught by experts in their field, offering a level of scrutiny and academic rigor that cannot be matched by freely available online content. This process ensures that students are not only learning but learning accurately and effectively from recognized authorities.

Conclusion

While self-learning and the pursuit of knowledge outside of formal educational institutions should be encouraged, presenting it as a complete substitute for college education is misleading. Education is not just about the accumulation of facts; it's about learning how to think critically, engage with complex ideas, and understand the world from multiple perspectives. Charlie Kirk's comments, while appealing to a growing sentiment against the high costs of college education, fail to acknowledge the essential benefits of a structured educational experience. It's critical to approach such claims with skepticism and to recognize the irreplaceable value of comprehensive, academic education in shaping informed, critical, and engaged citizens.
