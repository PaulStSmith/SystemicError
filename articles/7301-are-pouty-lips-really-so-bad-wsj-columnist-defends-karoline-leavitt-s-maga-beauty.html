'Are pouty lips really so bad?' WSJ columnist defends Karoline Leavitt's 'MAGA beauty'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 26, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Politicization of Aesthetics: A Critique

White House Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt speaks during a press briefing at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., September 9, 2025. REUTERS/Jonathan Ernst

Ridicule of White House Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt’s change in appearance since she joined Donald Trump’s administration led a Wall Street Journal columnist to rush to her defense by arguing that, while there are some obvious enhancements at work, there is nothing wrong with it.

On the day before Christmas, columnist Louise Perry argued that the furor of a photo of Leavitt in Vanity Fair last week, where injection marks could be plainly seen around her inflated lips, were hypocritical because women on the left have their own aesthetic that is less obvious, but just as calculated.

Calling criticism of Leavitt an example of “catty ad feminam attacks on the women of the Trump administration,” she added, “Critics of so-called ‘MAGA beauty’ compare the aesthetic to a drag show, not only because it is extremely feminine—lots of makeup, big hair, form-fitting clothing—but also because of the emphasis on cosmetic enhancements, including lip filler.”

Reporting that “The full ‘Mar-a-Lago Face‘—a suite of cosmetic procedures including Botox, a face-lift and the rest—costs $90,000,” she claimed it is often a target of the left.

“Are long blond hair and pouty lips really so bad?’ she asked. “It doesn’t surprise me at all that Ms. Leavitt, tasked with being the face and voice of the Trump administration, would want to accentuate her beauty. And the hyperfeminine bombshell look is an obvious model for conservatives to follow, given its association both with the confidence of the 1950s, and with the South. ‘The higher the hair, the closer to God,’ as Dolly Parton was supposedly fond of saying.”

Perry expressed surprise at the anti-Leavitt vitriol, writing, “A more interesting question is why the anti-MAGA crowd rejects this look with such vehemence. There is no end of research indicating that beauty is socially and professionally advantageous, particularly for women. And emphasizing one’s femininity is a classic way of emphasizing one’s beauty. Why pass up the chance to look as good as possible?”

Singling out a casual look labeled “West Village Girl (”a plain cropped T-shirt, straight bluejeans and sneakers”) she explained the “anti-MAGA aesthetic also requires considerable effort, even if the results don’t look as aggressively feminine. Note that carefully curated casual outfits are typically paired with high-maintenance hair and makeup, just not of the ‘overdone and underblended’ style.”

Perry concluded, “Looking effortlessly perfect is often the most effective way of displaying status. Aestheticians who can do barely perceptible work cost more than those who can’t. You can look attractive in baggy jeans only if you are lithe and youthful. ‘No makeup makeup’ looks good only if your complexion has been perfected by an elaborate skin-care regime. The MAGA and the anti-MAGA looks are both fake. But only one is honest in its fakeness.”

Our Analysis:

The Politicization of Aesthetics: A Critique

The recent article from a Wall Street Journal columnist defending White House Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt’s cosmetic enhancements under the Trump administration ventures into a murky intersection of politics, aesthetics, and societal expectations. It’s a rare spectacle that elucidates how deeply entrenched and polarizing the discourse on appearance has become in the political arena. Let’s dissect the arguments presented and the underlying implications with the precision of a scalpel that, apparently, many seem to prefer wielding in a cosmetic surgeon’s office rather than in analytical discourse.

The Defense of “MAGA Beauty”

The columnist, Louise Perry, jumps to Leavitt’s defense against what she terms “catty ad feminam attacks,” suggesting critics of the so-called ‘MAGA beauty’ are hypocrites given the left’s own calculated aesthetic. This framing is problematic for several reasons. First, it reduces the complex individual decisions around appearance to mere political alignment, ignoring the multifaceted reasons people may choose to embrace or reject certain beauty standards. The comparison to a “drag show” not only trivializes individual agency but also unnecessarily drags (no pun intended) an entire art form and culture into a political spat, disrespecting its origins and significance.

The Cost of Beauty — Literally

Reporting on the eye-watering $90,000 price tag for the full ‘Mar-a-Lago Face,’ Perry inadvertently highlights a grotesque manifestation of wealth inequality. The insinuation that such expenditures are justifiable, or even desirable, for women in the public eye underscores a disturbing valorization of wealth and class status over substantive contributions to public discourse. It’s not just about whether “long blond hair and pouty lips” are objectionable; it’s about what prioritizing and normalizing such expensive and extreme aesthetic standards says about our societal values.

A False Dichotomy

Perry’s surprise at the rejection of this hyperfeminine aesthetic by the “anti-MAGA crowd” is laced with a false dichotomy that oversimplifies the spectrum of beauty standards and political beliefs. The assumption that emphasis on one’s femininity (read: adherence to traditional, often oppressive beauty standards) should be universally embraced for its social and professional advantages is a tired argument that fails to acknowledge the broader push for accepting diverse expressions of identity and beauty.

Moreover, the critique of the “West Village Girl” aesthetic for being equally calculated misses the point entirely. The issue is not whether effort is put into appearance — because, in a society obsessed with image, it often is — but the transparency and honesty about such efforts. To claim that the MAGA aesthetic is “honest in its fakeness” while condemning another for its subtlety is to ignore the more significant issue of why society demands such performances of femininity in the first place.

The Real Issue at Hand

The crux of the matter, which Perry’s article dances around without ever truly engaging with, is the toxic politicization of women’s bodies and choices. By framing the debate as MAGA beauty versus anti-MAGA aesthetics, the discourse perpetuates a cycle of judgment and division based on appearance, distracting from more critical discussions on the policies and principles that should define political allegiance.

In conclusion, while Perry attempts to defend Leavitt against ridicule, the argument unwittingly lays bare the deeply ingrained societal and political fissures surrounding women’s autonomy over their bodies and appearances. The real question we should be asking is not who wears their beauty more honestly, but why we continue to allow these superficial judgments to influence our perceptions of competence, intelligence, and worth.
