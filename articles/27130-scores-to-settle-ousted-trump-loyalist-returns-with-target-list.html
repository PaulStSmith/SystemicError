'Scores to settle': Ousted Trump loyalist returns with target list

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 7, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power was never hersGossip replaces accountabilityLoyalty is a one-way contractThe system protects the center

What happened

AlterNet reports that Kimberly Guilfoyle resigned as U.S. ambassador to Greece on October 13 after the White House asked her to step down. The move followed leaked messages in which former Kentucky congressional hopeful Eric Deters claimed Guilfoyle sought payment of a $100,000 American Express bill in exchange for access to Donald Trump’s inner circle. Guilfoyle denied the allegations as false and said she would defend her reputation.

Unnamed sources quoted by the Daily Mail said Guilfoyle returned to the United States on October 30 with “scores to settle,” naming Donald Trump Jr., his wife Bettina Anderson and Deters as people who should be nervous. But the reporting establishes no actual target list, retaliatory plan or action by Guilfoyle. It establishes only anonymous predictions about her anger.

Before her resignation, Trump said his administration was “not happy” about the reports, while a senior U.S. official told the Associated Press that Trump and Secretary of State Marco Rubio were considering her removal. Guilfoyle nevertheless described herself as a loyal supporter of Trump and his political mission.

The power was never hers

The decisive actors were Trump and the White House. They held the authority to preserve Guilfoyle’s position or end it; she resigned after being asked to leave. Rubio reportedly participated in the administration’s deliberations, but the account identifies presidential displeasure as the force behind the outcome.

Guilfoyle had proximity to power, an ambassadorship and years inside the Trump political network. Proximity is not control. Her position depended on continued approval from the president, and that approval disappeared once the allegations became a liability.

Gossip replaces accountability

The article’s most consequential allegation concerns an alleged exchange of money for political access. Yet the narrative quickly veers into romantic betrayal, an expensive former home and speculation about revenge. That is tabloid misdirection: an alleged attempt to monetize access to the president becomes supporting material in a celebrity feud.

The reporting supplies Guilfoyle’s denial but offers no evidence resolving the allegation. It also does not establish that she threatened anyone. Turning unnamed-source gossip into a “target list” overstates what the account supports and obscures the institutional question: how political access was allegedly presented as something that could be bought.

Loyalty is a one-way contract

Guilfoyle’s reported complaint about gratitude exposes the governing logic of patronage politics. Her defense was not an account of diplomatic performance. It was loyalty: she had helped Trump win, remained committed to his movement and expected protection in return.

That expectation is politically revealing even though the alleged private remarks come through unnamed sources. In a system organized around personal allegiance, appointments look less like public trusts than rewards—and removal feels like betrayal rather than accountability. The president retains the power to distribute favor; subordinates retain only the hope that prior service will purchase another exemption.

The system protects the center

Guilfoyle absorbed the immediate consequence: loss of office. Trump’s inner circle remained intact. The administration could remove one appointee and present the problem as contained without answering the larger question raised by Deters’ allegation: whether presidential access had acquired an informal market around the people closest to power.

That pattern is bigger than one ousted loyalist. Patronage networks personalize public authority, reward access over competence and discard individual operatives when scandal reaches the principal. Coverage that treats the aftermath as a revenge drama helps the system perform that containment. The personalities rotate; the structure that made their proximity valuable survives.
