'Cheering for job destruction?' Experts stunned as Trump guts industry he hates

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Paulo SantosAugust 19, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The White House Chose Job Destruction

Power, Not Confusion

This story has a clear center of gravity: Donald Trump and the White House. They halted future offshore wind permitting, canceled 12 leases, and issued the stop-work order that stranded workers tied to Empire Wind. That is not a market correction and it is not some mysterious industry wobble. It is executive power used to break a sector.

The result was immediate and measurable. A U.S. offshore wind buildout projected at 39 gigawatts by 2035 was cut to 6 gigawatts by the end of Trump’s first year back in office, according to BloombergNEF. That is not rhetoric. That is sabotage with a spreadsheet.

The Decision Makers

The people who decided this outcome sit in Washington, not on a construction site. Workers like Gerard Mullin and Anthony Hibbard absorbed the hit, but they did not cause it. Mullin had packed for a five-week offshore stint before the stop-work order sent him back onshore. Hibbard went from offshore pay to roughly one-fifth of that amount. The administration made the call; the workforce paid the bill.

That distinction matters because political coverage often launders power into abstraction. It turns a deliberate reversal into “uncertainty,” as if policy arrived by weather. It did not. Trump reversed course. Agencies followed orders. Projects died.

The Framing Is the Scam

The White House calls this an economic win and credits Trump with “creating thousands of good-paying energy jobs.” That line deserves contempt, not deference. You do not create jobs by blowing up functioning projects, canceling leases, and stranding trained workers in lower-wage work. You create churn, wasted public investment, and a permanent warning to anyone considering the next buildout.

The administration’s favorite trick is to rename harm as liberation. Taylor Rogers casts the rollback as a victory over the “Left’s” agenda, but the facts in the story point in the opposite direction. The policy did not just oppose offshore wind. It dismantled an industry that had already absorbed public and private commitments.

Workers Ate the Cost

Unions and states poured millions into training programs, with per-worker costs ranging from $10,000 to $30,000, on the assumption that the work would actually exist. Massachusetts lost a $34 million grant for an offshore wind terminal in Salem, along with roughly 800 construction jobs. New Jersey’s Salem County lost plans for 1,500 jobs. Prysmian Group abandoned planned U.S. facilities altogether.

This is the part the official messaging skips: the state did not merely stop future growth. It stranded sunk costs. It left trained workers with credentials but no projects, infrastructure without throughput, and regions that planned around an energy transition now left holding the bag.

What This Reveals

Tim Sullivan’s reaction cuts through the euphemisms: he had “never seen people cheering for job destruction.” That is the right frame. The administration is not confused about the consequences. It is willing to wear them because the damage itself is the point. A market cannot function when the federal government tells investors, states, manufacturers, and workers that long-term commitments can be erased on a whim.

That is the larger pattern: arbitrary presidential power used to punish a sector associated with political enemies, while the costs are pushed onto labor, local governments, and private investment. The story is not about wind’s weakness. It is about a ruling faction that treats industrial policy as a venue for revenge and calls the wreckage “leadership.”
