'Too fat to be Commander in Chief?' Hegseth speech backfires as Trump becomes punchline

By Paulo Santos

U.S. President Donald Trump gestures as he golfs at Trump Turnberry resort in Turnberry, Scotland, Britain, July 27, 2025. REUTERS/Phil Noble

Lawmakers and influencers are taking Secretary Pete Hegseth’s speech to heart after he railed against “fat” generals and admirals in the military.

“It’s completely unacceptable to see fat generals and admirals in the halls of the Pentagon, and leading commands around the country and the world. It’s a bad look,” said Hegseth in his speech on Tuesday.

Gov. Gavin Newsom (D-CA) quickly pointed out the physical plumpness of the top military leader.

“I guess the Commander in Chief needs to go!” he wrote on X. Newsom included a photo of Trump from his volunteer shift at McDonald’s from the 2024 election season.

“Psst: Whiskey @PeteHegseth thinks that Trump is too fat to be Commander in Chief. Pass it on,” needled national security expert Marcy Wheeler.

She then wondered, “What would happen if a credentialed DOD reporter asked @SeanParnellUSA if it was permissible to report that @SecWar thinks Donald Trump is too fat to be Commander in Chief?” speaking about the DOD spokesperson.

She also couldn’t help but notice that several Republican lawmakers and commentators shared Hegseth’s view.

“It’s a bad look...except for our Commander in Chief and my personal hero, Donald Trump, who wears his fat beautifully. I gaze in awe at his corpulent majesty,” former active duty Marine Sergeant Harrison Lansing wrote, making up a fake quote from Hegseth.

“Yeah, Obama could bop down stairs because he was in his late 40s as president not a fat geriatric 80 year old. It’s hilarious to me that he’s so old that he’s amazed at when much younger presidents had the ability to do things Trump could only do in his dreams. But do go on Hegseth about fat generals …” constitutional conservative Camille MacKenzie wrote on X.

“When Hegseth went on his ‘fat troops & fat generals’ rant did he think about the BF% of an average Trump rally? Or has he ever seen the dude he was opening for? Donnie Meatloaf wears a ‘fn’ girdle,” asked stand-up comedian Noel Casler.

Longtime radio host ‪David Waldman‬ also had his own fake Hegseth quote to summarize the speech: “You’re all too fat! And now, ladies and gentlemen, President Donald J. Trump.”

Our Analysis:

The Hypocrisy Parade: Hegseth’s “Fat Generals” Rant

In a recent display of selective outrage and hypocrisy, Secretary Pete Hegseth has taken a bold stance against what he perceives as the unacceptable physical condition of the military’s top brass. His comments, targeting “fat generals and admirals” as a “bad look” for the military, have ignited a firestorm of responses from both sides of the political aisle. Yet, the irony seems lost on Hegseth and his supporters, given their allegiance to Donald Trump—a figure not known for his athletic physique.

The Pot Calling the Kettle “Fat”

Firstly, let’s address the elephant in the room—Donald Trump’s physical fitness. Governor Gavin Newsom’s quick jab, pointing out Trump’s own “physical plumpness,” is not just a witty retort but a glaring spotlight on the double standard at play. Newsom’s use of a photo from Trump’s volunteer shift at McDonald’s during the 2024 election season not only adds a layer of humor but underscores the absurdity of Hegseth’s criteria for leadership fitness.

Marcy Wheeler’s commentary further exposes the ridiculousness of this situation. By questioning whether it’s permissible to critique Trump’s fitness for the role of Commander in Chief based on his physique, Wheeler highlights a critical flaw in Hegseth’s argument: the selective application of physical standards based on personal biases and political allegiances.

Satire as a Weapon

The responses from various commentators, including the sarcastic quips from Harrison Lansing and David Waldman, use satire effectively to underscore the hypocrisy inherent in Hegseth’s remarks. Lansing’s fabricated quote, praising Trump’s “corpulent majesty,” is a masterstroke of irony, pointing out the absurdity of criticizing military leaders’ physical fitness while idolizing Trump’s. Waldman’s summary of Hegseth’s speech juxtaposed with the introduction of Trump is a poignant reminder of the cognitive dissonance plaguing some political circles.

Camille MacKenzie’s and Noel Casler’s comments bring another dimension to this farce, emphasizing the age and physical capabilities of past presidents and the average physique of Trump’s rally attendees. Their observations are not just humorous but highlight a fundamental truth: leadership effectiveness is not measured by waistline but by competence, integrity, and vision.

The Real Issue at Hand

Beneath the surface of this debate lies a more serious concern: the focus on superficial attributes over substantive policy and leadership qualities. By fixating on the physical appearance of military leaders, Hegseth and his supporters divert attention from critical issues that require urgent attention, such as national security, veterans’ welfare, and military readiness.

The reactions from both political figures and social commentators reveal a deep-seated issue within American political discourse: a tendency to prioritize partisan point-scoring over meaningful dialogue. It’s crucial to recognize that leadership—whether in the military or the Oval Office—is about far more than physical appearance. It’s about character, capability, and commitment to service.

In summary, Hegseth’s comments and the ensuing backlash serve as a vivid illustration of the pettiness and hypocrisy that often characterize political debates today. Instead of contributing to a productive discussion about military leadership and fitness standards, this episode has devolved into a circus of mockery and finger-pointing. As we wade through the muck of political theater, let’s not lose sight of what truly matters: integrity, competence, and leadership in service to the country.
