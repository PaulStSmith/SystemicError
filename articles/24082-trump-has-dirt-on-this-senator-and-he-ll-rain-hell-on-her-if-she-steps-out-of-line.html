Susan Collins’s “Concern” Is a Political Asset, Not a Check on Power

The Performance of Dissent

The article’s strongest evidence is not its speculation about secret leverage or personal enrichment. It is the public record it cites: Senator Susan Collins repeatedly voices reservations about Republican power, then supplies votes that help that power operate. In 2025, according to the cited CQ Roll Call analysis, she voted with Donald Trump’s stated positions 94.6% of the time. She also supported Russell Vought and Tulsi Gabbard for powerful administration posts and says she does not regret voting to confirm Brett Kavanaugh.

That gap between language and action is the story. “Concern” lets Collins advertise independence without consistently imposing consequences on the officials and policies she claims to question.

The Senator Has the Power

Collins is not a spectator trapped behind a commentary desk. She is a senator with confirmation votes, appropriations authority and the institutional standing to withhold support.

That distinction matters because political coverage often treats her statements as events in themselves. But disapproval is not a meaningful restraint when followed by the vote the administration needs. The decisive act is the confirmation, appropriation or procedural choice—not the carefully calibrated sentence issued before it.

The weaker actors in this arrangement are not senators enduring uncomfortable questions. They are the people who live under decisions senators had the power to block.

Moderation as Branding

Collins’s political value comes partly from being presented as a “pro-choice moderate” willing to depart from her party. Yet the votes cited in the article show how that reputation can serve Republican power even when she does not exercise it against Republican priorities.

Her Kavanaugh vote is the clearest example offered. Collins later told the Portland Press Herald that she did not regret the vote, and an audio recording obtained by The New York Times reportedly captured her saying she would vote for him again. Whatever reservations surrounded the confirmation, her institutional decision was unambiguous.

Calling that posture moderation confuses presentation with outcome. A senator can sound conflicted while acting reliably.

Allegations Are Not Proof—and Speculation Is Not Analysis

The article cites ProPublica reporting about an alleged illegal $150,000 contribution involving defense contractor Navatek and a pro-Collins super PAC, alongside federal contracts Collins helped secure. Collins denies wrongdoing. Those are serious reported allegations, but the supplied material does not establish bribery, guilt or a corrupt exchange.

The article weakens its own case when it leaps from those allegations to an unsupported theory that Trump holds Collins in a “blackmail situation.” It also invites suspicion from an asserted increase in her net worth without establishing that her assets resulted from official misconduct. Questions about campaign money, contracting and financial disclosure deserve evidence, not insinuation dressed as certainty.

There is no need to invent a hidden mechanism when the visible one is already politically consequential: Collins chooses how she votes.

The Misdirection Protects the System

Personal attacks on Collins’s courage may be satisfying, but they can obscure the machinery that gives her conduct force. Senate rules, committee authority and the confirmation process convert individual votes into durable government power. Party leaders benefit when a senator can reassure skeptical voters with dissenting rhetoric while still advancing the party’s agenda.

The relevant question is therefore not whether Collins secretly cares. The supplied evidence cannot answer that. The question is what her decisions enabled. On the examples presented, her votes helped place Vought and Gabbard in powerful offices and Kavanaugh on the Supreme Court. Those outcomes are concrete; speculation about her private motives is not.

Concern Without Consequence

The larger pattern is institutionalized symbolic dissent. Officials preserve reputations for independence through tone, hesitation and public discomfort, while their formal actions continue to support the governing project.

That arrangement distributes responsibility away from the people who possess it. Policy becomes something that simply happened; confirmation becomes an unfortunate development; a decisive senator becomes an anxious observer. The language of concern is useful precisely because it softens accountability. Power should be measured at the moment it is exercised—and Susan Collins’s record, as presented here, lies in her votes.
