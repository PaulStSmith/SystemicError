'Collision course': Experts warn Trump’s 'last refuge' is his most dangerous

Based on an article by Elizabeth Preza

The Presidency Is Not a Spotlight, but Trump Still Holds the Switches

Decline Is Not Disarmament

The Guardian describes a president suffering political setbacks roughly 20 months into his second term: courts blocked several administration moves, the Federal Reserve resisted his demands on interest rates, and congressional Republicans reportedly feared he could cost them seats in the November midterms. Yet the article’s central paradox is more important than its portrait of personal decline: Donald Trump’s influence may have been weakening when the piece was published, but the powers of the presidency were not.

The Power Is Institutional

Trump remained president. That meant command of the executive branch, influence over a Justice Department the Guardian characterized as politicized, and access to broad pardon authority. A blocked ballot restriction, an unfavorable interest-rate decision, or a court defeat over White House press access demonstrated that other institutions could still impose limits. None of those setbacks removed his capacity to make consequential decisions.

This distinction matters because approval and authority are not interchangeable. Rick Wilson cited polls placing Trump at 29 percent approval and argued that voters associated him with war, higher energy prices, and higher food prices. Those claims describe political liability as Wilson saw it. They do not describe a president stripped of office, personnel, or executive machinery.

Republicans Built the Dependency

The article risks turning congressional Republicans into nervous wildlife suddenly discovering a predator. Wilson’s sharper observation supplies the missing agency: the party handed Trump extraordinary political deference for a decade. Its reported distancing, motivated by fear of losing seats, was therefore not evidence of principled resistance. It was an attempt to manage electoral exposure after years of enabling him.

Republican lawmakers held institutional power of their own. Congress could legislate, investigate, control appropriations, and conduct oversight. If members waited until Trump became a liability before showing independence, the relevant story was not merely that his “magic” had stopped working. It was that their obedience had been conditional on his usefulness.

Psychology Is the Convenient Distraction

Much of the Guardian’s framing rests on speculation about Trump’s emotional response to fading attention. Bill Galston described the spotlight as his “oxygen.” Charlie Sykes said he would rage against irrelevance. Historian Allan Lichtman wondered what he might do to reclaim attention.

These assessments may illuminate a familiar political performance, but they cannot establish motive or predict a specific act. More importantly, fascination with Trump’s temperament can obscure the machinery available to him. The public consequence would not come from wounded pride by itself. It would come from officials carrying out directives, lawmakers declining to intervene, and institutions failing—or choosing not—to enforce limits.

Calling that structure a personality crisis lets every subordinate and ally disappear behind one man’s appetite for attention.

“Dangerous” Requires a Mechanism

The article repeatedly warns that a weaker Trump could become more dangerous, but its strongest evidence concerns capability, not prophecy. The presidency retained executive authority. The Justice Department could be used politically, according to the Guardian’s characterization. The pardon power remained extensive. Lichtman alleged that Trump did not care about courts or Congress, while the reported court rulings showed that judicial constraints still operated in several disputes.

The credible risk, then, was not mystical unpredictability. It was escalation through identifiable powers, enabled by people with names, offices, and choices. Any account that dwells on “lashing out” while treating those enablers as scenery reproduces the central misdirection of Trump-era coverage: spectacle occupies the foreground while institutional cooperation does the work.

The System Protects Power Longer Than Popularity

A president can lose public approval without losing the instruments of government. A governing party can retreat from its leader without accepting responsibility for empowering him. Courts can block particular actions without neutralizing an administration. Those are not contradictions. They are features of a political system in which authority is distributed, resistance is optional until exercised, and accountability often arrives later than the damage.

The larger pattern is not simply that an unpopular leader may behave recklessly. It is that institutions routinely personalize abuses they helped make possible, then describe their own belated alarm as evidence of independence. Trump’s reported decline did not absolve the Republican Party or the officials implementing his agenda. It exposed how much of his power had always depended on their decisions.
