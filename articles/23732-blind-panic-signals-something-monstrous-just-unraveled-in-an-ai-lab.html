Blind panic signals something monstrous just unraveled in an AI lab

Based on an article by Jason Miciak

The AI Panic Is Real. The Evidence in This Story Isn’t.

Power Sits With Government and Industry

The institutions capable of shaping AI’s development are not mysterious. The White House can set federal policy and convene international negotiations. Congress can impose safety standards, disclosure requirements, liability rules, and worker protections. Companies such as Anthropic, OpenAI, and xAI control the laboratories, capital, and technical decisions driving the race.

Workers do not control that race. Neither do the communities expected to absorb mass displacement or infrastructure failures. The people exposed to the consequences are being offered speculation while government officials and corporate executives retain the power to decide what risks are acceptable.

Corporate Warnings Are Not Public Accountability

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei’s request to slow development, researcher Jacob Coxon’s resignation, and warnings from AI-safety advocates deserve scrutiny. They are not, by themselves, proof that a secret laboratory breakthrough has placed the internet—or civilization—on the brink of collapse.

The source repeatedly converts proximity into evidence: several warnings appeared close together, therefore something terrifying probably happened. That is conjecture, not reporting. “Only an idiot wouldn’t assume” is a device for intimidating readers past the absence of substantiation.

If executives possess evidence of an imminent danger, they should disclose it to regulators under oath. If they do not, journalists should not manufacture certainty on their behalf. Corporate alarm can reflect genuine fear, strategic positioning, a campaign for favorable regulation, or some combination of the three. Public policy cannot rest on guessing which.

Trump’s Policy Is a Choice, Not an Absence

Donald Trump’s quoted position reduces AI governance to a contest with China: whoever “wins” supposedly gains the advantage, while warnings become “negative forces.” Speaker Mike Johnson uses the same competition narrative to justify delay, claiming regulation could harm the country and benefit China.

That is not governmental confusion. It is a policy choice to privilege speed, corporate latitude, and geopolitical dominance over precaution. Trump’s presence at his Irish golf property is politically revealing only at the level of symbolism. The consequential fact is that his administration favors deregulation while the congressional leadership with agenda-setting power declines to move quickly.

The article weakens this indictment by obsessing over Trump’s temperament and anatomy. A “reptilian brain” explains nothing. Institutions do not deregulate themselves because one man lacks nuance. Officials make decisions, donors and companies benefit from them, and legislators provide the votes or inertia that sustain them.

“Skynet” Distracts From the Damage Already Authorized

Invoking The Terminator supplies an unforgettable villain and a poor political framework. Sentient software launching nuclear war is speculative. Concentrated ownership, weakened oversight, worker displacement, and private control over essential technology are already intelligible problems.

The source mentions unemployment as high as 35 or 40 percent, but provides no evidence establishing those figures as forecasts. It nevertheless identifies the central distributional question: if AI produces enormous gains while eliminating large categories of paid work, who owns the gains and who bears the loss?

Universal Basic Income is one possible answer, not an automatic remedy. Without confronting ownership, taxation, labor power, housing costs, and access to health care, cash payments could become a subsidy for an economy in which a small group owns the productive machinery and everyone else rents survival from it.

The China Excuse Serves the Race

“China might get there first” is the standard solvent for restraint. It allows American officials to describe every safeguard as unilateral surrender and every corporate acceleration as national defense. Companies pursuing market dominance are recast as strategic assets; demands placed on them become aid to an adversary.

International monitoring may be worth pursuing, and arms-control agreements offer precedents for verification. But the analogy to nuclear weapons does not create an agreement by itself. It requires specific limits, inspections, enforcement mechanisms, and governments willing to constrain their own favored industries. None of that emerges from declaring AI both an existential danger and a race that must be won.

The System Rewards Danger, Then Outsources Responsibility

The larger pattern is not that government has vanished. Government is present in the permissions it grants, the rules it refuses to write, and the risks it allows corporations to impose on everyone else.

Industry leaders can accelerate development, warn that acceleration is dangerous, and still retain control of the evidence. Political leaders can invoke China to avoid binding rules. Commentators can fill the resulting information vacuum with apocalyptic inference. Each actor then points elsewhere: companies to geopolitical competition, politicians to innovation, and alarmists to hypothetical machines.

The machine has not displaced human agency here. Powerful people are making choices—and the fog of inevitability is protecting them from being held responsible for those choices.
