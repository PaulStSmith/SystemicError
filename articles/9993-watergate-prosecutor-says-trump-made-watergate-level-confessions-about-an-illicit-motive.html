Watergate prosecutor says Trump made 'Watergate-level confessions about an illicit motive'

Donald Trump and Richard Nixon (Composition / RawStory)

Donald Trump left more evidence of criminal intent than Richard Nixon, according to Watergate lawyer Nick Ackerman Saturday.

Ackerman appeared on MSNBC over the weekend, where he was asked about how Trump demanded the prosecution of his perceived political enemies, including ex-FBI director James Comey.

The host asked, “What do you see in the echoes of Watergate? And has Trump made Watergate-level confessions about an illicit motive?”

“Oh, there’s no question,” Ackerman replied. He said his “biggest problem” when prosecuting Nixon was “getting evidence that actually showed criminal intent and motive.”

“Here, Donald Trump lays it out on Truth Social. He lays it out in executive orders,” Ackerman said. “He lays it all out. If I ever had this kind of evidence back then, we would have had a field day.”

He said that type of evidence was harder to come by back then, but that Trump is more open about it.

Our Analysis:

A Stark Comparison: Trump vs. Nixon

In a statement that manages to be both shocking and unsurprising, Nick Ackerman, a lawyer from the Watergate era, illuminated the depths of Donald Trump’s legal quagmires on MSNBC. Ackerman’s comparison between Trump and Richard Nixon reveals a stark, unsettling reality: Donald Trump has left a more damning trail of evidence of criminal intent than Nixon ever did. Nixon’s downfall during Watergate is etched in the annals of political infamy, but if Ackerman’s observations hold water, Trump has not only surpassed Nixon’s legacy of corruption but has done so with a brazen openness that is almost beyond belief.

The Boldness of Indifference

Ackerman points out a critical distinction that sets Trump apart: the boldness with which he displays what appears to be criminal intent. While Nixon’s team went through great lengths to hide their actions, using secret recordings and covert operations, Trump, on the other hand, “lays it out on Truth Social... in executive orders”. This isn’t just a difference in tactics; it’s a fundamental shift in how political figures engage with the concept of accountability. Trump’s approach seems to stem from a belief in his untouchability, a confidence that no matter the evidence laid bare, consequences will not follow.

The Implications of Transparency

One might argue that there’s a perverse form of transparency in Trump’s methodology. While Nixon’s subterfuge led to a crisis of confidence in American politics, Trump’s overt actions, as described by Ackerman, offer the public and prosecutors clear insights into his motivations and intentions. However, this “transparency” does not equate to accountability. It highlights a grim reality in American politics: that a certain level of power and influence might render one immune to the consequences of even the most blatant wrongdoings.

A Field Day for Prosecutors, In Theory

Ackerman’s assertion that he “would have had a field day” with the kind of evidence available against Trump is a damning indictment of the current state of affairs. It underscores not just the abundance of evidence but the gravity of the alleged actions. Yet, this raises an essential question: If the evidence is as clear-cut as Ackerman suggests, why hasn’t there been a more significant move towards holding Trump accountable?

The comparison between Trump and Nixon isn’t just about the evidence of criminal intent; it’s a reflection on how the American political and legal system deals with such evidence. Nixon’s resignation came about due to a combination of legal pressure and a loss of political support. For Trump, despite multiple impeachments and ongoing investigations, the political will to decisively act on what Ackerman describes remains fragmented.

Conclusion: A Test of American Democracy

The observations made by Nick Ackerman are not merely a commentary on two presidencies but a litmus test for American democracy and its legal system. If the evidence of wrongdoing is as clear and abundant as stated, the failure to act upon it not only undermines the rule of law but also signals to future leaders that accountability may be more about political calculus than justice.

Donald Trump’s legacy, as compared to Richard Nixon’s, may well be defined not just by what he did, but by the societal and political response to his actions. In the end, it’s a test of whether the system can hold the powerful to account or if it bends under the weight of their influence. The answer to that question will shape American democracy for generations to come.
