They Didn’t Kill Cable. They Rebuilt It With a Plus Sign.

How Hollywood took the greatest innovation in television distribution in decades, dismantled it in pursuit of corporate greed, and made consumers pay for the privilege.

There was a brief, beautiful moment when the entertainment industry accidentally stumbled into something that actually worked for consumers.

It was called Netflix.

Not the Netflix we know today, with its escalating subscription prices, advertising tiers, password-sharing restrictions, and increasingly elaborate methods of extracting money from subscribers.

The original streaming Netflix.

The one that emerged from a DVD-by-mail business in 2007 and offered a revolutionary proposition:

Pay one subscription. Open one application. Watch movies and television shows from multiple studios.

Simple.

Convenient.

Almost suspiciously reasonable.

And for a while, it seemed that Netflix had finally figured out how to kill cable television.

Except that wasn’t quite what Netflix had accomplished.

In a fascinating historical irony, Netflix had effectively turned back the clock on television distribution by several decades.

And Hollywood, unable to resist the prospect of squeezing a few additional dollars out of every viewer, proceeded to dismantle the entire arrangement.

Not because it had failed.

Because it had succeeded, and the studios couldn’t stand watching someone else collect part of the profits.

Back to the Future: Television, 1950s Edition

For much of the twentieth century, American television was dominated by three national networks: ABC, CBS, and NBC.

There were independent broadcasters, local stations, and later public television, but the basic commercial structure was remarkably straightforward.

Production companies made entertainment.

Networks acquired the rights to distribute it.

Audiences turned on their televisions and watched.

Consumers didn’t need separate contractual relationships with Warner Bros., Paramount, Universal, or every other company responsible for producing the programs they enjoyed.

They didn’t need a different television set for each studio.

They didn’t need to know which corporation owned which intellectual property.

The network handled distribution. The studios got paid for their content. Advertising financed the service.

This system had significant shortcomings, including concentrated gatekeeping power and limited programming choices. But it established an important principle:

Producing entertainment and distributing entertainment did not have to be the same business.

When Netflix launched streaming in 2007, it essentially resurrected that principle using the Internet.

The studios owned movies and television programs.

Netflix licensed them.

Subscribers paid Netflix to watch them.

There was no technological reason why every movie studio needed its own streaming infrastructure, billing department, recommendation engine, customer-service operation, or application.

Netflix handled the distribution.

The studios received licensing revenue.

And consumers enjoyed a centralized library without paying a separate bill for every production company’s catalog.

It was not a perfect arrangement. Netflix controlled access, licensing windows could expire, and regional restrictions remained. But it was an enormous improvement in convenience over the fragmented cable landscape.

More importantly, it demonstrated that the Internet could eliminate layers of distribution complexity rather than add new ones.

For a moment, the future looked promising.

Then Hollywood’s accountants started paying attention.

The Most Dangerous Question in Hollywood

Imagine you’re an executive at a major entertainment studio.

Your company owns decades of movies and television programs.

Some were enormous successes. Others were forgettable. Many haven’t generated particularly significant revenue in years.

Their theatrical runs ended long ago.

Their physical-media sales have slowed.

Their cable syndication opportunities are limited or declining.

Then Netflix approaches with an offer:

We’ll pay you to make those programs available to our subscribers.

Think about the economics.

A film produced in 1985 does not require another production budget in 2015.

The sets don’t need to be rebuilt.

The actors don’t need to be rehired.

The cameras don’t need to roll again.

There are continuing expenses, of course: residuals, licensing obligations, preservation, restoration, distribution preparation, and contractual complications.

But the original production costs have already been incurred.

Licensing an existing catalog can therefore produce revenue with relatively modest incremental costs.

Netflix gets content.

The studio gets money.

The consumer gets entertainment.

Everybody wins.

Or at least everybody should.

But somewhere in the executive suites, somebody inevitably looked at Netflix’s growing subscriber base and asked the question that would eventually poison the entire industry:

“Why are we letting Netflix make money from our movies when we could charge those customers ourselves?”

And there it was.

The moment when a functioning commercial relationship became an opportunity for corporate extraction.

Notice what the question doesn’t ask.

It doesn’t ask whether consumers would benefit.

It doesn’t ask whether operating an independent streaming platform would be more efficient.

It doesn’t ask whether the market could sustainably support a dozen competing subscription services.

It doesn’t even necessarily ask whether the studio would earn more profit after accounting for the enormous costs of running its own platform.

It asks why somebody else is collecting money that the studio believes it could collect instead.

This is an important distinction.

The studios weren’t simply trying to make money from their intellectual property. They were already doing that. They wanted to capture a greater share of the money flowing through distribution, even if doing so made the distribution system worse.

And because every studio reached essentially the same conclusion, the industry collectively began destroying the very convenience that had made streaming attractive.

The Great Plus-Sign Gold Rush

Netflix was successful.

Therefore, every studio decided it needed to become Netflix.

Disney created Disney+.

Paramount created Paramount+.

Apple launched Apple TV+.

Discovery operated discovery+.

NBCUniversal launched Peacock, apparently deciding that the absence of a plus sign could be compensated for with a bird.

Warner’s streaming branding went through HBO Max, Max, and back again, as though changing the sign on the building were a substitute for developing a coherent business model.

And every new platform needed something that would convince consumers to subscribe.

What better incentive than withholding familiar content from existing distributors?

Movies and series that had previously been available through broadly licensed catalogs became increasingly tied to exclusive services.

Want Disney’s catalog?

Subscribe to Disney+.

Want Paramount’s?

Subscribe to Paramount+.

Want something from Warner?

Find out what its streaming service is called this year.

Suddenly, consumers were expected to maintain multiple subscriptions simply to recreate the content access that a more centralized distribution system had previously offered.

And each company presented its own service as an exciting expansion of consumer choice.

A remarkable feat of marketing: take something that used to be available through one subscription, divide it among several subscriptions, and advertise the result as innovation.

This was vertical integration dressed up as technological progress.

And the executives congratulated themselves for it.

The Corporate Prisoner’s Dilemma

The problem was almost inevitable once every studio decided it needed its own platform.

From an individual company’s perspective, exclusive distribution could appear attractive.

If Disney kept its biggest franchises on Disney+, those franchises might attract subscribers.

If Paramount withheld its catalog, Paramount+ might become more valuable.

If Warner reserved its most desirable programming, consumers might pay for another subscription.

Each decision could be defended as rational corporate strategy.

Collectively, however, those decisions made the entire market less convenient and more expensive to navigate.

The studios turned entertainment distribution into a corporate prisoner’s dilemma.

Everybody wanted to maximize their individual share.

Everybody weakened the collective proposition.

And consumers paid for the resulting fragmentation.

The consequences are measurable.

According to Deloitte’s 2025 Digital Media Trends report, American subscribing households paid an average of $69 per month for four streaming services, a 13% increase from the previous year.

The report also found that:

  • 47% of surveyed consumers believed they were paying too much for streaming.

  • 41% believed the available content wasn’t worth the price.

  • 39% had cancelled at least one paid streaming service during the preceding six months.

  • 60% said a $5 monthly price increase would make them likely to cancel their favorite service.

These are not signs of consumers enthusiastically embracing Hollywood’s magnificent new distribution ecosystem.

They are signs of people increasingly questioning why entertainment has become such an expensive, fragmented nuisance.

And notice the additional irony.

Consumers originally fled cable television because of expensive bundles filled with channels they didn’t want.

Now they are navigating multiple streaming platforms, each filled with content they may not want, simply to reach the handful of programs they do.

The interfaces changed.

The billing mechanisms changed.

The distribution technology changed.

The underlying corporate impulse to maximize how much consumers pay for access remained remarkably intact.

From Innovation to Extraction

There is a particularly revealing difference between how streaming companies behaved when they were trying to acquire customers and how they behave once they believe they have secured enough of them.

During the growth phase, everything revolves around value.

Low introductory prices.

Generous libraries.

Convenient features.

Password sharing is tolerated, sometimes even encouraged.

Customers are treated as people whose loyalty must be earned.

Then the business reaches a certain scale.

Investors stop rewarding growth alone.

Executives begin demanding higher margins.

And the vocabulary changes.

Suddenly, features that made the service attractive become revenue opportunities.

Password sharing becomes a problem to monetize.

Advertising becomes something consumers can pay extra to avoid.

Subscription prices increase.

Content budgets face tighter scrutiny.

The objective shifts from attracting subscribers to extracting more revenue from those who remain.

Consider a simplified example.

A streaming service has one hundred subscribers paying $80 annually.

That’s $8,000 in subscription revenue.

The company doubles the price to $160.

Thirty subscribers cancel.

Seventy remain.

The company now collects $11,200 annually.

It lost 30% of its customers and increased subscription revenue by 40%.

A news headline might describe this as a subscriber exodus.

An executive looking at the revenue figures might describe it as an excellent quarter.

Of course, actual profitability depends on costs, customer acquisition, content spending, and long-term retention. But the underlying arithmetic explains why companies can tolerate substantial cancellations when raising prices.

This is the logic behind the industry’s transition from maximizing subscribers to maximizing revenue per subscriber.

And it’s worth remembering that profitability itself is not objectionable.

Companies need to earn money.

Workers need salaries.

Productions require financing.

Investors reasonably expect returns.

The problem isn’t profit. It’s the assumption that profitable businesses must continually discover new ways to charge consumers more, even when those methods degrade the product that attracted them in the first place.

There is a difference between making a profit by providing value and increasing profits by systematically removing that value.

Wall Street may not care very much about that distinction.

Consumers certainly do.

The Myth That There Was No Alternative

The studios will argue that launching proprietary streaming services was necessary.

Traditional cable revenue was declining.

Physical media was shrinking.

Netflix was gaining enormous influence over distribution.

Relying on a powerful intermediary carried genuine commercial risks.

All of that is true.

Netflix itself was becoming an increasingly formidable gatekeeper, and allowing a single company to control digital entertainment distribution would have created its own problems.

But here’s what those arguments don’t establish:

That every studio needed its own exclusive streaming service.

Nothing prevented the entertainment industry from supporting competing aggregators with overlapping catalogs.

Imagine a market in which Netflix, Amazon, Apple, and other distributors competed to offer consumers access to broadly similar collections of licensed content.

Consumers could choose platforms based on price, interface quality, video performance, recommendations, accessibility, and customer service.

Studios could negotiate licensing agreements with multiple distributors.

Popular titles could command higher licensing fees.

Competition would occur at the distribution level without requiring consumers to purchase a separate subscription from every company whose content they wanted to watch.

This is not some radical economic experiment.

It’s how bookstores work.

You don’t need separate subscriptions to Penguin Random House, HarperCollins, Hachette, and Simon & Schuster to read their books.

You visit a bookstore or choose an online retailer.

Different publishers supply the merchandise.

The distributor sells it.

The customer buys what they want.

Imagine if every major publisher announced tomorrow that its books would henceforth be available exclusively through its own proprietary retail service.

And then insisted this fragmentation represented a glorious new era of literary freedom.

We would rightly consider it absurd.

Yet entertainment companies have spent years treating essentially the same idea as a visionary business strategy.

The Industry Is Reinventing the Thing It Destroyed

Perhaps the most absurd chapter of the streaming story is what’s happening now.

Having spent years convincing consumers to abandon cable bundles, streaming companies have rediscovered the benefits of bundling.

Having built separate applications, they are experimenting with aggregation.

Having promoted advertising-free television, they are expanding advertising-supported subscriptions.

Having celebrated flexible monthly subscriptions, they are increasingly encouraging longer-term commitments.

Having fragmented their catalogs across competing corporate ecosystems, they are searching for ways to make those ecosystems easier to navigate together.

In other words:

Hollywood spent years destroying the convenience of aggregated entertainment distribution and is now paying people to figure out how to restore it.

Deloitte’s 2025 research explicitly identified renewed bundling and aggregation as responses to consumer fatigue, rising prices, and competition for viewers’ limited budgets.

And its 2026 findings suggest that the pressure has hardly disappeared. Average monthly streaming spending remained at approximately $69, while 61% of surveyed consumers said a $5 price increase would make them cancel their favorite streaming service.

The studios have discovered, apparently to their astonishment, that consumers do not have unlimited money, unlimited time, or unlimited patience.

Who could possibly have anticipated that?

There is a bitter symmetry to the whole enterprise.

Cable companies built expensive bundles because bundling improved their economics.

Netflix demonstrated that Internet distribution could offer a simpler, more flexible alternative.

Studios fractured that alternative to capture subscription revenue directly.

Now streaming companies are reconstructing bundles because fragmented services have their own economic limitations.

The industry has traveled in an enormous circle.

The shareholders have been entertained.

The consultants have been paid.

The executives have collected their compensation.

And the consumer is increasingly back where the whole revolution began.

Piracy Evolved. Hollywood Apparently Didn’t Notice.

There’s another consequence of Hollywood’s great streaming fragmentation that deserves more attention.

Piracy.

Not the piracy of twenty years ago, with its confusing websites, dubious downloads, mysterious executable files, and videos that might or might not contain the movie advertised.

Piracy evolved.

And in some respects, the technology surrounding unauthorized distribution now offers exactly the kind of unified entertainment experience that legitimate streaming companies have spent years dismantling.

Consider Crunchyroll.

Today, Crunchyroll is an established anime streaming service, distributing licensed Japanese programming to audiences around the world.

But it didn’t start that way.

Founded in 2006, Crunchyroll originally operated as a user-upload platform where fans shared anime, including unauthorized copies and fan-subtitled episodes.

It was, by any reasonable description, part of the anime piracy ecosystem.

Then something remarkable happened.

Crunchyroll recognized that the people watching those unauthorized uploads represented a genuine market.

Anime fans wanted convenient access to Japanese programming.

They wanted subtitles.

They wanted episodes available shortly after the original Japanese broadcasts.

And they were willing to pay for a service that delivered those things.

Crunchyroll negotiated licensing agreements, removed unauthorized material, and transformed itself into a legitimate distributor.

By 2011, it was already demonstrating that a legal streaming business could compete with free unauthorized distribution by offering superior convenience and timely access.

The pirate site became a legitimate company by understanding what customers wanted and finding a lawful way to deliver it.

There is a lesson here.

Unfortunately, it’s one Hollywood seems determined to forget.

The Streaming Interface Without Corporate Borders

Now consider Stremio.

Stremio is an extensible media center that allows users to search for movies, television shows, and other video content through a unified interface.

It is not inherently a piracy application. It supports legitimate streaming sources, and its functionality depends on the add-ons users install.

But it also supports BitTorrent streaming, and third-party add-ons can expose content distributed without authorization.

That distinction matters legally.

Technologically, however, Stremio demonstrates something fascinating.

The application doesn’t have to organize its entire existence around the boundaries of one entertainment conglomerate’s intellectual-property portfolio.

Imagine looking for an obscure Indian film made in the 1920s.

It might be commercially irrelevant to Disney.

It might never appear in Netflix’s licensed catalog.

Paramount might have no interest in distributing it.

Yet a sufficiently comprehensive discovery system may still know that the film exists and identify possible sources.

Whether somebody is actually sharing a playable copy is another matter entirely.

But the essential difference remains.

In a conventional streaming service, the catalog is largely defined by corporate licensing agreements.

In a broader discovery ecosystem, the catalog can be defined by what has been indexed, preserved, and made discoverable.

Those are radically different approaches to organizing entertainment.

And there is a particularly embarrassing implication for Hollywood.

A relatively small software ecosystem can provide an interface for searching across an enormous universe of films and television, while some of the wealthiest entertainment corporations on Earth insist that their customers navigate a collection of incompatible proprietary libraries.

The technical challenge of unified discovery is solvable.

The commercial incentive to permit it is another matter.

Piracy Is Also a Form of Market Feedback

None of this means copyright should simply disappear or that unauthorized distribution has no victims.

Creators deserve compensation.

Production companies need revenue.

Legitimate distribution provides funding that unauthorized copying generally does not.

But dismissing every instance of piracy as evidence that consumers are dishonest or unwilling to pay is analytically lazy.

Sometimes consumers pirate because they refuse to pay.

Sometimes they pirate because a work is unavailable legally in their region.

Sometimes an obscure film has no active commercial distributor.

Sometimes the only authorized version has disappeared from every accessible platform.

And sometimes consumers simply refuse to maintain six subscriptions to watch six programs.

These situations are not morally or economically identical.

Treating them as though they were prevents the industry from understanding the problem.

Crunchyroll’s transformation demonstrated that an audience using unauthorized distribution channels can become a paying audience when offered a compelling legal alternative.

Stremio demonstrates that unified content discovery remains technically achievable, even in an industry determined to divide access along corporate boundaries.

And the broader history of streaming demonstrates that convenience can be a significant competitive advantage against unauthorized distribution.

Yet Hollywood increasingly seems to prefer combating piracy through enforcement while simultaneously making legitimate access more fragmented, more expensive, and less convenient.

That is an extraordinary commercial contradiction.

The pirates are not necessarily producing better movies. They are sometimes building better ways to find them.

And when the legal industry cannot compete on accessibility with applications developed outside its corporate ecosystem, perhaps its executives should spend less time blaming consumers and more time examining their own distribution decisions.

The lesson was never that everything should be free.

The lesson was that consumers value convenience, availability, and reasonable pricing.

Netflix understood that.

Crunchyroll understood that.

Even decentralized distribution ecosystems reflect that demand.

Hollywood understood it briefly.

Then somebody looked at a spreadsheet, imagined how much more money the company might extract through exclusivity, and decided convenience was somebody else’s problem.

Piracy didn’t need to defeat streaming.

The studios volunteered to dismantle streaming’s greatest competitive advantage themselves.

The Real Product Was Never the Platform

Somewhere in this process, entertainment companies appear to have forgotten what business they are supposed to be in.

People don’t subscribe to Disney+ because they feel a profound emotional attachment to Disney’s billing infrastructure.

They don’t subscribe to Paramount+ because they admire Paramount’s authentication system.

They don’t subscribe to Max because they’re fascinated by Warner’s server architecture.

They subscribe because they want to watch stories.

Movies. Television series. Documentaries. Comedy. Drama. Science fiction.

The platform is merely the delivery mechanism.

And yet the industry increasingly treats that mechanism as though it were the product itself.

The distinction matters.

A studio creates value when it produces a film worth watching.

A distributor creates value when it makes that film accessible, reliably and conveniently.

But ownership of the content does not automatically mean that building another subscription platform creates additional value for the audience.

Sometimes it merely creates another tollbooth.

And a collection of tollbooths is not an innovation simply because each one has a different corporate logo.

The Future Hollywood Could Have Chosen

None of this means Netflix should have become the sole gatekeeper of global entertainment.

Replacing the cable oligopoly with a streaming monopoly would hardly have been a triumph.

The better outcome would have been a competitive marketplace of distributors carrying broadly overlapping catalogs, with content owners free to license their work under commercially negotiated terms.

Some exclusivity would still exist.

Some studios would still operate specialized platforms.

New productions would still need substantial investment.

And consumers would still have to pay for entertainment.

The difference is that distribution competition could have focused primarily on delivering value rather than deliberately withholding access.

Hollywood had the opportunity to build that market.

Instead, its largest corporations largely chose to compete by carving the available content into proprietary territories.

Not because the Internet required it.

Not because consumers demanded it.

But because capturing recurring subscription revenue became more attractive than participating in a broadly accessible distribution system.

That decision wasn’t dictated by technology.

It was made by people.

Executives.

Boards of directors.

Investors.

Corporate decision-makers who looked at a functioning market and concluded that owning another piece of it mattered more than preserving its convenience.

And now many of those same companies are trying to sell consumers bundles that partially repair the fragmentation they helped create.

At a price, naturally.

Conclusion: The Plus Sign Was a Warning

Netflix’s early streaming success offered a glimpse of something genuinely transformative.

Not because watching video over the Internet was inherently miraculous.

But because the technology made it possible to simplify distribution, lower barriers to access, and bring enormous catalogs of entertainment into people’s homes through a single convenient interface.

For a while, the interests of studios, distributors, and consumers were reasonably aligned.

The studios had content.

Netflix had customers.

Consumers had access.

Everyone could earn or receive something of value.

Then the studios looked at Netflix and saw not a profitable distribution partner, but a pile of subscription revenue they wanted for themselves.

And because each corporation pursued the same ambition, the industry collectively dismantled much of what had made streaming attractive.

There were legitimate economic pressures behind the transition. But those pressures did not make the resulting fragmentation inevitable, nor do they excuse every price increase, exclusive licensing decision, or attempt to squeeze additional revenue from customers.

This was not simply the unavoidable evolution of technology.

It was the consequence of corporate choices.

Choices that prioritized ownership of the customer relationship over convenience.

Choices that treated exclusivity as a substitute for better service.

Choices that turned existing catalogs into instruments for extracting recurring payments across increasingly fragmented platforms.

And now, as consumers cancel subscriptions, rotate between services, and complain about prices, the industry is discovering that there are limits to how much inconvenience people will tolerate.

The great streaming revolution promised to liberate consumers from the cable model.

Hollywood took one look at that liberation and asked how it could monetize the chains.

They didn’t kill cable.

They rebuilt it with a plus sign.

And then they sent everyone the bill.
