Trump sparks defiance as multiple Republicans balk at latest policy plan

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 2, 20255 min read

U.S. President Donald Trump looks on as he speaks with the media in the Oval Office, at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., September 5, 2025. REUTERS/Brian Snyder

GOP members are turning on the Trump administration as they point to a flawed policy that they would not personally back.

One representative observed there were clear “disadvantages” to a 50-year mortgage policy floated by Trump, while another said they simply “wouldn’t take one out.” Trump’s 50-year mortgage plan was floated earlier this year as the president claimed it would mean you “pay less per month.”

But economists have pointed out the interest on a 50-year mortgage would end up being far larger than that of a 30-year mortgage. Trump said, “All it means is you pay less per month. You pay it over a longer period of time. It’s not like a big factor. It might help a little bit.”

Speaking to The Hill, several Republican senators aired their concern over the proposed mortgage plan, with one saying they would appreciate a “hearing” on the finer details of such a policy.

Sen. John Kennedy (R-LA) said, “I want to hold a hearing on it. There are some advantages of it in terms of lowering the cost of owning a home, but there are some disadvantages, too. There’s a lot of interest, extra interest, that has to be paid between a 30 and 50-year mortgage.”

Sen. Ron Johnson (R-WI) stated he could simply not back such a plan personally, making it clear that if he were offered a 50-year mortgage, he would not take it. He said, “I wouldn’t take one out. It’s not a good idea.”

Sen. Rand Paul (R-KY), who Trump came to blows with earlier this week after calling Paul a “sick wacko,” believes the 50-year mortgage doesn’t make sense financially.

He said, “I don’t know how you create it. The people that I have talked with say that 50 years is longer than most people are in a house — a lot longer. It would lower your payments a little bit, but your overall what you pay — I think a $300,000 house becomes a $1 million house over that period of time.”

Some senators believe it is not defiance of Trump’s administration but representing their voters that matters most. Sen. Thom Tillis (R-NC) said, “We really are a mouthpiece for our constituents. I don’t view it as much as a defection or a disagreement with the president but saying, ‘Look, these are serious issues that we need to address in a way that best positions us for re-election next year.’”

Our Analysis:

GOP’s 50-Year Mortgage Misstep: A Dissection

The recent rumblings within the GOP about Trump’s 50-year mortgage proposal reveal a cacophony of confusion and concern among its ranks, showcasing a rare, albeit tepid, willingness to question the administration’s policy proposals. Let’s unpack the layers of this policy debacle and the reactions it has sparked.

Firstly, the pitch for a 50-year mortgage, as floated by Trump, comes across as a simplistic solution to a complex problem. The president’s claim that it would mean you “pay less per month” skims over the glaringly obvious downside: the exorbitant amount of interest paid over the life of the mortgage. This isn’t rocket science; it’s basic math. The allure of lower monthly payments masks a financially unsound proposition for the average homeowner. Economists have been quick to highlight this, pointing out the painfully obvious fact that this would significantly increase the total amount paid by homeowners, turning a $300,000 house into a $1 million liability. Yet, Trump’s reductionist view reduces this to “not like a big factor.”

Enter the GOP’s response. Senators John Kennedy, Ron Johnson, and Rand Paul have each expressed their reservations, ranging from a desire for more detailed scrutiny to outright rejection of the concept. Kennedy’s call for a “hearing” on the policy is particularly noteworthy, suggesting a sliver of due diligence in an often rubber-stamp Congress. Yet, the underlying tone isn’t one of staunch opposition but a cautious dance around the policy’s pitfalls. Johnson’s personal refusal to entertain such a mortgage and Paul’s blunt financial breakdown underscore the plan’s impracticality, yet this dissent within the party is muted, wrapped in the polite language of concern rather than outright condemnation.

Sen. Thom Tillis’s statement, however, takes the cake for political doublespeak. Framing the issue as not a “defection or disagreement” but as representing constituents’ concerns is a clever sidestep. It attempts to preserve party unity in the public eye while acknowledging the policy’s unpopularity. This isn’t about economic sensibility; it’s about political survival. The mention of re-election positions this not as a moral or fiscal stance but a strategic maneuver to maintain favor with the electorate.

What Does This All Mean?

The GOP’s internal discord over the 50-year mortgage proposal highlights a broader issue within the party: the struggle to align with Trump’s erratic policy propositions while maintaining economic credibility and electoral viability. The criticism, as soft as it is, signals a rare acknowledgment that the emperor may, in fact, have no clothes—or in this case, no viable economic policy.

Yet, the reluctance to firmly oppose such a clearly flawed policy speaks volumes about the current state of Republican politics. It’s less about the financial well-being of the American people and more about navigating the treacherous waters of Trump’s influence over the party and its base.

In essence, the GOP’s handling of the 50-year mortgage proposal is a microcosm of the party’s broader existential crisis: caught between fealty to Trump’s whims and the harsh realities of governance. As the debate unfolds, one thing is clear: in the world of GOP politics, pragmatism is often a casualty of political expediency.
