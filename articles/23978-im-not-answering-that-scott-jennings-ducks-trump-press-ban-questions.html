'I’m not answering that': Scott Jennings ducks Trump press-ban questions

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosSeptember 23, 20263 min read

The Press Ban Exposed the White House’s Real Weakness: Control Without Competence

A Ban That Shrunk the Presidency

The reporting describes a White House press ban that prompted other outlets to boycott presidential events, depriving Donald Trump of the professional equipment and distribution normally supplied by the press pool. The immediate result was not tighter message control but degraded audio, sparse coverage, litigation from banned outlets, and a president reaching fewer people.

This is the central political fact: the White House held the institutional power and used it. Journalists did not create the exclusion. Trump decided to restrict access, and his administration accepted the predictable operational consequences.

Jennings Would Optimize the Machine

CNN commentator Scott Jennings, described as a leading candidate to replace former press secretary Karoline Leavitt, did not defend the press’s right to operate. Nor did he commit to reversing the ban if appointed. Asked directly, he refused: “I’m not answering that.”

Instead, Jennings criticized the policy as ineffective. It reduced Trump’s reach, denied him useful confrontations with reporters and might fail in court. That is advice about improving presidential communications, not limiting presidential power.

The distinction matters. Jennings’s objection was that exclusion injured Trump. He presented access as valuable because Trump benefits from the spectacle of attacking reporters—not because an accountable government owes the public meaningful scrutiny.

The Job Interview Was Happening on CNN

The segment’s personal drama was obvious: Jennings was discussing a possible White House job while appearing on a network Trump had attacked as “fake news.” But reducing the exchange to whether Jennings looked “job hungry” trivializes the institutional conflict.

Jennings had incentives on both sides. CNN gave him a platform and paycheck; Trump could give him government power. His evasions protected both possibilities. He declined to endorse the ban, but he also declined to establish a principle that might constrain a prospective employer.

That is not confusion. It is calculated ambiguity, observable in the refusal itself even if Jennings’s private motive cannot be established from the reporting.

The Administration Created Its Own Information Failure

Washington Post columnist Josh Rogin argued that Trump had built a cocoon in which unwelcome information could not reach him. The report does not independently prove the full extent of that claim. It does, however, describe a narrower and demonstrable failure: the White House removed reporters who also supplied the infrastructure needed to transmit the president’s events clearly.

The humiliating part was not merely that online critics mocked poor audio. It was that an administration seeking absolute control apparently depended on the people it excluded to make its power visible.

Authoritarian methods are often sold as demonstrations of strength. Here, the reported consequences exposed dependency and incompetence instead. The White House could deny access, but it could not effortlessly replace the institutional capacity that access supported.

Solidarity Changed the Balance

The other outlets’ boycott mattered because it imposed a collective cost. A ban against selected journalists can function as a warning to everyone else: cooperate, flatter or risk removal. Collective withdrawal disrupted that coercive logic.

The resulting lawsuit also moved the dispute from presidential preference to legal process. According to the report, the banned outlets sought a temporary restraining order, with a hearing scheduled for Wednesday. The outcome was not provided, so the legality of the ban remained unresolved at the time described.

What was already clear was the allocation of agency. Trump initiated the exclusion. Other outlets chose solidarity. The White House then absorbed the reduced reach and harsher coverage that followed.

Access Is Not Accountability

This episode reveals a durable political pattern: governments attack independent institutions, discover that they still need those institutions, and then search for loyal personnel who can preserve the attack while reducing its costs.

Jennings’s audition, as portrayed in the report, fits that pattern. He did not challenge the premise that press access could be withheld at presidential discretion. He argued that the tactic was self-defeating. That makes him a potential technician of the same power structure, not necessarily an opponent of it.

The larger failure belongs to coverage that treats this mainly as palace intrigue—who gets the press-secretary job, who embarrassed whom on television, who was mocked online. The consequential story is simpler: the administration exercised exclusionary power; journalists collectively raised its price; and a prospective official declined to say he would stop it.
