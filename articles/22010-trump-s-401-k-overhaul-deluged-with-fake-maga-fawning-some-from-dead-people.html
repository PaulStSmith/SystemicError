Trump's 401(k) overhaul deluged with fake MAGA fawning — some from dead people

Dead Voices for a Retirement Giveaway

The Evidence

Bloomberg reports that Trump’s 401(k) proposal was backed by a swamp of fake support: nearly 12,000 comments tied to names that could not be verified, including some belonging to people who are dead. By contrast, more than 30,000 opponents signed their names and identified themselves. The asymmetry is the story. The opposition was real, traceable, and accountable. The support was anonymous, templated, and in part fabricated.

Who Holds Power

This is not a mystery about public opinion. Power sits with the Trump administration and the interests aligned behind the rule. Rulemaking is not a town hall. Agencies can solicit comments, but they still decide whose interests count. When a proposal for retirement policy is padded with ghost signatures and copy-paste praise, that does not create legitimacy. It reveals how badly the proposal needs it.

The Manufacture of Consent

This is astroturfing in its plainest form: engineered support presented as citizen input. Bloomberg says the supportive comments used five templates, carried no signatures or personal details, and showed no variation in text. That is not a spontaneous chorus. That is a message operation. The point is not persuasion in good faith. The point is to create the appearance of consensus so regulators can pretend the ground beneath them is firm.

The Real Blame

The weak actor in this story is not the public. It is the institutional process that treats fake participation as a manageable nuisance instead of a structural corruption of rulemaking. Bloomberg notes that such campaigns are difficult to police and rarely punished. That is the actual scandal: a system that knows it is being gamed and mostly shrugs. The rulemakers, the sponsors, and the intermediaries all benefit from ambiguity as long as it can be dressed up as democratic input.

The Financial Class Behind the Curtain

Bloomberg says it contacted more than two dozen investment firms, trade groups, and advocacy organizations that supported the rule, and none claimed to know who ran the campaigns. That is convenient ignorance from a sector that always seems to know how to find the levers of power when money is on the line. Whether the comment flood was organized directly by firms, trade groups, or cutouts, the political function is the same: launder private interest through fake public voice.

The Pattern

This is how authoritarian politics and deregulatory politics converge. Not always through spectacle. Often through paperwork, templates, and invented consensus. The administration pushes the policy; aligned actors fabricate the appearance of popular demand; institutions that should police the fraud absorb it as routine. That is not a glitch. It is a governing method.
