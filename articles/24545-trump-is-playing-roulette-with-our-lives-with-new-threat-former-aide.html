Trump is 'playing roulette with our lives' with new threat: former aide

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 8, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Former Department of Homeland Security chief of staff Miles Taylor says President Donald Trump is “playing roulette with our lives” by dismantling the DHS Office of Countering Weapons of Mass Destruction. AlterNet reports that Taylor linked the office’s loss of staff and capacity to America’s vulnerability to biological attacks and other mass-casualty threats.

Taylor wrote that hundreds of employees were fired, reassigned, or pushed out after Elon Musk’s DOGE team targeted the office and Trump told Congress it was unnecessary. According to Taylor, more than half its staff is gone, while lawmakers have asked government auditors whether its former responsibilities are still being performed.

His warning was prompted by reports that 28-year-old laboratory technician Darya Shipilova died after allegedly breaking a tube containing pneumonic plague bacteria at a Russian research institute. The circumstances of her death remain uncertain, and the reporting does not establish that the incident endangered the United States. Taylor’s argument is that such episodes illustrate why specialized biological-threat capacity exists.

The power was in the White House

The displaced specialists did not decide that their expertise was expendable. Trump’s administration did. Taylor identifies a clear chain of institutional authority: DOGE targeted the office, Trump told Congress he did not need it, and DHS personnel were removed, scattered, or reassigned.

That distinction matters because bureaucratic language can make an imposed decision sound like an office simply evaporated. It did not. The executive branch used its authority to dismantle a unit created to detect biological, chemical, radiological, and nuclear threats. Whatever rationale the administration had, AlterNet’s account does not provide it.

Expertise cannot be stored in a drawer

A specialist reassigned on paper is not necessarily a functioning defense capability. Taylor says lawmakers are now asking auditors whether anyone remains responsible for work once performed by the office. That is not streamlining demonstrated by results. It is institutional uncertainty created after the capacity was broken apart.

Years of technical knowledge, working relationships, and threat-detection experience cannot be instantly reconstructed when an emergency begins. The foreseeable consequence of dispersing that workforce is slower recognition, weaker coordination, and confusion over responsibility precisely when delay carries the highest cost.

The Russian incident is evidence of exposure, not proof of attack

The plague-related death in Russia gives Taylor’s warning urgency, but it does not prove that a biological attack is imminent or that this particular incident threatens Americans. The reported circumstances remain unclear. Treating it as proof would outrun the evidence.

The stronger indictment requires no speculative outbreak. Governments build specialized defenses because biological accidents and deliberate attacks are rare, unpredictable, and potentially catastrophic. Demanding a present disaster before preserving preventive capacity reverses the purpose of preparedness.

The missing defense

AlterNet foregrounds Taylor’s explosive metaphor and his history of criticizing Trump. That context may describe the messenger, but it can also distract from the decision that deserves scrutiny. Whether Taylor dislikes Trump does not answer whether the office’s duties are still being performed, by whom, or with what resources.

The administration holds the relevant records and authority. Yet the account contains no substantive White House explanation for eliminating the office, no replacement structure, and no evidence that equivalent capacity survives elsewhere. The central unanswered question is therefore institutional, not personal: what protection replaced the one Trump’s administration dismantled?

Government by subtraction

This episode reflects a broader political habit: treating public capacity as waste until the missing capacity becomes visible through failure. The immediate savings or ideological satisfaction belong to those who cut. The risk is transferred to people who had no role in the decision and may never know which safeguard disappeared.

That arrangement also diffuses accountability. DOGE can identify the target, the president can authorize the destruction, administrators can scatter the staff, and auditors can later search for whoever still owns the mission. Power acts decisively; responsibility becomes difficult to locate. That is not mere confusion. It is the predictable architecture of governing through demolition.
