A new Supreme Court decision is suffocating local TV stations

The Cheapest Airtime Is Becoming a Megadonor Subsidy

A Discount Built for Candidates

Federal law gives candidates access to broadcasters’ lowest rates in the final weeks before an election. The stated purpose is straightforward: keep soaring television prices from shutting candidates out of communication with voters. But a succession of federal decisions has begun converting that candidate protection into something much larger—a spending advantage available to party committees, joint fundraising committees and, potentially, super PAC money routed through them.

The numbers from Maine expose the stakes. During the week of September 1–7, Sen. Susan Collins’ campaign and one of her joint fundraising committees each paid Portland station WMTW-TV $2,200 for a 30-second fixed placement during the September 4 episode of “Wheel of Fortune.” Pine Tree Results PAC, a super PAC supporting Collins, paid $10,000 apiece for two spots in a weekday rotation without guaranteed air dates.

The Institutions Made This Possible

This was not a loophole discovered in nature. Federal institutions built it.

A 2024 Federal Election Commission advisory opinion allowed super PACs to participate in joint fundraising committees alongside candidates. In March, the Federal Communications Commission interpreted federal law to extend candidate-level advertising rates to political parties and joint fundraising committees. In June, the Supreme Court eliminated longstanding limits on party spending coordinated with federal candidates.

A Fourth Circuit panel pushed back in late August, ruling that the statute “unambiguously” reserves the lowest unit charge for candidates. Republican congressional committees appealed, backed by a Trump administration brief disputing the Democratic candidates’ standing. On September 4, the Supreme Court temporarily reinstated the FCC interpretation while the litigation continued.

The order was not a final ruling. Its immediate effect, however, was to preserve a system in which coordinated party and joint-fundraising ads could be treated as “candidate use” and purchased at the candidate rate.

Independence on Paper, Coordination in Practice

Pine Tree Results PAC is not merely an outside organization buying ads for Collins. Federal Election Commission records cited by OpenSecrets show that it also participates in the Collins Victory Committee, a joint fundraising committee that includes Collins’ campaign, her leadership PAC and the National Republican Senatorial Committee.

That structure matters. According to Campaign Legal Center attorney Erin Chlopak, a super PAC participating in such a committee could finance campaign-style advertising while the committee claims the candidate discount. The Campaign Legal Center warned in an amicus brief that these committees could become vehicles for outside groups seeking lowest-unit-charge rates.

Whether and how aggressively groups use that route remains to be seen. But the institutional architecture is already visible: an organization described as independent can share a fundraising vehicle with the candidate it supports, while that vehicle may receive a price reserved historically for candidates themselves. The formal categories survive; the financial separation becomes harder to take seriously.

The Real Winners Have Large Checkbooks

The immediate advantage belongs to organizations already holding the most money. FEC records through June 30 showed $92 million in cash for the National Republican Congressional Committee and $56 million for the National Republican Senatorial Committee, compared with $80 million for the Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee and just under $40 million for the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee.

That gives the Republican committees a short-term edge, according to Issue One’s Michael Beckel. It does not make this merely a partisan trick. Both parties have strong incentives to copy any method that makes campaign money more efficient, and experts quoted by OpenSecrets expect them to adapt.

The deeper transfer of power runs upward, toward major donors. An individual could give no more than $7,000 directly to a candidate across the primary and general elections under the limits described in the reporting. The same donor could give as much as $44,300 annually to each party committee. With coordinated-spending limits gone and party dollars potentially buying airtime at discounted rates, Beckel said megadonors could channel hundreds of thousands through parties for spending coordinated with a particular candidate—and potentially more than $1 million through large joint fundraising structures.

Transparency Does Not Cure Leverage

Wesleyan Media Project co-director Erika Franklin Fowler identified a possible benefit: party-sponsored advertising may carry clearer labels than ads from opaque outside groups, helping viewers recognize the sponsor. Parties are also generally more accountable to voters than independent organizations.

That is a case for honest disclosure, not for pretending concentrated financial power has disappeared. A clearly labeled advertisement can still be financed through a system that gives wealthy donors vastly more practical influence than ordinary contributors. Transparency tells voters who purchased the message. It does not equalize who can afford to saturate the airwaves.

Republican National Committee chairman Joe Gruters described the advantage without euphemism: access to candidate rates could let the party buy ads at “20 cents on the dollar,” coordinate directly with candidates and achieve “maximum efficiency.” That is the operative logic—not broader participation, but greater purchasing power for political organizations already equipped to collect enormous sums.

Public Protection, Private Amplifier

The systemic failure is not simply that political advertising will increase. It is that a rule intended to protect candidates from commercial pricing is being expanded into a subsidy for powerful fundraising networks.

The FEC loosened the structure of joint fundraising. The FCC broadened eligibility for discounted airtime. The Supreme Court removed coordination limits and then temporarily restored the FCC’s expansive interpretation after a lower court rejected it. Each decision can be described as technical. Together, they weaken the boundary between candidates, parties and nominally independent money.

The result is a campaign-finance system that preserves distinctions in its paperwork while erasing them where power is exercised. Candidates remain candidates, super PACs remain “independent,” and megadonors remain formally limited—until the money enters a shared vehicle, coordinates its purpose and buys influence at a public-protection price.
