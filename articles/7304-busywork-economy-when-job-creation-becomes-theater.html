Busywork Economy: When “Job Creation” Becomes Theater

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 25, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

LinkedIn loves a certain kind of story. A company “creates jobs.” An administration “boosts employment.” An organization proudly announces new management roles, new departments, new layers of coordination. Charts go up. Applause follows.

And yet, somehow, everyone feels more exhausted, less productive, and vaguely aware that very little of substance has improved.

Two simple memes circulating online capture this contradiction with brutal clarity.

In the first, two people pay each other to dig and refill the same hole. Money changes hands. GDP rises. Employment is sustained. Nothing useful exists at the end.

In the second, a lone worker—Dave—does the actual labor, surrounded by a ring of managers, supervisors, and coordinators who appear deeply concerned yet entirely unnecessary.

Together, these images form a devastating critique of how modern economies and organizations confuse activity with value.

GDP Is a Measure of Motion, Not Meaning

Gross Domestic Product was never meant to be a moral scorecard. It measures economic activity—transactions—not usefulness, sustainability, or human flourishing. If money moves, GDP goes up. Full stop.

This is why the hole-digging meme works so well. From a GDP perspective, it’s flawless:

  • Labor is compensated

  • Services are exchanged

  • Currency circulates

The fact that the final outcome is indistinguishable from the starting point is irrelevant to the metric.

This isn’t a bug. It’s the definition.

The problem begins when policymakers, executives, and pundits start treating GDP growth as synonymous with progress. At that point, digging holes for the sake of digging holes stops being a joke and starts being policy.

We see this logic everywhere:

  • Infrastructure projects optimized for spending velocity rather than utility

  • Corporate initiatives designed to “show momentum” rather than solve problems

  • Entire industries built around compliance, reporting, and internal churn

The economy looks busy. Society looks stuck.

Employment Is Not the Same as Value Creation

“Jobs” have become a fetishized outcome, disconnected from what those jobs actually do.

Creating employment is important—people need income, dignity, and stability. But employment that exists solely to justify itself eventually becomes parasitic. It consumes time, energy, and attention while contributing nothing back.

The hole-digging loop is the purest abstraction of this phenomenon. But the real-world version is far more insidious because it hides behind professionalism, jargon, and organizational charts.

Which brings us to Dave.

The Managerial Spiral

Dave is doing real work. The hole exists because Dave digs it. If the hole needs to be deeper, Dave adjusts. If the soil changes, Dave adapts. Reality pushes back, and Dave responds.

Everyone else in the picture exists in a different universe.

They manage process, not outcomes. They optimize communication, not results. They generate documents, meetings, and frameworks whose primary function is to justify their own existence.

This is not an attack on management as a concept. Coordination matters. Leadership matters. Good managers amplify the effectiveness of people like Dave.

But modern organizations have drifted far beyond that point.

What we increasingly see is management detached from production:

  • Layers added to supervise other layers

  • Roles created to translate between people who should be talking directly

  • Performance systems that measure compliance rather than contribution

At some point, the ratio flips. The work exists to sustain the organization, not the other way around.

Bullshit Jobs Are a Structural Outcome

Anthropologist David Graeber famously described “bullshit jobs” as roles even the people doing them secretly believe should not exist. These memes are not a rebuttal to his thesis—they are visual footnotes.

A system obsessed with growth metrics but indifferent to outcomes will naturally produce bullshit jobs. They are not accidents. They are stabilizers.

They absorb surplus labor.
They create the illusion of progress.
They protect existing power structures.

Most importantly, they keep the machine running without asking whether the machine should exist in its current form.

Dave is dangerous. Dave knows what real work looks like.

The Cost No One Accounts For

Busywork economies don’t just waste money. They waste human potential.

People trapped in meaningless roles experience:

  • Chronic stress and burnout

  • A persistent sense of fraudulence

  • Moral injury from participating in systems they know are hollow

Meanwhile, genuinely productive workers are buried under reporting requirements, status updates, and managerial rituals designed to feed the overhead.

The result is an economy that feels simultaneously overheated and stagnant—everyone running, nowhere to go.

What Real Job Creation Actually Looks Like

Real job creation is not about maximizing headcount or spinning up titles. It is about increasing the amount of useful work society can perform with the resources it has.

That means:

  • Fewer layers, not more

  • Better tools, not more meetings

  • Trust in expertise over obsession with oversight

It means asking uncomfortable questions:

  • What would stop existing if this role disappeared?

  • Does this job make someone else’s work meaningfully easier or better?

  • Are we solving problems, or just processing them?

If the answer is “nothing changes,” the hole is being dug again.

A Final Thought

The memes are funny because they’re true. They land because people recognize their own workplaces in them.

An economy can be very busy and deeply unproductive at the same time. Organizations can be full of jobs and starved of value. Growth can be real on paper and fictional everywhere else.

Dave doesn’t need another manager.

He needs fewer people digging holes around him—and more people willing to ask whether the hole needed digging in the first place.
