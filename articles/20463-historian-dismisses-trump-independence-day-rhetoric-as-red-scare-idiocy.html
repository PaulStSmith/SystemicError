Trump’s Red-Scare Theater Is a Cover for Real Power

The Performance

Trump used an Independence Day speech at Mount Rushmore to warn about communists, citing recent primary victories by democratic socialists. Historian Doug Brinkley called it “Joe McCarthy red scare idiocy,” and he was right about the function of the speech if not its novelty: this was not an argument, it was a signal. The point was never to describe an actual communist takeover. The point was to manufacture a menace that can be blamed, mobilized against, and run on.

Who Has the Power

The actual institutional power here sits with the president and the Republican apparatus that amplifies him. Trump is not a commentator reacting from the sidelines. He is using the prestige of the office, the symbolism of Mount Rushmore, and the reach of a holiday address to set the terms of political attention. Brinkley’s line that Trump wants Republicans to run on this “in this election cycle” gets closer to the machinery at work: the speech is campaign material dressed up as national unity.

The Misdirection

The framing is insultingly familiar. Trump warns about a communist menace as if a fringe left current were the central danger in American life, while the real story is a president reaching for Cold War panic to obscure his own politics. That is the trick: invent a threatening label, then demand everyone react to the label instead of the power behind it. Alex Witt’s question on MS NOW cut through the nonsense plainly: who said America was becoming communist? Exactly. The alarm is not evidence. It is a tool.

McCarthyism With Better Lighting

Brinkley’s McCarthy comparison is useful because it names the method. McCarthyism was never just about ideology. It was about intimidation, list-making, and forcing institutions to prove loyalty under manufactured suspicion. Trump’s speech follows the same logic: identify an enemy, inflate the danger, and turn the country’s anxiety into partisan fuel. Calling it “utter nonsense” is accurate, but that phrase should not soften the political meaning. Nonsense can still be disciplined propaganda when it comes from the bully pulpit.

Holiday Patriotism as Cover

The bitter joke in the source is that Brinkley ultimately advises people to block out Trump’s rhetoric for 48 hours and get back to family, barbecue, and the holiday. That instinct is understandable, and it is also exactly why this kind of politics works. Authoritarian performance depends on being treated as background noise until it has already set the agenda. The vacation vibe is not a defense against propaganda; it is often the condition that lets propaganda pass unchallenged.

The Larger Pattern

This story is not about communists. It is about ruling through invented enemies because real accountability is harder. Trump’s red-baiting is a familiar authoritarian trick: convert governing failure into moral panic, convert political opposition into infiltration, and convert a holiday into a loyalty test. The deeper pattern is not confusion. It is deliberate misdirection by people with enough power to define what the country is supposed to fear.
