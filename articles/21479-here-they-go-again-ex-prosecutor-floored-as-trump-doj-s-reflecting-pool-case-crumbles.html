'Here they go again!' Ex-prosecutor floored as Trump DOJ's Reflecting Pool case crumbles

The Power Behind the Charge

The relevant power here is not the former federal prosecutor or the man charged over peeling paint at the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool. It is Donald Trump’s Department of Justice, which chose the target, pushed the felony charge, and now has to live with a witness reportedly admitting the pool was damaged before David Hearn ever touched it.

That is the center of gravity. Not confusion. Not misunderstanding. A federal prosecution was launched, and the facts now appear to be moving in the opposite direction from the accusation.

A Case Built Backward

The reporting describes a case so thin it collapses under the first useful witness. Hearn, a former Olympic canoeist, was arrested and charged with felony damage to property after touching peeling paint in the pool. Then a grand jury witness undercut the government’s claim that Hearn caused the damage.

That is not a minor evidentiary problem. It is the difference between lawful prosecution and a political search for somebody to punish. When a case begins with a charge and only later asks the facts to cooperate, the institution is not enforcing law. It is laundering intent through procedure.

The Misdirection Machine

The weak actor in the story is not the person indicted. The weak actor is the public narrative that treats this kind of prosecution as ordinary error, as if a federal indictment can just accidentally materialize around a convenient target.

Former federal prosecutor Glenn Kirschner points to the same pattern in other cases involving James Comey, Letitia James, and the Broadview Six, all dismissed by judges for prosecutorial misconduct. That is the important connective tissue. The problem is not one isolated mistake by one overeager lawyer. It is a prosecutorial culture comfortable enough with abuse that misconduct becomes a recurring feature, not a rare exception.

Jeanine Pirro and the Politics of Dirty Hands

Kirschner’s reference to Jeanine Pirro matters because it identifies the political character of the operation. This is not neutral legal administration. It is the Trump DOJ, operating under a president who treats federal power as an extension of personal grievance and factional revenge.

When prosecutors aligned with that project bring a case that cannot survive the facts, the institution itself becomes the story. The charge is not just wrong. It is revealing. It shows how easily federal machinery can be turned from public enforcement into political theater with handcuffs.

What the Framing Hides

The article’s real weakness is that it still has to explain why a damaged reflecting pool became a felony case at all. That question should not need much unpacking. The answer is that the state chose to escalate a trivial or dubious incident into criminal punishment, then tried to make the evidence fit after the fact.

That is how selective enforcement works. The powerful decide who gets turned into an example, and prosecutors supply the paperwork. The damage is not just to Hearn, but to the idea that law is being applied rather than deployed.

The Larger Pattern

This is what authoritarian politics looks like at the institutional level: grievance in the White House, ambition in the Justice Department, and a legal system pressured to perform certainty where there is none. The public is then asked to confuse prosecutorial collapse with normal legal correction.

It is not normal. It is a pattern of state power used carelessly when it fails, and maliciously when it succeeds. The lesson is not that this one case went too far. The lesson is that a DOJ willing to manufacture a felony over a reflecting pool is already operating far outside democratic restraint.
