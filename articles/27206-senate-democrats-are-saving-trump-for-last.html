Senate Democrats are saving Trump for last

Based on an article by John Stoehr

Paulo SantosNovember 12, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet argues that Senate Democrats may postpone a direct confrontation with Donald Trump while first investigating officials, corporations, and billionaires around him. The article does not establish that this is an agreed Democratic strategy. It draws the theory primarily from Senator Richard Blumenthal’s remarks that impeachment could be a “blind alley” and that the Senate may have enough votes to impeach Pete Hegseth, but not Trump.

Blumenthal said that after Congress is seated on January 3, the Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations would pursue inquiries “with urgency.” He identified alleged crypto corruption, Elon Musk and Starlink, vanity projects, and corporate donations connected to an East Wing ballroom project as areas already under examination.

The piece also reports leadership friction among Democrats. Bernie Sanders and Chris Van Hollen questioned automatically retaining Chuck Schumer, while Blumenthal reaffirmed his support for him. In the House, Representative-elect Don Leonard alleged that senior Democrats threatened retaliation unless he backed Hakeem Jeffries; AlterNet disputes the allegation’s plausibility but does not independently resolve what was said.

Power sits with the investigators

The operative power here is not held by activists debating leadership on podcasts. It belongs to senators who control committees, subpoenas, hearings, and the sequence in which misconduct is exposed. Blumenthal’s prospective chairmanship would give him institutional machinery capable of imposing costs on administration officials and private interests benefiting from government access.

That distinction matters. Impeachment without the votes to convict can become a contained spectacle. Sustained investigations can expose networks of influence, force sworn testimony, and make corporations and political appointees answer for specific conduct. The choice is not between accountability and inaction. It is between different instruments of accountability, wielded under different political constraints.

Trump last is a theory, not a fact

AlterNet’s strongest claim outruns its evidence. Blumenthal described the available votes and questioned whether impeachment would be productive. The article converts those remarks into a possible strategy: isolate Trump by first damaging the Republicans and wealthy interests around him, then let those investigations run toward the next election.

That is a plausible interpretation, not a reported Democratic plan. The distinction is essential because strategic patience and institutional hesitation can look identical from outside. Democrats may be preserving leverage. They may also be improvising because they lack consensus, votes, or confidence. The reporting supports the existence of tactical calculation; it does not prove a coordinated campaign to make Trump “the last man standing.”

Accountability is being translated into affordability

Blumenthal connected alleged corruption to household costs, arguing that financial dependence on foreign governments and corporate pay-to-play ultimately burdens ordinary Americans. That formulation is politically useful because it refuses to quarantine corruption as an ethics seminar for professionals. Government favors have economic beneficiaries, and the public pays for policy structured around those beneficiaries.

But the argument must be demonstrated case by case. AlterNet separately cites estimates that inflation accelerated after Trump returned to office and polling in which midterm voters blamed his policies for higher costs. Those findings can establish political vulnerability. They do not, by themselves, prove that every alleged corrupt arrangement caused a particular price increase.

The sharper case is institutional: concentrated wealth purchases access, access shapes policy, and policy distributes costs downward. Investigators have the authority to trace those transactions. If they substitute slogans for that work, they will reproduce the very vagueness that protects powerful actors.

Leadership drama can become misdirection

The article is right to distinguish actual leverage from performative rebellion, but too quick to settle disputed motives. Leonard’s allegation against senior House Democrats may be exaggerated, misunderstood, or accurate; the supplied reporting does not resolve it. Jeffries having ample votes would make coercion unnecessary, but unnecessary conduct is not impossible conduct.

Still, the larger imbalance is clear. A representative-elect’s viral video does not carry the institutional weight of House leadership, just as online suspicion of Schumer does not itself determine the Senate caucus vote. Treating every intraparty grievance as equivalent to decisions made by leaders with control over money, committee assignments, and legislative access obscures who can impose consequences.

The reverse misdirection is also possible. Labeling critics “agents of chaos” can turn scrutiny of leadership into a personality defect rather than a political dispute. Jeffries and Schumer possess power; questioning how they use it is legitimate. The relevant test is whether critics are organizing votes and policy commitments or merely monetizing resentment.

The system protects itself through sequence

This story reveals a party trying to reconcile two competing imperatives: use institutional power against alleged corruption and preserve enough political cohesion to govern. The sequence of investigations, impeachment efforts, and leadership contests will determine whether accountability reaches decision-makers or stops with expendable subordinates.

Saving Trump for last could be disciplined strategy. It could also become an excuse for never reaching him. The difference will be visible in what Senate Democrats actually do: whom they subpoena, which financial relationships they expose, whether they follow evidence upward, and whether powerful private actors face consequences rather than hearings designed only for television.

Political systems rarely announce that they are avoiding accountability. They call the delay prudence, realism, or timing. If Democrats dismantle the machinery around Trump and then confront the authority at its center, the sequence will have served accountability. If they prosecute the perimeter while protecting the center from consequence, “Trump last” will mean Trump spared.
