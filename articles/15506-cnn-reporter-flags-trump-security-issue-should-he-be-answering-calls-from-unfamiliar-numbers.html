CNN reporter flags Trump security issue: 'Should he be answering calls from unfamiliar numbers?'

Although President Donald Trump has angrily attacked journalists Ashley Parker and Michael Scherer — he described Parker as a "radical left lunatic" and Scherer as a liar — he granted them an interview for an article published by The Atlantic early Monday morning, April 28.

In their article, Parker and Scherer explain how they got the interview. And that explanation caught the attention of CNN's Hadas Gold.

"Apparently, as word of our meeting spread through Trump’s inner circle, someone had reminded him of some of the things we (specifically Ashley) had said and written that he didn’t like," Parker and Scherer recall. "We still don't know who it was — but we immediately understood the consequences: no photo shoot, no tour of the newly redecorated Oval Office or the Lincoln Bedroom, and definitely no interview. But we've both covered Trump long enough to know that his first word is rarely his final one."

The reporters continue, "So at 10:45 on a Saturday morning in late March, we called him on his cellphone. (Don’t ask how we got his number. All we can say is that the White House staff have imperfect control over Trump's personal communication devices.) The president was at the country club he owns in Bedminster, New Jersey. The number that flashed on his screen was an unfamiliar one, but he answered anyway. 'Who's calling?' he asked."

In response to Parker and Scherer's article, Gold went to X, formerly Twitter, and posted, "Ummm should the President be answering phone calls from unfamiliar numbers??? With how easy it is to spoof voices of people he trusts??"

Gold's April 28 tweet isn't going unnoticed.

Philadelphia-based attorney Christopher Moyer posted, "Um, nooooooooo."

Our Analysis:

The Paradox of Access: Trump's Dance with the Media

In an article that reads like a political thriller more than a standard journalistic piece, President Donald Trump's latest foray into the media circus is as bewildering as it is revealing. Despite hurling venomous attacks at journalists Ashley Parker and Michael Scherer, labeling Parker a "radical left lunatic" and Scherer a liar, Trump paradoxically granted them an interview. This move, detailed in their report for The Atlantic, exposes a multifaceted drama involving ego, access, and the precarious balance of power between the press and the presidency.

The Curious Case of the Granted Interview

First, let's dissect the sheer irony of Trump's decision. After lambasting Parker and Scherer with his typical arsenal of insults, he turns around and offers them a golden ticket—an interview. This decision is emblematic of Trump's tumultuous relationship with the media: one where his need for attention seemingly outweighs his disdain for the press. It's a classic Trump maneuver, keeping both allies and adversaries on their toes, proving once again that his "first word is rarely his final one."

The backstory of how the interview came to be is equally intriguing and concerning. The revelation that White House staff have "imperfect control" over Trump's personal communication devices is a glaring red flag. It paints a picture of a presidency that's not just unconventional, but alarmingly lax in terms of security. The fact that journalists could simply call him on his cellphone raises questions about the accessibility of the most powerful individual in the United States to potential security threats.

Security: A Laughing Matter?

Hadas Gold's reaction on X (formerly Twitter), questioning the wisdom of the President answering calls from unknown numbers, is more than just a quip—it's a legitimate security concern. In an era where voice spoofing and identity theft are not just possible but easy, the casual nature with which Trump handles his personal communication is astonishing. The responses from the public, ranging from disbelief to concern, underscore the bizarre reality in which such a scenario is not only possible but actually happened.

The Bigger Picture

This episode, while seemingly trivial on the surface, encapsulates the broader issues plaguing Trump's presidency. It's not just about a phone call or an interview—it's about the ongoing erosion of norms and the blurring of lines between personal whims and presidential duties. Trump's approach to governance, characterized by impulsiveness and a penchant for drama, continues to raise eyebrows and questions about the implications for national security and the integrity of the presidential office.

Moreover, the willingness of Parker and Scherer to engage with Trump, despite his previous attacks, speaks volumes about the media's role in this dance. It's a delicate balance between holding power to account and not becoming a pawn in its games. This incident, while seemingly a minor footnote in the grand scheme, serves as a microcosm of the larger, more complex relationship between Trump, the media, and the public.

In conclusion, this episode, though it may be dismissed by some as just another day in Trump's America, is a stark reminder of the unconventional and often precarious state of American politics. The implications for security, presidential conduct, and media relations are profound, serving as a cautionary tale of what happens when the lines between personal conduct and presidential responsibility become blurred.
