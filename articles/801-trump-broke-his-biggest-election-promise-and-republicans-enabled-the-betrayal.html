Trump broke his biggest election promise — and Republicans enabled the betrayal

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosJune 15, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Man With the AuthorityThe Promise Was the ProductCongress Is Not Watching From the SidelinesMisdirection as MethodThe Pattern Underneath

War by Denial, Power by Enabling

The Man With the Authority

The point of the source material is simple: Donald Trump made a promise, broke it, and is now trying to erase the promise from memory. That is not a misunderstanding. It is an act of political self-exoneration by the person who holds the highest executive power and made the decision to use it.

Trump is not a commentator on this war. He is the one who started it, defended it, and then lied about what he told voters. The institutional power is not abstract here. It sits in the Oval Office, attached to a man who now wants the country to forget that he sold himself as the anti-war candidate.

The Promise Was the Product

The campaign messaging was not accidental rhetoric. It was the product being sold. Trump repeated, in multiple settings, that he would have “no more wars,” “no wars,” and “I’m not going to start a war, I’m going to stop wars.” The source lays out the record because Trump is now pretending the record does not exist.

That is the core political move: promise peace to win power, then relabel escalation as something else once power has been secured. The lie is not just that he broke the promise. The lie is that the promise was never made in the first place.

Congress Is Not Watching From the Sidelines

The source correctly broadens responsibility to Republicans in the House and Senate. That matters. They are not victims of Trump’s war policy. They are the governing class that could restrain him and chooses not to.

Their role is enabling, not accidental. They repeatedly refused to stop his wars. They repeatedly enabled his aggression. That is what congressional power looks like when it is used for cowardice instead of oversight: loud when campaigning, absent when consequences arrive.

Misdirection as Method

Trump’s interview strategy is familiar. Deny the pledge. Redefine the war. Treat a deliberate policy choice like a semantic dispute. This is not confusion. It is misdirection aimed at voters who may be persuadable enough to believe that language can be rewritten after the fact.

The source also notes the war in Iran was a war of choice, not a response to an attack on the United States. That distinction matters because it strips away the pretext. When there is no direct attack and no honest claim of necessity, the only thing left is presidential will and the machinery that surrounds it.

The Pattern Underneath

What this story reveals is not just Trump’s dishonesty. It is the bipartisan architecture of impunity that lets executive war-making proceed while legislators perform disappointment after the damage is done. The public gets slogans about peace. The governing class gets to avoid responsibility. The war goes on.

That is the pattern: power makes the decision, enablers normalize it, and propaganda shifts blame downward or outward until accountability disappears. When a president can promise “no wars,” start one anyway, and then be covered by a compliant Congress, the problem is bigger than one liar. It is a political system that rewards deception and treats resistance as optional.
