The never-Trumpers are losing — and the GOP establishment knows it

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosJuly 2, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Populism Is Not the Story. Power Is.

Source in Brief

The article argues that the real split in American politics is not left versus right but bottom versus top, with MAGA on one side and a younger Democratic socialist wave on the other. It says both party establishments will try to discredit those insurgencies and that the answer is to pair anti-racist politics with material reforms like affordable housing, healthcare, childcare, and eldercare.

The Real Axis

The most useful thing in the piece is also the most inconvenient: power in America is concentrated above everyone else. The author says it plainly, then mostly soft-pedals the consequences. This is not a story about two noisy factions discovering the same truth from different directions. It is a story about who can still set the terms of politics, who gets boxed out of decision-making, and who is forced to live with the results.

That distinction matters because “populism” is not a moral category. MAGA and democratic socialism are not symmetrical expressions of the same democratic frustration. One channels resentment downward and outward, toward immigrants, Black people, Muslims, and “coastal elites.” The other names class conflict and the rigging of economic life by those above. The article knows this, but it still tries to flatten the difference into a broad anti-establishment mood. That is too neat for a country where one movement organizes exclusion and the other organizes redistribution.

Who Actually Holds Power

The article names corporate and financial elites, party moderates, Trump, Sanders, AOC, and Mamdani. That list is not a map of equal forces. It is a map of asymmetry.

Corporate and financial elites do not need to win elections to shape the field. The article even admits they are comfortable with Trump’s tax cuts and regulatory rollbacks, and with incremental Democratic governance that leaves the economic order intact. That is the real center of gravity: not voters chanting in the street, but institutions that can absorb almost any rhetoric as long as property, profit, and hierarchy stay protected.

So when the article says the establishment will “fight to regain control,” it understates the situation. The establishment never stopped controlling the levers that matter. It can fund campaigns, define “seriousness,” reward compliance, and punish rupture. It is not a stranded victim of populism. It is the operating system populists have been forced to fight through.

The Misdirection Machine

The article correctly warns that the establishment will try to smear insurgents. In the GOP, the script is easy: racist, xenophobic, misogynistic, fascist. In the Democratic world, the counterattack becomes antisemitic, communist, reckless, unrealistic. There is truth in some of those charges, the article says, which is exactly why they work.

That is the old trick: take real or alleged excesses on the margins and use them to shield the center from accountability. If the public can be made to fear the language of redistribution more than the machinery of inequality, then the people who benefit from the machine can keep governing while posing as adults in the room.

The article is weakest when it treats this as a communications problem. It is not. It is an allocation problem. Who gets housing, healthcare, childcare, eldercare, wages, and agency is not settled by tone. It is settled by institutions, law, budgets, and organized pressure. The opposition to those changes is rarely confusion. It is interest.

The Pattern Beneath the Parties

The deepest insight in the piece is its claim that power is zero-sum. That is almost certainly why the elite class dislikes the current insurgencies in both parties. One camp threatens racial hierarchy; the other threatens economic extraction. Both threaten the people who have grown accustomed to managing decline from the top.

But the article stops short of saying what that means in practice. It means the real conflict is not between moderates and extremists, or even between left and right. It is between people trying to preserve an order that works for capital and people trying to make the system answer to ordinary life. The labels change. The defensive choreography stays the same.

Conclusion: The System Protects Itself

The author is right that material improvement would drain some of the poison from politics. Make housing affordable, make healthcare accessible, make care work bearable, and the market for grievance shrinks. But that is precisely why the people who control the system resist those changes. They are not confused about the cure. They are attached to the disease.

That is the larger pattern here: American politics keeps producing insurgency because the governing order keeps refusing repair. When elites cannot or will not deliver agency, they get backlash. When institutions answer injustice with branding instead of redistribution, they get extremism. The problem is not that voters have become unruly. The problem is that power has become too concentrated to be answered honestly.
