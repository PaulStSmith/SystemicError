‘Your political circus isn’t welcome here’: Trump eyes federal crackdowns in more cities

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 24, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

U.S. President Donald Trump gestures, as he departs for Pennsylvania, on the South Lawn of the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., May 30, 2025. REUTERS/Kent Nishimura TPX IMAGES OF THE DAY

US President Donald Trump said Friday that Chicago is the next city in his crosshairs for the kind of federal invasion and occupation currently underway in Washington, DC—a threat that sparked defiant pushback from officials in the Windy City and beyond.

"After we do this, we'll go to another location, and we'll make it safe also," Trump told reporters in the Oval Office, referring to his federalization of Washington's Metropolitan Police Department and deployment of National Guard troops from the district and five Republican-controlled states.

"We're going to make our cities very, very safe. Chicago's a mess. You have an incompetent mayor. Grossly incompetent and we'll straighten that one out probably next," the president said, referring to progressive Brandon Johnson. "That will be our next one after this. And it won't even be tough."

On August 11, Trump dubiously declared a public safety emergency in Washington, DC, despite violent crime being down 26% from a year ago, when it was at its second-lowest level since 1966, according to official statistics. Critics have noted that Trump's crackdown isn't just targeting criminals, but also unhoused and mentally ill people, who have had their homes destroyed and property taken.

On Friday, Trump threatened to completely take over Washington and oust Mayor Muriel Bowser if she does not stop pointing out that crime has decreased in the city, which the president called a "crime-infested rat hole."

In addition to Chicago, Trump has threatened to send federal forces into cities including Baltimore, Los Angeles, New York, Oakland, and San Francisco. Violent crime is trending downward in all of those cities—with some registering historically low levels.

Unlike in Washington, DC, where home rule laws allow the federal government to take control of local police, Trump would face greater obstacles to intervention in other cities.

"President Trump can't seize control of the Chicago police or any other local department outside of DC," Congressman Raja Krishnamoorth (D-Ill.) noted on social media Friday. "The military cannot and will not patrol the streets of Chicago, and I will work with state and local officials to ensure that doesn't happen."

Mayor Johnson said in a statement that "the problem with the president's approach is that it is uncoordinated, uncalled for, and unsound."

"If the Trump administration is serious about driving down violence in Chicago, or anywhere else in America, then he should not have taken over $800 million away from violence prevention," he added.

Other elected officials in Illinois also expressed anger and alarm at the prospect of a Trump intervention in Chicago.

"After usingLos Angelesand Washington, DC as his testing ground for authoritarian overreach, Trump is now openly flirting with the idea of taking over other states and cities," Democratic Illinois Gov. JB Pritzkersaidon X.

"Trump’s goal is to incite fear in our communities and destabilize existing public safety efforts—all to create a justification to further abuse his power," the governor continued. "He's playing a game and creating a spectacle for the press to play along with."

"We don't play those games," Pritzker added. "Our commitment to law and order is delivering results. Crime rates are improving. Homicides are down by more than 30% in Chicago in the last year alone. Our progress in lowering crime has been made possible with [community violence intervention] programs that they’re defunding."

Lt. Gov. Juliana Stratton, a Democrat running for US Senate, said that "if Trump wants to take his ego trip on tour, he picked the wrong city."

"Chicago doesn't bow down to kings or roll out the red carpet for dictators," she added. "As a Black woman from the South Side, I can assure you... your political circus isn't welcome here."

Congresswoman Robin Kelly (D-Ill.) wrote on social media, "President Trump: You are not welcome in Chicago."

"Sending the National Guard endangers Black communities already overpoliced and under-invested in," she added. "If you cared about saving lives, you'd pass gun safety laws and fund community violence intervention."

Our Analysis:

Analysis: Trump's Threat to "Straighten Out" Chicago

In yet another display of audacious overreach, President Donald Trump has set his sights on Chicago, threatening what can only be described as a federal invasion under the guise of public safety. This narrative, while not new, has reached a boiling point, sparking outrage and defiance from officials in the Windy City and beyond.

Dubious Declarations and Real Intentions

Trump's threat to send federal forces into Chicago, following a similar federalization in Washington, DC, comes under the questionable pretext of making cities "very, very safe." However, this assertion is fundamentally flawed. Trump's decision to declare a public safety emergency in Washington, DC—despite a 26% decrease in violent crime—reveals a disturbing trend of using 'safety' as a smokescreen for authoritarian overreach.

It is particularly concerning that Trump's crackdown targets not just criminals but also the unhoused and mentally ill, laying bare an agenda that seems less about public safety and more about control and suppression. His dismissive reference to DC as a "crime-infested rat hole" and his cavalier attitude towards Mayor Muriel Bowser's factual corrections are indicative of a deeper disregard for the complexities of urban crime and governance.

Obstacles and Opposition

While Trump's ambitions for Chicago echo his actions in DC, they face significant legal and logistical hurdles. Unlike in Washington, DC, where unique home rule laws present a loophole for federal intervention, Chicago's police force and governance structure are protected from such overreach. Congressman Raja Krishnamoorthi's assertion that the military will not patrol Chicago's streets underscores the constitutional and practical barriers to Trump's intentions.

Moreover, the unified front presented by Illinois officials, including Mayor Brandon Johnson and Governor JB Pritzker, not only challenges Trump's narrative but also highlights a commitment to community-based public safety strategies. Their emphasis on violence prevention programs and community investment directly contradicts Trump's reductionist approach to crime as a problem solvable by federal force alone.

A Dangerous Game

Governor Pritzker's characterization of Trump's strategy as an attempt to "incite fear" and "destabilize existing public safety efforts" is particularly poignant. It speaks to a larger pattern of governance by spectacle, wherein Trump seeks to manufacture crises that only he can solve. This tactic, while politically expedient, is dangerous and divisive, undermining the fabric of local communities and sowing distrust in public institutions.

The resistance from Chicago's leaders, especially Lt. Gov. Juliana Stratton and Congresswoman Robin Kelly, highlights a broader rejection of Trump's authoritarian impulses. Their statements reflect not just a defense of Chicago's autonomy, but a defense of democratic principles themselves.

Conclusion: Beyond the Spectacle

Trump's threat to invade Chicago is emblematic of a larger, more troubling trend towards federal overreach and authoritarian governance. The opposition from Chicago's officials is not merely about protecting jurisdictional boundaries; it is a battle for the soul of American democracy, where public safety is not used as a weapon against the people it is meant to protect.

In this context, Trump's actions and rhetoric serve as a stark reminder of the importance of vigilance and resistance in the face of attempts to erode democratic norms under the guise of law and order. The real work of reducing violence and ensuring public safety lies not in the spectacle of federal intervention, but in the hard, often unseen work of community building and investment.
