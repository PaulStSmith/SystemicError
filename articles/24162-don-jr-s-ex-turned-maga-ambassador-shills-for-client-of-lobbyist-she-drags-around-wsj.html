Don Jr.'s ex turned MAGA ambassador shills for client of lobbyist she drags around: WSJ

When Public Office Becomes a Private Sales Desk

The Embassy Has the Power

Kimberly Guilfoyle is not merely a well-connected promoter. She is the U.S. ambassador to Greece, appointed by President Donald Trump and backed publicly by Secretary of State Marco Rubio. According to reporting attributed to The Wall Street Journal, she repeatedly urged Balkan governments to work with Aktor Group, a Greek construction company newly active in natural gas, while appearing alongside the company’s paid lobbyist, Christos Marafatsos.

That institutional distinction is the story. Foreign officials were not meeting private entrepreneurs on equal terms. They were receiving an American ambassador whose words could reasonably be understood as carrying the weight of the U.S. government.

A Lobbyist Wrapped in Diplomatic Authority

Federal records cited in the reporting show that Aktor paid Marafatsos $160,000 through June to cultivate relationships with the White House and the State and Energy departments. Several officials who encountered him beside Guilfoyle reportedly said he did not disclose that relationship; some mistook him for her chief of staff.

Marafatsos says he promotes initiatives that advance U.S. interests. Guilfoyle’s lawyer says her official meetings were arranged through embassy channels and that she worked with companies across the energy sector. Those defenses do not resolve the central conflict. Embassy scheduling does not neutralize a lobbyist’s private client, and invoking “U.S. interests” does not establish that one foreign company’s commercial ambitions are identical to them.

The Private Jet Clarifies the Problem

The arrangement became harder to dismiss as ordinary commercial diplomacy when, according to the Journal’s reporting, Guilfoyle traveled to Bulgaria in the spring on a private jet supplied by Aktor’s CEO and encouraged officials there to do business with the company. Aktor’s lawyers say its involvement in Bulgaria began before any contact with the ambassador. That may answer a question about chronology, but it does not answer why an American ambassador accepted company-provided transportation while advocating an outcome beneficial to that company.

One person familiar with the talks said there was “no doubt” the advocacy appeared to represent an official U.S. position. That perception was foreseeable. It is precisely what diplomatic office confers: access, status and implied government backing unavailable to an ordinary salesperson.

The Misdirection Is “Effectiveness”

Rubio called Guilfoyle an “incredibly effective advocate for the President’s agenda.” The phrase substitutes loyalty for scrutiny. It avoids the question raised by former National Security Council official Robin Brooks: Were these meetings advancing American interests or a private interest?

The available reporting does not establish an illegal bargain or prove Guilfoyle’s motive. It does establish conduct that blurred public authority and private commercial advocacy. Calling that effectiveness merely restates the administration’s preference that officials be judged by service to the president’s agenda, not by whether their use of office can withstand an elementary conflict-of-interest test.

Access Is the Commodity

Aktor paid for Washington connections. Its lobbyist then appeared so consistently beside an ambassador that foreign officials reportedly misunderstood his role. The ambassador, meanwhile, promoted the company and traveled aboard its CEO’s aircraft. Whatever the participants intended, the practical effect was to make private access look like American policy.

That is how influence works when institutions stop enforcing meaningful boundaries. The valuable product is not necessarily a written directive or explicit favor. It is proximity to state power—the ability to enter a room beside an ambassador and let everyone else draw the obvious conclusion.

Patronage Foreign Policy

This episode reveals a broader system in which personal networks, presidential loyalty and commercial influence can converge inside public office without anyone accepting responsibility for the convergence. The lobbyist describes private promotion as regional opportunity. The ambassador’s lawyer points to official channels. The secretary of state praises results. Each explanation isolates one piece of the arrangement so no one has to account for the whole.

The political failure is not confusion among Balkan officials. It is an American power structure that gave them ample reason to confuse a foreign company’s lobbyist with a representative of the United States—and then treated that confusion as someone else’s problem.
