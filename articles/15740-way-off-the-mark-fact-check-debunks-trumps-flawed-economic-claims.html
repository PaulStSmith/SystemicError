'Way off the mark': Fact-check debunks Trump’s flawed economic claims

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 23, 20255 min read

President Donald Trump's economic policies, from steep new tariffs to calling for U.S. Federal Reserve Chairman Jerome Powell to be fired, are creating a great deal of volatility in the stock market. The Dow Jones Industrial Average has plunged more than once, and Americans are increasingly worried about their 401k's and other retirement accounts.

Trump, however, insists that his policies will lead to a manufacturing renaissance in the United States and a period of tremendous prosperity.

In his April 22 column, the Washington Post's Glenn Kessler fact-checks and debunks some of Trump's claims about the economy.

Trump recently said, "On trade and other things, we're doing great. We're taking in billions and billions of dollars.… We were losing $2 billion a day.… This is the biggest deal ever made. Now we're making $3 billion a day."

But according to Kessler, Trump's figures are even more problematic than those of his trade adviser, Peter Navarro — who, Kessler notes, said "that Trump's tariffs would raise $600 billion a year, or $6 trillion over 10 years."

"We showed that Navarro appeared to have assumed that a 20 percent tariff on $3 trillion of imports would result in a bonanza — without considering the effects of people changing their buying habits when tariffs increase, countries retaliating and the subsequent impact on other revenue sources," Kessler explains. "Outside organizations, such as the Tax Foundation and the Budget Lab at Yale University, used sophisticated models to estimate that the overall tariff revenue would end up being closer to $2.4 trillion, or about $660 million a day."

Kessler continues, "But it turns out that Navarro's math, while nonsensical, is even more conservative than Trump's math. Navarro estimated a little over $1.6 billion a day. Trump almost doubles that — to $3 billion a day."

According to Kessler, there is no evidence to support Trump's claim that the U.S. was "losing $2 billion a day" under former President Joe Biden.

"Best we can tell," Kessler writes, "Trump was talking about something completely different — and equally wrong."

Kessler also pushes back against the claim that Trump's tariffs won't result in higher prices for businesses and consumers.

"As we regularly remind readers," Kessler notes, "economists agree that tariffs — essentially a tax on domestic consumption — are paid by importers, such as U.S. companies, which in turn pass on most or all of the costs to consumers or producers who may use imported materials in their products. As a matter of demand and supply elasticities, overseas producers will pay part of the tax if there are fewer goods sold to the United States. Domestic producers in effect get a subsidy because they can raise their prices to the level imposed on importers."

Kessler continues, "So Trump is wrong to suggest this money is being collected by countries. Americans will face higher prices for goods, which is why just about every economist predicts inflation will increase, not slow down as previously predicted. Put simply, this is a massive tax increase on consumers. In any case, both the Treasury Department and the data released by Customs and Border Protection show Trump is way off the mark."

Our Analysis:

Trump's Economic Fantasies: A Reality Check

In the chaotic theatre of Trumpian economics, the former President's policies—spanning from audacious tariffs to his infamous calls for the ouster of U.S. Federal Reserve Chairman Jerome Powell—have not just stirred the pot but sent it spinning off the stove. The Dow Jones Industrial Average's dramatic tumbles are symptoms of a broader disease: a concoction of volatility and uncertainty infecting the market. Despite this, Trump heralds these policies as precursors to an American manufacturing golden age and unparalleled prosperity. However, a closer examination, such as the one offered by Glenn Kessler in the Washington Post, peels back the layers of Trump's claims, revealing a core of dubious assertions and economic fantasies.

The Billion-Dollar Daydream

Trump's proclamation that his trade policies are "taking in billions and billions of dollars," transforming a supposed daily loss of "$2 billion" into a profit of "$3 billion," is a striking illustration of his penchant for economic exaggeration. Kessler's analysis exposes the chasm between Trump's assertions and the realm of mathematical reality. The disjunction is so profound that even Peter Navarro's highly criticized figures appear conservative by comparison. Navarro's arithmetic gymnastics, which somehow turn a 20 percent tariff on $3 trillion of imports into a $600 billion annual windfall, blatantly ignore the dynamic nature of global trade and consumer behavior. When subjected to rigorous examination by entities like the Tax Foundation and the Budget Lab at Yale University, the projected tariff revenue deflates to a significantly less impressive figure of approximately $2.4 trillion over a decade, or about $660 million a day—not even close to Trump's fantastical $3 billion.

A Tax by Any Other Name

The narrative that these tariffs are a financial boon conveniently omits a critical victim: the American consumer. Kessler reminds us that tariffs are, in essence, a tax on domestic consumption. This tax is not borne by abstract entities but by U.S. companies and, ultimately, consumers, who face higher prices for goods. This runs counter to Trump's implication that these costs are shouldered by foreign countries. The reality is starkly different: it's a tax hike on the American public, cloaked in the guise of patriotic trade policy.

A Misdiagnosis of Economic Ailments

Trump's claims of reversing a daily $2 billion loss under the Biden administration are not just unfounded, they're indicative of a troubling trend to misrepresent and manipulate economic facts for political gain. This distortion of economic principles and data does not merely mislead—it has real consequences for policy and public perception.

The Cost of Fantasy

Kessler's critique is more than an academic exercise; it's a clarion call to recognize the tangible impact of these policies on everyday Americans. As the market wobbles under the weight of uncertainty and consumers brace for higher prices, the disparity between Trump's economic fantasies and Americans' economic realities has never been starker.

In sum, Trump's economic policies, wrapped in the flag of American prosperity, instead weave a tapestry of misinformation and misguided strategies. As we navigate the fallout, it becomes increasingly clear that the path to genuine economic strength lies not in fanciful rhetoric but in policies grounded in reality and beneficial to the broad swath of American society.
