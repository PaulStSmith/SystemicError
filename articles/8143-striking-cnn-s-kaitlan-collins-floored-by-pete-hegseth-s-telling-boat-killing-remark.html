'Striking': CNN's Kaitlan Collins floored by Pete Hegseth's 'telling' boat killing remark

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 3, 20255 min read

Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth speaks during a cabinet meeting with U.S. President Donald Trump, at the White House in Washington, D.C. on Dec. 2, 2025. REUTERS/Brian Snyder

CNN anchor and chief White House correspondent Kaitlan Collins was taken aback Tuesday by Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth‘s “telling” remark on his boat strikes during a cabinet meeting with President Donald Trump.

“I think what was most striking to me is what Jim [Sciutto] was just referencing there, which was the comment by Secretary Hegseth when he was directly asked if he was aware that there were survivors after that first initial strike happened during this boat strike that happened on Sept. 2, the one that has generated so much controversy here in Washington and even has Republicans on Capitol Hill now demanding answers about how this went down,” Collins said.

“And I thought what was really telling there was he was asked about a comment that Secretary Hegseth had made in the aftermath of this strike,” she added.

“Remember, at that time, the administration had released about 30 seconds of a video that the president had posted of this strike. That is something that did not show the second subsequent strike that happened there,” she said.

“And Secretary Hegseth was asked, given he said at the time that he watched this strike happen live from obviously his situation [room] at the Pentagon. If he had seen that second strike actually take place. And I want you to listen to what the Secretary had to say about the timeline here, because I think this is critical information and is also something that we have not heard yet from the Defense Department,” Collins added.

Hegseth had criticized the press asking him questions during the cabinet meeting, and also revealed something unknown — that he wasn’t present when the second strike was ordered.

“So I said ‘I’m going to be the one to make the call’ after getting all the information and making sure it’s the right strike,” Hegseth said. “That was Sept. 2.... I watched that first strike live. As you can imagine, at the Department of War, we’ve got a lot of things to do, so I didn’t stick around for the hour and two hours, whatever, where all the sensitive site exploitation digitally occurs. So I moved on to my meeting.”

He also said that he did not see any survivors during the second strike, and put the “onus back on Admiral Bradley,” she added.

“I did not personally see survivors, but I stand... because the thing was on fire. It was exploded. Fire and smoke. You can’t see anything, you’ve got digital... This is called the fog of war. This is what you in the press don’t understand,” Hegseth said, criticizing the reporters and press.

Collins said this new information could prompt more questions from Congress.

Our Analysis:

Dissecting Hegseth’s Remarks: A Closer Look

The art of evasion is a skill well-honed by politicians and government officials, but Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s recent comments during a cabinet meeting with President Donald Trump are a masterclass in obfuscation and deflection. CNN anchor and chief White House correspondent Kaitlan Collins provided a crucial recap of Hegseth’s remarks, which deserve a thorough analysis for their strategic ambiguity and the profound implications they carry.

The Fog of Accountability

Hegseth’s remarks about a controversial boat strike on September 2, which has sparked bipartisan concern in Washington, are emblematic of a troubling trend in political communication. When asked about his awareness of survivors after the initial strike, Hegseth’s response was less about clarity and more about casting a veil over the events that unfolded. It’s telling that even as Republicans on Capitol Hill demand answers, the narrative remains as murky as ever.

Selective Visibility

Hegseth’s admission that he “watched that first strike live” but wasn’t present for the second strike’s ordering raises significant questions about accountability and oversight. It’s convenient, to say the least, that his involvement seemingly ends just when things get complicated. His claim of not seeing any survivors during the second strike due to the “fire and smoke” and then putting the “onus back on Admiral Bradley” is a classic maneuver to divert responsibility. Hegseth’s reference to the “fog of war” as a justification for his lack of visibility on the matter is a cynical use of military terminology to obscure the lack of transparency and accountability.

The Critique of the Press

Hegseth’s criticism of the press for their lack of understanding of the “fog of war” is a red herring, detracting from the core issue at hand: the need for accountability and transparency in military operations. The role of the press is to question, to hold power to account, and to shed light where there is darkness. Hegseth’s disparagement of these efforts only adds to the growing concern over what is being hidden from public view.

Congressional Scrutiny Ahead

Collins correctly points out that this new information “could prompt more questions from Congress.” One can only hope that this scrutiny will bring about a greater level of transparency and accountability. The American public, and indeed the global community, deserve clear answers about the conduct and consequences of military operations carried out in their name.

In Conclusion, Hegseth’s comments reveal more through what they conceal than what they clarify. As the situation unfolds, it is imperative that the press and Congress continue to press for the truth. The “fog of war” cannot become a catch-all excuse to sidestep accountability. In the quest for clarity and responsibility, evasion is the enemy of truth.
