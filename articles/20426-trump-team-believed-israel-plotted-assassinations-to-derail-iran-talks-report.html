Trump team believed Israel plotted assassinations to derail Iran talks: report

Based on an article by Jake Johnson, Common Dreams

Paulo SantosJuly 4, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A Negotiation Under FireWho Had PowerThe Decision to SabotageMisdirection as PolicyNetanyahu’s IncentivesThe Pattern

Peace Was the Target, and Israel Took Aim

A Negotiation Under Fire

The reporting here is not about a mysterious breakdown in diplomacy. It is about an allied state allegedly considering assassination of the very Iranian officials involved in ceasefire talks with the United States. That is not confusion, and it is not an accident of escalation. It is an attempt to destroy the negotiating channel itself.

Who Had Power

The institutional power in this story sits with the Israeli government, led by Benjamin Netanyahu, and with the U.S. administration that kept operating inside a war it helped wage. The Times says U.S. officials believed Israel intended to target Abbas Araghchi and Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf even as delicate talks were underway, and Washington reportedly had to warn other countries in the region about the danger. That is a grim picture of leverage: the U.S. trying to preserve diplomacy while its partner reportedly works to nullify it with force.

The Decision to Sabotage

The key decision is the alleged Israeli choice to keep the negotiators in the crosshairs even after Araghchi and Ghalibaf were removed from the target list in late March. That detail matters because it shows intent. Once the possibility of high-level negotiations opened, the response was not restraint. The response, according to the reporting, was continued pressure on the people those talks required.

Misdirection as Policy

The usual media habit is to treat this kind of episode as a flare-up, a complication, or a dangerous misunderstanding between allies. That framing is too weak. If a government is trying to eliminate the counterpart’s negotiators during ceasefire talks, the issue is not miscommunication. It is political sabotage. The burden of instability does not belong equally to both sides when one side is reportedly trying to assassinate the diplomats.

Netanyahu’s Incentives

The reporting also points to motive, and it is not hard to read. Netanyahu is described here as having strategic and personal reasons to keep war alive: preserving Israeli dominance, weakening Iran, and protecting his own political survival. The article notes his weaker reelection prospects and his corruption case. That is the real architecture behind the violence: not national security, but a leader with shrinking room for maneuver who may need conflict more than peace.

The Pattern

This story is part of a familiar authoritarian pattern: attack diplomacy, criminalize compromise, and use permanent war to protect political power at home. Israel’s leadership is not being portrayed here as accidentally stumbling toward catastrophe. It is being described as actively hostile to any settlement that limits domination. The U.S., for its part, is left managing the fallout from a war machine it still treats as an ally. That is not partnership. It is strategic dependency on a government willing to sabotage peace when peace threatens its control.
