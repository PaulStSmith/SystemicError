Trump admin's last-minute election scheme shredded in new lawsuit: 'So desperate'

Based on an article by Matthew Chapman

Paulo SantosOctober 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

The Democratic National Committee sued the Trump administration after the Pentagon removed an option from the Federal Post Card Application used by Americans abroad to register and request absentee ballots. RawStory, citing NPR and The Guardian, reports that the deleted checkbox covered U.S. citizens born overseas who have never lived in the United States—a group permitted to vote in at least some elections by 38 states and Washington, D.C.

The Pentagon sought emergency approval for the change in August as the overseas-ballot deadline approached, and the Office of Management and Budget approved it almost immediately. Pentagon official Sean O’Keefe argued that the former wording could confuse voters about federal requirements and that the election calendar precluded ordinary review. The DNC counters that the government had handled this category of voters for at least 15 years and had no legitimate reason to bypass its usual process.

Federal law did not change, according to U.S. Vote Foundation president Susan Dzieduszycka-Suinat. The danger is practical: eligible citizens may no longer recognize themselves on the form and may abandon the process. The lawsuit was pending when the story was published, and the reporting does not establish how many voters were affected.

The power was federal

This was not a dispute created by overseas voters. The Pentagon controlled the form, OMB controlled its approval, and administration officials chose to revise it through an emergency procedure near an election deadline.

That distinction matters because bureaucratic language can disguise political agency. A checkbox did not disappear by accident. Federal officials requested its removal, approved that request, and placed the burden of deciphering the revised form on citizens with less institutional power and little time to respond.

Confusion as a governing instrument

O’Keefe’s stated justification was voter confusion. But the reported change removed the language that allowed a specific class of eligible voters to identify itself. Dzieduszycka-Suinat’s warning exposes the contradiction: the revision did not alter eligibility; it made eligibility harder to recognize.

The DNC alleges that the administration’s explanation is hollow because the government possessed years of experience administering the same issue. That allegation remains for the court to assess. Still, the sequence described in the reporting—a familiar form, an emergency request, immediate approval and no change in underlying law—supports scrutiny of who benefits when official clarity is suddenly treated as a defect.

A burden aimed downward

The entire population of Americans abroad is estimated at 2.2 million eligible voters, but only about 11 percent voted in 2024. The subset born abroad who never lived stateside is much smaller and uncertain: a 2016 government estimate placed it near 11,590, while DNC lawyers say it has since grown.

That uncertainty should limit claims about electoral impact. It does not diminish the power imbalance. The administration could change the federal form in days; an individual voter abroad could only confront the revised wording and decide whether to proceed. Administrative friction is most effective against people already navigating distance, deadlines and unfamiliar rules.

The framing should name the decision

Calling this merely a “scheme” risks substituting heat for precision. The stronger indictment lies in the documented machinery: executive officials used emergency review to remove a longstanding voter category shortly before ballots had to be distributed.

Nor should the story be reduced to partisan accusations about desperation. Ken Martin’s statement is the DNC chair’s characterization, not proof of motive. The substantiated issue is narrower and more consequential: officials exercised institutional authority in a way that could deter eligible voters without changing the law that protects their eligibility.

Disenfranchisement by interface

Modern vote restriction does not always arrive as an explicit ban. It can operate through forms, deadlines, compliance rules and procedural shortcuts that preserve a right on paper while making it harder to exercise in practice.

That is the larger pattern exposed here. Power sits with the officials who design the gateway; risk is transferred to the citizen trying to pass through it. When government removes the words voters need to recognize their own eligibility, “confusion” is not an adequate description of the consequence. It is the mechanism.
