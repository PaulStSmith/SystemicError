Marriage, Markets, and the Myth of the Perfect Partner

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 28, 20267 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Lie Behind Traditional NostalgiaThe Missing Conversation About WomenThe 90-10 TrapRelationships Under CapitalismThe Invisible Value of StabilityCivilization’s Real Crisis

There is a certain genre of modern commentary that treats the decline of marriage as if civilization simply woke up one morning and collectively forgot how to commit.

According to this narrative, the culprit is usually one of three things:

  • feminism,

  • the decline of traditional masculinity,

  • or modern sexual permissiveness.

Recently, another such article floated across the internet lamenting the decline of “marriageable men.” The piece framed the problem largely as a cultural and moral collapse. Men, it argued, have lost the institutions and expectations that once pushed them toward maturity and commitment. Modern society, skeptical of masculinity and addicted to permissive sexual norms, has supposedly eroded the incentives for marriage.

The article was deeply nostalgic for the “Daddy Knows Best” family model of the 1940s and 1950s.

What it conveniently ignored was the gigantic economic machinery that made that model possible in the first place.

This is the recurring flaw in modern relationship discourse: people want the aesthetics of the past without the infrastructure that supported it.

They want the stable nuclear family without affordable housing.

They want the traditional provider without stable middle-class wages.

They want male leadership without male economic viability.

They want long-term commitment in an economy specifically engineered to produce instability.

And layered on top of that structural instability is another modern poison: the endless search for the missing 10%.

The internet loves the so-called “90-10 rule” — the idea that someone leaves a partner who fulfills 90% of their needs in pursuit of the missing 10%, only to regret it later.

Like many oversimplified ideas, it contains a kernel of truth wrapped in a mountain of distortion.

Because the reality is that modern relationships are collapsing under pressure from two directions at once:

  • economic destabilization,

  • and impossible emotional expectations.

These forces are not mutually exclusive.

They reinforce each other.

The Lie Behind Traditional Nostalgia

One of the most dishonest aspects of modern conservative relationship commentary is the way it romanticizes the postwar family structure as though it emerged purely from superior values.

It did not.

The “traditional” household of the 1940s and 1950s rested atop extraordinary historical circumstances:

  • massive industrial expansion after World War II,

  • strong unions,

  • rising real wages,

  • cheap housing,

  • expanding infrastructure,

  • affordable education,

  • pensions,

  • and unprecedented economic mobility.

A factory worker could realistically buy a home, support children, and sustain a family on a single income.

That economic foundation mattered.

It mattered enormously.

But modern nostalgia conveniently strips away the material conditions and leaves behind only the authority structure.

What some commentators appear to want is not truly the 1950s.

They want a fantasy version of the 1950s:

  • the obedient wife,

  • the respected husband,

  • the stable family,

  • the social cohesion,

  • the clear gender roles.

But they want all of this while preserving the economic order of the 2020s:

  • gig work,

  • stagnant wages,

  • permanent debt,

  • housing speculation,

  • healthcare insecurity,

  • algorithmic labor,

  • and hyper-individualistic consumer culture.

In other words, they want patriarchal expectations on a late-stage capitalist budget.

That contradiction is impossible to sustain.

The traditional masculine ideal — provider, protector, leader — depended on men actually having a viable path toward becoming providers.

Destroy the economic ladder, and the cultural role eventually collapses with it.

Then society blames the men standing in the rubble.

The Missing Conversation About Women

There is another layer to this nostalgia that often goes unspoken.

Many women in earlier generations remained in marriages not because those marriages were deeply fulfilling, but because leaving was economically and socially catastrophic.

Divorce carried an enormous stigma.

Financial independence for women was heavily restricted.

Domestic abuse was often hidden or normalized.

Marital rape was not legally recognized in many places.

Social pressure was relentless.

When modern commentators point nostalgically toward the “stable family” of the past, they often ignore how much suffering was quietly buried underneath that stability.

This does not mean modern society has solved these problems.

Far from it.

Modern dating culture has introduced entirely new forms of dysfunction:

  • isolation,

  • atomization,

  • disposable relationships,

  • algorithmic validation,

  • pornography addiction,

  • social media narcissism,

  • and the commodification of intimacy.

But reverting to a romanticized patriarchal structure is not a serious solution.

It is historical cosplay.

The social and economic conditions that supported that world no longer exist.

And many of the people longing for that past would likely hate the actual realities of living in it.

The 90-10 Trap

At the same time, modern relationship culture suffers from a very real psychological problem.

People increasingly undervalue stability because stability becomes invisible.

This is where the “90-10” idea resonates.

A person may have:

  • loyalty,

  • emotional support,

  • trust,

  • reliability,

  • shared history,

  • financial cooperation,

  • companionship,

  • and genuine care.

But the missing 10% begins to dominate their attention.

Perhaps the relationship lacks excitement.

Perhaps physical attraction faded.

Perhaps one partner feels emotionally disconnected.

Perhaps they encounter someone new who appears to embody that missing piece.

And because modern culture relentlessly markets perfection, the temptation becomes overwhelming.

The internet amplifies this endlessly.

Dating apps create the illusion of infinite alternatives.

Social media showcases carefully curated fantasy relationships.

Pornography rewires expectations around intimacy.

Algorithms constantly whisper:

“You could do better.”

The result is a civilization increasingly incapable of appreciating imperfect stability.

But the internet often twists this phenomenon into simplistic gender warfare.

Certain online communities portray women as uniquely hypergamous and perpetually dissatisfied.

That framing quickly devolves into resentment-driven pseudoscience.

The truth is far less flattering to everyone.

Men also abandon stable relationships, chasing fantasy.

Men also destroy marriages for novelty, ego, validation, or the illusion of recaptured youth.

Human beings are simply very good at adapting to what they already possess and obsessing over what they lack.

The problem is not female nature.

The problem is a marketplace culture that trains everyone to optimize endlessly.

Relationships Under Capitalism

Modern relationships increasingly resemble consumer products.

People are encouraged to evaluate partners the same way they evaluate smartphones:

  • compare features,

  • identify flaws,

  • search for upgrades,

  • maximize personal satisfaction.

The language itself reveals the shift.

People speak of:

  • “high-value” partners,

  • “settling,”

  • “marketplace value,”

  • “upgrading,”

  • “options.”

Human beings are reduced to optimized lifestyle accessories.

Under this framework, commitment becomes irrational.

Why fully invest in someone imperfect when the next swipe might reveal someone marginally better?

The tragedy is that this mindset destroys the very conditions required for lasting intimacy.

Long-term relationships are not built through perpetual optimization.

They are built through accumulated trust, resilience, forgiveness, compromise, and shared hardship.

But those traits emerge slowly.

Consumer culture hates slowness.

It hates patience.

It hates permanence.

Late-stage capitalism wants human beings permanently dissatisfied because dissatisfaction drives consumption.

A stable, content, emotionally grounded population is terrible for engagement metrics.

So modern society simultaneously:

  • raises expectations for relationships to impossible levels,
    while

  • systematically destroying the economic and psychological stability required to sustain them.

People now expect one partner to provide:

  • romance,

  • emotional support,

  • intellectual stimulation,

  • sexual fulfillment,

  • financial stability,

  • validation,

  • personal growth,

  • excitement,

  • friendship,

  • and lifelong meaning.

That is an astonishing burden to place on two exhausted primates trying to survive modern life.

The Invisible Value of Stability

One reason the “90-10” phenomenon resonates is because people often fail to recognize the value of stability until it disappears.

The emotional infrastructure of a long-term relationship becomes normalized:

  • trust,

  • reliability,

  • emotional shorthand,

  • mutual care,

  • shared memories,

  • practical cooperation.

These things stop feeling exciting precisely because they are stable.

Then, after a breakup or divorce, people suddenly discover how much invisible labor that relationship had been performing.

The silence feels louder.

The uncertainty feels heavier.

The emotional safety feels irreplaceable.

This does not mean people should remain trapped in genuinely unhealthy relationships.

Sometimes the missing “10%” is actually fundamental.

A relationship can appear successful externally while lacking:

  • respect,

  • affection,

  • intimacy,

  • attraction,

  • or emotional safety.

At that point, staying together becomes emotional inertia rather than maturity.

But modern discourse increasingly struggles to distinguish between:

  • genuine incompatibility,
    and

  • the ordinary imperfections of long-term human relationships.

That distinction matters.

Because no human being can compete with an idealized fantasy assembled from social media, pornography, nostalgia, and algorithmic comparison.

Civilization’s Real Crisis

The decline of marriage is not simply a moral crisis.

Nor is it solely an economic crisis.

It is a crisis of social architecture.

People commit when the future feels believable.

Earlier generations, despite their many flaws and injustices, often possessed a coherent social contract:

“Work hard and you can build a stable life.”

That promise has been steadily dismantled.

Today, the message feels more like:

“Compete endlessly. Optimize constantly. Rent forever. Stay productive. Stay desirable. Stay marketable.”

Under those conditions, relationships begin to resemble temporary alliances between economically anxious individuals.

And then society acts surprised when commitment weakens.

The answer is not to resurrect the mythology of the 1950s.

Nor is it to pretend modern hyper-individualism is healthy.

The answer requires rebuilding something modern civilization increasingly lacks:

  • economic stability,

  • community,

  • institutional trust,

  • realistic expectations,

  • and a culture capable of valuing imperfect but meaningful human connection.

Without that foundation, people will continue oscillating between two equally broken fantasies:

  • nostalgic traditionalism,

  • and endless consumerist individualism.

Neither can sustain human beings for very long.

And somewhere in between those extremes lies the uncomfortable truth:

People do not merely need freedom.

They need a future worth committing to.
