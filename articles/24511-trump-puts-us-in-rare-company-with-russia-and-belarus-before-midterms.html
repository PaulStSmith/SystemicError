Trump puts US in rare company with Russia and Belarus before midterms

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 7, 20263 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that the Trump administration did not invite observers from the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe to monitor the November 3 midterm elections. If that remains unchanged, this would be the first U.S. federal election since 2002—and the first of seven midterm elections during that period—without OSCE observers.

The State Department said the OSCE should devote its limited resources to elections where it could have greater impact. The OSCE called the decision “regrettable” and inconsistent with commitments made by participating countries under the 1990 Copenhagen Document. Human Rights Watch noted that Russia and Belarus have also recently denied OSCE observers access to national elections.

At the same time, the Justice Department said it planned to deploy as many as 1,000 federal monitors to polling places, which AlterNet describes as a record. A UC San Diego survey conducted the month before the report found that 49 percent of respondents expected the midterms to be free and fair, while 23 percent did not and 28 percent were unsure.

The administration made the choice

The relevant institutional power rests with the Trump administration. International observers cannot invite themselves, and election administrators did not collectively abandon the practice. The administration withheld the invitation.

The State Department’s resource argument does not explain why the United States should decide where the OSCE’s resources matter most. Nor does it address the OSCE’s claim that participating states committed themselves to election observation. It substitutes bureaucratic language for a defense of the actual decision: excluding an independent outside assessment after 24 years of accepting one.

Scrutiny on government terms

The contrast with the Justice Department’s proposed operation is the story’s central power dynamic. The administration is rejecting outside monitors while preparing an unprecedented deployment of its own federal monitors.

Those groups do not perform identical functions. OSCE observers assess the electoral process independently; Justice Department personnel enforce federal law under the authority of the executive branch. Presenting the second as a practical replacement for the first would erase the crucial question of control. One provides scrutiny the administration does not direct. The other exercises power through an institution the administration leads.

Distrust is not the cause

The polling numbers describe public uncertainty; they do not explain it. Treating voter distrust as the main political problem would shift attention from officials making consequential choices to citizens reacting to them.

Sen. Jeanne Shaheen said the refusal risks undermining confidence in the election system. That is plausible, but “confidence” is too soft a frame by itself. Independent observation is not merely reassurance for nervous voters. It creates a record produced outside the government conducting the election. Removing that layer matters whether public opinion rises, falls, or remains unchanged.

The comparison has limits—and meaning

The United States has not become Russia or Belarus merely because all three governments declined OSCE observation. AlterNet’s comparison establishes a shared act, not identical electoral systems or conditions.

But the shared act is politically revealing. Human Rights Watch’s Philippe Bolopion pointed out that the U.S. government has criticized Russia and other governments for avoiding external scrutiny. An administration cannot credibly present election observation as essential abroad and dispensable at home without exposing the principle as conditional.

Accountability without permission

The larger pattern is institutional power narrowing the forms of oversight it cannot control while expanding the machinery it can. The reporting does not establish why the administration chose that arrangement, and it does not prove planned election misconduct. It establishes something more precise: officials rejected an established channel of independent scrutiny and offered a rationale that never confronted the loss.

That is how accountability is weakened without being formally abolished. No one needs to ban observation everywhere. Those in power can simply decide that outside examination is unnecessary here, this time, for reasons they define themselves.
