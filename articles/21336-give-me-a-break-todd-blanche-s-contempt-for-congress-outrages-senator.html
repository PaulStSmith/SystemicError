‘Give me a break!’ Todd Blanche's ‘contempt’ for Congress outrages senator

The 1776 Slush Fund Was Not Subtle

The Setup

Sen. Dick Durbin confronted Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche as the Senate Judiciary Committee moved to advance Blanche’s nomination. The dispute centered on Trump’s $1.776 billion “anti-weaponization fund,” a taxpayer-funded settlement meant to pay Jan. 6 rioters, and on Blanche’s refusal to acknowledge why that amount was chosen. Durbin’s point was simple: the number was not accidental, and the administration’s posture was not confused.

The Number Was the Message

This was not ordinary bureaucratic accounting with a patriotic accident embedded in it. The sum was selected to invoke 1776, and that symbolism does political work. It tells the base that rioters who attacked the Capitol are being recast as heirs to the revolution. That is not neutral governance. It is partisan mythmaking with public money.

The important fact is not merely that the fund existed. It is that the federal government, under Trump’s direction, was turned toward rewarding people who tried to overturn democratic process and then dressing that reward in revolutionary costume.

Power Sits With the Enablers

The people with actual institutional power here are not the rioters and not the senators objecting after the fact. It is the Trump apparatus that created the settlement, and the Justice Department leadership that refuses to own what it did. Blanche’s role matters because he is not some outside commentator. He is the official being asked to permanently occupy a top law-enforcement post while declining to answer for a political payout to insurrectionists.

That is the structure: executive power creates the damage, and confirmation politics is forced to normalize it.

Refusal Is Not Confusion

Blanche did not stumble into silence. He refused to answer, and that refusal should be read as a tactic, not a misunderstanding. When confronted with the obvious symbolism of the $1.776 billion figure, he treated the committee as a venue for evasion. Durbin called that contempt, and he was right.

This is how authoritarian politics survives institutional scrutiny: not by defending the act plainly, but by burying it in procedural fog, false innocence, and performative non-responsiveness. The point is to exhaust the room until cruelty starts to look like a formatting issue.

Misdirection as Policy

The real misdirection here is the attempt to make the story about whether Blanche can be pinned down on a symbolic number, instead of the more serious fact that a Trump administration used federal authority to validate Jan. 6 offenders. The scandal is not the lack of candor alone. It is the underlying decision to treat people who invaded the Capitol as beneficiaries of state favor.

That should not be softened into a debate about messaging or optics. The messaging is the mechanism. It turns state power into political consolation for anti-democratic violence.

The Larger Pattern

This is the governing pattern of the Trump era and its hangers-on: take an assault on democracy, rebrand it as grievance, and then use institutions to launder the result. The law is invoked only when it can be bent into cover. Accountability is treated as disrespect. And the people charged with enforcing public trust act as if their job is to protect the lie, not the republic.

Durbin’s outrage mattered because it named the thing plainly. The administration chose symbolism that honored Jan. 6 offenders. Blanche chose evasion. That is not a misunderstanding to be managed. It is a political project that treats democratic violence as something to be subsidized, sanitized, and normalized.
