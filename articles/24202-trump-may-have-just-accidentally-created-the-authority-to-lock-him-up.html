Trump may have just accidentally created the authority to 'lock him up'

Based on an article by Sabrina Haake

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that veterans are protesting a proposed 250-foot triumphal arch near Arlington Memorial Circle because it would obstruct the historic sightline connecting the Lincoln Memorial, Arlington Memorial Bridge, and Arlington National Cemetery. Vietnam veterans have also sued, arguing that the project lacks congressional authorization and would damage historically significant views.

According to the outlet, a federal panel approved the plans in May, the National Park Service later found they would adversely affect several historic sites, and the Federal Aviation Administration subsequently approved them despite aviation-safety concerns. A federal judge then ordered the administration to provide at least 48 hours’ notice before undertaking project activity while the lawsuit proceeds.

Trump later said he had agreed, at the military’s “strong request,” to turn the proposed monument into a combined military complex and triumphal arch containing drones, snipers, and ammunition. The reporting provides no independent support for that account. Nor did Trump accidentally create present authority to imprison himself: the headline refers to the author’s hypothetical that an expansive judicial ruling for presidential power could let a future president construct a display prison around Trump under a purported national-security rationale.

Power built in stone

The veterans in Memorial Circle have moral authority, personal standing in the ordinary sense, and banners. Trump controls the executive branch agencies processing the project. His appointees sit on the federal panel that AlterNet says approved it. His FAA supplied another approval. The National Park Service identified adverse effects, but an agency finding is not the same thing as power to stop the president.

That is the actual hierarchy. Protesters can object, experts can warn, and preservationists can sue. The administration can move the project through institutions it controls unless Congress or the courts impose a binding limit.

The arch is therefore not merely an argument about taste. It is a test of whether public land, historic design, and national memorial space remain governed by law—or become raw material for presidential self-mythology.

A rationale arrives on schedule

The sequence reported by AlterNet matters. The arch was presented and reviewed before Trump publicly supplied the military-complex explanation. He raised that rationale only after litigation produced a notice requirement and after another dispute had demonstrated the legal usefulness of invoking national security.

That timing does not, by itself, prove the justification was fabricated. It does make scrutiny mandatory. A genuine security project should ordinarily produce a documented operational need, responsible agency, site analysis, threat assessment, and explanation of why drones, snipers, and ammunition must be housed inside a monumental arch beside Arlington. The account supplies none of that evidence.

“National security” cannot function as a verbal trapdoor through which an ornamental presidential ambition escapes ordinary review. Deference is not evidence, and a president’s assertion is not a substitute for an administrative record.

The standing shell game

The deeper institutional danger described here lies in standing doctrine. AlterNet says the Supreme Court allowed Trump’s ballroom project to proceed after concluding that the National Trust for Historic Preservation had not shown the kind of injury required to sue, even though Chief Justice John Roberts wrote that the project was likely unlawful.

That creates an accountability vacuum: an action may violate the law, yet the institution created to protect historic places may be denied a hearing because courts classify its injury as insufficient. The executive does not need to prove legality if judges prevent challengers from reaching the merits.

This is not presidential authority created through legislation. It is practical impunity produced through judicial gatekeeping. The court can preserve the appearance of legal limits while making those limits exceptionally difficult to enforce.

The protesters are not the obstruction

Coverage of a dispute like this can easily reduce it to aggrieved veterans versus Trump’s architectural preference. That framing would misplace the burden. The veterans did not choose the site, approve the plans, disregard the Park Service’s adverse-effects finding, or introduce an unsupported national-security explanation after litigation began.

The consequential decisions belong to the president and the officials empowered to approve, review, or restrain the project. The protesters are responding to state action; they are not co-authors of the conflict.

Their objection is also concrete. The reported design would interrupt a deliberately aligned memorial landscape, while an observation deck could place tourists above active funerals. Treating those consequences as mere offense or distaste strips physical changes of their political meaning.

The reusable weapon

The prison-cell fantasy is not law, analysis, or an authority Trump has “accidentally created.” It is a polemical warning about precedent—and a sloppy one if read literally. A future president could not lawfully imprison a person by building a monument around him; detention requires its own legal authority and process.

The stronger warning needs no cartoon ending. When executive assertion, agency compliance, and restrictive standing doctrine converge, public assets can be transformed before anyone obtains a ruling on whether the transformation was legal. Each successful evasion becomes a template for the next administration.

That is the systemic pattern: power expands less often through a single declaration than through accumulated permissions—an approval here, a courthouse door closed there, and a belated invocation of security treated as though saying it were the same as proving it.
