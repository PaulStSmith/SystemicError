Trump ​will try 'self-pardon' but he can still be held accountable: ex-federal prosecutor

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosJuly 15, 20263 min read

Trumpism Is Not Corruption. It Is Extraction.

Former federal prosecutor James D. Zirin’s point is simple: Donald Trump and his circle are using the machinery of government to enrich themselves, and the usual political remedies are too weak to stop it. Trump is bringing in billions, much of it from crypto while his administration weakens the industry’s main regulator. His sons, son-in-law, and political allies are tied to deals, financing, and regulatory changes that read less like governance than like sanctioned self-dealing.

Power Sits at the Top

The first question is always who has the leverage. Here, it is Trump. He controls the executive branch, benefits from the presidency’s exemption from federal conflict-of-interest laws, and can use pardon power to shield allies and family on the way out. That is not a side effect of the system. That is the system, as currently abused.

The article is right to frame this as potentially illegal, but the deeper point is structural: when the presidency becomes a platform for private enrichment, law trails power instead of constraining it. Trump does not need to hide the corruption in the old-fashioned sense. He can do it in public, from inside the office.

The Government Is the Business Model

This is not a story about a few bad actors freelancing around the edges. It is about policy being bent to produce profit. The administration’s weakening of crypto oversight lines up neatly with Trump’s own gains from the sector. The effort to eliminate restrictions on guns by mail would enrich Don Jr. through his stake in an online gun store. The Kazakhstan tungsten deal involving Trump’s sons, with up to $1.6 billion in federal financing, puts the state itself in service of the family balance sheet.

That is the governing pattern. The office sets the conditions, then the family and orbit cash in. The corruption is not merely adjacent to policy. It is the policy rationale.

Pardons Are a Shield, Not a Solution

Zirin is candid about the limits of conventional accountability. Impeachment is structurally unavailable if Republicans hold enough Senate seats to block conviction. Criminal prosecution after Trump leaves office is even less reliable, especially if he can pardon allies or try to pardon himself. Whether a self-pardon survives is a legal fight, but the real issue is political: Trumpism depends on treating law as optional for insiders and harsh for everyone else.

That is why pardon power matters so much. It is not just clemency. It is a loyalty coupon for criminality. It tells subordinates they can participate in abuse now and collect immunity later.

Civil Suits Are Not Symbolic

The article’s strongest argument is also its least glamorous: civil actions may be the only practical path to recover money and impose consequences. That is not justice in the full moral sense. It is not a democratic reset. It is not a clean purge of the damage. But it is one of the few tools left that Trump cannot simply cancel with a signature or bury inside a loyalist Justice Department.

That should be read as an indictment of the political system, not as reassurance. If the best available remedy for presidential looting is a stack of lawsuits after the fact, the institutional guardrails have already failed. Still, financial recovery matters. Taking back ill-gotten gains is not cosmetic. It is one way to make corruption expensive.

What This Story Really Shows

The article is accurate, but its most important implication is even uglier than its framing allows: this is a regime of normalized predation. Trump has turned public office into a private revenue stream, and the people around him are not confused bystanders. They are participants, beneficiaries, and enablers.

The larger pattern is familiar. Authoritarian politics does not only seek obedience; it creates permission structures for theft. It weakens regulators, captures institutions, launders greed through patriotic language, and counts on procedural limits to protect the people doing the looting. If Democrats want to meet that reality, they have to treat corruption not as a campaign talking point but as a central democratic crisis: the state is being stripped for parts, and the bill is being pushed onto everyone else.
