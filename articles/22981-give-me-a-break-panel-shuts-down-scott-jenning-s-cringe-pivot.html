'Give me a break': Panel shuts down Scott Jenning's cringe pivot

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosSeptember 3, 20262 min read

Republicans Can’t Fund a Senate Race, So They Blame Democrats

The Story They’re Trying to Sell

A CNN panel on Texas became a small display of Republican damage control. Scott Jennings, sounding less like an analyst than a press shield for a sinking party, tried to move attention away from Ken Paxton’s weak candidacy, fundraising trouble, and polls showing James Talarico ahead or essentially tied. When that failed, he did the familiar thing: he reached for Democratic infighting and a contrived story about Jasmine Crockett supposedly hurting Talarico.

Who Actually Holds Power

The power in this story sits with Republicans. They control the national party machinery, the donor network, the White House ecosystem Jennings is angling toward, and the candidate selection that produced Paxton as the face of their Texas Senate push. If a supposedly favorable state is not “going gangbusters” for them, that is not a communications puzzle. It is a political failure created by the people who made the bet, chose the messenger, and are now trying to escape the bill.

The Usual Trick: Reassign the Blame

Jennings’s move is textbook misdirection. Instead of accounting for a bad candidate and a weak operation, he pivoted to internal Democratic tension, as if a few loud comments on the left explain why Republicans cannot raise money or close the gap. That is not analysis. It is blame laundering. The point is to turn Republican incompetence into a story about Democratic disorder, because that is always easier than admitting the right wing’s own bench is taking on water.

The Real Problem Is Not Confusion

Nothing in the exchange suggests mystery. The source itself gives the structure away: polls are bad for Republicans, donor enthusiasm is not converting into money, and attacks on Talarico are not landing. That is not an enigma. It is the result of a party that keeps mistaking agitation for competence and media performance for political strength. Jennings’s discomfort is useful here because it exposes the limit of the performance. Once the numbers stop cooperating, the talking point becomes naked panic.

The Pattern Beneath Texas

This is how authoritarian politics and its adjacent media class operate when the message starts failing. They do not correct course. They search for a weaker target, exaggerate internal friction, and pretend the problem is everyone else’s lack of discipline. The technique depends on Democratic self-criticism being turned into self-sabotage while Republican failures get packaged as mere turbulence. That is the deeper lesson here: when the right cannot win on governance, money, or message, it falls back on misdirection, and it expects the press to help carry the load.

The Larger Lesson

The Texas segment is not really about Texas. It is about a political movement that keeps handing power to weak candidates, then demanding that everyone else explain the collapse. The strategy is simple: lose upward, blame sideways, and keep the donor class calm. But the facts in this story are stubborn. Republicans are the ones with the power, the decision-making, and the responsibility. They own the candidate. They own the mess.
