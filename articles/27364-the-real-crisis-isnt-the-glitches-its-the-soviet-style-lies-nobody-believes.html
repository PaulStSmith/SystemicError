The real crisis isn’t the glitches —it’s the Soviet-style lies nobody believes

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosNovember 20, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A cascade of failures across the systems Americans rely on—from air travel and banking to email and classified communications—has produced no public proof of a coordinated attack. The deeper crisis, as AlterNet argues, is that the Trump administration has exhausted the credibility required to reassure anyone that these were isolated technical problems. When officials with a record of falsehood and concealment say “glitch,” even members of the president’s own party hear something else.

The reported sequence was extraordinary. The National Security Agency’s classified internal network went offline, an outage the agency attributed to scheduled maintenance. A day earlier, a software problem reportedly blocked Chase customers from accessing their money while hackers inserted malicious code into the websites of 178 major organizations. That followed an FAA stoppage of airline departures nationwide and a worldwide failure of Microsoft’s email system, amid other disruptions involving Apple, Wells Fargo and Charles Schwab.

Asked about the failures in Manila, President Donald Trump offered no explanation and little concern. “It’s life,” he said.

That shrug did nothing to resolve the obvious question: coincidence, institutional decay or hostile action? The supplied reporting establishes no answer. But the administration’s insistence that the disruptions were ordinary accidents now competes with the distrust its own conduct has cultivated.

Even Trump’s allies are rejecting the reassurance

Republican Sen. Josh Hawley called the cluster of incidents potentially “the greatest coincidence in the history of the world” and said the claim that a malfunctioning router could disable the national air-traffic system was “a little hard to believe.”

Hawley did not present evidence of sabotage. His remark matters for another reason: a Republican senator publicly signaled that explanations coming from Trump’s executive branch no longer command automatic belief.

That is the political consequence of treating official communication as an instrument of obedience rather than a public obligation. The Washington Post documented 30,573 false or misleading claims during Trump’s first administration, according to AlterNet. The article also reports that administration nominees appearing before the Senate have refused to state that Joe Biden won the 2020 election. The point is not merely that Trump lies. It is that loyalty to his lies has become a test for officials seeking power under him.

Once a government makes factual surrender a condition of advancement, truthful statements lose their institutional backing. The public is then left to guess whether an official explanation reflects evidence, political convenience or fear of contradicting the president.

A credibility deficit built by officials

AlterNet points to two more episodes that have damaged the administration’s standing. It reports that officials labeled Alex Pretti, a 37-year-old ICU nurse at a Veterans Affairs hospital, a “domestic terrorist” after he was killed by ICE; Vice President JD Vance called him an “assassin.” According to the article, those characterizations were not retracted.

The publication also cites New York Times reporting that Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and Pentagon officials concealed the extent of deaths and damage from Iranian strikes on U.S. forces and facilities. It further cites The Intercept’s finding that the official casualty tally fell far short of more than 900 service members reportedly killed or wounded. Those are attributed claims, not findings independently established by the supplied material. But they illustrate why routine assurances about routers, maintenance and software bugs now meet suspicion.

This does not prove that officials lied about the recent outages. Nor does the proximity of several failures establish coordination. Modern infrastructure is tightly connected, software is fragile, and attackers can exploit vulnerabilities quickly. The administration’s credibility problem is not evidence of a cyberattack; it is the reason plausible explanations fail to settle the matter.

Washington weakened the agency charged with watching the systems

Distrust becomes more consequential when paired with decisions that reduce the government’s ability to prevent and investigate cyber failures. AlterNet reports that Trump and Elon Musk’s Department of Government Efficiency pushed out roughly one-third of the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency’s workforce. It says the administration eliminated CISA’s “Red Team,” whose specialists searched government systems for weaknesses before adversaries could exploit them, while congressional Republicans sought another $707 million cut to the agency.

Those are choices made by people with institutional power, not mysterious malfunctions. If the government dismisses cyber defenders, dismantles specialized testing capacity and reduces the remaining agency’s budget, it makes critical infrastructure harder to protect regardless of what caused this particular series of outages.

The article also notes an earlier warning from U.S. intelligence agencies that Chinese government hackers had penetrated American communications, energy, transportation and water systems to prepare for possible disruption during a crisis. AlterNet says Trump ignored that warning. Against that background, demanding a serious investigation is not paranoia. Declaring without evidence that the week’s failures were a coordinated demonstration by China or Russia would be.

AlterNet itself crosses that line only as an explicitly fictional exercise, imagining Vladimir Putin and Xi Jinping using the disruptions to coerce Trump over Ukraine and Taiwan. That scenario is speculation, not reporting. It should not be smuggled into the factual case. The established political indictment is already substantial: the administration weakened defensive institutions while its history of dishonesty made its subsequent reassurances less credible.

Secrecy creates the opening for conspiracy

Hawley and House Democratic Leader Hakeem Jeffries have demanded answers, according to AlterNet. Immediate public hearings could establish what failed, which warnings existed, how agencies responded and whether personnel cuts impaired prevention or recovery. They would also force officials to defend their explanations under scrutiny rather than repeat them from behind executive authority.

The article further raises National Security Presidential Memorandum 7, saying it has been used against protesters and to organize investigations of groups, nonprofits, individuals and writers characterized as “anti-capitalist, anti-Christian, and anti-American.” Its relevance here is not proof that the outages were engineered to justify emergency powers. No such evidence is supplied. The danger lies in a government expanding coercive machinery while weakening oversight and forfeiting public trust.

A state does not become credible by demanding that people stop noticing contradictions. Nor can congressional Republicans spend years enforcing submission to Trump and then act astonished when explanations from his administration are doubted. Democrats, for their part, hold enough institutional power to demand records, convene hearings where they control the relevant bodies and make obstruction visible where they do not.

Bad routers can be replaced. Software can be patched. Cybersecurity teams can be rebuilt, if the people controlling budgets and appointments choose to rebuild them. Restoring credibility is harder because it requires officials to abandon a political system in which demonstrable falsehoods are rewarded, correction is treated as betrayal and accountability begins only after the machinery stops working.
