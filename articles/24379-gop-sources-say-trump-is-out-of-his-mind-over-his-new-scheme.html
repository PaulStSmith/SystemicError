GOP sources say Trump is 'out of his mind' over his new scheme

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 3, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that CNN’s John King said some Trump voters reacted with disbelief to reporting about President Donald Trump’s cryptocurrency ventures. One voter called Trump “out of his mind,” then asked whether the story was legitimate. CNN’s Anderson Cooper cited financial filings that he said show the ventures putting more than $1 billion a year in Trump’s pocket.

King also described a Georgia voter who said she lost about $20,000 after investing in two Trump crypto funds and now plans to vote for the Democratic Senate candidate. He said other past Trump voters believe the president is preoccupied with himself rather than the economic concerns that helped return him to office. The report does not establish how representative these voters are or document a broader Republican revolt.

The power belongs to Trump

The central fact is not that some Republicans are shocked. It is that the president holds federal power while private ventures bearing his political identity allegedly generate extraordinary personal income.

Voters can complain, sell investments, or change their ballots. Trump controls the presidency and chooses whether to maintain financial arrangements that create this conflict. Republican officials and institutions also possess agency: they can investigate, demand disclosure, impose political costs, or continue treating presidential self-enrichment as background noise.

Disbelief is not accountability

The “out of his mind” remark supplies the headline, but astonishment is the least important part of the story. The conduct described did not emerge from nowhere. It operated in public, alongside a media ecosystem that King said often withholds unfavorable or ethically troubling information from Trump’s most loyal supporters.

The voter who immediately wondered whether CNN’s report was “fake news” illustrates the protective function of that ecosystem. Discrediting outside reporting does more than preserve loyalty. It raises the evidentiary threshold for facts harmful to Trump while lowering it for claims that serve him.

Economic grievance meets private gain

Trump reportedly insists inflation is under control and faults his own messaging for public dissatisfaction. Cooper’s response was more concrete: costs remained higher and were rising faster than when Trump took office.

That contrast matters because some swing voters backed Trump to improve their material conditions. If they instead see a president earning enormous sums from crypto ventures while their own costs rise—or, in one reported case, losing $20,000 in Trump-linked funds—the political problem is not inadequate explanation. It is the collision between promised economic stewardship and reported private enrichment.

The framing lets institutions disappear

By centering colorful voter reactions, the coverage risks making the scandal a story about consumer disappointment and electoral mood. That shifts attention downward, toward people with little institutional power, and away from the officials, party leaders, regulators, and congressional majorities capable of imposing consequences.

The stronger question is not whether voters have finally noticed. It is why notice must substitute for enforcement. Elections can punish a party, but they are a blunt and delayed mechanism for addressing conflicts created by a sitting president’s business interests.

Grift as a governing pattern

King said Democrats increasingly use “grift” to describe the issue, although affordability remains voters’ largest concern. Those subjects are not separate. Political power becomes materially legible when the person promising relief appears to profit while supporters absorb higher costs or investment losses.

The systemic failure is the conversion of accountability into polling analysis. A presidency entangled with lucrative private ventures is treated as an intriguing voter-perception problem; party actors are reduced to spectators; and institutional inaction vanishes behind anecdotes about surprise. The public’s reaction matters, but it should not be carrying the entire burden that governing institutions have declined to lift.
