Trump's Approval Myth Is a Governing Strategy

Power, Not Polling

The reporting here starts with a familiar dodge: Donald Trump behaves as if popularity is a property right. It is not. He may inhabit the Oval Office, but the only thing that matters politically is that his approval is weak and the Republican Party is carrying the consequences into the midterm season. That is the real story, not his self-congratulation.

One Short Summary

Jake Sherman said Trump walked into a strange interview insisting he remains broadly popular, while Sherman’s own read was the opposite: the president’s ratings are poor, voters are angry, and Republicans are taking the punishment. He also pointed to the party’s dependence on a tax cut bill and an unpopular war as its thin remaining argument.

The Real Decision-Maker

This is not a mystery of public mood. It is the result of decisions made at the top of the Republican system. Trump is not being misunderstood by his party; he is being protected by it. Republicans chose to build their electoral machinery around his ego, then act surprised when that ego cannot process blame, decline, or limits.

Blame Slides Downward

Trump’s favorite move is to split responsibility from power. If the party is in trouble, voters must simply be "angry at Republicans," while he remains untouched by the same backlash. That is political misdirection, not analysis. The man who claims the successes gets to disown the failures. The people closest to him help by repeating the fiction that his personal standing is somehow separate from the party he dominates.

Media Deference Becomes Part of the Damage

Sherman’s regret that he did not push back matters. It shows how often journalists allow Trump’s self-mythology to survive contact with the record. When a reporter leaves the room wishing he had challenged the premise, that is not just a missed exchange. It is evidence of a larger habit: treating deliberate distortion as if it were merely eccentricity.

The Pattern

The deeper pattern is a party that has traded political judgment for loyalty theater. Republicans are not being crushed by an accidental misunderstanding of Trump’s popularity; they are paying for a structure that rewards denial, excuses the strongest actor in the room, and dumps the consequences onto everyone below him. That is how authoritarian politics works in practice: power concentrates at the top, responsibility gets shoved downward, and the public is asked to mistake the whole arrangement for normal governing.
