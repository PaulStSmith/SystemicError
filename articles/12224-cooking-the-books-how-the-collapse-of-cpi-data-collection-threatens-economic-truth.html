Cooking the Books: How the Collapse of CPI Data Collection Threatens Economic Truth

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 31, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

In a functioning democracy, statistics are sacred. Not in the sense of being divine, but in their role as the shared foundation upon which policy is built, accountability is measured, and trust in governance is maintained. When those statistics are tampered with—or worse, fabricated—the entire edifice begins to rot from within. Enter the Consumer Price Index (CPI), the inflation metric that underpins everything from interest rates and pensions to wage negotiations and economic forecasts. The latest data on CPI imputations from the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), as visualized by Bloomberg, is not just troubling—it is an alarm bell screaming at full volume.

Since 2019, the share of price data that the BLS actually collects remained relatively steady, fluctuating modestly between 85% and 95%. That is, until mid-2025. In the span of a few short months, the amount of directly collected data plummeted to just over 60%. Inversely, the proportion of CPI data that is "imputed"—estimated based on guesses, less comparable products, or prices from other regions—has exploded, now constituting over 30% of the dataset.

Let that sink in: One out of every three data points used to calculate the nation's inflation rate is now an educated guess.

This is not a minor technical detail. This is the economic equivalent of a bridge inspector reporting that a third of the bolts holding the structure together might be imaginary, and asking you to trust the bridge anyway.

It gets worse when you consider the political context. We are living under an administration helmed by a man with a proven track record of lying, exaggerating, and manipulating the truth to serve his personal and political ends. In such an environment, where the very concept of objective reality is treated as malleable clay, the shift from collected data to imputed data is not just a technical issue—it is a glaring vulnerability waiting to be exploited.

If inflation statistics can be massaged, reinterpreted, or outright fabricated under the guise of “imputation,” then the CPI becomes a weapon of narrative rather than a tool of analysis. Who's to say this administration won't inflate or deflate numbers as suits their campaign messaging, economic boasting, or monetary policy manipulation? When real data vanishes, what remains is whatever the ruling party finds convenient.

This isn't conjecture. We've seen similar playbooks in authoritarian regimes. In Venezuela, Zimbabwe, Russia, and Turkey, official inflation rates are routinely at odds with the reality experienced by ordinary citizens. Why? Because when governments feel the pressure of unflattering numbers, the first instinct isn't always to fix the problem—it's to fix the perception. The United States, long seen as a bastion of transparency and statistical rigor, now finds itself flirting with the same tactics it once decried.

But make no mistake: this isn't just about abstract principles or academic concerns. When CPI loses its credibility, the consequences are real and immediate.

Wages indexed to CPI lose their purchasing power. Social Security payments fall behind real cost-of-living increases. Interest rate decisions made by the Federal Reserve become disconnected from economic reality. Investment decisions, both domestic and foreign, become riskier as confidence in the data erodes.

And perhaps most crucially, the working class—already battered by housing costs, medical debt, and stagnant wages—will be the ones to suffer most. Because while the elite can hedge against inflation with diversified assets, real estate, and offshore accounts, the average worker lives paycheck to paycheck. If inflation is understated, their raises won't match price increases. If it's overstated, austerity policies might be imposed unnecessarily.

All of this traces back to the simple act of collecting real data—or failing to.

One might ask: why did this collapse in data collection happen now? Theories abound. Budget cuts, institutional sabotage, pandemic aftershocks, or simple incompetence could all play a role. But given this administration's naked hostility to independent oversight, the more sinister interpretation cannot be dismissed: this might be deliberate. A slow, quiet corruption of the data pipeline, allowing those in power to shape the economic narrative with minimal resistance.

The collapse of statistical integrity does not happen overnight. It begins with small cracks: a shortcut here, a missing dataset there. Over time, the rot spreads until no one can be certain what's real. And once the public loses faith in the numbers, governance itself becomes guesswork.

We must not allow that to happen.

There must be a public reckoning with the collapse in CPI data collection. Journalists, economists, watchdogs, and legislators must demand answers from the BLS and the administration. Transparency must be restored. Funding for proper data collection must be reinstated. And any hint of political interference in statistical agencies must be treated as the democratic crisis it is.

Because if the numbers can't be trusted, nothing else can.

This isn't just a matter of economic policy. It's a matter of whether we want to live in a reality-based society, or one where power manufactures its own truth and forces the rest of us to pretend it's real.

The numbers are broken. The alarm has sounded. What happens next will determine whether the damage is repaired—or institutionalized.
