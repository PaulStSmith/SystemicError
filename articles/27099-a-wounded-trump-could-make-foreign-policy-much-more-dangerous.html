A wounded Trump could make foreign policy much more dangerous

Based on an article by The Conversation

Paulo SantosNovember 5, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Republicans suffered sweeping midterm losses after voters turned against high prices, the war with Iran, and allegations of Trump administration corruption. According to the article, Democrats captured seats in heavily Republican states, including James Talarico’s victory in Texas, while Donald Trump remained president with substantial authority over foreign policy.

The article argues that this defeat could make US foreign policy more dangerous. With his domestic agenda constrained and the midterms finished, Trump could resume military escalation or tariff confrontations that he reportedly moderated before the election. Democrats could investigate the Iran war, Trump’s foreign financial dealings, and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s Pentagon management, but restraining presidential action would probably require Republican cooperation sufficient to overcome vetoes.

The power remains in the presidency

Voters can punish a president’s party without removing the president. That is the central institutional fact here. Congress holds constitutional powers over funding, war, and treaties, but the modern presidency possesses enormous practical freedom to deploy force, impose economic pressure, and manufacture faits accomplis before legislators act.

The election therefore changes the political terrain without automatically changing foreign policy. Democratic victories create investigative power and legislative friction. They do not, on the facts AlterNet presents, transfer command of the military or control over executive decisions.

Republicans still have agency

The article correctly identifies Republican members of Congress as the pivotal actors. Democrats may lack the supermajorities needed to override Trump’s vetoes. If so, meaningful restraint would depend on Republicans deciding that congressional authority matters more than continued obedience to their party’s president.

That is not helplessness. It is a choice. Presenting Congress merely as structurally weak would conceal the agency of legislators who can vote against funding, reclaim tariff authority, support investigations, or refuse to do any of those things. The relevant question is not whether Republicans feel nervous about 2028. It is whether enough of them will use the power they still hold.

“Wounded” is not an explanation

AlterNet’s weakest move is its speculation that an angry Trump might “vent his frustrations abroad.” The article establishes opportunity: fewer electoral constraints, diminished prospects for domestic legislation, and broad executive latitude. It does not establish that personal frustration will cause a particular military or economic decision.

Psychologizing the president risks turning policy into mood. Wars and tariff confrontations require decisions, advisers, appropriations, military compliance, and congressional acquiescence. Calling Trump “wounded” may describe his political position, but it should not obscure the institutions and officials capable of enabling whatever follows.

Oversight is leverage, not control

Investigations into the Iran war, foreign financial dealings, and Pete Hegseth’s management of the Pentagon could expose misconduct, force testimony, and raise the cost of evasion. They could also reveal whether public power served private interests. But hearings are not self-executing restraints.

Even Hegseth’s possible resignation or dismissal, described by AlterNet only as speculation, would not itself change presidential policy. Replacing an adviser can alter execution. It cannot substitute for Congress reclaiming authority or for legislators accepting responsibility for the votes that make executive overreach possible.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is a democracy that permits voters to reject a governing agenda while leaving its most coercive machinery largely untouched. Domestic defeat can narrow a president’s options at home even as the institutional architecture of foreign policy preserves room for escalation abroad.

That danger should not be reduced to one man’s temperament. The system concentrates power in the executive, rewards Congress for avoiding responsibility, and allows partisan loyalty to disable formal checks. A repudiated administration can remain formidable because the officials empowered to restrain it may prefer political submission to institutional conflict.
