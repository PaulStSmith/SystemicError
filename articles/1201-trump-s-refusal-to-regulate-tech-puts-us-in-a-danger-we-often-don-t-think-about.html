Trump's refusal to regulate tech puts us in a danger we often don't think about

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 10, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

AI Risk Is Not a Mystery. It Is a Choice.

Power Sits With the White House and the Industry Lobby

The source story is straightforward on the facts: Biden’s team tried to build AI testing and bio-risk oversight, while Trump is described as refusing meaningful regulation. The real power here sits with the federal executive, Congress, and the firms building these systems. The companies are not passive bystanders. They are pushing to keep regulation off their business while expanding their reach into public life.

That is the core political structure the article exposes, even if it does not say it bluntly enough: the people with the capacity to slow this down are the same people profiting from speed.

The Decision Was Political, Not Technical

Ben Buchanan’s comments make the key point plain. These models are not just cyber tools; they are “generally capable” systems that can assist with bio tasks, including ones that raise biosecurity risk. The Biden administration responded with an executive order, a long bio section, and voluntary testing through the AI Safety Institute.

That matters because it shows the issue is not whether the danger is imaginable. The risk is already acknowledged at the highest levels. What remains is whether government chooses to constrain it or indulge it. Trump’s refusal is not a neutral lack of interest. It is a decision to leave a dangerous technology in the hands of firms that want minimal friction.

“Complexity” Is a Convenient Shield

Chris Krebs’s line about technology moving faster than democratic oversight sounds sober, but it also risks hiding the human choices underneath. Complexity is not the cause of regulatory paralysis. It is the excuse used by officials and executives who prefer to pretend the problem is too technical for public control.

The source also leans on the familiar American ritual of “light touch” and “free markets” as though that were a law of nature rather than an ideological preference. It is not. It is the policy environment that leaves private actors free to expand capability first and negotiate restraint later, after the damage is already distributed.

The Blame Gets Spread Downward

The article notes that several tech leaders are making their relationships with Trump visible, while OpenAI’s Sam Altman has stayed quiet as he prepares for an IPO. That silence is not absence. It is strategy. When the industry is seeking capital, it has every incentive to keep regulation vague, soft, and delayed.

The story also mentions a rupture between Anthropic’s Dario Amodei and the Trump administration after Pete Hegseth demanded a violation of Anthropic’s ethics code. That is not a sign of principled governance. It is a glimpse of how the state and the firms negotiate power when everyone involved wants authority without accountability.

What This Story Really Shows

The larger pattern is simple: AI is being treated like a commercial race while the risks are being handled like a side conversation. That is how institutional cowardice works. The danger is acknowledged in public, named in executive language, and then pushed into voluntary testing, weak oversight, or ideological slogans about innovation.

This is not a story about machines outrunning politics. It is a story about political leaders and tech executives choosing not to govern because governance would impose costs on the people making money from the race. The result is a familiar American arrangement: public risk, private profit, and a government content to describe the problem instead of exercising power over it.
