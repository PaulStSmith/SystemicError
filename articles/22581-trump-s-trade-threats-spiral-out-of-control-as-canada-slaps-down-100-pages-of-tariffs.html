Trump's trade threats spiral out of control as Canada slaps down 100 pages of tariffs

Based on an article by David Edwards

Paulo SantosAugust 26, 20262 min read

Tariffs, Grievance, and the Lie of Strength

The Setup

Donald Trump spent Tuesday morning performing outrage online while Ottawa published a retaliatory tariff schedule he could not stop. Canada’s cabinet moved with a calendar, a list, and a price tag; Trump responded with threats, petulance, and fantasies about renaming Lake Ontario. That is the real shape of this story: one side governs through institutions, the other through spectacle.

Who Actually Decided

The decision with consequence came from Ottawa. Prime Minister Mark Carney promised a dollar-for-dollar response after talks collapsed, and four cabinet ministers announced tariffs covering about 700 American products, taking effect September 8. Canada also doubled tariffs on U.S. steel and aluminum to 50 percent and set aside $7.5 billion for businesses and workers hit by the trade war.

Trump, by contrast, is using the presidency as a megaphone for economic coercion. He set the terms, escalated the penalties, and then tried to recast retaliation as Canadian hostility. That is not diplomacy. It is domination with a social-media interface.

The Blame Game

Trump’s complaint that the United States runs a trade deficit with Canada is political theater disguised as grievance. The source itself notes that the gap exists largely because Americans buy Canadian crude oil. That matters, because Trump has excluded oil, natural gas, potash, and many minerals from his tariffs. He is not building a coherent trade doctrine. He is selectively punishing sectors while preserving the flows he still wants.

Then comes the misdirection: Canada is “unreasonable,” Ontario is the problem, maybe even Lake Ontario can be renamed into obedience. This is not analysis. It is an attempt to turn a policy fight into a childish loyalty test, where the stronger country pretends the weaker one is the aggressor for refusing to kneel.

Retaliation With Teeth

Canada is not pretending this will be painless. Economists expect price increases to hit Canadian companies too, and Canada’s economy is only about one-twelfth the size of the U.S. economy. But there is still a political difference between absorption and submission. Polls before the tariffs took effect already showed broad Canadian support for retaliation, with Ontario pushing hard for it and Alberta urging restraint.

That split matters. It shows a government trying to preserve leverage while managing internal pressure, not a country stumbling blindly into conflict. The tariffs are blunt, but they are also legible: Canada is choosing to make Trump’s aggression expensive rather than treating it as background noise.

The Pattern

This is what authoritarian politics looks like in economic form. The leader manufactures conflict, inflates his own toughness, and shifts blame outward when others answer back. He wants the public to see chaos where there is actually decision-making, and weakness where there is resistance.

The larger pattern is simple: Trump uses state power to stage grievance, then calls any response “unreasonable.” That trick depends on confusion. It depends on people mistaking coercion for negotiation and retaliation for provocation. Ottawa’s tariff list cuts through that fog. It shows what happens when a government refuses to flatter a bully and instead treats him like what he is: an actor with power, and a limit.
