Fuel costs are brutalizing Trump’s fans in these red states: report

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosJune 25, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Power, Not WeatherWho Eats the CostThe Familiar Scapegoat TrickBig Vehicles, Bigger ExposureThe Weak FrameThe Pattern

Trump’s War, Red-State Bill

Power, Not Weather

Gas prices did not rise by some neutral act of nature. According to the reporting here, the spike tracks a Trump administration war with Iran that jolted global fuel markets and pushed up costs across the country. That is the real center of gravity: the institution with power was the White House, and the decision that mattered was the one that turned foreign policy into a domestic fuel tax.

Who Eats the Cost

Arizona, Utah, and Wyoming are taking the sharpest hits, with annual fuel costs jumping by $963, $967, and $1,209 in just four months. New York saw a much smaller increase. The political irony is obvious, but the larger point is less amusing: voters do not control oil markets, and they certainly do not control presidential war policy after the ballots are counted.

The Familiar Scapegoat Trick

The standard lie in stories like this is that “gas prices went up,” as if prices emerged from nowhere and then politely landed on the public. They did not. The administration made a decision that shocked supply and price. Then ordinary drivers absorbed the consequence while political loyalists were left to explain why their own government made their commute more expensive. That is not confusion. It is governance by externalized pain.

Big Vehicles, Bigger Exposure

The report also shows how the burden lands unevenly inside Trump’s own coalition. Drivers of trucks and SUVs are taking the hardest hit, and the study says those vehicle classes are more common among Trump voters than among Democrats. The Nissan Armada, the Toyota Sequoia, the gas-guzzling machines marketed as symbols of freedom, become little more than rolling bills when fuel spikes. The market did not punish ideology. It punished dependence.

The Weak Frame

The article’s strongest move is naming the war as the cause. Its weaker move is leaning too hard on red-state schadenfreude, as if the story were mainly about Republicans getting what they voted for. That flatters the system by turning policy failure into a partisan joke. The real issue is that a president and his administration can choose escalation, trigger global consequences, and leave households to pay for the damage long after the slogans have faded.

The Pattern

This is the recurring arrangement of American power: leaders manufacture risk, markets transmit the cost, and the public is told to treat the result as unfortunate weather. War becomes “pressure on prices.” Policy becomes “market volatility.” Responsibility gets diluted until no one is answerable except the people buying gas. That is how institutional power protects itself: it acts first, then calls the fallout unavoidable.
