'It's so unbelievable': Lawyer floored as Trump DOJ seeks to revive case without evidence

Based on an article by Bennito L. Kelty

Paulo SantosAugust 19, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

When the DOJ Prosecutes Its Own Defeat

The Source Material

The reporting here is simple: a case against Olympic canoer David Hearn was dismissed after the U.S. Attorney’s Office for D.C., under Jeanine Pirro, said the damage to the Reflecting Pool came from a rushed, botched installation by the Interior Department, not from Hearn vandalizing it. Now the Trump DOJ is reportedly considering reviving that same case.

Who Actually Holds Power

This is not a story about a disputed fact pattern. It is a story about who gets to turn federal power into an instrument of display. The Department of Justice controls the charge, the dismissal, and the revival. That makes the institutional author of the confusion obvious: the same federal machinery that conceded the evidentiary collapse is now contemplating whether to pretend that collapse never happened.

Pirro’s office already put the government’s own conclusion on the record. If another filing walks that back, the problem is not ambiguity. It is executive power trying to overwrite its own factual posture for political convenience.

The Blame Game Is the Point

The article’s strongest detail is also its most revealing: the government’s own filing points away from Hearn and toward the Interior Department’s installation failure. That should end the matter. Instead, the possibility of revival turns a botched government job into a target for prosecution. That is misdirection with a badge on it.

This is how weak cases survive in authoritarian-adjacent politics. The state does not need to prove much. It needs to keep the target under suspicion long enough for the accusation itself to do the work. If the evidence fails, the story can still be preserved by changing the venue, changing the tone, or simply pretending the state’s prior admission was never binding.

Institutional Cowardice in Uniform

Norm Eisen’s reaction in the source is telling because he points to the real institutional failure: prosecutors know this looks indefensible, and yet the possibility of reviving it is still in circulation. That means the system is not merely mistaken; it is participating in its own degradation. The DOJ is not a passive referee here. It is the actor deciding whether federal law will be used as a tool of embarrassment management.

There is also a deeper rot in the premise. A government that admits “no evidence beyond a reasonable doubt” but still toys with recharging the case is not pursuing justice. It is signaling that power can be used to harass first and justify later.

What This Reveals

The larger pattern is straightforward: the state can manufacture procedural absurdity fast enough that the legal system starts to resemble a press operation. One branch of the DOJ says the case is dead because the damage was caused by government error. Another branch may try to resurrect it anyway. That is not merely incoherence. It is the conversion of prosecution into messaging.

This is what democratic accountability has to name directly. When federal law enforcement is willing to chase a person after its own office has undercut the charge, the issue is not one bad case. It is a regime habit: use public authority to signal dominance, then hope the record gets buried under the noise.
