The Government Built a Court Where the Accused Cannot Answer

Secrecy Is the Instrument

Nazira Haji Zada, a 47-year-old lawful permanent resident, was deported to Afghanistan through the Alien Terrorist Removal Court without being charged with a crime or permitted to see the classified evidence against her. Her lawyers could not examine that evidence either. Attorney General Todd Blanche nevertheless presented the result as a victory for “national security and the rule of law.”

That phrase reverses reality. The proceeding did not demonstrate the rule of law; it demonstrated how legal machinery can be designed to evade its substance.

Congress Built It, Trump Used It

The immediate responsibility belongs to the Trump administration, which chose to activate a court no previous administration had used. But the power did not materialize from nowhere. Congress created the court amid the political panic following the Oklahoma City bombing, placing an authoritarian mechanism into federal law and trusting future presidents to leave it untouched.

That was institutional cowardice disguised as prudence. Legislators authorized extraordinary state power while avoiding responsibility for how it might eventually be exercised. Trump’s contribution was not invention but deployment: his administration found the dormant weapon and pulled the trigger.

A Defense Lawyer Forbidden to Defend

Under the statutory process described in the source, the judge reviews classified evidence privately. Ordinary evidentiary rules do not apply. Material obtained through surveillance or warrantless searches may be used, while the accused cannot seek its suppression. A security-cleared attorney may inspect the secret file but faces prison for revealing its contents to the person whose fate it determines.

Calling this representation empties the word of meaning. A person cannot rebut an allegation she is forbidden to know, challenge a source she cannot identify, or explain conduct the government refuses to describe. The secrecy is not an unfortunate complication surrounding the process. It is the source of the government’s advantage.

Zada’s agreement to removal does not cure that coercion. When the state controls the evidence, the forum, detention, and the prospect of punishment, “consent” may describe capitulation more accurately than choice. Her public defenders explicitly rejected any suggestion that her departure legitimized the court.

“National Security” as a Liability Shield

The administration may possess serious evidence against Zada. The public record described here cannot establish whether it does, precisely because the government constructed a proceeding that prevents meaningful scrutiny.

That uncertainty benefits the state, not the accused. Officials can imply grave danger while withholding the material needed to test the implication. If the evidence is strong, secrecy shields its methods; if it is weak, secrecy shields the officials. Either way, executive power escapes the discipline of contradiction.

The source weakens its own strongest case when it races through sweeping historical analogies and declares motives more confidently than its evidence permits. Kafka, East Germany, and fascism may illuminate the experience of arbitrary power, but they cannot substitute for institutional analysis. The documented structure is damning enough: government officials sought removal, a secret court accepted undisclosed evidence, and the target was denied an effective opportunity to answer it.

The Scapegoat Makes the Precedent

Immigrants are useful testing grounds for coercive government because their rights are routinely treated as conditional and their removal as an administrative detail. That framing misdirects attention toward the supposed dangerousness of the individual and away from the officials choosing the procedure.

The central question is not whether Zada deserves public sympathy. It is whether the government must substantiate its accusations before imposing severe consequences. Once officials can answer that question with a classified file and a closed door, the accusation begins doing the work that proof was meant to do.

The immediate burden falls on a noncitizen with less political power than the institutions arrayed against her. The durable benefit flows upward: to prosecutors, intelligence agencies, and presidents who now possess a tested precedent for punishment without public accountability.

Due Process Cannot Survive as Decoration

This case exposes a recurring pattern in American government. Congress creates exceptional powers during panic. Presidents preserve them. Courts normalize them. When an administration finally uses them aggressively, each institution points elsewhere: lawmakers blame the executive, the executive invokes the statute, and judges invoke classified evidence.

The result is deliberate harm laundered through procedure. Forms are filed, attorneys are assigned, and a judge signs an order, allowing officials to claim legality while withholding the basic conditions that make adjudication legitimate.

A secret accusation does more than conceal evidence. It reorganizes power. The government no longer has to persuade the accused, the public, or an ordinary court. It needs only to persuade itself.
