Protect and Serve? More Like Punish and Enslave

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 17, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Militarization of MayberryWarrior Training: Manufacture the EnemyThe Legal License to Not CareThe Poisoned OrchardBeyond Reform: Toward Rebuilding

In a country where a police officer simply doing their job with empathy and humanity becomes a viral sensation, we must ask the obvious: why has the bar for policing fallen so low that basic decency is considered exceptional?

Consider the now-famous image of Officer Tim Purdy, calmly sitting on the pavement beside a distressed autistic high school student. Purdy didn’t shout. He didn’t brandish a weapon. He sat. He talked. He listened. He offered presence, not dominance. And in doing so, he defused a potentially dangerous situation with nothing more than human connection.

It’s a beautiful moment. It’s what policing should be. And that’s the problem.

The fact that this act is hailed as extraordinary speaks volumes. It is a moment of humanity in a profession that has become increasingly dehumanized. Officer Purdy didn’t just fulfill a duty—he acted against the current that runs through American law enforcement. His calm and compassion are a rebuttal to the prevailing ethos of militarized enforcement and suspicion.

We are not talking about a broken system. We are talking about a system working exactly as it was designed. That design is not about safety. It’s not about justice. It’s about power.

Let’s dispense with the sugarcoating.

Why are there armored tanks rolling down streets in rural Alabama? Why does a town of 5,000 have access to equipment meant for the battlefields of Iraq and Afghanistan? Why are officers trained in tactics used against guerrilla insurgents? Why is the public—the people they swear to protect—perceived as the enemy?

The answers aren’t hidden. They’re etched into policy, training, and budget spreadsheets. The transformation of police departments into paramilitary forces wasn’t accidental—it was deliberate, systemic, and bipartisan.

The Militarization of Mayberry

After 9/11, the federal government opened the floodgates. Through the Department of Defense’s 1033 Program, local departments began receiving surplus military gear at little or no cost. MRAPs (Mine-Resistant Ambush Protected vehicles), tactical body armor, automatic rifles, surveillance drones, and even grenade launchers made their way into sleepy suburbs and rural towns.

The rationale? “Preparedness.”

But what exactly were they preparing for?

This wasn’t about community safety. It was about shifting the very mission of policing—from protectors of the peace to enforcers of order. The order they protect, let’s be clear, is not moral or equitable. It is hierarchical and transactional. It protects property. It protects capital. It protects the status quo.

Traffic stops became battle drills. Protesters became enemy combatants. Neighborhoods became potential warzones. And civilians? Too often they’re just the unlucky ones caught in the crosshairs of paranoia armed to the teeth.

Warrior Training: Manufacture the Enemy

The hardware is only part of the problem. The real weaponization lies in the training and mindset.

Police academies no longer teach service—they teach survival. And survival, in this context, is predicated on perceiving everyone as a threat. Training videos loop footage of officers getting ambushed. Every traffic stop is presented as potentially fatal. Every hesitation is shown as a fatal flaw.

What they’re taught is not public safety—it’s us versus them. It’s “better to be judged by twelve than carried by six.” It’s preemption over empathy. They are taught to control the encounter, not to understand it. To dominate, not to de-escalate.

And when those lessons are combined with a badge, a gun, and legal immunity, the consequences are inevitable and often fatal.

The Legal License to Not Care

All of this might be marginally less terrifying if the police were, in fact, legally obligated to protect the people. But they aren’t.

In Warren v. District of Columbia (1981), the court ruled that police owe no specific duty to protect individuals, even in emergency situations. That was reaffirmed in Castle Rock v. Gonzales (2005), where the Supreme Court found that a restraining order did not entitle a woman to police enforcement, even after her estranged husband kidnapped and murdered their children.

In plain terms: police are not required to protect you.

Their obligation is to the state. Their function is to enforce law—not to defend life. If they fail to intervene while you are assaulted, robbed, or even murdered, the courts have said: that’s unfortunate, but not illegal.

So what does this mean?

It means the next time an officer fails to act, it’s not a failure of the system. It is the system.

The Poisoned Orchard

We are long past the tired “few bad apples” defense. The orchard is rotten. The soil is poisoned. The roots are cracked and stained.

The system we have now protects property over people, corporations over communities, and power over peace. It criminalizes poverty. It racializes suspicion. And it rewards brutality with paid leave, union protection, and promotions.

It produces Derek Chauvins and then shields them. It kills George Floyds and then asks us to remain calm. It brutalizes protestors and then gaslights the public with statements about “restraint.”

So when someone like Officer Tim Purdy sits on the ground beside a vulnerable young man, it doesn’t just seem unusual—it seems revolutionary. Because in a profession designed to punish, kindness becomes resistance.

Beyond Reform: Toward Rebuilding

Let’s be honest: reform has been tried. Body cams. De-escalation training. Implicit bias seminars. Community policing programs. None of it has fundamentally altered the architecture of policing in this country.

You cannot reform a structure built on coercion and impunity. You cannot train away fear-based conditioning. You cannot workshop your way out of a militarized culture. You can only dismantle and rebuild.

We need to start over—with new goals, new accountability mechanisms, and new definitions of safety. Safety should not come at the barrel of a gun. It should come from schools, healthcare, housing, and mental health services. From community investment, not occupation.

Because right now, what we have is not policing.

It is occupation by another name.

And it’s time we call it what it is—and demand something better.

As the Decepticon Barricade’s police cruiser mockingly declared in Transformers: “To Punish and Enslave.”

A fictional villain's slogan. A real-world mission statement.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
