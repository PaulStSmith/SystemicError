'Real shot': Data expert flags how Texas Dems could break their 30-year losing streak

By Paulo Santos

Democrats haven’t won a statewide race in Texas in over 30 years — but after an intense primary that ended with poll confusion drama and alleged voter suppression, they have perhaps their best opportunity in all that time to finally come through, political data analyst G. Elliott Morris wrote on Substack Wednesday morning.

“This year, Democrats might have a real shot at winning a statewide election in the Lone Star State,” wrote Morris. “On Tuesday, March 3, Democratic primary voters picked state House Rep. James Talarico over U.S. Rep. Jasmine Crockett to become their party’s nominee for the November election to the U.S. Senate. On the Republican side, no candidate won above 50%, so incumbent John Cornyn and Ken Paxton are headed to a May 26th runoff election.”

Morris compiled together several reasons he is unusually bullish on the Texas Democratic Party this year.

For one thing, potential GOP frontrunner Ken Paxton is laden down with scandal, from marital infidelity to accusations of securities fraud, bribery, and abuse of power that even led a number of Republicans to impeach him: “The University of Houston’s Hobby School of Public Affairs found the same pattern across 12 hypothetical matchups last fall: Paxton’s average margin of victory was just 2.25 points, compared to 3.5 for Cornyn and 4.5 for Wesley Hunt.”

For another he said, Democrats likely picked the stronger candidate to challenge him: “Across polls, Talarico consistently performs 1-2 points better than Crockett in hypothetical general election matchups across most polling. In the Emerson poll, Crockett loses to Cornyn by 6, while Talarico only loses by 3, and both tie with Paxton at 46% each.”

All of this, he continued, is occurring as Democrats blew the roof off Republicans in enthusiasm and voter turnout, as the demographics in Texas shift to favor them more and more, and the GOP is hemorrhaging support with Latinos, who make up around 40 percent of the state’s electorate.

To top it all off, Morris said, Donald Trump polls underwater in Texas, acting as an anchor on the party: “Historically, statewide presidential approval is a loose predictor of statewide midterm election results. It’s not perfect, but when the president is running at 45% approval in your state, the odds are better than not that you’ll lose the upcoming election.”

Texas has consistently disappointed Democrats, Morris concluded, but this is perhaps the perfect storm to let them truly compete: “In a Paxton-Talarico matchup, the average poll today has the candidates within a point or two. The fundamentals — the midterm penalty, presidential approval, and enthusiasm gap — all push in Talarico’s direction. If Paxton wins the Republican primary, I’d rate the Texas Senate race a pure toss-up, with a 45-50% Democratic win probability.”

Our Analysis:

The Mirage of a Blue Texas: A Closer Look

The recent article heralding the potential for a Democratic victory in Texas is a classic example of political optimism clashing with the harsh reality of long-standing voting trends and systemic issues. While the analysis provided by G. Elliott Morris suggests a turning tide in the Lone Star State, a deeper dive into the facts reveals a more complicated and less promising picture for the Democrats.

The Illusion of Scandal as a Decisive Factor

First and foremost, the article leans heavily on the scandals surrounding GOP frontrunner Ken Paxton as a significant liability. While it’s true that scandal can sway public opinion, the assumption that this will be enough to turn the tide in favor of the Democrats is overly optimistic. The political landscape is littered with the careers of politicians who have survived and even thrived despite scandals. The notion that Paxton’s legal troubles will be the straw that breaks the camel’s back underestimates the partisan loyalty and ideological motivations of voters.

The Misplaced Confidence in Demographics

The article also trumpets the changing demographics of Texas and the increasing turnout and enthusiasm among Democrats as indicators of a potential victory. However, this analysis glosses over the critical issue of voter suppression and the systemic barriers that continue to disenfranchise many of these same demographic groups. The mention of “poll confusion drama and alleged voter suppression” is treated almost as an afterthought, when in fact, these issues are central to the struggle for Democratic victories in Texas and elsewhere.

Furthermore, while the Latino vote is indeed crucial, the assumption that this demographic will uniformly swing towards the Democrats is a gross oversimplification. Recent elections have shown that the Latino vote is far from monolithic, with significant portions of this community voting for Republican candidates due to a variety of factors, including economic concerns and social conservatism.

The Overreliance on Polling Data

The confidence placed in polling data, especially in predicting a tight race between Paxton and Talarico, is another point of contention. Time and again, we’ve seen that polls can be misleading, failing to capture the full complexity of voter behavior and sentiment. The article’s reliance on these numbers, without a critical examination of their limitations, is a glaring oversight.

Ignoring the Republican Counterstrategy

Moreover, the analysis fails to account for the Republican Party’s ability to adapt and counter Democratic advances. The assumption that Donald Trump’s underwater approval ratings in Texas will act as an “anchor” on the GOP overlooks the party’s proven track record of rallying its base and turning out voters in key races. It also underestimates the potential for a unified Republican front post-primary, especially if Paxton is not the nominee.

Conclusion: Wishful Thinking vs. Reality

In conclusion, while the enthusiasm for a potential Democratic breakthrough in Texas is understandable, the analysis presented in the article falls into the trap of wishful thinking. It overlooks significant hurdles, including voter suppression, the complexities of demographic voting patterns, the limitations of polling, and the adaptability of the Republican Party.

The dream of a blue Texas is not new, and while demographic and political shifts suggest that it’s not entirely out of reach, the reality is that such a change is likely to be a slow and arduous process. The factors cited by Morris, though intriguing, do not in themselves constitute a “perfect storm” but rather a continuation of the long, hard slog for Democrats in Texas. To suggest otherwise is to ignore the systemic issues and entrenched political dynamics that have kept Texas firmly in Republican hands for over three decades.
