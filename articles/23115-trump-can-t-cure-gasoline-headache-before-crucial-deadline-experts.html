Trump can't cure gasoline headache before crucial deadline: experts

Based on an article by Jake Bittle, Grist

Paulo SantosSeptember 6, 20264 min read

The Pump Is a Political Stage, Not a Solution

The Real Choke Point

This story is not really about gasoline. It is about who controls the bottleneck and who gets to pretend they do not. The source makes that plain: crude is available, but refining capacity is not. That is the constraint driving the price spike, and it is the constraint that no Oval Office performance can wish away.

Trump is treating the price at the pump like a command problem. It is not. The market is tight because refineries are already running near max capacity, maintenance has been deferred, and the global supply of refined fuels has been battered by conflict and disruption. The consequence is simple: oil companies are collecting the upside while consumers eat the cost.

Who Actually Has Power

The power in this story sits with the refiners and the owners of the infrastructure, not with the man staging a White House meeting about them. The article notes that the refiners left that meeting without public statements and the White House did not even release a list of attendees. That is not transparency. It is ritualized weakness.

The largest refineries are operating at almost maximum capacity and choosing to defer maintenance because the margins are so lucrative. That is the actual decision structure here. Trump can complain that they are making “too much money,” but complaint is not control. The people who can actually shape gasoline supply are the ones already extracting record profits from the shortage.

Venezuela as a Political Prop

The administration’s Venezuela moves are being sold as supply policy, but the article shows the real function: political optics and long-term leverage, not near-term relief. Washington is easing sanctions, leaning on Delcy Rodriguez to rewrite oil laws, and cutting deals through the Pentagon with a private Venezuelan company tied to Alejandro Betancourt. Chevron is expanding too.

None of this is a serious short-term fix for U.S. gas prices. The source says so directly. Venezuelan crude is heavy, only a limited number of U.S. refineries can process it, and those refineries are already overextended. Even if production rises, the timeline is measured in years, not a campaign season. So the administration is selling a future that will arrive long after voters have already been used.

That is the misdirection: present-day pain is being dressed up as a diplomatic breakthrough that cannot solve present-day pain.

The Waivers Are Marginal, Not Transformative

The EPA’s early end to summer ethanol blending requirements and its refinery waivers are being presented as another pressure valve. They may shave costs at the margins. They will not break the broader price structure. The article is careful on that point, and the caution matters.

The debate around the waivers is itself revealing. Biofuel advocates say the policy will hurt farmers and reduce demand for corn. Oil lobbyists say sudden exemptions create unpredictability. Both camps are arguing over whose balance sheet gets protected, not whether the country should keep treating fuel markets as a hostage system governed by fossil infrastructure. The issue is not whether ethanol rules are slightly more or less convenient for refiners. The issue is that the entire system is already organized around scarcity, private gain, and public dependence.

Midterm Math, Not Public Relief

The political logic is naked. Trump wants the price down because gasoline is electoral weather. The article even cites historical evidence that spike years punish the president’s party in midterms. So the administration is not responding to households as such; it is responding to the ballot box.

That distinction matters. When an administration treats fuel policy as a campaign repair job, it is not governing. It is trying to manipulate the visible symptom while leaving the structure intact. The article’s final warning is blunt: even if the Strait of Hormuz reopened tomorrow, the gasoline market would remain tight because the refining system is still the constraint. That means the public is being asked to credit presidential improvisation for a problem the president cannot actually solve.

The Pattern Beneath the Story

This is the larger political pattern: crisis capitalism paired with executive theater. A shortage creates room for profiteering. The profiteering gets blamed on distant geopolitics. The White House stages action against foreign governments and regulatory rules, while the domestic bottleneck stays in private hands and the public absorbs the cost.

That is what makes this more than a gas-price story. It is a demonstration of how power works when a government is more interested in managing perception than restructuring the system that produces the pain. The refineries run hot, the margins widen, the president performs outrage, and consumers are told the problem is complicated. It is not complicated. It is concentrated.
