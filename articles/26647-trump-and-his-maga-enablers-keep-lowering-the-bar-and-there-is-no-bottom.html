Trump and his MAGA enablers keep lowering the bar — and there is no bottom

Based on an article by Chauncey DeVega

Paulo SantosOctober 15, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

At a midterm campaign rally in Grand Island, Nebraska, Donald Trump said of the reported Iran war, “Let them take out Los Angeles. Let them take out San Diego,” while arguing that higher gasoline prices were a small cost for keeping the country safe. AlterNet reports that the crowd responded with laughter, applause, and cheers, and that Trump continued without correcting the apparent interpretation of his words.

White House communications director Steven Cheung later said Trump meant that American cities would have faced danger if a nuclear-armed Iran had not been stopped. Trump offered a similar explanation on Truth Social, saying he had contrasted temporarily higher gasoline prices with the far greater cost of an attack on Los Angeles or San Diego. The wording at the rally was unmistakably reckless; the reporting does not establish that Trump literally invited Iran to launch such an attack.

AlterNet argues that Trump’s remark, the audience response, the administration’s defense, and the news cycle’s rapid retreat from the episode demonstrate a broader normalization of political cruelty. That conclusion is partly interpretation, but the underlying sequence is clear: the president invoked the destruction of two American cities, supporters greeted the line approvingly, and his aides blamed the resulting controversy on the press.

Power was on the stage

The central actor was not an anonymous provocateur or powerless rallygoer. It was the president, who controls the executive branch and commands the country’s nuclear forces. The audience’s reaction matters as evidence of the political culture around him, but Trump chose the words, supplied the scenario, and declined to correct the response in the room.

That distinction is essential. Treating the episode primarily as another example of MAGA voters behaving badly shifts attention downward. Supporters can reward cruelty, but presidential rhetoric gives it authority, visibility, and institutional consequence. The person with the most power also had the greatest agency.

The cleanup preserved the tactic

Cheung and Trump did not dispute the words. They replaced their ordinary meaning with a cleaner retrospective explanation: Trump was supposedly describing the danger he had prevented and the comparative insignificance of higher fuel costs.

That defense may describe what Trump intended to argue, but it does not erase what he actually said. A competent clarification would acknowledge the malformed and dangerous phrasing. Instead, Trump called the press “Degenerates” and accused reporters of knowingly fabricating the controversy. The correction therefore became another attack: responsibility moved from the official who spoke carelessly to the people who accurately reported his language.

This is propaganda’s mundane machinery. The leader creates the scandal, aides manufacture an exonerating interpretation, and scrutiny itself becomes the offense. Ambiguity is useful because it permits the original audience to enjoy the transgression while defenders later insist that no transgression occurred.

Cruelty became audience participation

AlterNet reasonably emphasizes the crowd’s laughter and applause, but it pushes beyond its evidence when it suggests this establishes that the audience accepted the possible deaths of millions. The reaction supports a narrower, still damning conclusion: in that room, rhetoric about two major American cities being “taken out” functioned as entertainment.

Los Angeles and San Diego were not neutral examples. The article places the remark within Trump’s reported pattern of threatening Democratic-led jurisdictions with military or federal intervention and the loss of federal money. Against that background, the cities appear not merely as geographic locations but as partisan symbols—American communities rhetorically separated from the nation because they are governed by political opponents.

That is how authoritarian political culture advances: not only through explicit orders, but through repeated permission to regard disfavored populations as less entitled to protection. The joke establishes the hierarchy before policy enforces it.

The press cannot outsource judgment

AlterNet’s criticism of mainstream coverage is broad and thinly documented; it names no specific report that characterized the remark as mere confusion or hyperbole. That weakens its media indictment. A claim about systematic normalization requires more than asserting that the story disappeared from attention.

The underlying warning nevertheless stands. Journalism misdirects responsibility when it treats a president’s inflammatory language as an interpretive puzzle created by public misunderstanding. The relevant facts are simpler: Trump selected the words; the crowd supplied the approving response; his administration supplied the revision; and news organizations decide whether that sequence remains politically significant.

Neutrality does not require pretending every explanation carries equal weight. It requires distinguishing the contemporaneous record from the later cleanup and refusing passive language that turns an official’s choice into a free-floating “controversy.”

The lowered bar is a governing resource

The systemic issue is not that one grotesque line proves every allegation AlterNet attaches to it. It is that repetition changes the cost of misconduct. Each episode consumes outrage, invites semantic debate, and makes the next breach feel less exceptional. Exhaustion then protects the person with power.

Trump’s advantage lies in that asymmetry. He can introduce cruelty faster than institutions can examine it, while allies convert every demand for accountability into evidence of persecution. The consequence is not merely degraded discourse. It is a political order in which targeted cities are discussed as expendable, presidential recklessness is repackaged as media failure, and the public is trained to regard the extraordinary as background noise.
