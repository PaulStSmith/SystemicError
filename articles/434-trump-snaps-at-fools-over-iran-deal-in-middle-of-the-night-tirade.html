Trump snaps at 'fools' over Iran deal in middle of the night tirade

The Peace Deal as a Power Move, Not a Policy

What the Source Actually Shows

Trump has cut an Iran peace deal, critics from both parties are attacking it, and he responded on Truth Social by calling them “fools,” while bragging about the stock market and falling oil prices. NBC’s reporting suggests the deal is a difficult political sell, and one insider bluntly called it “an embarrassing way to get out of this.”

Who Holds Power

The institutional power here sits with the president. Trump made the deal, Trump owns the retreat, and Trump is the one trying to launder a strategic decision through branding and volume. The critics are noise. The decision-maker is the one with the authority to end, start, or repackage the conflict.

That matters because the story is not really about whether the deal is admired on cable news. It is about how executive power gets exercised: privately through negotiation, then publicly through insult, as though contempt can substitute for justification.

The Blame Transfer

Trump’s response does a familiar trick. He turns substantive criticism into a personality defect in other people. They are “jealous,” “bad,” or “stupid.” That is not rebuttal. It is an attempt to move the frame away from the policy and onto the emotional fitness of the audience.

That maneuver is especially useful when the deal itself looks weak. If the agreement is politically awkward, the easiest escape is to make opposition seem unserious. If the exit is embarrassing, the second-best option is to shout over it until embarrassment sounds like strength.

The Exit Strategy

The source makes the central fact plain: this is a deal designed to get out. That is the whole architecture. Not victory, not clarity, not a durable resolution. An exit, wrapped in triumphal language, sold through market numbers and slogans.

The insider quote is the tell. “Everyone just wants to get out of it” is not statesmanship. It is institutional exhaustion. The elite consensus here is not confidence in the outcome; it is relief that the problem can be pushed off the table. That is how bad policy survives: not because it is persuasive, but because powerful people are tired.

The Performance of Toughness

Trump’s defense rests on a crude equation: if stocks are up and oil is down, then the deal must be strong. That is not a strategic argument. It is a substitution of market mood for statecraft. He is trying to convert a temporary economic signal into proof of geopolitical seriousness.

This is how authoritarian-style politics often presents itself in miniature: not through formal dictatorship, but through the constant demand that reality conform to the leader’s self-description. Criticism becomes disloyalty. Doubt becomes stupidity. Consequence becomes a branding problem.

The Larger Pattern

The pattern is not confusion. It is managed misdirection. A powerful actor makes a consequential decision, then frames resistance as irrational so the decision itself never has to be defended on the merits. The damage is not only in the policy choice, but in the method: authority used to evade accountability, then turned outward as ridicule.

That is the real story here. Not that Trump is being criticized. It is that he treats criticism as an obstacle to be shouted down while he sells retreat as triumph.
