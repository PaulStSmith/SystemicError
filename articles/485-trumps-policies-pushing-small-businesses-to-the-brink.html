Trump’s policies pushing small businesses 'to the brink'

Based on an article by Alex Henderson

Paulo SantosJune 21, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Pro-Business for the Powerful, Punishing for Everyone Else

The Promise

Donald Trump sold himself as a defender of small business, the kind of president who supposedly understood the scrape of keeping a shop open and payroll intact. Eighteen months into his second term, the reporting says the opposite: small-business sentiment has collapsed, hiring has stalled, expansion plans have been shelved, and owners are describing survival as the daily job.

Who Actually Holds Power

The relevant power is not with the café owner or the remodeling contractor trying to make rent. It sits with the president, his administration, and the policy apparatus that drives tariffs, energy shocks, labor disruptions, and war. If a White House can help push up fuel costs, make import duties bite harder, and then announce a preliminary deal only after the damage is done, it owns the outcome whether it admits it or not.

That is the point the “pro-business” label is designed to erase. It treats power as a slogan instead of a set of decisions with measurable costs.

The Damage Is Not Mysterious

The source gives the mechanism plainly: the months-long war with Iran pushed up fuel and material costs; inflation accelerated; interest-rate cuts look less likely; tariffs are raising prices; immigrant labor interruptions are adding another layer of strain. Large corporations still post solid earnings and the stock market booms, while smaller firms, lacking cash reserves and bargaining power, are freezing hiring and postponing expansion.

That is not an economic accident. It is a distributional choice. The system protects scale and punishes fragility.

The Favorite Cover Story

The administration’s ally at the Small Business Administration, Kelly Loeffler, repeats the usual pageant of optimism about “America First” and the “American dream.” That line works only if you ignore the people the policy is grinding down. The article does not hide the reality: small businesses with fewer than 10 workers have been shedding employees for years; bankruptcies rose after new import duties; owners are raising prices, then fearing they will lose already-fragile customers if they raise them again.

This is how political language launders harm. It rebrands pressure as patriotism and calls the damage a temporary challenge, as if the business owner’s balance sheet were just collateral in a morale campaign.

The Smaller Actor, The Bigger Sacrifice

Bruce Jovaag in Missouri and Francesca Costa in Houston are not the agents of this crisis. They are the ones absorbing it. Their testimony matters because it shows who actually pays when the state turns volatility into policy: the operators without reserves, not the firms large enough to absorb shocks, hedge risk, or wait out demand destruction.

The article’s framing is weak where it lets the administration’s self-description linger too long. “Pro-business” is not analysis. It is camouflage for a governing style that concentrates advantage upward and pushes the resulting pain onto businesses least able to survive it.

Systemic Error

This story is bigger than one president or one war. It shows a familiar political pattern: power promises broad prosperity, then uses macroeconomic force to protect the already strong and discipline the weak. The rhetoric stays warm and managerial. The consequences are cold and concrete.

That is the recurring trick of modern political economy: call it support for small business while transferring risk onto small business, then ask the public to mistake the language of help for the exercise of power.
