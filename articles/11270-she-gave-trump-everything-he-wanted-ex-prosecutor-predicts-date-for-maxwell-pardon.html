‘She gave Trump everything he wanted’: Ex-prosecutor predicts date for Maxwell pardon

A former state attorney predicted that not only will Ghislaine Maxwell be pardoned by President Donald Trump, but said exactly when such a pardon would take place.

Maxwell is currently serving a 20-year prison sentence for sex-trafficking charges related to Jeffrey Epstein, who was also facing sex-trafficking charges until his death in 2019. Epstein was alleged to have operated a blackmail operation targeting powerful figures, and Maxwell to have been Epstein’s accomplice.

Trump has been caught up in scandal over his past ties with both Epstein and Maxwell and, in an effort to quell the uproar, particularly among his own base, a key member of his administration, Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche, recently interviewed Maxwell with the DOJ now releasing the transcripts and audio of their conversations .

“As to what (Maxwell) wants, she wants a pardon,” said Dave Aronberg, former state attorney for Palm Beach, Florida,on MSNBC.

“It's been dangled towards her, and, I do think she probably gets it at the end of this administration because she gave Trump everything he wanted in that transcript! There's nothing else for her to do in her minimum security prison except perhaps wait for, hopefully, in her mind, a pardon.”

Speculation has grown that Trump may offer Maxwell a pardon in exchange for information that exonerates – or at least appears to exonerate – him for any wrongdoing as it relates to Epstein. One expert suggested Trump may offer Maxwell a pardon in exchange for damaging information on his political opponents, including former President Bill Clinton.

“She does have her appeal pending before the Supreme Court, but her best chance of getting sprung from prison is through Donald Trump,” Aronberg continued.

“He pardoned Steve Bannon on the eve of his leaving office, why wouldn't he pardon Ghislaine Maxwell? Trump doesn't care about the Republican Party, he wants to award those who are good to him; he's transactional.”

Our Analysis:

The Alleged Pardon Strategy: A Dissection

The recent speculation surrounding a potential pardon for Ghislaine Maxwell by President Donald Trump is not just a storm in a teacup—it's a tempest teeming with implications, both legal and moral. At the heart of this storm is the insinuation that Trump, mired in controversy due to his historical connections with convicted sex trafficker Jeffrey Epstein and his accomplice, Ghislaine Maxwell, may resort to presidential pardoning powers to manipulate the legal and political narrative to his advantage.

The Art of the Deal: Legal Pardons as Bargaining Chips

The assertion by Dave Aronberg, a former state attorney for Palm Beach, Florida, that Maxwell's pardon has been "dangled" before her, paints a picture of presidential pardons being used as nothing short of bargaining chips. This is not just legally dubious; it's ethically abhorrent. The suggestion that Maxwell could be pardoned in exchange for information that either exonerates Trump or implicates his political adversaries is a stark illustration of the transactional nature Trump is accused of—where loyalty and utility to him personally outweigh any considerations of justice or morality.

A Pattern of Controversial Pardons

This wouldn't be Trump's first rodeo with controversial pardons. The mention of Steve Bannon's pardon is a poignant reminder that Trump's pardoning history is marked by decisions that seem more aligned with personal allegiance and political strategy than with any discernible commitment to justice. To consider pardoning Maxwell, implicated in heinous crimes, further underscores a disturbing willingness to leverage presidential power in ways that fundamentally undermine the integrity of the justice system.

The Dangerous Implications of a Maxwell Pardon

Maxwell's involvement in a sex-trafficking ring that preyed on vulnerable individuals makes the idea of her pardon a sickening prospect. It's not just about the miscarriage of justice for Maxwell's many victims; it's about what such a pardon would signify regarding the value—or lack thereof—that the administration places on the rule of law, the severity of sex trafficking crimes, and the suffering of victims.

Moreover, the very notion that Maxwell could be considered for a pardon because she "gave Trump everything he wanted" in their conversation, as Aronberg suggests, is emblematic of a broader issue: the commodification of justice, where freedom can be traded for favorable information, irrespective of the truth or moral righteousness of that exchange.

In Conclusion: A System in Disrepute

The speculative pardon of Ghislaine Maxwell is not merely a legal anomaly; it's a litmus test for the state of American democracy and its justice system. If the presidency can be wielded to pardon individuals deeply implicated in crimes of such a vile nature, purely for political or personal gain, then the system is not just flawed—it's broken.

This scenario, with its undercurrents of transactional justice and political expediency, should alarm not only those concerned with the integrity of the legal system but also anyone vested in the principles of democracy and moral governance. The potential pardon of Ghislaine Maxwell is not just about one individual's freedom; it's about the soul of the American justice system and the values it purports to uphold.
