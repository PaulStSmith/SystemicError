The surreal reason Trump doesn't care about the USS Lincoln

A President Who Performs War Is Not Commanding It

The Story Beneath the Carrier

The reporting is about the USS Abraham Lincoln: nine months at sea, at least two sailors reportedly trying to kill themselves by jumping overboard, and families describing shortages, spoiled food, broken toilets, no hot water, and missing basics like soap and toothpaste. That is the surface. The political reality is sharper: this is what it looks like when a president turns war into theater and leaves the people carrying it to rot.

Power Sits Upstairs

The institution with actual power here is the White House. Not the crew. Not the families. Not the commentators parsing “deteriorating conditions” as if decay arrived by weather. The decisions that matter are made by the commander-in-chief: whether to sustain a deployment, whether to relieve a ship, whether to treat service members as human beings or as disposable props in a staged performance of toughness.

The source gets closest to the truth when it names Trump as the one who “doesn’t care.” That is the center of the story. Neglect is not an accident of administration. It is a mode of governance.

Readiness Is the Decoy

The familiar Washington move is to convert a political failure into a management problem. Suddenly the issue is “military readiness,” as if this were a maintenance lapse and not the consequence of an endless war driven by impulse, no strategy, and no exit plan. That language matters because it softens responsibility. It turns deliberate abandonment into bureaucratic drift.

But the source itself points past that frame. If troops are overworked, underfed, and demoralized, the problem is not abstract readiness. The problem is command. A president who wants appearances more than outcomes will always produce this: a force that looks expensive and behaves broken.

The Paper Tiger Presidency

The deeper indictment is that Trump is not even trying to win. He is trying to look like he is winning. That distinction is the whole game. Real strategy requires discipline, sacrifice, planning, and the willingness to absorb failure. Trump’s politics are built on the opposite: performative dominance, televised lying, and the fantasy that image can substitute for power.

That is why the source’s Iran example lands harder than its rhetoric does. If Tehran believes it can ignore him, that is not a communications problem. It is a signal that coercive credibility has collapsed. A president can spend like a militarist and still be treated like a showman if adversaries think his threats are empty.

The Cost of Pretending

The most revealing line in the source is the one about the price tag: roughly $9 trillion spent on defense since 2016, producing a “deeply flawed, deteriorating force.” That is not a budgeting glitch. It is a warning about American militarism as a fraud with a national-security logo.

The money flows. The hardware accumulates. The sailors still eat rotten food. The ship still sits too long at sea. The president still decorates his office and performs grandeur while the people he sent into the world are treated like collateral in his self-promotion. This is not strength wasted by bad luck. It is power degraded by vanity.

What Democrats Should Say

There is a political opening here, and it is not “the president mishandled a deployment.” That is too small. The correct indictment is that Trump treats the military as a prop, the country as a stage, and war as a branding exercise. Democrats should not narrow the issue to oversight theater or institutional cleanup. They should name the governing logic: endless war, executive contempt, and the routine sacrifice of enlisted people to presidential ego.

The Larger Pattern

This is how authoritarian politics works in practice. It does not always arrive with jackboots and slogans. Sometimes it arrives with neglect, luxury, and lies. A commander-in-chief who cares about appearance more than consequence will always create the same result: decay below, delusion above, and a country that keeps paying for both.
