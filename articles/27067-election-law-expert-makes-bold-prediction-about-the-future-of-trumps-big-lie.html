Election law expert makes bold prediction about the future of Trump’s big lie

Based on an article by Brenda Alexander

Paulo SantosNovember 4, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower sets the boundaryA tactical retreat is not accountabilityThe framing softens the conductThe lie remains available

What happened

AlterNet reports that election-law expert Rick Hasen expects Republicans around President Donald Trump to keep “dancing around” his false claim that he won the 2020 election because they do not want to offend him. Despite the headline, the article supplies no clear prediction about when or whether Trump’s “big lie” will disappear.

Republican candidates and strategists reportedly see election denial as a liability in general elections, where voters are more focused on the economy. California gubernatorial candidate Steve Hilton has rejected Trump’s voter-fraud claims, while Senator Lisa Murkowski criticized a Justice Department lawsuit concerning an Alaska ballot issue involving two candidates named Dan Sullivan.

The retreat is selective. According to the reporting, some Republicans are distancing themselves publicly, some are staying quiet, and advisers are urging Trump to discuss more current issues. The party has not reached a principled reckoning; it is making an electoral calculation.

Power sets the boundary

Trump holds the institutional power identified in this story: the presidency, control over the MAGA base, and enough influence within the Republican Party that candidates and consultants fear contradicting him directly. Hasen’s description of advisers avoiding offense exposes the hierarchy. Trump establishes the permissible fiction; everyone else decides how delicately to evade it.

The officials and operatives around him still have agency. They can reject election denial plainly, as Hilton reportedly has, or challenge related government action, as Murkowski has. Those who remain silent are not trapped by confusion. They are choosing political safety within a system that rewards submission to the party’s dominant figure.

A tactical retreat is not accountability

The Republicans quoted here object mainly because the message is losing usefulness. Consultant Samuel Chen argues that economic conditions will drive more voters than “election integrity.” Consultant Christopher Nicholas says 2020 is old and that newer subjects are available.

That is not a repudiation of election denial on factual or democratic grounds. It is campaign triage. The concern is that the claim distracts from issues more likely to win votes—not that rejecting legitimate outcomes corrodes public trust and licenses future attacks on election administration.

The framing softens the conduct

AlterNet describes Trump as “flirting” with election denialism and Republicans as walking a “fine line.” Those phrases turn sustained false claims and calculated avoidance into awkward social behavior. The relevant actors are not struggling to understand what happened in 2020. According to the article, they are deciding how much truth they can state without angering Trump.

The headline also oversells Hasen’s remark as a bold prediction. What the reporting actually establishes is narrower: Republicans are trying to move away from an electorally damaging message while avoiding a direct confrontation with the man who continues promoting it.

The lie remains available

A message abandoned because it polls badly has not been discredited inside the institution that amplified it. It has merely been shelved. If Republican leaders refuse to say clearly that Trump lost in 2020, they preserve the underlying permission structure: election results may be treated as legitimate when convenient and suspect when defeat requires an explanation.

That is the larger pattern. Party elites tolerate propaganda while it mobilizes supporters, then quietly change subjects when it becomes costly. Their restraint is not powerlessness. It is an exercise of agency designed to preserve access to power without accepting responsibility for how that power was built.
