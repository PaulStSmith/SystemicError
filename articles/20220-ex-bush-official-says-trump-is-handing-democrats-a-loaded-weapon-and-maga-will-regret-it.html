Ex-Bush official says Trump is handing Democrats a loaded weapon — and MAGA will regret it

Based on an article by David McAfee

Paulo SantosJuly 2, 20263 min read

Rule by Decree Always Finds a New Target

The Source, Briefly

A former Republican State Department official, Kim R. Holmes, warned that Trump’s habit of ruling through executive decrees, memos, and rule-breaking will not stay trapped on the right side of history. The immediate argument in the piece is simple: if one president uses reversible power to cut, purge, and improvise, the next president inherits the same weaponry.

The Real Center of Power

The actor with actual institutional power here is the president. Not the commenters, not the analysts, not the MAGA audience cheering the spectacle. Trump, by the article’s own account, is the one choosing to govern through directives instead of durable law. That matters because executive power is not a vibe; it is a mechanism. Once used to replace legislation, it trains the office to behave like a temporary machine of personal will.

Holmes says Trump “addresses a legitimate problem” badly, by using memo power instead of passing permanent law. That is the polite version. The harder truth is that this is not a failure of style. It is a decision to prefer unilateral action because unilateral action is faster, less accountable, and easier to reverse without paying the cost of governance.

Complicity Is the Point

The article centers a warning to MAGA supporters that the damage may boomerang onto them when Democrats inherit the same powers. That warning is not wrong, but it still softens the present. It turns an active dismantling of institutional restraint into a future regret story, as if the real problem is that the weapon might later be used by someone else.

That is misdirection by timeline. The harm is not hypothetical. The harm is the decision to normalize spoils politics now and call it strategy. When Trump’s allies accept that, they are not innocent bystanders awaiting consequence. They are consenting to a system that treats public institutions as loot.

Civil Service as Sacrifice

The thread’s strongest concrete example is the federal workforce. Barb McQuade’s warning about the Slaughter case points to the danger plainly: overturning precedent and returning to a spoils system lets a president “clean house” every four years and destroy an independent civil service.

That is the real institutional casualty. Not a culture war abstraction, but the personnel structure that keeps government from becoming a personal property management scheme. If firing half the civil service becomes normal, then professional administration becomes optional and loyalty becomes the point. That is not administrative reform. It is political intimidation with paperwork.

The Sugar High of Breaking Things

Holmes calls the satisfaction of rule-breaking a “sugar high,” which is accurate in a limited sense and revealing in a larger one. The rush comes from visible destruction: norms shattered, procedures bypassed, opponents humiliated. But the article still treats this as a boomerang problem, when it is also a domination problem. The aim is not only to govern poorly. It is to prove that restraint is for losers and that institutions can be bent by whoever controls the Oval Office.

That is why the excuse about progressive rule-breaking matters so little. It is a defensive alibi for escalation. Once every violation is justified as retaliation, no one is accountable for initiating the break. Power gets laundered through grievance.

The Larger Pattern

The pattern here is authoritarian crudity dressed up as pragmatic governance. Strip away the partisan costume and the logic is familiar: concentrate power, break constraints, normalize reversibility, and train the public to confuse speed with legitimacy. The article is right that such power will not remain exclusive forever. It is wrong if it implies the real lesson is merely “be careful what you wish for.”

The deeper lesson is worse: when a president turns law into a disposable instrument, the state stops being a shared framework and becomes a temporary prize. Whoever wins next inherits not a government, but a method of extraction.
