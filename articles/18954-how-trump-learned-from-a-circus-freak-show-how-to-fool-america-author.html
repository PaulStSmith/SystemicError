How Trump learned from a circus 'freak show' how to fool America: author

By Paulo Santos

Journalist and author Kurt Andersen believes he’s identified the secret behind Donald Trump’s political success: his lifelong stupidity combined with an American culture that’s vulnerable to being conned.

Speaking on The Daily Beast Podcast, Andersen said his theory was on full display in Trump’s recent foreign policy moves, which he said are fundamentally uninformed. Regarding Trump’s Iran strategy, Andersen said that Trump has “no clue” about the region’s history and dynamics.

“He’s an idiot,” he said. “He’s always been stupid. And his stupidity has been an under-remarked-upon, unheralded part of his — along with the lying, along with the mental disorders — the stupidity is important.”

Drawing from his book “Fantasyland: How America Went Haywire; A 500-Year History,” Andersen argues that America possesses a particular vulnerability to manipulation rooted in its religious and cultural history. The foundational American belief, he contends, is, “I can believe what I want because it’s the truth and it feels right.”

And Trump is an expert at exploiting that.

“All that stuff, which is not uniquely American, but it is definingly American,” he said. “America has always been the world leader in that kind of weak-mindedness and slippery sense of the difference between reality and fiction.”

Andersen drew parallels to P.T. Barnum, the 19th-century showman who successfully marketed obvious hoaxes by maintaining plausible deniability. When confronted about false claims, Barnum would respond: “How do you know it’s not? If you can’t prove it’s not and people enjoy it, then that’s entertainment.”

As an example, the author described a 161-year-old woman who was paraded to circus-goers — and which was obviously a hoax.

“He didn’t hide it,” he said. “He didn’t pretend it was true. He said, ‘How do you know it’s not?’ [That] was basically his response to people. If you can’t prove it’s not and people enjoy it, then that’s entertainment.”

Andersen characterized Trump’s relationship with truth as similarly ambiguous.

“It’s just such an American story. This combination of religiosity, I guess sincere, and this kind of hucksterism. And that’s part of the story of America and how Trump came to be, even though he is irreligious and a nonbeliever, I think, pretty clearly.”

Andersen identified evangelical Christians as Trump’s most devoted supporters, explaining that in a cultural environment where citizens believe “any old thing you want and hear and disbelief in things that are true, anything goes.”

“It always tended to be the case in America a little bit, but then it got out of control in the last 60 years and, along with the internet, gave us Donald Trump,” he concluded.

Our Analysis:

The Misdiagnosed Roots of Trump’s Political Ascendancy

Kurt Andersen’s recent commentary on Donald Trump’s political success, as reported by The Daily Beast, is a provocative blend of personal attack and cultural critique. While Andersen’s observations about American cultural vulnerabilities and Trump’s exploitation of these traits are compelling, they risk oversimplifying and misdirecting the responsibility for Trump’s rise to power. Let’s dissect the narrative, identify the actual holders of power, and assess the framing’s accuracy.

Who Holds Institutional Power?

The discussion around Trump’s success often veers into personal characteristics—his intelligence, his rhetoric, his relationship with the truth. However, the real power behind Trump’s ascendancy lies not solely within the man himself but within the institutions that enabled his rise: the Republican Party, mainstream media, and, critically, the Electoral College system. These entities have institutional power and decision-making capabilities that far exceed those of the individual.

Decision Makers Behind the Outcome

Trump’s political success stemmed from a series of calculated decisions by the Republican Party to rally behind his candidacy, despite early resistance, and the decision by major media outlets to give unprecedented coverage to his campaign, often at the expense of substantive policy discussion. Additionally, the Electoral College, a foundational mechanism of American democracy, facilitated Trump’s 2016 victory despite his loss of the popular vote. These are the decision-makers who produced the outcome, not merely Trump’s personal attributes or the general susceptibility of the American populace to “being conned.”

Misdirection of Responsibility

Andersen’s framing leans heavily on the idea that Trump’s stupidity and America’s cultural “weak-mindedness” are primarily to blame for his political success. This perspective, while it contains grains of truth regarding American cultural characteristics and Trump’s appeal, misdirects responsibility away from the systemic and institutional flaws that allowed him to rise to power. It places undue emphasis on Trump’s personal attributes and a generalized American gullibility, overshadowing the critical roles played by party politics, media complicity, and electoral systems.

Rejecting False Equivalence and Lazy Narratives

While Andersen’s analogy to P.T. Barnum and the historical critique of American culture are thought-provoking, they risk creating a false equivalence between Trump’s methods and those of a showman like Barnum. Trump’s actions and decisions as a political leader have far-reaching consequences beyond mere entertainment or individual gullibility. The narrative, therefore, requires a more nuanced analysis that takes into account the complex interplay of individual behavior, systemic failures, and institutional power dynamics.

Conclusion

In essence, while Andersen’s critique of American cultural susceptibility to manipulation holds some merit, it falls short of accurately diagnosing the reasons behind Trump’s political ascendancy. A more comprehensive analysis must consider the systemic and institutional enablers of his rise. By focusing primarily on Trump’s personal characteristics and broadly painting American culture as uniquely prone to deception, we risk overlooking the critical structural reforms needed to address the underlying issues in American democracy. The real story behind Trump’s success is not just about individual stupidity or cultural vulnerability—it’s about systemic error.
