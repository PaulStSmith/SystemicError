Trump rages at ‘failing’ NYT in 2 AM posting spree

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 12, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power is in the presidencyThe complaint concerns HabbaOutrage as misdirectionThe loyalty pipelineThe grievance system

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump posted twice on Truth Social early Wednesday, attacking The New York Times over its descriptions of a C-SPAN call and a settlement involving a former waitress at his Bedminster golf club.

Trump said his eight-minute appearance on C-SPAN’s new prime-time program had been scheduled through White House Communications at the network’s request. The Times reportedly described it as “apparently without notice,” while CNBC called it unannounced and quoted the host reacting as though the call were unexpected. AlterNet says Trump’s account was not independently confirmed.

Trump also objected to the Times covering a sexual-harassment settlement negotiated by his former lawyer Alina Habba, stressing that he was not involved in the underlying suit. He was not a defendant, and the Times did not accuse him of wrongdoing. The actual development was a 23-page disciplinary complaint filed Tuesday by New Jersey’s Office of Attorney Ethics, accusing Habba of divided loyalties and alleging that terms in the $15,000 nondisclosure agreement violated state law.

The power is in the presidency

The central actor is not merely an aggrieved media subject. Trump holds the presidency and commands the White House communications apparatus. When he uses that position and his platform to denounce reporting as lies, the power imbalance matters: the government’s chief executive is directing public hostility at institutions covering him and his associates.

That does not make every disputed description defensible. Whether the C-SPAN call was formally scheduled is a factual question. But Trump’s response converted a narrow dispute over “apparently without notice” into a sweeping accusation that the Times lies to demean him. The escalation overwhelms the underlying issue because overwhelming the issue is politically useful.

The complaint concerns Habba

On the Bedminster matter, Trump rebutted an accusation the Times reportedly did not make. The article identified a settlement at his club involving Habba; Trump answered that the harassment suit did not involve him personally. Both can be true, and AlterNet reports that the Times expressly did not accuse him of wrongdoing.

The institutional action belongs to New Jersey ethics regulators. Their complaint alleges that Habba, then Trump’s personal lawyer and a dues-paying member of the club, had divided loyalties while helping secure the 2021 settlement for Alice Bianco, who had accused her supervisor of sexual harassment. The regulators also allege that provisions in the accompanying nondisclosure agreement violated New Jersey law. Those are allegations in a disciplinary proceeding, not findings of guilt.

Outrage as misdirection

Trump’s post shifts attention from the ethics complaint to his own claimed victimization. The relevant questions concern Habba’s professional duties, the legality of the settlement terms, and the treatment of a 21-year-old employee who alleged harassment. Trump instead placed himself at the center of a story that, by his own account and AlterNet’s reporting, did not name him as a defendant.

That maneuver directs scrutiny upward at the press and away from the lawyer whose conduct regulators challenged. It also recasts accurate proximity—his club, his former personal lawyer, a settlement she negotiated—as a fabricated allegation against him. The distinction between association and culpability matters. So does the distinction between correcting culpability and trying to make the entire matter disappear behind a grievance.

The loyalty pipeline

Habba’s trajectory gives the story its larger political significance. She moved from serving as Trump’s personal lawyer to holding federal prosecutorial authority as interim and acting U.S. attorney for New Jersey from March until December 8, 2025. The state complaint concerns conduct predating that appointment, and AlterNet reports that regulators had been investigating her since early 2024.

No inference of guilt follows from that timeline. But it exposes a governing pattern: personal loyalty can become a route into institutional power while professional accountability proceeds elsewhere. A lawyer closely tied to Trump gained authority within the federal justice system even as state regulators examined whether her earlier conduct violated ethical rules.

The grievance system

These posts are not important because they appeared after midnight. They are important because they show how presidential power can be used to collapse separate matters into one permanent persecution narrative. A disputed description of a television call and a formal ethics complaint against a former lawyer become interchangeable proof that the press is supposedly corrupt.

The systemic error is allowing the loudest powerful actor to dictate the subject. The subject is not Trump’s irritation. It is how a president uses grievance to redirect scrutiny, how loyalty connects private representation to public office, and how accountability for powerful associates can be buried beneath an attack on whoever reported it.
