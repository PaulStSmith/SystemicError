Liberal Supreme Court Justices reveal unexpected shift behind closed doors

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosSeptember 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power remains with the majorityExplanation is not accountabilityEmergency procedure becomes governing powerThe weaker actors are not the causeThe systemic error

What happened

AlterNet reports that Justices Elena Kagan and Ketanji Brown Jackson see a limited improvement in how the Supreme Court handles emergency appeals: more justices are publicly explaining fast-track decisions instead of issuing consequential orders without reasoning. Jackson called that shift “good and positive” because lower courts and the public need guidance to understand and apply the Court’s decisions.

The change concerns process, not outcomes. The article says the Court’s conservative majority has repeatedly sided with the Trump administration in emergency cases, including a ruling permitting the administration to cut National Institutes of Health funding. In dissent, Jackson accused the majority of changing its rules to produce administration-friendly results.

AlterNet also recounts Kagan’s criticism of political rhetoric intended to intimidate judges. She distinguished legitimate criticism from intimidation and connected such rhetoric to threats against justices.

The power remains with the majority

Kagan and Jackson can dissent, speak publicly and expose inconsistencies. They cannot make five votes appear. The Court’s conservative bloc retains the institutional power to decide emergency applications, determine how much reasoning to disclose and shape the instructions lower courts must follow.

That makes the reported shift real but narrow. Written explanations improve transparency; they do not redistribute authority or reverse the decisions already benefiting the administration. The same majority that controls the outcome also controls how fully it accounts for that outcome.

Explanation is not accountability

AlterNet’s framing risks making a procedural concession sound like a substantive retreat. A majority that explains why it grants extraordinary relief is more legible than one that offers silence. It is not necessarily more restrained, consistent or accountable.

The central question is what the explanations reveal. If the Court applies stable principles across cases, written opinions help establish law. If it changes standards according to which party seeks relief, more prose merely documents the inconsistency. Jackson’s “Calvinball” criticism alleges precisely that deeper failure: not an absence of words, but an absence of fixed rules.

Emergency procedure becomes governing power

The administration’s repeated emergency appeals place major policy disputes on an accelerated track. According to the reporting, the NIH funding case reached a result allowing Trump to act unilaterally while the liberal justices objected to the majority’s reasoning.

That procedure matters because speed favors the actor already holding executive power. An emergency order can permit government action before ordinary litigation fully tests its legality. Even when temporary in form, such permission can produce immediate institutional and material consequences. Funding can stop; programs can be disrupted; lower courts can be left reconstructing doctrine from abbreviated instructions.

The weaker actors are not the cause

The story should not be reduced to liberal justices finally persuading conservative colleagues to communicate better. Kagan and Jackson did not create the underlying problem. Lower courts and the public did not misunderstand sufficiently clear rulings. The majority chose when to provide reasons, while the Trump administration chose to press emergency appeals seeking rapid permission for executive action.

Nor should intimidation be blurred into generic political “tension.” Kagan’s distinction is useful: criticism challenges judicial conduct; intimidation attempts to influence judges through fear. AlterNet reports her warning that political figures and their followers have used language she viewed as intimidating and that justices have faced real threats. The article does not establish who made those threats or prove that particular rhetoric caused them, so the connection should not be overstated. But the institutional consequence is plain: judges are operating amid pressure that extends beyond legal argument.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is rule by exception. Executive officials seek rapid clearance, the Supreme Court majority supplies it through an emergency process, and dissenters are left to explain why the governing standards appear unstable. Public reasoning may make that system easier to inspect, but inspection is not correction.

A court does not earn legitimacy merely by attaching explanations to exercises of power. Legitimacy depends on whether its rules constrain allies as well as opponents. If outcomes dictate doctrine rather than doctrine constraining outcomes, the problem is not a shadow over the docket. It is the use of judicial authority to make political preference look like law.
