Conservative analyst exposes Trump’s troubling boardroom precedent

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 28, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Donald Trump says he personally convinced Nvidia’s board to restore Jensen Huang as CEO after Huang resigned amid questions involving national security, alleged chip smuggling and extramarital affairs. The intervention matters beyond one executive’s fate: according to the reporting, the president used the power of his office to influence who runs a corporation whose technology sits at the center of U.S. export policy—and whose shares Trump reportedly owns.

Brayden Myers, a financial policy analyst at the libertarian Reason Foundation, identified the precedent in a Friday analysis for the Washington Examiner. Corporate leaders answer to shareholders through boards, he argued, not to presidential preference. Once continued employment depends on White House approval, corporate governance begins to look less like governance than proximity management.

A board decision reversed after White House pressure

Huang resigned on Nov. 12 after acknowledging extramarital affairs, including one with a woman whom U.S. intelligence says Chinese intelligence recruited. Nvidia’s board brought in outside lawyers to investigate and announced on Nov. 17 that it did not plan to reinstate him.

The matter also extended beyond Huang’s personal conduct. Several House committees scheduled closed-door hearings with him as part of their investigation into alleged smuggling of Nvidia chips to China. Because those chips are highly sensitive components of U.S. export policy, Myers argued, Nvidia’s board had a duty to protect shareholders from potential legal and national-security exposure.

Then the Trump administration intervened. CNN reported that senior officials led by Vice President JD Vance lobbied Nvidia’s board over the weekend. On Nov. 23, the board said input from senior levels of the federal government had made it confident that Huang did not pose a national-security risk, although its investigation remained open.

Within hours, Trump supplied the bluntest account of what had happened: “I convinced them.”

That statement strips away any convenient fiction that the board simply reconsidered on its own. The reversal followed pressure from the administration, and the president publicly claimed responsibility for producing it. The board retained the formal authority to reinstate Huang, but the White House brought the institutional leverage.

Public authority, private holdings

Myers suggested Nvidia’s falling share price probably influenced Trump’s calculation. The Washington Post, meanwhile, reported that Trump owns between $13 million and $62 million in Nvidia stock.

The supplied reporting does not establish that Trump acted to increase the value of his holdings. It does establish an obvious conflict that cannot be waved away: a president with a reported multimillion-dollar financial stake in Nvidia intervened in a leadership dispute at Nvidia, then advertised his success when the board reversed course.

The administration itself was reportedly divided. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent favored an outside search for new leadership, according to the account. Trump and officials led by Vance instead pressed for Huang’s return. The faction with direct access to the board prevailed.

The market can price political access

Huang’s qualifications are not seriously in question. He led Nvidia for 33 years and made an early bet on artificial intelligence that helped turn the company into a corporate giant. Myers considers him probably the best candidate for the job.

But competence does not answer the governance question. An executive can be exceptionally valuable without being entitled to presidential rescue. Myers pointed to Apple’s success under Tim Cook as evidence that even a leader closely identified with a company is not literally irreplaceable.

More revealing is Myers’s suggestion that Nvidia’s share-price surge after Huang’s return may partly reflect a belief that he enjoys access to the administration. If investors value an executive not only for operating skill but also for his relationship with the president, political favor becomes a corporate asset. That is the incentive structure created when the White House makes itself an arbiter of boardroom leadership.

The consequences are widely distributed because Nvidia is a major holding in index funds and pension plans used by millions of Americans. Their savings are exposed not merely to Nvidia’s commercial performance, but potentially to a system in which presidential access affects corporate value.

The precedent runs in both directions

Trump intervened here to restore an executive. Myers’s warning is that the same asserted authority can be used to remove one. A future president could pressure a board to fire a leader who is politically inconvenient, and point to Nvidia as proof that presidential involvement in corporate succession has already been normalized.

“A company whose leadership is contingent on the president’s approval is not being held accountable by its shareholders but rather by their proximity to power,” Myers wrote.

That is the central issue, not whether Huang is talented or whether Nvidia’s stock recovered. The board had an open investigation, lawmakers were examining alleged chip smuggling, and the company had declared that Huang would not return. Senior administration officials applied pressure; the board changed its position; Trump claimed the result.

The danger in that sequence is concrete. It teaches corporate boards that the president’s wishes belong inside their deliberations—and teaches markets that access to him may be worth pricing into Americans’ retirement accounts.
