'Doesn’t make any sense': Guardsmen blast MAGA governor over protest deployment

Gov. Greg Abbott's (R-TX) decision to activate thousands of National Guard troops for protests has left guardsmen scrambling, with at least one officer slamming the move as senseless.

Abbott said more than 5,000 Texas National Guard troops — along with over 2,000 state police officers — were deployed across the state ahead of what's expected to be large-scale protests against federal immigration raids and President Donald Trump’s actions. Nationwide "No Kings" protests were scheduled for the weekend, including in Houston, Austin, Dallas, and San Antonio.

The Austin American-Statesman reported Friday afternoon that internal memos showed military leaders were forced to scramble to find and train enough personnel for their new orders.

Of the 5,000, half were pulled off a border security mission. The memos illustrated what the report characterized as a "potentially rushed timeline for training on crowd control and de-escalation methods."

Two Guardsmen expressed serious concerns to the newspaper over the deployment, including an officer who said they should "never have been mobilized in the first place."

“I was shocked that they were mobilizing the amount of people that they were mobilizing,” the officer told the Statesman. “It doesn’t make any sense to me why we would be activated in such large numbers against the citizens we’re sworn to protect.”

The officer lamented, “Unless someone does something and grows a backbone in Congress or somewhere else, they’re going to continue to use us as political tools."

The second National Guard member told the outlet that despite some characterizations that Abbott is “being cautious,” the deployment “does strike me as a suppression of free speech ahead of time.”

“Did I swear an oath to the president? Did I swear an oath to the governor?” the member asked. “Or did I swear an oath to our basic, inalienable rights?”

Our Analysis:

Analysis: The Militarization of Protest Response in Texas

Governor Greg Abbott's decision to deploy over 5,000 Texas National Guard troops, in addition to more than 2,000 state police officers, in anticipation of protests against federal immigration raids and President Donald Trump’s policies, is a glaring example of what could be dubbed the militarization of protest response. The move, ostensibly aimed at maintaining order, has been met with internal skepticism and outright criticism, suggesting a deep misalignment between the governor’s office and the individuals tasked with carrying out these orders.

Rushed Deployment and Training Concerns

First, let's dissect the nature of this deployment. According to the Austin American-Statesman, military leaders were "forced to scramble" to meet the governor's demands. This scramble involved pulling half of the troops from a border security mission, underlining the ad-hoc nature of this decision. The report points to a "potentially rushed timeline for training on crowd control and de-escalation methods." This raises a significant question: How effective can such a deployment be if the personnel involved are not adequately trained for the specific context they are being thrust into? It's not just about having bodies in a location; it's about having personnel equipped with the skills to de-escalate tensions—something that rushed training cannot guarantee.

Voices of Dissent Within the Ranks

The criticism from within the ranks of the National Guard itself is particularly telling. One officer's assertion that the troops "should never have been mobilized in the first place" speaks volumes about the perceived necessity and appropriateness of this decision. This officer's shock at the scale of mobilization against U.S. citizens underscores a fundamental concern: the use of military force against peaceful protests. Their fear of the National Guard being used as "political tools" is a damning indictment of Governor Abbott's decision, suggesting an abuse of power for political ends rather than genuine public safety concerns.

Another National Guard member's doubts further illuminate the issue, framing the deployment as a potential "suppression of free speech." This sentiment touches on a core tenet of democracy—the right to protest. When military and police forces are used preemptively against protesters, it sends a chilling message about the state of free speech and the right to dissent.

The Oath to Protect

At the heart of this controversy is a profound question about loyalty and duty. The National Guard member's rhetorical questions—"Did I swear an oath to the president? Did I swear an oath to the governor? Or did I swear an oath to our basic, inalienable rights?"—highlight the existential crisis facing those ordered to carry out these deployments. It is a reminder that the military and police swear an oath to uphold the Constitution and protect the country's citizens, not to serve the political interests of individual leaders.

Conclusion: The Misuse of Military Might

Governor Abbott's decision to activate such a large contingent of the National Guard in anticipation of protests is not just a logistical nightmare; it's a troubling indication of how quickly the lines between maintaining public order and suppressing free speech can blur. The internal backlash and concerns raised by those within the National Guard underscore the danger of deploying military forces as a knee-jerk reaction to anticipated protests. This strategy not only risks the efficacy and morale of the National Guard but also undermines the fundamental democratic rights of the citizens they are sworn to protect.

In a democracy, the right to protest is sacred. The preemptive militarization of the response to these protests is a systemic error that needs immediate correction. Unless we wish to tread down a path where political dissent is met with overwhelming force, it's imperative that decisions like these are scrutinized, criticized, and ultimately, reversed. The voices within the Texas National Guard speaking out against this deployment deserve to be heard, not just as members of the military, but as guardians of the democratic ideals we all hold dear.
