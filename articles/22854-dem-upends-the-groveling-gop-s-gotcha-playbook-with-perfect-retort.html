Dem upends the 'groveling' GOP's 'gotcha' playbook with 'perfect retort'

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosSeptember 1, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Hand-TrapThe Record That MattersDemocratic Weakness Is Not the CenterThe Press Likes a False BalanceThe Pattern

The Real Gotcha Is Republican Submission

Frank Bruni’s point is simple enough: Republicans keep trying to trap Democrats with stale culture-war bait, while Democratic candidates like Abdul El-Sayed can refuse the bait and return to bread-and-butter politics. He also notes the sharper contrast sitting right in front of him: Trump-endorsed Republican Mike Rogers once denounced Trump after Jan. 6, then crawled back for his endorsement and praised his election lies anyway. That is the story. Everything else is noise.

The Hand-Trap

The supposed scandal here is not that El-Sayed has progressive positions. The real scandal is that political journalism keeps treating those positions as if they are the decisive fact, while the Republican Party’s open submission to Donald Trump gets filed under strategic nuance. One side is asked to explain its language on policing, pronouns, and the Green New Deal. The other side is allowed to erase a failed coup attempt, then call it discipline.

That is not symmetry. It is a laundering operation.

The “gotcha” tactic works only because too many Democrats still volunteer to stand in the trap. But the deeper problem is not the existence of the trap. It is the habit of acting as though the trap itself is the main arena of politics. It is not. Power sits with the people demanding fealty to Trump, not with the people being asked whether they once supported a policy that terrified centrist consultants.

The Record That Matters

Rogers’s record is the politically relevant one because it shows where authority actually flows. In January 2021, after Trump tried to overturn Joe Biden’s victory, Rogers denounced him as “more gangster than presidential” and said refusing to accept free and fair elections had to end. Then, when Trump’s endorsement became the price of admission, Rogers took the endorsement, touted it, and went quiet about the coup language.

That is not inconsistency in the abstract. It is submission under pressure.

Bruni is right to call that out, and right to say Republicans have more to answer for. The party that once pretended to care about constitutional order has spent years teaching its candidates that honesty is optional if Trump is the patron. The result is a political class that can condemn a failed coup in the morning and bless its architect by evening, as long as the fundraising and the ballot line stay intact.

Democratic Weakness Is Not the Center

There is a Democratic problem here, but it is not the one Republicans want highlighted. Democrats do too often hand over the entire field of conflict by letting consultants and press narratives define their party through the most vulnerable slogans and social-media fragments. That is a strategic failure. It is also a solvable one.

El-Sayed’s refusal to take Fox’s bait mattered because he understood the point of the exchange: not to test his ideas, but to pin him to caricature and keep voters away from material concerns. That is the correct response. Democrats should spend less time apologizing for every inch of internal debate and more time forcing the conversation back onto wages, health care, housing, and the people actually sabotaging democratic norms.

But let’s keep the scale honest. Democratic overreach on language is not remotely in the same category as Republicans normalizing the man who tried to overturn an election. One is clumsy politics. The other is organized democratic vandalism.

The Press Likes a False Balance

Bruni’s column helps because it drags the camera away from the usual fake equivalence. Yet the larger press habit remains intact: elevate Democratic inconsistency as a character flaw, treat Republican loyalty to Trump as a regrettable wrinkle, and then call the result “analysis.”

That framing distorts the public record. Republicans are not merely “changing their tunes.” They are rewriting the meaning of Jan. 6 on command. They are learning that the only principle required is obedience. When that becomes normal, journalism that obsessively spotlights a Democrat’s awkward phrasing while soft-pedaling Republican complicity does more than miss the story. It helps discipline the opposition into playing defense on terms the right invented.

This is how reactionary politics wins without having to win the argument. It narrows the debate to personality, tone, and tactical purity while insulating the actual machinery of anti-democratic power.

The Pattern

The broader pattern is straightforward: the Republican Party keeps choosing movement loyalty over democratic legitimacy, and too much political coverage still treats that choice as a messaging problem rather than a governing fact. That is why the false scandal of progressive “wokeness” keeps getting replayed while the real scandal, Trump’s grip on Republican officeholders, is treated as background.

Democrats do not need to imitate Republican dishonesty to fight this. They need to stop acting as if every internal disagreement is equally consequential, and start naming the actual hierarchy of danger. The question is not whether a Democrat once flirted with a slogan a consultant dislikes. The question is whether a Republican will stand up to a man who tried to overturn an election and then came back begging for his blessing.

That is the line. Everything else is misdirection.
