Earthquake as ‘anti-MAGA wave’ rattles deep-red state —and Moms for Liberty

Based on an article by Ben Sessoms, Tennessee Lookout

Paulo SantosOctober 9, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Democrats made significant gains in August local elections in Hamilton and Knox counties, Tennessee, but did not overturn Republican dominance. They flipped three seats across county commissions and school boards, won control of the Knox County school board, and reduced large prior deficits in both county-mayor races to four points.

The gains coincided with sharply higher turnout. East Ridge turnout rose from 9.21% in August 2024 to 28.61%, while Knox County turnout increased from 18.07% in its 2022 countywide races to 33.43%. Democratic organizers credited sustained door-knocking and engagement with voters who had assumed defeat was inevitable.

Several victories came against Republican nominees aligned with Moms for Liberty or the party’s MAGA faction. Democratic commissioner Brandon Huckaby called the result an “anti-MAGA wave,” though the evidence remains suggestive rather than conclusive: Republicans still hold most local seats, and Tennessee’s Republican legislative supermajority remains intact.

Power did not change hands

An electoral swing is not an earthquake merely because it occurred somewhere Republicans usually win. The Tennessee GOP still controls the state government and most of the local offices discussed here. Its institutional power includes the ability to write education law, establish censorship criteria and shape the public services that counties must administer.

That distinction matters. Democrats demonstrated that Republican margins can shrink when neglected voters are contacted and given competitive candidates. They did not dislodge the governing structure responsible for deteriorating infrastructure, affordability pressures or the state’s campaign against disfavored material in public schools.

The story is therefore about vulnerability, not transformation. Republican control has become less electorally effortless in these counties. That is politically meaningful, but it is not the same as losing power.

The culture war had authors

The conflicts over books and student services did not descend on Tennessee as an inexplicable outbreak of polarization. State lawmakers enacted the 2022 content law. Republican school-board members applied it when they initially found that a depiction of rape under slavery made Roots impermissible. Superintendent Jon Rysewyk reversed that decision after national backlash.

Republican officials in Hamilton County also voted to end supplemental mental-health services after objections concerning discussions of identity, sexuality and gender. They later restored the provider, alongside other parental options, following local opposition. Those were decisions made by officials with governing authority, not merely “culture-war stuff” floating above local politics.

Moms for Liberty supplied candidates and pressure from the right, but officeholders and party institutions converted that pressure into policy. Treating the organization as the sole villain would let the officials who held the votes escape responsibility for using them.

Voters rejected candidates, not a faction by plebiscite

The defeats of Moms for Liberty-backed candidates are concrete. Bill Sofield lost the Knox County school-board race to Brandy Jenkins after defeating incumbent Republican Kristi Kristy in the GOP primary. Sheri Super, a leader of the local Moms for Liberty chapter, lost a county-commission race to Huckaby.

Calling those outcomes an “anti-MAGA wave” is a campaign interpretation, not a settled measurement of voter intent. The reporting does not present exit polling establishing why voters chose each candidate. Higher Democratic participation, polarizing Republican nominees, local organizing and broader dissatisfaction may all have contributed.

The stronger conclusion is narrower: Republican primaries elevated candidates further to the right, and general-election voters then rejected some of them. A party that replaces viable incumbents with ideological enforcers creates its own exposure. That is not voter confusion. It is a foreseeable consequence of letting a faction use low-turnout primaries to choose who will govern everyone else.

The red-state label conceals the mechanism

“Tennessee is a red state” describes accumulated election results while hiding how those results are produced. Uncontested seats, low-turnout local elections and Democratic organizational neglect can make Republican control appear more unanimous than it is. In East Ridge, a Democrat contested the commission seat for the first time since 2014; turnout then tripled relative to August 2024, and Josh Wichman won.

That does not prove a latent Democratic majority. Republicans also increased their participation, and both Democratic county-mayor candidates still lost. But it exposes the convenience of treating partisan dominance as an immutable cultural trait. Parties benefit when voters regard the outcome as predetermined: incumbents face less scrutiny, challengers struggle to recruit support, and abstention reproduces the power structure that encouraged it.

Democrats bear agency here as well. Wichman said his district had been neglected by the party. If a political organization fails to contest power and then cites the resulting defeats as evidence that competition is impossible, it is not diagnosing the electorate. It is describing its own retreat.

Dominance survives by looking inevitable

The larger pattern is not yet a partisan realignment. It is the erosion of manufactured inevitability. Competitive campaigns and higher turnout narrowed Republican advantages even where decades of election results had taught Democrats to expect humiliation.

Whether that becomes durable depends on what happens after the surprise. Democrats now holding local seats must govern effectively under institutions still dominated by Republicans. Republicans must decide whether factional purity is worth sacrificing offices—and whether culture-war governance can substitute indefinitely for affordable living, functioning infrastructure and competent public services.

The useful political lesson is less dramatic than an earthquake and more damaging to entrenched power: strongholds are often maintained not by universal consent, but by low participation, weak opposition and the disciplined conversion of narrow electoral victories into broad institutional control. Once those conditions change, the map stops looking like destiny and starts looking like what it always was—a record of who organized, who voted and who was allowed to govern.
