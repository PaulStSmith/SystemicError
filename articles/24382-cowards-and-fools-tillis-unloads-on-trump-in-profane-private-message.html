'Cowards and fools': Tillis unloads on Trump in profane private message

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 3, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that retiring North Carolina Republican Sen. Thom Tillis privately told President Donald Trump on June 6 that, had Trump directly threatened him with a primary challenge, Tillis would have immediately told him to “f—— yourself” and announced his retirement. Tillis added that Trump should reserve such “threats and head games” for “cowards and fools.”

The message disputed Trump’s public claim that he had driven Tillis from office. Tillis announced in June 2025 that he would not seek another term after opposing Trump’s tax and spending bill over its Medicaid provisions; he later said he had already been considering retirement. The exchange was disclosed ahead of the scheduled Oct. 6 release of Tillis’ memoir.

AlterNet says Trump had also attacked Tillis amid a dispute over Todd Blanche’s nomination for attorney general, after Tillis demanded that Blanche condemn the Jan. 6 rioters who assaulted police officers. Trump called Tillis a “loser” and attributed his departure to losing presidential support. Tillis’ account is that Trump never directly threatened him with a primary.

The power was never in the profanity

Tillis supplied the memorable insult, but Trump held the institutional leverage. A president commanding intense loyalty inside the Republican electorate can turn endorsements, public attacks and primary threats into instruments of discipline. Senators still possess votes, committee authority and public platforms, but Republican dissent carries electoral costs precisely because Trump can mobilize punishment.

The central fact is therefore not that Tillis spoke harshly. It is that he became openly confrontational after deciding he would no longer face voters. His freedom arrived with political retirement.

Defiance with an expiration date

Tillis did exercise agency. He opposed Trump’s spending legislation over Medicaid, placed a condition on supporting Blanche and directly rejected Trump’s version of his retirement. Those were choices, not accidents.

But the timing exposes the boundary of that resistance. AlterNet describes Tillis as increasingly willing to criticize Trump once another election was no longer ahead of him. That is not evidence of universal helplessness among Republican officeholders. It is evidence that many calculate the price of dissent and find courage more affordable when voters, donors and party organizations can no longer end their careers.

Trump’s claim rewrites cause as conquest

Trump cast Tillis’ retirement as proof of presidential dominance: Tillis was supposedly leaving because Trump would not support him. Tillis denied that account and said no direct primary threat occurred. The reporting does not establish which consideration ultimately determined his decision, but it does establish that Trump publicly claimed credit for forcing him out while Tillis attributed the announcement to a longer-running retirement calculation sharpened by their clash.

That boast serves a political purpose regardless of whether it accurately describes Tillis’ decision. It advertises punishment to every Republican still seeking reelection. The intended audience is larger than one departing senator: comply, or watch the president define your exit as his victory.

The weaker actor should not become the whole story

The article’s hook is Tillis’ profanity, which risks turning a structure of presidential and party power into a personality feud between two angry men. Tillis’ language is vivid; it is not the consequential act.

The consequential acts were Trump’s public attack, his attempt to control the explanation for Tillis’ retirement, and the pressure surrounding Senate votes and nominations. Focusing on whether Tillis was sufficiently tough mistakes the conduct of the pressured official for the machinery applying the pressure.

Retirement is not a governing strategy

This episode reveals a familiar Republican pattern: private contempt, public caution and belated candor after electoral accountability disappears. Tillis’ message shows that at least one senator understood Trump’s intimidation tactics clearly enough to name them. His retirement shows how little that private clarity guarantees while political careers remain at stake.

A party governed through threatened retaliation does not need every official to admire its leader. It needs them to believe resistance is costlier than submission. When blunt honesty routinely arrives only on the way out, the system has already converted personal fear into institutional obedience.
