'Opportunity for change': Barack Obama shares what Dems can do to take down Trump

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 22, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Barack Obama (AFP)

Former President Barack Obama has shared what Democrats can do to start fighting back against Donald Trump.

According to Politico, the 44th President of the United States told freshman lawmakers to be vigilant and compared the 2024 Republican election sweep to how the Democratic Party felt in 2004. Adam Wren wrote that Obama spoke of a “similar sense of despair” felt now as there was two decades ago in 2004.

Obama said, “Because when I was — everybody remembers the Democratic National Convention in 2004 — when you were. …well, you were in elementary school. But what people don’t recall is that John Kerry lost that election.”

“And we didn’t control the House, and we did not control the Senate. And Tom Daschle, who was then the Democratic leader of the Senate, lost, which is unheard of. And Karl Rove, who was the chief architect of George Bush’s campaigns and political career was – could be found on all the TV stations, talking about the ‘permanent Republican majority’ that had been created.”

Obama went on to compare the strength of the Democratic Party in those two instances, calling on the future of the party to create the momentum for change.

He added, “And two years later, Nancy Pelosi was the first woman speaker of the House of Representatives. And four years later, somehow, I ended up being president. The reason I tell you that is not for you to, you know, feel complacent.”

“It’s to indicate that the work that you are doing right now, the investment you’re making, the focus that you’re applying, the issues that you are developing, the interactions that you’re having with your constituencies. All that is creating the momentum and the opportunity for change.”

Obama would also say he wished he had not overestimated the Republican Party’s willingness to work with his administration over two terms. He said, “We wasted a lot of time trying to engage the ideas of Republicans on a good faith basis.”

The former president has also aired his desire to “move from player to coach” within the Democratic Party. An aide close to Obama said, “His goal is to build a sustainable Democratic Party that can survive without him.”

Our Analysis:

Obama’s Playbook: A Retrospective Call to Arms

Former President Barack Obama, in a recent Politico piece, offers a vintage Obama-esque beacon of hope to the Democratic freshmen, drawing parallels between the current political despair and that of 2004, a year marked by Democratic losses and Republican triumphs. However, the simplistic nostalgia fails to fully confront the complexities of today’s political quagmire.

The 2004 Déjà Vu

Obama recollects the Democrats’ disarray post-2004 election as a mirror to today’s challenges. He reminds us, “John Kerry lost that election.” and how Democrats were ousted from the power corridors of both the House and the Senate. It’s a strategic move, evoking a sense of resilience by highlighting the party’s comeback with Pelosi’s historic speakership and, eventually, his own presidency in 2008.

However, this reminiscence, while heartening, oversimplifies the seismic shifts in political landscape since then. The rise of social media, the deepening of political polarization, and the resurgence of authoritarian and populist sentiments globally present challenges that are fundamentally different from those faced two decades ago.

The Misjudgment of Bipartisanship

“We wasted a lot of time trying to engage the ideas of Republicans on a good faith basis.” This admission from Obama is both a mea culpa and a stark warning. It highlights a critical miscalculation that arguably characterized much of his presidency: an overestimation of the possibility for bipartisan cooperation in an era where political divisions only deepened.

The acknowledgment of this “wasted time” is crucial, yet it begs the question: what concrete strategies does Obama propose now to ensure Democrats do not fall into the same trap? The answer remains elusive in his advice, hinting at a lack of forward-looking strategic direction beyond the call for increased focus and engagement with constituents.

The Coach Ambition

Obama’s expressed desire to “move from player to coach” within the Democratic Party is intriguing. It suggests a shift to a more behind-the-scenes role in shaping the party’s future. However, what does this coaching entail? The article hints at building a “sustainable Democratic Party that can survive without him”, yet offers scant details on the playbook for achieving such sustainability.

Conclusion: A Call for Deeper Reflection

Obama’s reflections, while imbued with optimism, seem to skate on the surface of the deeper, more intractable challenges facing the Democratic Party. The parallels drawn with 2004, though emotionally resonant, may not fully equip today’s Democrats to navigate the complexities of our current political epoch.

The call for increased focus and engagement is undoubtedly important. Still, without a more nuanced understanding of today’s political divisions and a clear, actionable strategy for fostering bipartisanship or effectively countering GOP strategies, Democrats risk repeating the same mistakes of the past.

Obama’s narrative serves as a reminder of the cyclical nature of political fortunes—but as we look ahead, the Democratic Party, under the guidance of coaches like Obama, must forge new strategies that are as sophisticated and multi-dimensional as the challenges they aim to overcome.
