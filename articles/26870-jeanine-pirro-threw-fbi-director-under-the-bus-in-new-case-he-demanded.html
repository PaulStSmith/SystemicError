SYSTEMIC ERROR / COMMENTARY

Jeanine Pirro threw FBI director under the bus in new case he demanded

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower ordered the caseThe case weakened under examinationThe headline obscures the chain of commandCriminalization as disciplineThe systemic error

What happened

AlterNet reports that FBI Special Agent Bryan Witt testified at a Tuesday hearing that FBI Director Kash Patel personally directed an investigation of agents Nikki Floris and Bradley Benavides. Witt said the July assignment, routed through the Kansas City field office, was unlike any tasking he had received from an FBI director during his career.

Defense questioning also exposed serious weaknesses in the resulting case. Witt acknowledged that neither agent had been sworn to tell the truth, despite his affidavit saying they were, and that House Judiciary Committee counsel Steve Castor was absent from the interviews in which the alleged false statements were made. Witt had spoken with Castor only the day before the hearing, and the Justice Department declined to disclose Castor’s knowledge, citing the Speech or Debate Clause.

The headline’s claim that Jeanine Pirro “threw” Patel under the bus is not established by the reporting. The damaging attribution came from Witt’s testimony: Patel ordered the investigation. Pirro’s role, as presented by AlterNet through national security writer Marcy Wheeler’s analysis, was in pursuing charges that gave defense lawyers an opportunity to expose how the case originated.

Power ordered the case

Floris and Benavides did not choose this confrontation. Patel held the institutional power to initiate an extraordinary investigation from the top of the FBI, and Witt carried out the assignment through the chain of command. The unusual feature was not merely bureaucratic untidiness. It was the director’s direct intervention in a case targeting agents whose 2020 work included resisting foreign election interference.

That distinction matters because “the FBI investigated” conceals agency. According to the testimony AlterNet recounts, Patel directed it. Career personnel then inherited the legal and professional risk created by that decision.

The case weakened under examination

The government’s theory depended on alleged false statements to a congressional committee. Yet its investigating agent lacked direct knowledge of what the committee’s counsel considered material, had only just spoken with Castor, and admitted a basic factual error in his own affidavit about whether Floris and Benavides testified under oath.

An inaccurate affidavit does not by itself prove intentional deception. It does, however, expose a glaring asymmetry: officials were attempting to criminalize alleged inaccuracies by the defendants while presenting their own inaccurate description to the court. A prosecution built around truthfulness cannot treat precision as optional when the government supplies it.

The headline obscures the chain of command

Casting this as Pirro betraying Patel turns an institutional abuse story into palace intrigue. The consequential fact is not that one Trump official embarrassed another. It is that the FBI director allegedly initiated an unusual investigation, prosecutors advanced a defective complaint, and the Justice Department invoked a constitutional protection that limited scrutiny of a congressional staff member central to its theory.

That framing redirects attention from state power toward personality conflict. Floris and Benavides face the coercive machinery of federal prosecution; Patel, Pirro, and senior Justice Department officials control that machinery. Those positions are not interchangeable, and the reporting should not flatten them into a spectacle of allies “throwing” one another around.

Criminalization as discipline

Wheeler argues that the case sought to make rejecting frivolous election-fraud claims legally dangerous inside the FBI. The hearing does not independently prove that motive, but the reported sequence supports scrutiny of it: Patel personally ordered an unusual inquiry into agents associated with protecting the 2020 election, and the resulting case faltered when tested against its own evidence.

The foreseeable message to other agents is plain. Professional judgment can become personal exposure when it conflicts with the political leadership’s preferred account. Even a weak case can punish through legal fees, reputational damage and the threat of prosecution.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is the conversion of law enforcement hierarchy into a channel for political retaliation, followed by procedural language that disguises who exercised power. Orders become “taskings.” Political choices become ordinary casework. Evidentiary defects become confusion.

This hearing disrupted that laundering process. It placed the director’s instruction, the investigators’ omissions and the prosecutors’ choices into the same record. The scandal is not that the officials involved failed to coordinate their stories cleanly. It is that federal authority was reportedly mobilized against agents tied to election-protection work—and that the case’s internal weaknesses emerged only after the targets had already been forced to defend themselves.
