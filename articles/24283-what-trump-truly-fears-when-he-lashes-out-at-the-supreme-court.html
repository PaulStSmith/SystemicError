What Trump truly fears when he lashes out at the Supreme Court

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 1, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Atlantic writer Adam Serwer identifies Trump’s fear as the possibility that public criticism can influence Supreme Court justices. Serwer argues that Trump projects this fear when he attributes rulings against him to pressure from the “Radical Left,” then attacks the Court himself in hopes of shaping its next decision.

During his 2024 campaign, Trump suggested that criticizing Supreme Court rulings should bring “very serious fines and beyond that.” After a majority of the justices rejected his effort to restrict voting by mail in the midterms, however, he publicly disparaged them as “a shell of their original selves.”

Serwer notes that Trump had also recently lost high-profile disputes involving tariffs and birthright citizenship. But he argues that the Court has otherwise repeatedly expanded Trump’s power or endorsed outcomes he favored, including presidential immunity and greater presidential control over nominally independent agencies.

Power, not principle

Trump’s contradiction is not difficult to decode. Criticism is intolerable when directed at judges delivering outcomes he wants and legitimate when those judges resist him. The operating principle is permission: allies may exercise power; opponents should be punished for using the same tools.

That matters because a president commands institutional power unavailable to ordinary Court critics. He appoints justices, directs the executive branch and possesses an immense political platform. His denunciations are therefore not merely commentary from another dissatisfied litigant. They tell judges selected under his authority that favorable rulings are expected and deviation will be treated as betrayal.

The appointment as obligation

Trump’s complaint that the justices were not the people he “interviewed to serve” strips away the customary fiction that judicial selection is detached from anticipated results. Presidents have always chosen nominees partly for their legal commitments. Trump’s formulation goes further: the appointment resembles a personal transaction, with continued loyalty as the expected return.

The justices still possess independent authority and made the reported decision themselves. Nothing in the account establishes that Trump’s criticism changed a vote. But his language reveals how he understands the relationship: judicial independence is acceptable only until it frustrates presidential will.

The convenient referee

“Playing the ref” was Trump’s accusation when other people criticized the Court. His own response shows that he was not defending judicial insulation from political pressure. He was arguing for a monopoly on that pressure.

The misdirection lies in presenting weaker actors’ criticism as coercion while minimizing the leverage of the presidency. Protesters, advocates and voters can speak. A president can nominate judges, enforce judgments, shape the federal government and mobilize a party against officials who displease him. Treating those forms of influence as interchangeable launders power out of the story.

A Court that is never loyal enough

Serwer’s sharper point is that Trump’s anger followed isolated defeats from a Court that, in his assessment, has often produced outcomes advantageous to Trump. The grievance is therefore not evidence of sustained judicial opposition. It is evidence that substantial institutional accommodation does not satisfy a leader who treats every adverse ruling as illegitimate pressure or personal disloyalty.

That pattern converts independent institutions into conditional allies. Compliance becomes normal and resistance becomes scandal. Each favorable decision resets the baseline; each unfavorable one triggers another demand for submission.

The system beneath the outburst

The larger danger in this episode is not hypocrisy by itself. Hypocrisy is merely the visible residue of a power theory: law protects the leader’s freedom of action while restricting everyone else’s.

AlterNet’s account shows a president attacking judicial criticism when it threatened useful rulings, then deploying criticism when the Court blocked him. The systemic failure begins when institutions and political coverage treat that asymmetry as inconsistency rather than hierarchy. Trump is not confused about who should be allowed to pressure the Court. His position, as described here, is brutally coherent: power should answer to him, and dissent should carry consequences.
