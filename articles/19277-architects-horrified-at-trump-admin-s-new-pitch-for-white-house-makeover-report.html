Architects 'horrified' at Trump admin's new pitch for White House makeover: report

By Paulo Santos

Following the demolition of the White House’s East Wing to make room for President Donald Trump’s ballroom, the Trump administration has its sights set on another key fixture of the historic building, one that left architects and designers “baffled or even horrified,” The Washington Post reported Sunday.

Rodney Mims Cook Jr., who Trump tapped to lead the Commission of Fine Arts, is “proposing” to replace the columns at the White House’s front entrance – known as the North Portico – with more ornate-style Corinthian columns with a more luxurious appearance, a style of architecture Trump has “long preferred,” the Post reported.

“Corinthian is the highest order [of column], and that’s what our other two branches of government have,” Cook Jr. said in an interview last week, per The Post. “Why the White House didn’t originally use them, at least on the north front, which is considered the front door, is beyond me.”

The suggestion that the White House’s iconic front entrance may see its columns – which have stood for nearly 200 years – ripped out and replaced left several architects and designers floored.

“It is a completely inappropriate idea and at odds with universally held historic preservation standards,” said Bruce Redman Becker, architect and former member of the Commission of Fine Arts under President Joe Biden, speaking with the Post.

Steven Semes, an architect and professor at the University of Notre Dame’s School of Architecture, told the Post that Corinthian columns would be wildly “inappropriate” for the White House, calling the proposal on its face “completely absurd.”

“It’s like surgically adding or removing a couple of inches to one of your legs, thinking that you could still walk,” he said. “It becomes a very different animal. And it becomes a completely absurd animal.”

Two people close to Trump, speaking with the Post on the condition of anonymity, confirmed to the outlet that the president had “mused” about having Corinthian columns constructed for both new government buildings and at the White House. A White House spokesperson, however, told the Post that no plans currently exist to replace the White House’s existing columns at its front entrance, and that Cook Jr.’s proposal has yet to be presented to Trump.

Our Analysis:

Unpacking the Column Controversy: A Closer Look at Power and Decision-Making

The recent uproar surrounding the proposed replacement of the White House’s iconic columns, as reported by The Washington Post, presents a fascinating case study into the dynamics of power, decision-making, and public perception in political narratives. This situation, involving Rodney Mims Cook Jr.’s suggestion to replace the North Portico’s columns with Corinthian ones, ostensibly to match President Donald Trump’s architectural tastes, has sparked a significant backlash from architects and preservationists. However, a critical examination reveals a more nuanced distribution of responsibility and influence than the initial outcry might suggest.

Institutional Power and Decision-Making

1. The Role of the Commission of Fine Arts: Rodney Mims Cook Jr., appointed by Trump, leads the Commission of Fine Arts, a body with advisory powers over aesthetic and design decisions on federal land in Washington, D.C. While influential, the Commission’s role is primarily consultative, lacking direct executive authority to implement changes without approval from higher echelons of power, namely the President or, in certain cases, Congress.

2. Presidential Preferences and Actual Influence: The article spotlights Trump’s preference for Corinthian columns, a stylistic choice reflecting his broader taste in architecture. However, stating a preference does not equate to a formal decision. The report clarifies that, despite Cook Jr.’s proposal and Trump’s musings, no official plan exists to replace the White House’s columns.

Misdirection and Responsibility Framing

The framing of the article potentially misleads readers into attributing the blame for the proposed changes directly to Trump, based on his architectural preferences and the actions of an appointee. This narrative simplifies the complex process of decision-making within the federal government, which involves multiple stakeholders and checks and balances. The mention of architects and designers’ horror at the proposal injects emotional weight into the issue but does not substantively address who has the actual power to make such a decision.

Moreover, the article notes that Cook Jr.’s proposal “has yet to be presented to Trump,” suggesting that the President himself has not actively pursued this change. The distinction between informal musings and concrete policy proposals is crucial here, highlighting a potential misdirection in attributing direct responsibility.

Conclusion: A Call for Nuanced Critique

In dissecting the layers of power and decision-making involved, it becomes evident that the controversy surrounding the White House columns is more a reflection of individual tastes and proposals within the Trump administration rather than a definitive policy shift. While Trump’s preferences certainly influence his appointees, the institutional mechanisms and checks in place necessitate a more nuanced approach before assigning blame.

Criticism directed towards the aesthetic direction of the White House under Trump should acknowledge the complexity of implementing such changes, recognizing the difference between suggestion and action, and between individual preference and formal decision-making. In this case, the premature outcry over the columns serves as a reminder of the importance of distinguishing between the symbolic and the substantive in political discourse.
