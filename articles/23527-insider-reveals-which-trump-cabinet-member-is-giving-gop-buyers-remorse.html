The Pentagon’s Loyalty Test Comes for the Army

A Vacancy Created by Dissent

Army Secretary Dan Driscoll resigned after objecting to the administration’s direction on military policy. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s allies are now promoting Hegseth spokesman Sean Parnell as his replacement, while the White House also considers Army Comptroller Marc Andersen and special operations official Derrick Anderson.

That is the necessary context. The political story is what Hegseth is trying to do with the vacancy.

Hegseth Is Consolidating Control

Hegseth holds the institutional power here. His allies are not merely supporting a qualified colleague; according to the reporting, they are campaigning against rival candidates to place his own spokesperson atop the Army.

This follows Hegseth’s firing of Gen. Randy George and his replacement with Gen. Christopher LaNeve, Hegseth’s former military adviser. Installing Parnell would put Hegseth loyalists in the Army’s two most powerful positions. The pattern is difficult to mistake: remove independent leadership, elevate personal allies, and convert military administration into an extension of the defense secretary’s political operation.

Parnell’s combat record may establish military experience. It does not explain why the Pentagon spokesman should outrank officials already responsible for Army finances or special operations. Loyalty does.

The Resignation Is Being Used to Erase the Warning

Driscoll’s departure was a judgment about the Pentagon’s direction. The response is not described as reconsideration, investigation, or policy correction. It is a drive to fill his office with someone closely tied to the official whose leadership prompted the resignation.

That turns dissent into an opportunity for deeper control. An official leaves because he believes the institution is moving dangerously; the people directing that movement answer by narrowing the space for another official to object.

The article calls this fallout. That word is too bloodless. Fallout sounds atmospheric, as though consequences simply descended. Trump set the administration’s military direction. Hegseth is exploiting the resulting vacancy. Their allies are organizing the personnel campaign.

The Scandal Is Relevant, but It Is Not the Structure

Parnell’s history deserves scrutiny. His former wife accused him in custody proceedings of physically abusing her and their children and of attempting to strangle her. A judge later awarded her primary physical and sole legal custody, while granting Parnell partial physical custody. Parnell also wrote a novel containing scenes of women being strangled, restrained, abused, and degraded.

Those facts raise obvious questions about judgment and fitness for high office. But concentrating exclusively on Parnell’s personal controversy would let the administration’s institutional maneuver disappear behind spectacle.

The decisive issue is not that one controversial man might receive a powerful appointment. It is that Hegseth’s allies are trying to install a subordinate whose advancement depends on Hegseth while discrediting competing officials. The personnel scandal matters; the patronage project matters more.

The Senate’s “Toughness” Is Still Permission

Representative Don Bacon suggested that the Senate may scrutinize the nomination more aggressively than it did two years earlier. That is not a restraint until senators actually use their authority.

The Senate can reject an unsuitable nominee. If it instead performs concern and confirms him, it will not have been outmaneuvered. It will have enabled the appointment. Institutional cowardice often arrives dressed as procedural hesitation: grave quotations, private reservations, and ultimately compliant votes.

Personal Loyalty Is Becoming the Chain of Command

This episode reflects a larger political pattern: leaders treating public institutions as collections of posts to be occupied by loyalists rather than responsibilities governed by independent judgment.

The Army is not merely another agency in a patronage network. Its leadership controls budgets, personnel, readiness, and the administration of organized force. Replacing dissenting or independent officials with people tied personally to the defense secretary weakens the distinction between service to the state and service to a political superior.

That is the systemic error: misconduct draws attention to the nominee while consolidation proceeds through the office. The controversy becomes the headline. The capture of the institution becomes routine.
