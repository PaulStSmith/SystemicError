'The wheels are coming off': How this week changed everything for the GOP

Based on an article by John Stoehr

Paulo SantosSeptember 27, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Republicans Can Stop Trump’s Iran War. They Are Choosing Not To.

Panic Is Not Opposition

The reporting describes a Republican Party staring at electoral disaster as an extended war with Iran drives fuel costs higher, punishes diesel-dependent rural communities, and erodes support in districts Donald Trump once carried comfortably. Yet the decisive fact is not that Republican consultants are frightened. It is that congressional Republicans still hold power to constrain the president—and overwhelmingly refuse to use it.

Their panic is real. So is their agency.

The Vote That Exposes the Fraud

Republican candidates now speak as though the war were an unfortunate weather system. Michigan Senate candidate Mike Rogers called for it to end. Ohio Senator Jon Husted said it must end quickly to lower gasoline prices. Marjorie Taylor Greene blamed Trump directly.

But when the Senate considered a resolution on Thursday to block further presidential strikes, all but four Republican senators voted against it. Husted joined them.

That vote matters more than every anxious quote and campaign advertisement. Republicans were given a concrete opportunity to challenge the policy they claim is hurting their constituents. Most chose continued deference to Trump. Their public concern is therefore not resistance; it is electoral insulation.

Voters Pay, Republicans Calculate

The political consequences described in the reporting are severe. Steve Deace said Iowa House Republicans were briefed that even members in districts Trump carried by more than 15 points could be within the polling margin of error. He compared the prospective 2026 environment to 2006, when backlash against the Iraq War cost Iowa Republicans three of the state’s five congressional seats.

Those claims come from partisan polling relayed by a conservative broadcaster, not independently presented data. But the Republican response gives them weight: candidates are scrambling to distance themselves rhetorically from the war while avoiding the institutional confrontation required to change it.

Their urgency is about endangered seats. The households absorbing higher transportation, farming, and consumer costs received no comparable urgency when senators cast their votes.

The President Is Not Acting Alone

Framing this solely as “Trump’s war” obscures the structure sustaining it. The president reportedly ordered the strikes and owns that decision. But Congress is not a spectator. Republican senators who rejected a measure to restrict further attacks became active enablers of the policy’s continuation.

That distinction is essential. Presidential domination does not erase legislative responsibility; it tests whether legislators will exercise it. Here, most Senate Republicans chose party discipline over the restraint they publicly suggested was necessary.

The result is institutional cowardice with material consequences: officials deplore prices at the microphone, then protect the policy they associate with those prices when their votes are counted.

Even the Revolt Demands More Trump

Deace’s proposed remedy reveals the limits of the right-wing backlash. After describing a potentially catastrophic environment, he called for Trump to campaign and explain the mission and the meaning of victory.

That is not a demand for congressional control, a defined legal limit, or withdrawal. It is a request for better salesmanship. The problem becomes insufficient messaging rather than a war whose duration, objectives, and costs have apparently exceeded what Republican allies expected.

This is how political movements preserve loyalty through failure: consequences are acknowledged, but responsibility is rerouted into a communications problem. The leader does not need less power. He merely needs another microphone.

A Party Trapped by Its Own Submission

The larger pattern is not Republican confusion. It is a party that concentrated authority around one leader and now cannot oppose him even when its own candidates believe his policy is destroying their electoral prospects.

Campaign panic will not end a war. Private polling will not lower fuel prices. Carefully worded distance from Trump will not substitute for a vote against him.

Republicans may lose because voters blame them for the consequences described in the reporting. If they do, it will not be because they lacked warning or power. It will be because, when forced to choose between protecting their constituents and defying Donald Trump, most of them made their choice in public.
