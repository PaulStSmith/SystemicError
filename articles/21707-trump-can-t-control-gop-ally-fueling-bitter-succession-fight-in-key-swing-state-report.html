Trump can't control GOP ally fueling  'bitter succession fight' in key swing state: report

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

Paulo SantosAugust 18, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

DeSantis Is Not Presiding Over Chaos. He Is Manufacturing It.

CNN’s account of Florida’s Republican gubernatorial race describes a party civil war: Ron DeSantis refuses to back the clear frontrunner, the field fills with rivals, and the race descends into race-baiting, legal fights, and factional sabotage. That framing is tidy. It is also too soft. This is not random disorder. It is what happens when a sitting governor uses his institutional weight to withhold legitimacy on purpose.

The Power Is Not the Same as the Noise

The central fact is simple: Ron DeSantis still holds the most consequential political authority in this story. He is the governor. He has the megaphone. He has the residual power of incumbency and the ability to confer or withhold permission on his party’s next move. The article treats his refusal to endorse Rep. Byron Donalds as one thread in a “Republican family feud.” That understates the reality. DeSantis is not a bystander watching conflict unfold. He is the person creating the conditions for it.

The “family feud” language also launders agency. It turns a decision into temperament, and temperament into theater. But withholding support is a decision with consequences. It weakens the putative frontrunner, invites challengers, and tells the party’s most poisonous operatives that the guardrails are off.

A Vacuum Filled by the Worst People

Once DeSantis declines to bless a successor, the race stops being a contest over governing and becomes a contest over who can survive the most degraded faction of the party. CNN notes that James Fishback has drawn support from Nick Fuentes, an antisemitic far-right influencer, while attacking Donalds, who is Black, with language about being a “slave to his donors” and turning Florida into a “Section 8 ghetto.”

That is not merely “sharp-edged politics.” It is open racist signaling. And it thrives because the party’s leadership environment rewards escalation while punishing restraint. The result is not an accident of one bad candidate. It is the predictable output of a political machine that tolerates racial humiliation as a governing language when it serves ambition.

The Legal Mess Is Not the Story’s Center

The article also notes that Fishback faces legal challenges over his claimed Florida residency and a dispute involving a loan Greenlight Capital says he owes. Those facts matter, but they are not the core moral or political issue. They are symptoms of a deeper problem: a party so unmoored that candidates can be simultaneously extreme, vulnerable, and competitive.

This is what elite rot looks like in practice. The field is not being sorted by principle, competence, or a coherent agenda. It is being sorted by who can survive public ugliness, private debt, procedural fights, and the blessing of the most powerful actor in the room. That is not a healthy succession process. It is a survival contest.

Trump Cannot Save a Governor Who Wants the Fight

The report says even Donald Trump has tried to talk DeSantis into backing Donalds. That detail is revealing, but not for the reason Trump-world would like. It shows that DeSantis is not merely defying a rival; he is choosing isolation over party order. He is gambling with his own political relevance, leaving allies frustrated and, according to CNN’s source, preparing for a backlash that strips him of a platform to defend himself.

This is the same pattern Republicans keep mistaking for strength: performative independence, followed by factional collapse. DeSantis has confused obstruction with leverage. He has also demonstrated something more specific: when an authoritarian-style politician loses momentum, he does not become more democratic. He often becomes more vindictive, more arbitrary, and more willing to let the party burn if it preserves his personal mythology.

The Racism Is Not a Sideshow

The article’s most important detail is the one the “Republican family feud” label risks burying: a Black candidate is being attacked with racist rhetoric while the party debates strategy instead of drawing a hard line. That is not background noise. That is the political culture the party has built.

When a campaign can casually host anti-Black dog whistles and blatant antisemitic association, the problem is not one “rogue” candidate. The problem is an ecosystem in which cruelty is an asset and accountability is optional. The same party that spends years demanding discipline from everyone else cannot enforce basic democratic decency inside its own primary.

The Pattern Is the Point

This race is not mainly about Florida. It is about what happens when institutional power is used to normalize chaos rather than manage it, when leaders prefer grievance to succession, and when racism is tolerated as tactical speech. DeSantis is not losing control of a process he intended to steward. He is demonstrating how Republican power behaves when it is detached from democratic responsibility: it becomes personal, punitive, and structurally corrupt.

That is the larger lesson here. The story is not that Florida Republicans are unusually dysfunctional. The story is that authoritarian politics eventually eats its own procedures. First it weaponizes the party. Then it empties out the meaning of leadership. Then it leaves the public with a degraded contest where the loudest hatreds are treated as just another intramural dispute.
