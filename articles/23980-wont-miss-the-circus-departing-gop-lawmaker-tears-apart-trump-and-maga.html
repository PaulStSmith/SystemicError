'Won’t miss the circus': Departing GOP lawmaker tears apart Trump and MAGA

Dusty Johnson’s Farewell Excuse: Congress Failed Because Voters Wanted a Fight

One Short Account of the Exit

As he prepares to leave Congress in January after losing South Dakota’s Republican gubernatorial primary in June, Rep. Dusty Johnson offered a familiar diagnosis: Washington has become more tribal, its incentives reward spectacle, President Donald Trump sometimes “lost focus,” and voters too often chose fighters over problem-solvers. Johnson conceded that Trump’s personal conduct, treatment of allies, and disregard for institutional norms troubled him—but still predicted near-term Republican policy victories.

Congress Is Not a Weather System

Johnson describes dysfunction as something that descended upon otherwise decent lawmakers: a “fever,” a field of political “landmines,” a conflict between serving and surviving. That framing obscures agency.

Members of Congress write the rules, choose their leaders, negotiate legislation, and decide which conduct they will reward or tolerate. Johnson was not watching this machinery from the visitors’ gallery. He served eight years in the House, joined negotiations over the Fiscal Responsibility Act, advised Speaker Mike Johnson, and remained in the speaker’s circle after Kevin McCarthy was removed.

His portrait of “great inputs” producing a “barely functional government” therefore raises the question he avoids: What did those supposedly capable officials repeatedly choose when competence threatened their careers? Johnson’s own answer is survival. That is not a mysterious systems failure. It is institutional cowardice made routine.

Blaming Voters Lets Officials Off Cheaply

Johnson places the central burden on voters who prefer someone “fighting” to someone “getting things done.” Voters certainly make choices, but they do not control congressional committees, leadership offices, legislative calendars, party money or the incentives imposed through endorsements and primaries.

Republican officials helped construct the political market Johnson now laments. They elevated combative personalities, treated attention as achievement and allowed ideological punishment to displace governing. Johnson himself says lawmakers fear career-ending consequences for positions on subjects including January 6, vaccines and data centers. That describes organized political enforcement—not merely an electorate suffering from inexplicable anger.

The weaker actors in this account are ordinary voters, yet they receive the strongest blame. The people with institutional authority become decent individuals trapped by incentives. That reversal is the story’s central misdirection.

Trump’s Conduct Is Not a Loss of Focus

Johnson’s criticism of Trump is sharper than a routine compliment sandwich, but it still retreats into euphemism. He says Trump is personally insulting, insufficiently values foreign allies and gives too little weight to institutional norms. Then he reduces the political problem to “focus,” placing those concerns beside disputes over a reflecting pool and renaming Lake Ontario.

A leader’s treatment of allies and institutions is not comparable to distraction by trivial controversies. Johnson’s own account identifies differences in values and conduct, then repackages them as failures of message discipline. The political consequence is clear: behavior he considers harmful to the country becomes a tactical problem because it may make Republicans perform poorly in November.

Johnson does not supply evidence establishing Trump’s motives, and none should be invented. But his stated concern is revealing enough. The standard being applied is not whether presidential conduct respects institutional constraints; it is whether the party can keep its preferred policy agenda on schedule.

Federalism for the Farewell Speech

Johnson warns against concentrating authority in Washington and criticizes “big government conservatism.” Yet the same remarks celebrate his proximity to powerful congressional leaders and anticipate Republican victories before the next Congress convenes.

The tension matters. Federalism becomes hollow when politicians oppose centralized power in theory but excuse its exercise by their own coalition as long as it advances favored outcomes. Johnson identifies a Republican appetite for greater federal authority, but he stops short of naming who promoted it, what powers were sought or which decisions he opposed.

That omission leaves federalism functioning less as an enforceable principle than as a line in a retirement speech.

The System Rewards What Its Leaders Protect

Johnson’s account is valuable despite its evasions. It shows a governing class capable of recognizing the machinery of dysfunction while declining to accept responsibility for operating it.

The larger pattern is not that good people mysteriously produce bad government. It is that officials preserve status by accommodating the forces they later describe as regrettable. Party leaders reward aggression, members calculate the risks of dissent, and retiring lawmakers blame public anger after years spent inside the institutions that organized and exploited it.

The circus did not build itself. The people holding office sold the tickets, protected the ring and called their choices an incentive structure.
