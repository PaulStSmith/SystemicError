Most Utahns wanted to protect their history — but Trump had other plans

Utah’s Monuments Weren’t Cut for Utahns

Public Opinion Was Already Clear

Trump’s decision to slash Bears Ears and Grand Staircase-Escalante by roughly 90 percent each was dressed up as deference to “the people of Utah.” That framing collapses the moment you look at the polling. Multiple surveys showed Utahns favored keeping the monuments in place, and a large majority opposed shrinking them. The story is not that the public suddenly asked for this. The story is that elected officials kept claiming public backing they did not have.

The Decision Belonged to Power, Not Residents

The people who actually made this happen were Trump and Utah’s Republican political leadership, standing together for the cameras. Trump signed the orders. Utah’s congressional delegation and Governor Spencer Cox amplified the justification. Rep. Celeste Maloy repeated the localism script. Senator Mike Lee and Cox have spent years attacking the monuments. The article’s own evidence makes the hierarchy plain: the decision came from federal executive power, backed by state and congressional Republicans, not from any demonstrated popular mandate.

“Fairness” Was a Cover Story

Trump’s talk about “fairness” and “public interest” is the usual inversion. The monuments were not blocking ordinary use in the way he described. Hunting, fishing, off-road driving on designated trails, and other recreation were already allowed. What the proclamation actually does is open the door wider for extraction, especially after the orders cited critical minerals like copper, nickel, and uranium as justification.

That is the real policy. The rest is misdirection. “Multiple uses” is the polite language of resource transfer, where land protected for historical, scientific, and tribal reasons gets reframed as underused because it is not being stripped fast enough.

Tribes Were Not the Priority

Bears Ears was not just land. It was a site with an intertribal commission that federal agencies consulted on management and protection. Trump disbanded that body. That is not neutral administration. It is the deliberate removal of Native governance from land tied to ancestral claims, while politicians pretend the issue is “access” or “fairness.”

The polls matter here too. Nearly 90 percent of Utahns supported tribes having a strong role in managing ancestral lands, and 81 percent said the incoming Trump administration should keep tribal agreements in place. Those facts make the political theft even clearer: the story is not that Utahns rejected tribal stewardship. It is that Republican officials overrode broad support for it.

The Extractive State, Plainly

This is a familiar pattern. Public land protections are denounced as overreach. Mining and extraction interests get presented as common sense. Republican officials claim to speak for “locals” while ignoring the actual locals in the data. Then a federal president does the dirty work and calls it alignment with the public interest.

That is not a policy disagreement. It is a coalition between executive power, state Republican politics, and extractive industry, with public opinion treated as decoration. The monuments were protected because previous presidents recognized that some places should not be reduced to industrial inventory. Trump and Utah’s Republican leadership chose the opposite: land, tribes, and public consent all subordinated to the demands of extraction.
