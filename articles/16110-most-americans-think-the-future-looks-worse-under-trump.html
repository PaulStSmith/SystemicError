Most Americans think the future looks worse under Trump

War by Fiat, Bills by the Gallon

The Cost of Presidential Power

This story is not about anonymous market turbulence. It is about a president using military power, then leaving ordinary drivers to absorb the bill. The source says gas prices rose after Donald Trump launched war with Iran, and that the price at the pump has climbed from under $2.99 on February 26 to $4.16 by Tuesday. That is not a mystery of supply and demand in the abstract. It is the material cost of a political decision made at the top.

Who Actually Holds Power

The people with real power here are obvious: the president, the military chain he commands, and the officials who helped convert escalation into policy. Motorists, voters, and even Republican respondents do not control any of this. They only receive the consequences after the fact. That imbalance is the point. Power decides. The public pays.

The Poll Is Not Noise

The poll matters because it shows the damage is not being filtered through partisan loyalty as cleanly as Trump would like. The source reports that 59 percent of respondents expect gas prices to keep rising, including more than eight in ten Democrats, more than six in ten independents, and more than one-third of Republicans. That last number is the political warning sign. Even inside Trump’s own coalition, a sizable minority is looking at the war and the economy and not buying the confidence routine.

Optimism as Cover

Trump is described as telling reporters that negotiations are “going well,” even while the conflict remains rocky and Iran and Israel have traded fire. That is the familiar executive script: claim momentum, deny damage, and speak as if reassurance can outrun reality. But the reporting makes clear the public is not sharing the president’s confidence. The gasoline line is already telling a different story.

What the Framing Tries to Hide

The useful fiction in stories like this is that prices “rise” on their own, or that hardship is just unfortunate complexity. The source does partly resist that by naming the decision that started the chain. Still, the broader political habit remains intact: deliberate escalation gets dressed up as uncertainty, and foreseeable fallout gets treated as an external shock. That is misdirection. It converts agency into weather.

The Pattern

This is the governing pattern: concentrated power creates a crisis, institutions normalize it, and the public is told to tolerate the consequences as if they were nobody’s fault. The Strait of Hormuz being “effectively closed,” the warning about further deterioration, and the lingering risk of another spike all point to the same structure. When leaders treat war as a maneuver and everyday life as an afterthought, the price of that contempt shows up where people cannot ignore it: at the pump, in the budget, and eventually in the political trust they no longer have to spare.
