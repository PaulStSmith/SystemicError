Trump caught using taxpayer cash for bizarre paving obsession

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosAugust 21, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Park Service Is Not Broken. It Is Being Used.

Context

The Atlantic’s Michael Scherer reported that under Trump, the National Park Service has been made to serve a selective agenda: more than 1,500 projects are delayed while a small set of White House favorites is fast-tracked. Interior Secretary Doug Burgum has reportedly created a “White House priorities” docket that moves cosmetic and symbolic projects ahead of routine public work, even as staffing cuts from the Department of Government Efficiency leave basic maintenance short-handed.

Power, Not Confusion

This is not administrative drift. The actual power sits with the White House and Interior leadership, which decide what counts as urgent and what can rot in place. When the president’s preferences decide which park projects move and which are frozen, the institution is not malfunctioning. It is being directed to reflect presidential vanity.

The important fact is not that the Park Service lacks capacity in the abstract. It is that capacity is being allocated politically. That is a choice, not a mystery.

The Real Priorities

The list of accelerated projects tells the story plainly: brick pavers outside Lafayette Park, a fence around a golf course, gilded equestrian statues, a $25 million helicopter replacement, Camp David landscaping, drones for regional law enforcement. Meanwhile, an archaeological survey at Camp Nelson National Monument and wildfire-resistant dumpsters sit in limbo.

That contrast matters. The state can find speed for prestige, spectacle, and presidential convenience. It can also find time to neglect preservation, safety, and the unglamorous work that actually serves the public. Scarcity is being weaponized as a filter: what flatters Trump moves; what merely functions waits.

The Cover Story

Trump’s public line about the Lafayette Park pavers is a perfect example of how power launders itself through confusion. He told reporters he was still “deliberating” over whether red brick should become gray granite, as if this were a thoughtful civic question rather than a president micromanaging a vanity renovation. He also claimed the work would be funded by “a contribution that I made,” even though internal documents reportedly show public funds being used.

That is the pattern: private ego dressed up as public decision-making, with public money quietly absorbing the cost. The language of choice disguises the reality of capture.

Neglect As Policy

The staffing cuts matter because they turn deliberate prioritization into broader institutional decay. When DOGE cuts the workforce hard enough, even ordinary duties go undone. That gives the White House more room to stage its preferred projects while pretending the rest of the system simply fell behind on its own.

This is how authoritarian-style governance often looks in practice: not a clean announcement of destruction, but selective maintenance. The regime keeps what serves it and lets the rest collapse. The neglect is not incidental. It is the mechanism.

The Pattern

The larger political lesson is simple. Trump is treating federal land, federal labor, and federal funds as extensions of his personal property. The border wall through Big Bend National Park, the destruction of the White House East Wing and West Wing Colonnade, and the plans to seize the Smithsonian all point to the same logic: institutions exist until they interfere with the leader’s preferences, and then they are rearranged or stripped.

This is not just bad management. It is a governing theory built on public surrender to private appetite. The Park Service story shows how that theory operates in miniature: starve the public mission, fast-track the spectacle, and call the whole thing administration.
