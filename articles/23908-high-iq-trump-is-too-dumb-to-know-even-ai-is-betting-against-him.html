'High IQ' Trump is too dumb to know even AI is betting against him

Based on an article by Ray Richmond

Paulo SantosSeptember 21, 20263 min read

The Chatbot Is Not in Charge. The President Is.

A Joke About the Wrong Power

The column presents a comic conversation with ChatGPT about elections, gasoline prices, the Kennedy Center, presidential aide Natalie Harp, artificial intelligence, and Mike Johnson’s obedience to Donald Trump. The exchange is amusing enough, but its political value lies in an accidental confession: the software repeatedly points back toward human institutions while the author keeps inviting it to become the story.

That is the central misdirection of the AI panic. A chatbot can produce errors, fabricate certainty, and launder speculation through an authoritative tone. But it cannot demolish a cultural institution, obstruct regulation, surrender congressional power, or authorize military force. Officials and executives do those things.

Trump’s Preferred Guardrail Is Trump

Trump’s claim that AI needs no safeguard beyond a “strong and smart” president is not a technology policy. It is an argument for personal rule.

Independent testing, transparency, human oversight, and enforceable limits all distribute authority beyond the president. Trump’s formulation eliminates those checks and substitutes his own intelligence and character. The danger described by the column is therefore not autonomous software seizing power. It is a president asserting that systems with enormous social consequences should be governed by his judgment alone.

That is a familiar authoritarian maneuver: portray institutional restraint as unnecessary because the leader himself is uniquely capable. The technology is new. The sales pitch is ancient.

Congress Is Not Being Programmed

The joke that Speaker Mike Johnson might be a robot programmed to satisfy Trump’s desires lands because it disguises agency as automation. Johnson is not malfunctioning machinery. He is a political official with constitutional power, partisan incentives, and responsibility for his choices.

Calling submission robotic can become a form of absolution. It suggests Johnson lacks volition when his usefulness to Trump depends precisely on exercising it: deciding what receives a vote, what Congress investigates, and when the legislative branch declines to defend its own authority.

Institutional cowardice is still institutional action. Congress does not lose power only when a president forcibly takes it. It also loses power when congressional leaders decide that protecting their position inside a party matters more than using the authority of their office.

Speculation Is Not Accountability

The exchange is sharpest when ChatGPT refuses to declare that Harp is Trump’s mistress or that retaliation is the established motive behind a possible Kennedy Center demolition. That restraint is not proof of superior machine wisdom. It is a basic evidentiary distinction the political press often abandons when gossip is more marketable than governance.

Harp’s intimate status is politically trivial without verified evidence connecting it to public power. The consequential facts are access, official influence, and accountability. Likewise, speculation about Trump’s private motive matters less than the administration’s reported willingness to entertain demolition and the institutional leverage available to carry it out.

The article’s framing becomes weak when it treats unsupported insinuation as more revealing than documented power. Personal scandal attracts attention; administrative control produces consequences.

Prediction Masquerading as Knowledge

The numerical forecasts about congressional control and gasoline prices illustrate another failure: synthetic precision. Percentages and price ranges sound empirical even when the source provides no visible model, assumptions, or evidence sufficient to evaluate them.

That matters politically because prediction can displace responsibility. Gas prices do not simply “go” somewhere, and elections are not weather systems. War, supply disruptions, executive decisions, corporate behavior, candidate recruitment, campaign spending, and voter access shape those outcomes. A confident number can conceal the actors making the future rather than clarify them.

The proper question is not whether a chatbot can guess correctly. It is whose decisions create the conditions the chatbot is guessing about.

The Machine Makes a Convenient Alibi

AI is powerful enough to amplify propaganda, fraud, surveillance, and administrative cruelty. But the software does not choose the political objective. Governments, companies, party leaders, and their contractors determine what systems are built, where they are deployed, and who bears the damage when they fail.

The fantasy of machine domination is politically convenient because it relocates agency from accountable people to an abstract technological force. It turns policy into destiny and deliberate choices into bewildering accidents.

The larger pattern is not that computers are replacing political power. It is that powerful people are eager to use computers while denying ownership of the results. The machine supplies scale, speed, and an alibi. Human institutions supply the authority.
