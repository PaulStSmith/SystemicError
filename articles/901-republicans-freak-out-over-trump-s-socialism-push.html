Republicans freak out over Trump's 'socialism push'

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosJune 15, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump’s AI Equity Scheme Is Not Public Interest. It Is State Power for Private Giants.

Power Sits at the Top

The actual institutional power here is not with the public, and not with the Republicans now posturing as skeptics. It sits with the president, who can turn a speculative idea into policy pressure with a few remarks, and with the AI executives who are close enough to him to shape the terms before anyone else sees them.

This is the real story: Trump is not talking about “the American people” in any meaningful democratic sense. He is talking about the federal government taking stakes in companies that are already large enough to dominate the market and rich enough to treat public anxiety as a financing opportunity.

Who Put the Idea on the Table

The source makes clear that Sam Altman initially floated the concept to Trump: voluntary share surrender by OpenAI as a way to blunt public anger over AI’s rise. That matters. This did not emerge from a bottom-up effort to democratize ownership or restrain monopoly power. It came from inside the power structure, from a company leader trying to convert political risk into a structured arrangement with the state.

Trump then repackaged that private proposal as a public-minded gesture, saying pieces of these companies could be given to “the American public.” That framing is convenient nonsense. If the government buys in, the public is not gaining control. The state is acquiring a stake in firms it is already helping to legitimize.

The Republican Objection Is Real, But Narrow

The Republican backlash is not a principled defense of the public good. It is mostly discomfort with the form, not the substance. Ted Cruz, Josh Hawley, Stephen Moore, and others object to government equity, government ownership, and government favoritism. They call it socialism, reverse privatization, and a threat to innovation.

But this objection is selective. The party has already normalized massive state intervention when it benefits favored industries, favored donors, or favored geopolitical narratives. The complaint is not that power is being concentrated. The complaint is that Trump may be concentrating it in a way that offends old conservative language and unsettles the party’s ideological wallpaper.

Misdirection: “The Public” as Cover

The article’s framing risks accepting Trump’s language too readily. “Broad public angst” is invoked as justification, but the policy move described is not a response to public demand. It is a negotiation over who gets equity, who gets leverage, and who gets to decide what counts as responsible AI governance.

That is the misdirection: turn public worry about data centers, concentration, and disruption into an excuse for a government-finance arrangement with the same firms causing the anxiety. The public becomes rhetorical decoration while the real transaction happens above it.

What the Story Reveals

The deeper pattern is not “AI socialism.” It is the recurring merger of state authority and private monopoly under a nationalist banner. The government is not being asked to discipline these companies. It is being invited to become one of their stakeholders.

That is a familiar political design: privatize the upside, socialize the leverage, and describe the result as pragmatic leadership. The language changes. The structure does not.
