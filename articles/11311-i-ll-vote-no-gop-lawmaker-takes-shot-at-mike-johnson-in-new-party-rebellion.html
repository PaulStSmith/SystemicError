'I'll vote no': GOP lawmaker takes shot at Mike Johnson in new Party rebellion

That lawmaker is Rep. Thomas Massie (R-KY), who has often clashed with House Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA) and President Donald Trump – not only over spending, but also over his push to unseal documents related to Jeffrey Epstein, which both Johnson and Trump have sought to block.

“Congress will likely pass a continuing resolution by Sept. 30, but what is a continuing resolution?” Massie wrote on X early Friday morning.

“A continuing resolution is a copy of last year’s omnibus spending bill. But since Congress passed a continuing resolution last year (FY25), this year’s continuing resolution (FY26) is a copy of the omnibus (FY24) of (former President Joe) Biden’s last year. I’ll vote no.”

Continuing resolutions have become far more frequent in recent years as partisan divides among lawmakers have deepened, making it harder for Congress to agree to regular appropriations bills that are needed to fund the government each year. Massie, among the more fiscally conservative members of Congress, has often clashed with GOP leadership over spending, which, despite the rhetoric of Republican leadership, has continued to climb every year.

Massie’s fight with Republican leadership over spending came to a head over Trump’s One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which is projected to increase the federal deficit by around $3.4 trillion over ten years, per the Congressional Budget Office. Massie was among the two House Republicans that voted no on the measure, drawing scorn from the White House.

“There’s a conservative school of thought that says if we would just keep passing continuing resolutions, we’d spend the same dollars each year, so government spending would effectively go down due to inflation,” Massie continued. “The problem with that theory is (that) Congress inserted FY26 spending increases in the OBBBA.”

Massie went on to draw a line in the sand on what he’d find acceptable, detailing a proposal he made under the Biden administration that would have seen spending cut by 1%, should Congress continue to pass continuing resolutions. That proposal, he said, was “killed” by Johnson, setting the stage for what will likely be a heated battle between House Republicans ahead of the looming Sept. 30 deadline.

“It’s been falsely stated I voted for a Republican continuing resolution that passed during Biden’s administration,” Massie wrote. “Wrong. I voted for a deal, coupled with a debt ceiling increase, that said if we keep doing continuing resolutions, then the next continuing resolution cuts all spending 1% (the Massie plan). Johnson killed that deal.”

Our Analysis:

The Theater of Fiscal Responsibility: A Critique of Congressional Paradoxes

In the latest act of what can only be described as political theater, Congress finds itself once again on the precipice of a government shutdown. The narrative, however, takes an intriguing twist with Rep. Thomas Massie's (R-KY) staunch opposition to the stopgap funding measure, known as a Continuing Resolution (CR), set against the backdrop of deepening partisan divides and fiscal irresponsibility veiled in rhetoric.

Massie's Crusade Against the Status Quo

Massie's defiance is not just a blip on the radar but a manifestation of a larger, systemic dysfunction within Congress. His critique of the CR is both poignant and scathing: it's a lazy copy-paste of past spending indiscretions, perpetuating a cycle of fiscal neglect. What makes Massie's stand noteworthy is his consistent clash with not only the Democratic opposition but also his own party's leadership, including House Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA) and erstwhile President Donald Trump.

Massie's opposition is rooted in a principle seemingly rare in today's political landscape: fiscal conservatism. His objection to the "One Big Beautiful Bill Act," projected to bloat the federal deficit by a staggering $3.4 trillion, underscores a critical analysis of Republican leadership's hypocrisy. The GOP's mantra of fiscal restraint is, as Massie's actions suggest, nothing but lip service when confronted with the allure of grandiose spending bills.

The Illusion of Fiscal Prudence

One of Massie's critical insights is the notion that continuous CRs might lead to reduced government spending, thanks to inflation. Yet, this is a flawed strategy sabotaged by Congress itself, which sneakily incorporates spending increases into these resolutions, negating any potential fiscal benefits. Massie's "school of thought" is a stark reminder of the superficiality of Congressional efforts towards genuine financial stewardship.

A Battle of Ideals Within the GOP

Massie's proposed solution, a modest cut of 1% in spending should CRs continue, was unceremoniously axed by Johnson. This move not only highlights internal conflicts within the Republican Party but also sets the stage for what promises to be a contentious battle leading up to the September 30 deadline.

Conclusion: The Farcical Cycle Continues

As Massie rightly points out, the current trajectory of congressional action (or inaction) is a farce, a cyclical dance around the real issues of fiscal responsibility and governmental oversight. His critique, while perhaps inconvenient for some, shines a light on the systemic errors plaguing our legislative process. The upcoming deadline is not just about avoiding a government shutdown; it's a litmus test for the GOP's commitment to its purported fiscal principles.

In an age where political expediency often trumps principle, Massie's stance is a refreshing, albeit polarizing, call to action. Whether Congress heeds this call or continues on its path of fiscal irresponsibility remains to be seen. However, one thing is clear: the status quo is no longer sustainable, and the clock is ticking.
