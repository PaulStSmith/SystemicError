Supreme Court has a 'self-serving' interest in helping the GOP win the midterms: expert

The Court Is Not Above the Fight. It Is In It.

What the reporting says

Lisa Graves argues that the Supreme Court is signaling a willingness to help Republicans preserve power after the midterms, and that a pending Mississippi case over counting mail-in ballots could become the next vehicle for that intervention. Her warning is rooted in the Court’s recent move in Louisiana v. Callais, which she reads as a break from the old posture of staying out of election-year interference.

Where the power actually sits

The institution with real leverage here is not the voter, not the state clerk, and not the civic-language machinery wrapped around “election integrity.” It is the Roberts Court. The justices can reshape rules that decide who gets counted, how districts are drawn, and which party gets an easier path to governing. That is power with immediate partisan effect, even when it is dressed up as legal administration.

The decision is the point

Graves’ core accusation is not confusion; it is intentionality. She says the Court has already shown Republicans it will help them, and she ties that to both the Louisiana redistricting move and the coming Mississippi mail-ballot case. Whether one accepts every inference or not, the structural point is hard to miss: the Court is not merely resolving disputes that happen to arrive at its door. It is helping define the terms of political competition while the competition is underway.

The familiar misdirection

The usual framing turns these fights into technical debates about maps, deadlines, and procedure. That framing is convenient because it strips agency from the people making the decisions. It converts partisan advantage into abstract jurisprudence and deliberate intervention into judicial restraint. Graves is pushing against that dodge. She names the incentives: a Republican majority would not investigate the Court, while a Democratic majority might. That is not incidental background. It is part of the machine.

Judicial self-protection

The most corrosive detail in the source is not just ideological bias. It is institutional self-preservation. Graves points to unresolved scrutiny around Clarence Thomas’s financial disclosures and loan forgiveness, and Sam Alito’s donor-funded travel, as examples of why Democrats controlling Congress would matter to the Court. If that argument is right, then the Court’s interest in shaping outcomes is not only partisan. It is defensive. Power protects itself first, then invents doctrine to explain why.

The larger pattern

This is what modern court politics looks like when the robe becomes a shield. The system does not always announce repression with force; it often does it through procedure, timing, and selective restraint. Courts can tilt elections while insisting they are merely interpreting law. That is the deeper story here: not a neutral referee under pressure, but an institution with enough power to choose sides and enough prestige to pretend it has none.
