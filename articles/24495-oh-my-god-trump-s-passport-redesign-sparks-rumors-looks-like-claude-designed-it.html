'Oh my god': Trump's passport redesign sparks rumors 'looks like Claude designed it'

Based on an article by Daniel Hampton

Paulo SantosOctober 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe state has the powerBranding the nationThe personality questionThe safer distractionThe systemic error

What happened

RawStory reports that online critics compared the redesigned U.S. passport to an AI-generated layout because its cover uses a small all-caps “PASSPORT” above an oversized country name—a typographic device associated by commenters with Claude. The reporting provides no evidence that Claude or any other AI system designed the passport.

Secretary of State Marco Rubio unveiled the “Flight of the Eagle” design on September 29, 2026, while launching the AI-powered America.gov. Scheduled for 2028, the 38-page passport will feature a larger gold seal, animated eagle imagery and scenes from U.S. history. Rubio’s presentation also showed Trump in one page image, according to One Mile at a Time, but Trump’s inclusion in the standard passport has not been confirmed.

The state has the power

Internet users supplied the jokes. Rubio and the State Department control the document.

That distinction matters because a passport is not promotional merchandise. It is an instrument through which the government certifies identity and regulates international movement. The consequential decisions concern what imagery appears in it, whose version of national history it presents and whether a sitting president enters that official narrative. The resemblance to an AI design habit is trivia beside that authority.

Branding the nation

Rubio described the redesign as putting “United States of America” where it belongs—“first, right on top”—and called the interior a story of “the greatest nation in the history of the world.” Those remarks frame design choices as a correction to insufficient patriotic emphasis.

The larger seal, more conspicuous gold, presentation box and historical procession turn a functional credential into a prestige object. None of that proves misconduct. It does show an administration using routine state infrastructure to stage its preferred nationalism: louder, grander and less interested in restraint.

The personality question

The most politically significant unresolved detail is Trump’s possible appearance. One displayed image reportedly included him, while The Bulwark previously reported that a commemorative edition would place his portrait before the Declaration of Independence. RawStory says the everyday 2028 passport has not been confirmed to include him.

That uncertainty should not be inflated into fact, but it should not be buried under typography discourse either. Depicting a sitting president inside an ordinary passport would collapse the distance between the country and its temporary officeholder. The question is not whether the portrait looks tasteful. It is whether public authority is being used to place one politician inside the state’s visual definition of America.

The safer distraction

The “Claude eyebrow” framing redirects scrutiny toward the weakest actors in the story: designers, design conventions and social-media spectators. It converts an exercise of institutional power into a debate about whether the cover looks machine-made.

That is politically convenient even if no one engineered the distraction. Aesthetic ridicule is easy to circulate; examining who authorized the imagery and what precedents it creates is harder. RawStory identifies the online reaction clearly, but its headline gives the meme more weight than the state decision behind it.

The systemic error

Modern political branding works by embedding ideology in objects presented as administrative upgrades. A redesigned website, a new application process and a revised passport arrive together as improved public service, while symbolism quietly defines who represents the nation and how citizens are expected to see it.

The larger pattern is not that government design has become embarrassing. It is that personalization and nationalist spectacle can enter ordinary institutions under cover of aesthetics. The jokes target the cover. The power lies with the officials deciding what—and potentially whom—citizens must carry.
