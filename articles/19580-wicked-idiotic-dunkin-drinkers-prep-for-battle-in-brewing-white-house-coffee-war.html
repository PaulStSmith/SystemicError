President Donald Trump’s administration just started a war that it has no hope of winning, according to coffee drinkers. And it has nothing to do with the Middle East.

The Boston Globe reported Wednesday that Health and Human Services Secretary Robert Kennedy Jr. fired verbal shots at Dunkin’ Donuts and Starbucks.

An Army veteran explained to those outside of New England that Dunkin’ Donuts is to the northeast what In-N-Out is to California.

Dunkin’ loyalists, in particular, rushed to social media with hostility after they felt shots were fired by HHS.

“We’re going to ask Dunkin’ Donuts and Starbucks, ‘Show us the safety data that show that it’s OK for a teenage girl to drink an iced coffee with 115 grams of sugar in it,’” Kennedy said. “I don’t think they’re gonna be able to do it.”

“This moron has no idea how much of a third rail this is. If he goes through with a public fight with Dunkin’ he will never live this down. In the words of my people “f—— ya motha,’” Ben Collins, CEO of The Onion, wrote on BlueSky.

“Wicked idiotic,” Wayne State University Law Professor Jen Taub agreed.

“I knew they’d go after the next Bluest city after they left Minneapolis… I didn’t think they’d go like this, watch out they’ll come for your chowder by nightfall,” said radio commentator Dara Moskowitz Grumdahl.

“If Dunkin’ Donuts iced coffee is dangerous, I’m f—— Evel Knievel,” quipped author Rob Delaney.

“Them’s fighting words, especially in Massachusetts,” one social media user wrote under the Globe’s post on Facebook.

“I love the handful of people responding to this to say that Dunks is bad coffee. I don’t think they understand that New Englanders’ affection for Dunks is only incidentally related to the quality of their products,” commented tech and crypto reporter Molly White. She later added, “Is it bad coffee? Yes. Do I love it? also yes.”

“As a New Englander, I can say: Dunkin’s default is to pour in way too much sugar, probably to mask the awful taste of their coffee,” said American Progress senior fellow Topher Spiro. Physician, Dr. Ashish K. Jha, appeared to chastise or question him with one word. “Dude.”

One woman couldn’t help but key in on a specific element of Kennedy’s comments. “Why is RFK Jr. focusing on the sugar intake of teenage girls over everyone else. Is that his id speaking? He never, ever fails to be a creeper.”

Our Analysis:

Dunkin’ Donuts, Starbucks, and the Sugar War: A Bitter Brew

The Trump administration, in its infinite wisdom, has decided to ignite a caffeine-fueled conflict with the giants of the coffee industry, Dunkin’ Donuts and Starbucks. This battle, as reported by The Boston Globe, is not over trade secrets or environmental concerns, but over the sugar content in their beverages. Yes, you heard that right. In a world teeming with pressing issues, the administration has zeroed in on the sugary menace lurking in our iced coffees.

The Unwinnable War According to Coffee Aficionados

Health and Human Services Secretary Robert Kennedy Jr. has decided to take on this sugary Goliath with a challenge that sounds like it was brewed in a pot of naivety: asking Dunkin’ Donuts and Starbucks to justify the safety of their sugary drinks for teenage girls. This move is akin to poking a bear with a stick, except the bear is a national caffeine addiction and the stick is federal oversight.

The response from the public and notable figures was swift and scathing. Ben Collins, CEO of The Onion, colorfully described Kennedy as a “moron” for not realizing the sacred status Dunkin’ Donuts holds in the Northeast. Meanwhile, Wayne State University Law Professor Jen Taub and radio commentator Dara Moskowitz Grumdahl predict a backlash as bitter as a poorly brewed espresso shot. Author Rob Delaney and an army of social media users have rallied to Dunkin’s defense, highlighting the cult-like devotion the brand inspires.

The Misguided Focus on Teenage Girls

A particularly troubling aspect of this saga is Kennedy’s specific focus on the impact of sugary drinks on teenage girls. This narrow scope not only reeks of paternalism but also bizarrely singles out one demographic, prompting one observer to question if this fixation is a revealing glimpse into Kennedy’s own idiosyncrasies.

The Real Issue: Sugar or Control?

Let’s boil this down to the real espresso shot of truth: this war isn’t about sugar. It’s about control and the administration’s misguided attempt to regulate personal choices under the guise of public health. The outcry from both public figures and everyday coffee drinkers underscores a fundamental truth: people don’t like being told what to do, especially when it comes to their morning brew.

Moreover, the commentary from individuals like Molly White and Topher Spiro highlights an undeniable fact - the affection for brands like Dunkin’ transcends the quality of their coffee. It’s about tradition, comfort, and a bit of sugar-laden rebellion.

Conclusion: A Bitter Taste Left Behind

This ill-conceived battle against Dunkin’ Donuts and Starbucks leaves a bitter taste for several reasons. It’s a stark reminder of the administration’s tendency to focus on trivialities instead of addressing the myriad of real issues facing the nation. It’s a war on personal freedom, thinly veiled as a health initiative. Most importantly, it’s a misunderstanding of American culture - one where coffee, no matter how sugary, is sacred.

In the end, this conflict will likely end as most do - with no real winners, just a lot of spilled coffee and soured public opinion. The Trump administration would do well to remember that in America, coffee is more than just a drink; it’s a declaration of independence.
