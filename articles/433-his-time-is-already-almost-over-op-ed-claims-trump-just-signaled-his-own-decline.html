'His time is already almost over': Op-ed claims Trump just signaled his own decline

Based on an article by Daniel Hampton

Paulo SantosJune 21, 20262 min read

When the Boss Is Counting the Exit

The Shape of the Deal

The reporting describes a familiar political fraud: Trump starts a war in late February, promises speed and low cost, then ends up with a memorandum that looks like an exit ramp. According to the column, Iran gets sanctions relief, the U.S. helps organize up to $300 billion in reconstruction, and the whole arrangement lands after billions in munitions spending, a damaged Strait of Hormuz, and at least 13 U.S. service members dead.

Who Actually Held Power

The institutional power sits with Trump and the American war machine that followed his order. He launched the conflict, he sold it, and now he is trying to control the public meaning of the outcome. That is not ambiguity. That is ownership. When a president begins a war and then frames the terms of retreat as leverage, he is not being forced by events so much as exposed by them.

The Misdirection Game

The weak framing is the pretense that this is about Iranian “misbehavior.” That language shifts attention away from the decision-maker who lit the fuse and then tried to narrate the smoke as strategy. Trump’s “I’m the boss” routine at the G7 is part of the same performance: dominance theater for an audience that can count. If he were holding the upper hand, the column notes, he would have published the favorable terms immediately. The delay itself is the tell.

Cost Dumped Downward

The bill lands where it always does. U.S. service members are dead. The region absorbs thousands more deaths. American stockpiles are depleted, and the economic shock from the Strait of Hormuz closure ripples outward. The political class gets to talk about leverage while soldiers, civilians, and public budgets absorb the damage. That is the recurring trick: escalate in the language of strength, then distribute the consequences as someone else’s problem.

The Strongman’s Dwindling Clock

Ball’s central point is not just that Trump made a bad deal. It is that his leverage is shrinking in public view. Midterms are looming, lame-duck status is ahead, and the posture of command is curdling into obvious overcompensation. “I’m the boss” sounds less like authority than panic when the terms of your own war begin to look like surrender.

The Pattern

This is the standard life cycle of political cruelty dressed as decisiveness: promise an easy victory, manufacture a costly crisis, then sell the retreat as toughness. The public is told to admire force while absorbing the consequences of failure. The real scandal is not that the deal looks like defeat. It is that the machinery of power still tries to market defeat as control.
