'Evil is being defeated': MTG heralds 'major shifts in global leadership' after Pope Francis death

Shortly after Pope Francis's death on Monday morning, Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene (R-Ga.) took to the social platform X and wrote, "Major changes in global leadership have occurred today. The hand of God is prevailing over evil."

Although Greene did not name the Pope, many reacted to the post asking her if she was celebrating his death.

"You are bragging about the death of the Pope?!?" wrote Armand Domalewski, a data analyst.

Writer David Dault said, "Congressperson Greene seems to be both an Anti-Catholic bigot and a coward. If you didn’t like the Pope, that’s fine. But say it with your full voice. Don’t mince your words."

Activist Chase Oliver, the Libertarian Party's 2024 presidential nominee, said, "Disgusted by this Catholics should be rightfully outraged."

Greene has faced considerable backlash for her comments regarding the Catholic Church, especially for alleging that it is operated by "Satan" and endorsing policies she considers detrimental, like assisting undocumented immigrants.

During a 2022 interview, she said the Church’s charitable efforts to support migrants represented "Satan controlling the church." Her remarks led to strong reactions from Catholic authorities and groups.

The Catholic League, along with others, criticized Greene's remarks as anti-Catholic and inflammatory. Despite the blowback, Greene said her comments were a defense of Christian principles and a rejection of what she perceives as political activism in religious organizations.

"The Catholic League's Bill Donohue accused me of slandering Catholics and Catholicism in an interview I gave to Michael Voris at Church Militant," she said at the time. "Nothing could be further from the truth, and he must apologize promptly and publicly for these words."

Our Analysis:

Greene's Comments: A Dissection of Controversy and Confusion

In a world where political subtlety is as rare as a quiet day on Twitter, Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene (R-Ga.) never fails to stir the pot with remarks that oscillate between the bizarre and the downright inflammatory. Following the death of Pope Francis, Greene took to the social platform X, formerly known as Twitter, and posted a message that left many either scratching their heads or seething with anger. Her comment, "Major changes in global leadership have occurred today. The hand of God is prevailing over evil," is a masterclass in vaguebooking—a post so cryptically worded that it's akin to throwing a grenade in a room and closing the door.

Celebrating Death or Divine Intervention?

Greene's refusal to name Pope Francis directly does little to mask her intent, especially given her history of remarks regarding the Catholic Church. This isn't a case of accidental ambiguity; it's a calculated move to stoke controversy without explicitly stating her position. Critics, such as data analyst Armand Domalewski, wasted no time in condemning what they perceived as a tasteless celebration of the Pope's death. This isn't Greene's first rodeo in the realm of contentious comments, but even by her standards, this feels egregious.

Anti-Catholicism or Political Posturing?

The accusations of anti-Catholic bigotry are not unfounded, considering Greene's past comments about the Catholic Church being under "Satan's control" for its humanitarian efforts towards undocumented immigrants. This latest insinuation that Pope Francis's death is somehow a divine rectification is not just a low blow; it's a demonstration of her willingness to leverage religious sentiment for political gain. Critics like writer David Dault and activist Chase Oliver have called her out, and rightly so. Dault's challenge to Greene to speak her mind openly rather than hiding behind ambiguous statements is particularly poignant.

The Catholic Reaction and Greene's Defense

The backlash from Catholic authorities and organizations was inevitable. The Catholic League's denouncement of Greene's comments as anti-Catholic and inflammatory is a testament to the gravity of her statements. Yet, Greene's defense—that her comments are in protection of Christian principles and a rejection of political activism in religious organizations—rings hollow. It's a convoluted attempt to backtrack on her inflammatory rhetoric without actually apologizing for the hurt caused.

Conclusion: A Study in Political Divisiveness

Greene's comments on the passing of Pope Francis encapsulate a broader issue within contemporary political discourse: the tendency to use religious and humanitarian issues as battlegrounds for ideological supremacy. Her claim that her critiques are a defense of Christian principles is a smokescreen, detracting from the real issues at hand, such as how faith-based organizations should engage with contemporary social issues.

In the end, Greene's remarks are less about theological debate and more about political grandstanding. Her insistence that Bill Donohue owes her an apology is not just misplaced; it's emblematic of her approach to politics—one where controversy trumps compassion, and soundbites matter more than substance.
