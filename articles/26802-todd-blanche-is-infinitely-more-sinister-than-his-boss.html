Todd Blanche is infinitely more sinister than his boss

Based on an article by Steven Harper

Paulo SantosOctober 24, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Attorney General Todd Blanche refused on NBC’s August 16, 2026, broadcast of “Meet the Press” to pledge independence from the White House, arguing that no attorney general should do so. The article’s central claim is that Blanche is more dangerous than President Donald Trump because he is converting Trump’s demands into Justice Department policy and legal action.

According to AlterNet, Blanche became acting attorney general after Trump fired Pam Bondi on April 1, secured a new indictment of former FBI Director James Comey before the end of that month, and won Senate confirmation as Bondi’s permanent replacement on August 8 by a 50–49 vote. The outlet also reports ethics complaints, a judicial referral concerning Blanche’s conduct, and repeated criticism from federal judges. The article does not report the final resolution of those matters.

Power belongs to the office

Trump holds presidential power and, as described here, supplies the targets and demands. Blanche controls the institution capable of turning those demands into subpoenas, searches, prosecutions and lawsuits backed by the United States government.

That distinction matters. Trump’s grievance politics may establish the objective, but an attorney general decides whether the Justice Department will resist political pressure or operationalize it. Blanche’s refusal to promise independence is therefore not an abstract dispute over constitutional theory. It is a declaration about how he understands his office: presidential loyalty first, institutional restraint second.

The machinery has an operator

The reported sequence strips away any pretense that these outcomes simply emerged from an unruly bureaucracy. Career prosecutors reportedly found insufficient evidence to charge Comey and New York Attorney General Letitia James. Trump pushed Bondi to act anyway. Lindsay Halligan obtained indictments, but a federal court dismissed them after finding her appointment unlawful; a later grand jury reportedly refused to indict James again.

Blanche then obtained Comey’s re-indictment after becoming acting attorney general. AlterNet further reports that he signed an agreement shielding Trump and his family from IRS investigations, despite an earlier recusal pledge, and participated in removing more than 200 prosecutors and FBI agents connected to Trump-related cases. Those are decisions by officials with names, offices and authority—not institutional weather.

Election interference dressed as enforcement

The most consequential allegations concern the 2026 midterms. AlterNet reports that Blanche’s department continued litigation seeking state voter rolls despite losses in 26 federal courts, sued California after demanding the removal of 20,000 voters using a disputed federal database, and searched Representative Ilhan Omar’s home 22 days before an election in which she was a candidate.

The article argues that these actions support Trump’s effort to discredit the election in advance. That motive remains the outlet’s interpretation, but the foreseeable effect is plain: federal law enforcement actions close to voting can stigmatize candidates, threaten eligible voters and furnish partisan claims that election systems are corrupt.

Blanche also reportedly campaigned for Republicans after relaxing Justice Department restrictions on political participation by presidential appointees. The problem is not “confusion” about departmental norms. The reported conduct collapses the boundary between enforcing federal law and advancing the governing party’s electoral interests.

The framing lets Trump off too easily

Calling Blanche “more sinister” personalizes what is fundamentally a structure of shared responsibility. Blanche may be the disciplined executor, but Trump reportedly selected the targets, demanded action and appointed the officials who complied. The Senate then confirmed Blanche by one vote after this record was already substantially visible.

The power structure is therefore broader than one ambitious lawyer. The president directs. The attorney general implements. Senators legitimize. Political appointees execute. When coverage turns that system into a contest over which individual is more malevolent, it risks making institutional capture look like a character defect rather than a governing project.

Personalized rule becomes policy

Watergate-era restraints were never self-enforcing. They depended on officials accepting that the Justice Department’s coercive power could not become a president’s private instrument. Blanche’s reported position rejects that premise at the top of the department.

The larger pattern is authoritarian administration through ordinary offices: personnel purges, selective enforcement, partisan legal theories and election-related interventions presented as routine governance. The durable harm is not merely that one president seeks retribution. It is that officials with institutional power are constructing a Justice Department in which presidential grievance can pass for federal purpose—and partisan loyalty can be exercised with the force of law.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
