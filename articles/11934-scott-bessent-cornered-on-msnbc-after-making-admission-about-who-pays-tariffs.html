Scott Bessent cornered on MSNBC after making admission about 'who pays' tariffs

Scott Bessent, Eugene Robinson (MSNBC screenshot)

Toward the end of an extensive interview with Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, MSNBC's Eugene Robinson was afforded an opportunity to question the Donald Trump official and put him on the spot over the tariffs that went into effect at midnight.

After making a pitch to co-host Joe Scarborough that the tariffs should soon increase employment numbers, Robinson asked Bessent, "Mr. Secretary, there's one question I've got to ask you, because there has been some confusion. So we've got a tariff of, what, 50 percent on Brazil now, I think. And so if someone here, an importer wants to buy Brazilian products today or tomorrow and import them, they're going to pay 50 percent to the Treasury. And so who writes that check?"

"Well, it's a couple of things," the Trump Cabinet member replied. "First, we could have substitutions. So there's very little that only comes from Brazil, you know that it could it could come from Argentina. It could come from ––."

"Right," Robinson interrupted. "But assuming it does come from Brazil, say, or it comes from any country with a tariff, who writes the check to the treasury?"

"Well, the check is written to the person who receives it at the dock in the U.S.," Bessent tentatively answered.

"Ummhmm, the check is written by the person who receives it at the dock. So tariff is paid in this country by the importer, is that right?" the smiling Robinson persisted.

"But the Brazilian exporter could decide that they want to keep market share," Bessent protested. "They could lower their price so that the full 50 percent of the tariff, say, and ––."

"You're right, they can eat part of the cost," Robinson interjected.

"Which is what we've seen," Bessent offered.

"Okay, okay, but the check is written by the importer right at the dock?" Robinson asked once again.

"Yeah, and then, you know, the importer can pass it on or not," the Trump official blandly suggested.

Our Analysis:

Tariff Tangle: Unraveling the Misconceptions

In an era where political discourse often resembles a chaotic whirlwind, clarity and precision are as rare as they are necessary. The recent interview with Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent on MSNBC, conducted by Eugene Robinson, provides a stark illustration of the muddled understanding that often surrounds economic policies, particularly tariffs. Let's dissect this interaction with the surgical precision it demands, revealing not just the misconceptions but the systemic issues at play.

The Dance Around the Tariff Question

Robinson's questioning aimed to elucidate a fundamental aspect of tariffs that is often glossed over or misunderstood: who actually pays them? The answer, as should be clear to those steering the ship of state economic policy, is that tariffs are paid by importers, in this case, American businesses that import goods from Brazil. Bessent's hesitant and roundabout answers only serve to muddy waters that should be crystal clear.

The Misdirection of Substitution

Bessent's initial attempt to divert the conversation towards the possibility of substitution is a classic misdirection. Yes, in some theoretical market, importers could switch to buying goods from Argentina or another country to avoid tariffs on Brazilian products. However, this sidesteps the issue rather than confronting it. The real question isn't about the potential for market adjustments but about the immediate impact of the tariffs on importers and, by extension, consumers.

The Reality of Cost-Sharing

When Bessent finally acknowledges that "the check is written by the importer right at the dock," he touches on the truth, only to obfuscate it moments later by suggesting that Brazilian exporters might lower their prices to maintain market share. This notion of cost-sharing between exporters and importers is not entirely without merit but oversimplifies the economic impact of tariffs. It's a band-aid solution to a bullet wound, hinting at the broader issue: tariffs are a tax on imports, and like any tax, the cost is ultimately borne by consumers.

The Unspoken Truth: Consumers Pay the Price

What Bessent and, by extension, the administration fail to address directly is that tariffs are not a foreign penalty but a domestic tax. The importer may write the check, but the cost is inevitably passed down the supply chain, landing squarely on the shoulders of American consumers. This is the unspoken truth of tariffs: they are a tax on consumers, wrapped in the flag of nationalistic economic policies.

The Bottom Line

The interview, for all its evasions and half-answers, reveals a stark reality about the administration's tariff policy. Despite the veneer of protecting American jobs and businesses, the policy acts as a regressive tax on American consumers, especially affecting those who can least afford it. The administration's reluctance to plainly state this fact speaks volumes about the political motivations behind the tariffs, prioritizing sound bites over sound policy.

In conclusion, as we navigate the stormy seas of economic policy, let us demand clarity and honesty from those at the helm. The American public deserves to understand the true cost of tariffs, not be placated by political sleight of hand. It's time for a candid conversation about who pays the price for these policies -- and why.
