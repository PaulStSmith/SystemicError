Judge forces Trump DOJ to unmask officials behind his golf course plan

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 14, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Public officials, hidden from the publicThe administration’s account unraveled in courtA public park treated as presidential property

The Trump Justice Department tried to conceal the identities of federal employees who worked on a taxpayer-funded overhaul of Washington’s East Potomac Golf Links, even as a federal judge investigated whether the administration advanced the project in defiance of her order. U.S. District Judge Ana C. Reyes rejected the secrecy request and compelled the government to disclose the names by 5 p.m. Thursday. The administration complied shortly before the deadline—then characterized routine judicial scrutiny of executive conduct as an unconstitutional intrusion.

Public officials, hidden from the public

Justice Department lawyers asked Reyes to seal the names of Interior Department employees who “drafted, edited, or provided input” on plans submitted to the National Capital Planning Commission. They argued that the nationally prominent case had generated outrage and death threats, leaving government workers reasonably fearful of publicity, harassment and possible harm.

Threats are serious. But the administration sought more than protection against specific dangers: It asked the court to “preserve the anonymity” of officials performing government work on a multimillion-dollar project involving public money and nearly 400 acres of public land.

Reyes found little risk that disclosure would prejudice employees acting within the scope of their jobs. Her order placed accountability where it belongs. The people preparing and approving federal plans are not private bystanders accidentally caught in a political controversy; they are participants in an official process whose legality the court is examining.

The administration nevertheless claimed that ordering production of the names was “not a legitimate exercise of the court’s authority” and infringed the executive branch’s constitutional right to manage its affairs. That argument attempts to convert judicial oversight into executive victimization. The relevant question is not whether the administration prefers confidentiality. It is whether officials proceeded in conflict with a federal injunction and whether representations made in court were truthful.

The administration’s account unraveled in court

The disclosure fight followed weeks in which the administration maintained that no closure of the park was imminent, despite Trump publicly describing his plans for the property on Truth Social. According to AlterNet, the Justice Department later acknowledged in court filings that preparations to close East Potomac Park on November 15 were underway.

That admission reportedly surprised Reyes. She appointed a former federal prosecutor to examine the government’s conduct and determine whether officials had lied or violated ethics rules. Those remain questions under investigation, not established findings. But the sequence explains why the names mattered: The court was trying to identify who participated in developing the plan, when they acted and how their work aligned with what the government had represented.

On October 26, Reyes issued a preliminary injunction prohibiting tree removal at the golf course. She also required 10 days’ notice before any “irreparable modification” of the site while a lawsuit brought by the D.C. Preservation League and two golfers proceeds.

Against that background, the anonymity request served an institutional purpose beyond employee privacy. Keeping the participants secret would have made it harder to reconstruct responsibility inside the executive branch precisely when the government’s previous account had become suspect.

A public park treated as presidential property

The dispute concerns what AlterNet described as potentially Trump’s largest Washington makeover project: a multimillion-dollar redesign of a public golf course within a nearly 400-acre park. The funding comes from taxpayers, the land belongs to the public and the planning work was performed by public employees. Yet the administration approached disclosure as though identifying those employees would violate the president’s own rights.

That framing reverses the actual power relationship. The judge did not order private citizens to expose themselves for criticizing the government. She required the government to identify its own personnel during an inquiry into possible noncompliance with a court order. The executive branch controls the agencies, the project planning and the proposed closure. The preservation group and golfers challenging it do not.

The Justice Department’s resistance also reveals the consequence of the administration’s earlier misdirection. After minimizing the immediacy of plans for East Potomac Park, it acknowledged in litigation that the closure preparations were real. It then sought secrecy for the officials involved and attacked the judge’s authority when she refused.

The government ultimately released the names because Reyes imposed a deadline it could not evade. Whether officials lied or breached ethics rules remains for the court-appointed investigator to assess. What is already clear is that the administration wanted the freedom to transform public land with public money while withholding the identities of the public officials doing the work—and treated accountability as the constitutional offense.
