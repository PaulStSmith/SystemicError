Trump's 'creaking' brain reveals a disturbing GOP calculation: analyst

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 27, 20263 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that New Republic editor Michael Tomasky interpreted President Donald Trump’s remarks at a Saturday rally in Perry, Georgia, as evidence that Trump may see political advantage in Republican midterm losses. That is Tomasky’s theory, not an established fact about Trump’s intentions.

At the rally, Trump acknowledged higher fuel costs while claiming the United States was making “a frickin’ fortune” from the war in Iran. AlterNet says Tomasky disputed that claim, citing reports that Persian Gulf oil exports had exceeded prewar levels while higher tanker costs had sharply reduced Asian orders for American crude.

Tomasky offered three possible incentives for Trump: indifference when he is not on the ballot, an opportunity to claim Republicans cannot win without him, and the prospect of using a defeat to generate investigations, conflict, and fraud allegations. He cited a Justice Department lawsuit concerning certification of Alaska’s Senate race, but the article does not establish that Trump wants Republicans to lose or that the administration will obstruct certification.

Power is not a personality quiz

Trump holds the presidency. His administration controls executive agencies, including the Justice Department. Republican candidates, by contrast, can distance themselves from him, but they cannot neutralize presidential power or prevent federal institutions from being used in election litigation.

That distinction matters more than Tomasky’s speculation about an “aging, creaking” mind. Insults about cognition substitute amateur diagnosis for institutional analysis. The relevant question is not whether Trump’s remarks reveal decay. It is what a president says, what his administration does, and which officials are positioned to impose consequences.

The decision-makers are visible

Trump chose to boast about supposed wartime profits while acknowledging that advisers wanted him to avoid the subject because people were paying more at the pump. Whatever his motive, the reported statement shows a deliberate communications choice: he understood the obvious political liability and spoke anyway.

The Justice Department, according to the cited analysis, chose to sue over possible certification of Alaska’s Senate result if Republican incumbent Dan Sullivan lost to Democrat Mary Peltola. The administration said a second Republican candidate with the same name could confuse voters. That stated rationale must be preserved; so must the harder fact that federal litigation aimed at certification carries consequences far beyond campaign messaging.

Speculation obscures the machinery

The article’s central claim—that Trump secretly wants a Republican wipeout—is provocative but unproven. It converts observable conduct into a psychological mystery and makes Trump’s appetite for conflict the organizing explanation. That framing risks misdirection.

Republican candidates are not merely victims of one man’s erratic rhetoric. Those who support him make a political choice; those who retreat only when he becomes electorally costly are managing exposure, not surrendering power. Nor should a Justice Department lawsuit be reduced to evidence of Trump’s emotional needs. It is an exercise of state authority and should be examined on its legal basis, timing, and requested remedy.

Conflict can be politically useful

Tomasky’s strongest point is structural, even if his claim about intent outruns the evidence. A congressional defeat could furnish Trump with antagonists, investigations, and grievances. Those conditions may be politically useful to a leader who portrays scrutiny as persecution.

But usefulness after a defeat does not prove a desire to lose beforehand. The grounded conclusion is narrower: Trump’s political method can convert setbacks into loyalty tests and institutional confrontation. That leaves allies carrying electoral risk while he retains the starring role whether they win or lose.

The system rewards the spectacle

The larger pattern is a politics organized around personal dominance rather than collective governance. Candidates are valuable when they validate the leader, expendable when they fail, and useful again when their defeat can be blamed on disloyalty or fraud.

Coverage weakens that analysis when it dwells on a politician’s allegedly “mildewed” brain. The consequential story is not mental deterioration inferred from a rally performance. It is concentrated executive power, party dependence, and the possibility that election administration becomes another arena for presidential grievance. Personality supplies the spectacle; institutions determine who pays for it.
