Ben Ferguson, host of the conservative talk show "The Ben Ferguson Show," claimed that the decline in violent crime seen across the U.S. was due to Trump's leadership.

Host Abby Phillip cited federal data showing that crime rates reached a 30-year low in 2024 after seeing a significant spike in 2023.

"I agree, and a lot of it is the leadership of Trump," Ferguson cut in.

Phillip was visibly taken aback by the claim.

"This happened before Trump," she explained.

Ferguson then tried to argue that Trump emboldened law enforcement with his rhetoric on the campaign trail, and therefore could be given credit for the declining crime rates.

"What evidence do you have that it was Donald Trump?" Phillip asked.

Federal data shows crime rates decreased by 50% between 2023 and 2024. Ferguson reiterated that law enforcement felt like they could do their jobs again because of Trump, a claim that Phillip and other panelists took issue with.

"Don't let facts get in the way of political talking points," Phillip responded.

Our Analysis:

The Illusion of Causality: Trump and the Decline in Crime Rates

In a remarkable display of cognitive dissonance, the panel on CNN's "NewsNight with Abby Phillip" became an arena for what can only be described as a fact-free assertion by Ben Ferguson, host of "The Ben Ferguson Show." Ferguson boldly attributed the decline in violent crime rates across the U.S. to the leadership of President Donald Trump. This claim not only stretches the bounds of credulity but outright snaps them.

Federal data, the kind rooted in reality and not wishful thinking, indicates that crime rates hit a 30-year low in 2024 after a significant spike in 2023. This statistical reality provides a grounding point for our discussion. Ferguson's attempt to credit Trump for this decline is a textbook example of the post hoc ergo propter hoc fallacy—assuming that because one event followed another, the first must have caused the second.

Host Abby Phillip's surprise at Ferguson's claim is understandable, given that Trump's presidency ended before this period of declining crime rates. This timeline alone should have been enough to steer Ferguson away from his argument. Yet, he persisted, suggesting that Trump's campaign rhetoric somehow emboldened law enforcement and led to a safer America.

Let's dissect this point. Ferguson's argument hinges on the notion that Trump's support of law enforcement translated directly into a tangible reduction in crime. However, this overlooks a myriad of factors that influence crime rates, including economic conditions, social programs, and local policing strategies—none of which have a direct line to Trump's rhetoric.

Phillip's pushback, "What evidence do you have that it was Donald Trump?" is the question that Ferguson, and indeed any proponent of this theory, fails to answer satisfactorily. The leap from Trump's support of law enforcement to a 50% decrease in crime rates between 2023 and 2024 requires evidence that simply isn't provided.

Ferguson's assertion that law enforcement felt they could "do their jobs again" because of Trump is a nebulous claim at best. It's an attempt to attribute complex societal changes to the perceived influence of a single individual's rhetoric, ignoring the multifaceted reality of how crime rates are influenced.

Phillip's retort, "Don't let facts get in the way of political talking points," serves as a fitting rebuke not only to Ferguson but to anyone who attempts to manipulate statistical data for political gain. The discussion on "NewsNight with Abby Phillip" exemplifies a broader issue within political discourse: the readiness with which some individuals discard objectivity in favor of a narrative that suits their biases.

In conclusion, the attempt to credit Donald Trump for the decline in crime rates is not only unsupported by evidence but also indicative of a larger trend of attributing complex societal outcomes to the oversimplified influence of individual leaders. This kind of rhetoric does not advance meaningful discussions on crime and its prevention but instead muddies the waters with baseless claims. It's a disservice to the public and to the nuanced understanding required to truly address and reduce crime in our communities.
