The media is lying to you about Trump’s 'failures'

Based on an article by D. Earl Stephens

Paulo SantosOctober 20, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet columnist D. Earl Stephens argues that Donald Trump’s agenda should not be described as a failure because, by Stephens’s measure, it has produced the fear, institutional damage, inequality, and personal benefit that Trump and his allies sought. Writing 17 days before the midterm elections, Stephens portrays the second term as a “catastrophic success” for Trump, wealthy supporters, and a political base hostile to equal rights.

Stephens cites troops and masked armed personnel in American streets, attacks on workers’ and equal rights, weakened public programs and environmental protections, higher prices, Trump’s personal use of public money, and threats to elections. The column, however, supplies no supporting documents, figures, official responses, or independent corroboration for those claims. Its central argument is an interpretation of purpose and political success, not a demonstrated investigation of each allegation.

Power sets the metric

Trump holds executive power. His administration controls federal agencies, directs enforcement priorities, shapes foreign policy, and can weaken or redirect public institutions. Congressional Republicans and wealthy political allies possess additional power to authorize, finance, normalize, or profit from that program.

That hierarchy matters because “failure” is meaningless until the beneficiary is named. An administration can fail workers while succeeding for employers seeking weaker labor protections. It can fail households facing higher costs while succeeding for wealthy interests receiving favorable policy. It can fail democratic accountability while succeeding at concentrating executive control.

Judging the project only by whether it improves ordinary life quietly adopts the public’s interests as the administration’s own. Stephens’s sharper contention is that this assumption is unsupported. The relevant test is not whether governance works for everyone, but whether those exercising power obtain what they pursued.

Harm is not administrative confusion

The column’s strongest insight is that repeated, directionally consistent harm should not automatically be reported as incompetence. When officials announce punitive goals and then use state power to pursue them, describing the results merely as dysfunction can erase agency.

But intent still requires evidence. Stephens repeatedly presents design as settled fact without showing, claim by claim, the orders, votes, budget decisions, or internal records that would establish it. His argument would be stronger if it connected each consequence to a named decision-maker and documented act. Moral certainty is not a substitute for a chain of responsibility.

The distinction is crucial: institutional failure can be accidental, foreseeable, accepted, or actively desired. Journalism should determine which applies. Calling everything chaos excuses officials; calling everything intentional without demonstrating intent weakens the indictment.

The media critique needs receipts

Stephens accuses mainstream outlets of falsely portraying Trump’s agenda as unsuccessful, but he identifies no particular report, headline, or performance measure. That leaves the alleged misdirection too abstract to evaluate. “The media” is not a decision-maker, and treating it as one reproduces the same imprecision the column condemns.

The defensible criticism is narrower. Political coverage often measures an administration against conventional governing objectives—stable institutions, broadly shared prosperity, competent services—even when its coalition may reward domination, exclusion, deregulation, or spectacle. That framing can make deliberate policy victories look like implementation failures because the reporter has chosen the wrong scoreboard.

A rigorous account would name the outlet, identify its stated measure of failure, and compare that measure with the administration’s announced objectives and concrete actions. Without that work, media criticism becomes accusation rather than analysis.

Accountability has a hierarchy

The column spends substantial energy condemning Trump’s voters, Christian nationalists, racists, billionaires, and the January 6 mob. Those constituencies may provide votes, money, ideological permission, or physical force, but they do not possess equal institutional authority.

Responsibility begins with the officials who command agencies, sign orders, propose budgets, enforce policy, and decide whether abuses carry consequences. It then extends to legislators, donors, executives, and institutional leaders who enable those choices. Voters matter, but blaming an undifferentiated mass can distract from the smaller group with direct authority to turn grievance into state action.

Stephens is right to reject a story in which damage simply happens. Yet replacing passive language with indiscriminate fury does not complete the accounting. Power must be traced through decisions, not merely through cultural affiliation.

The system protects the wrong definition of success

The larger pattern is a political press trained to recognize success as normal governance and failure as disorder. That vocabulary breaks down when powerful actors can benefit from weakened oversight, frightened opponents, hollowed-out institutions, and public exhaustion.

The systemic error is not that every destructive outcome proves a hidden master plan. It is that coverage too often treats stated extremism as theater, foreseeable consequences as surprises, and identifiable decisions as ambient chaos. The correction is exacting attribution: who had authority, what they did, who enabled it, who gained, and who paid.

An administration should not receive credit merely for causing harm. But neither should officials escape responsibility because their policies produced disorder instead of competent government. Political failure for the public can still be political success for the people holding power.
