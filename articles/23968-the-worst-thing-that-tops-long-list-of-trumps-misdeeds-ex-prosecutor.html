'The worst thing' that tops 'long list' of Trump’s misdeeds: ex-prosecutor

Trump’s Complaint Is Not Judicial Betrayal. It Is Judicial Independence.

The Evidence

Writing in The Hill, former federal prosecutor James D. Zirin identifies Donald Trump’s attacks on judicial independence as the gravest misconduct of his second term. Zirin cites Trump denouncing Supreme Court justices Neil Gorsuch and Amy Coney Barrett after an adverse tariff ruling, accusing the Court without evidence of foreign influence, and later attacking unnamed justices after losing a mail-ballot case.

The Power Runs From the White House

The central actor is not a frustrated litigant confronting an inaccessible system. It is the president of the United States using the authority and visibility of his office to condemn judges for failing to deliver his preferred outcomes.

Trump appointed Gorsuch, Barrett, and Brett Kavanaugh. But nomination is not ownership. A justice does not incur a political debt to the president who selected them. Trump’s reported language—calling unfavorable votes “disloyal” and complaining that the justices were no longer the people he interviewed—treats independence as personal defection.

That is an authoritarian conception of judicial appointment: power granted by a leader should remain answerable to the leader.

“Sore Loser” Is an Alibi

Zirin calls Trump a sore loser and a bully. The description fits the reported conduct, but it also shrinks an institutional assault into a personality defect.

A sore loser complains. A president who publicly defines judges as disloyal for ruling against him advances a political test for legitimate judging. The foreseeable effect is pressure: favorable decisions become proof of fidelity, while unfavorable ones invite public degradation and unsupported accusations.

The issue is not Trump’s temperament. It is his attempt, through rhetoric, to make personal loyalty the standard by which judicial authority is judged.

The Loyalty Trap

Trump’s attacks place his appointees inside a deliberately rigged frame. If they rule his way, he can present the result as vindication. If they do not, he can portray them as cowards, fools, or corrupted instruments.

That framing erases the actual constitutional function of a court. Judges are supposed to decide cases under law, not preserve the political identity assigned to them during confirmation. Calling independence “disloyalty” turns a structural safeguard into an offense against the executive.

It also signals what Trump appears to expect from future appointees: not merely a judicial philosophy, but dependable allegiance when his own power is at stake.

The Laundry List Obscures the Mechanism

The source article opens with a crowded catalogue of alleged abuses and controversies, most of which it does not substantiate within the text. That framing supplies outrage faster than evidence and invites readers to debate which offense is “worst.”

The ranking is less important than the mechanism Zirin documents. A president loses, attacks the legitimacy and character of the judges, and implies that appointment created an obligation. That pattern is concrete. It does not require every other allegation in the article to be accepted.

By presenting the issue as a competition among scandals, the article risks obscuring how power actually works: repeated pressure on institutions can be more consequential than any single eruption.

The Systemic Error Is Personal Government

Judicial independence is vulnerable precisely because courts command neither armies nor budgets. Their authority depends heavily on other political actors accepting that an adverse ruling is still a ruling.

Trump’s reported response rejects that restraint. His rhetoric recasts public office as a favor bank in which appointment purchases loyalty and institutional disagreement becomes betrayal.

The larger pattern is personal government pressing against constitutional government. The danger resides not merely in insult, ego, or incivility, but in a president teaching the country that institutions are legitimate only when they serve him.
