A degrading new political trend no longer rattles Americans

Based on an article by D. Earl Stephens

Paulo SantosOctober 26, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet argues that a degrading campaign ad from Ohio gubernatorial candidate Vivek Ramaswamy attacking Democratic opponent Amy Acton reflects a decade-long normalization of misogynistic and dehumanizing Republican rhetoric. The commentary says Donald Trump defended the ad as “very powerful” and blamed the press for portraying it negatively.

The piece contends that Trump’s repeated insults toward women, immigrants and political opponents lowered the party’s standards and gave figures such as Ramaswamy permission to escalate. But it supplies no polling or other evidence establishing that Americans generally are no longer rattled by such attacks. Desensitization is the author’s explanation, not a demonstrated national finding.

Power sets the standard

Ramaswamy made the immediate choice to run the ad. The larger institutional power, however, belongs to a Republican Party that repeatedly selected Trump as its presidential nominee and continued rewarding his politics after years of degrading rhetoric.

That distinction matters. This is not simply an outbreak of bad manners between candidates. Party leaders, donors, media allies and voters determine which conduct carries political costs. When humiliation generates attention, loyalty and presidential praise, candidates learn that cruelty is not a liability. It is campaign infrastructure.

The outrage is selective

The article’s strongest point is that treating one advertisement as an astonishing breach obscures the political system that made it predictable. Republicans who tolerated Trump’s language cannot credibly present Ramaswamy’s ad as an inexplicable departure without accounting for the standard their party already enforced through nominations, endorsements and votes.

Selective condemnation can become reputational laundering: reject one especially conspicuous offense while leaving the machinery that rewards it untouched. The problem is not that every Republican voter necessarily approves of every insult; the supplied reporting does not establish that. The problem is that disapproval without political consequence changes nothing.

Contempt is doing political work

Degradation is not merely decorative ugliness. It shifts attention away from a candidate’s authority, record and proposals and toward whether the target deserves basic dignity. In Acton’s case, the attack described by AlterNet turns a contest for public office into an exercise in gendered humiliation.

That framing also misdirects responsibility. The burden moves to the target to endure the abuse, to journalists to decide whether it is sufficiently shocking, and to the public to perform outrage. The candidate who commissioned the attack—and the political institutions that reward it—can disappear behind a fog of controversy.

The evidence has limits

AlterNet weakens its case when it substitutes sweeping denunciation for substantiation. Claims about what Republican voters think, why they vote and whether they possess moral standards are asserted rather than demonstrated. The article also invokes numerous grave allegations and policy harms without supplying supporting reporting in the text provided.

That excess gives defenders an easy diversion: dispute the broadest accusation and ignore the documented political choice at the center of the story. A sharper indictment needs less mind-reading. Ramaswamy ran the ad. Trump praised it. Republican institutions have repeatedly elevated Trump despite his public record of insults. Those facts already expose the incentive structure.

Normalization is an outcome

The systemic failure is not that political cruelty suddenly stopped shocking everyone. The source does not prove that claim. It is that degradation can become routine without losing its function.

Once contempt is normalized, each new offense is discussed as a question of taste rather than power. The useful questions are plainer: Who authorized it? Who amplified it? Who benefited? Who could impose consequences and declined to do so? In this case, the answer begins with Ramaswamy and extends through the party figures and institutions that made humiliation an electorally rational choice.
