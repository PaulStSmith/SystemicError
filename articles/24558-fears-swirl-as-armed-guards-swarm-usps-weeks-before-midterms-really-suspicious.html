Fears swirl as armed guards swarm USPS weeks before midterms: 'Really suspicious'

Based on an article by Alexandria Jacobson

Paulo SantosOctober 8, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power is inside USPSSuspicion is not evidencePrivatization is the durable storyFear can misdirect accountabilityThe system hides in the contract

What happened

RawStory reports that the U.S. Postal Service deployed nearly 1,000 armed officers supplied by private security company Prosegur at facilities in at least 55 locations before the Nov. 3 midterm elections. Postal officials said the guards were part of a broader security plan, not an election-specific operation, and were intended to deter threats to employees and the mailstream.

Current and former postal workers, union officials, and outside researchers questioned the timing, contractor oversight, and concentration of deployments in large facilities and politically competitive or Democratic-leaning states. Some speculated that contractors could interfere with ballots or furnish President Donald Trump with grounds to attack the election’s legitimacy, but RawStory reports no evidence that guards handled ballots, received political orders, or disrupted voting.

The article also identifies a concrete labor dispute. USPS has curtailed street patrols by its unionized Postal Police Officers while bringing armed contractors into facilities. A Charlotte-area union leader said workers had filed complaints alleging that Prosegur guards slept on duty and, in one case, assaulted an employee. Prosegur said it could not locate the incidents or complaints described to it.

The power is inside USPS

The institutional decision-maker here is USPS, not frightened employees, professors, or former officers. Postal management selected the contractor, determined where armed guards would operate, and adopted an arrangement that the Postal Police Officers Association says leaves day-to-day supervision with a private company.

That transfer matters even without an electoral plot. Armed authority inside public infrastructure should come with clear command, public standards, and enforceable accountability. Outsourcing obscures those lines while allowing USPS to describe the deployment as routine operational security.

Trump’s attacks on mail voting supply the political context, including his earlier executive action seeking restrictions and his later encouragement of Republican mail voting. But context is not proof that he ordered this deployment. RawStory does not establish that he selected Prosegur, directed the guards, or designed their locations.

Suspicion is not evidence

The article’s headline promises a sinister explanation that its reporting does not deliver. “Really suspicious” is a former postal officer’s judgment about timing, not evidence of ballot interference. The imagined scenario involving armored trucks removing ballots is explicitly speculation. So is the theory that Trump might threaten Prosegur with a future executive order.

Those distinctions are not technicalities. When a story gives lurid hypotheticals the same emotional weight as documented decisions, it converts legitimate scrutiny into insinuation. That weakens the case against the conduct the reporting actually establishes: privatized armed security, disputed oversight, substantial expense, and unresolved worker complaints.

The placement evidence is also less conclusive than the framing suggests. One researcher described deployments in mostly Democratic or swing states, while a union official said guards at her site were assigned to a package facility rather than the building processing standard mail. RawStory provides no systematic comparison showing that election operations determined the locations.

Privatization is the durable story

The clearest power shift is from unionized public officers to contractor-controlled guards. Postal police have spent years fighting USPS over restrictions that kept them from patrolling areas where letter carriers faced rising crime, according to their union. Management nevertheless found money and authority for a private armed force inside major facilities.

That arrangement fragments responsibility. USPS can point to the contractor; the contractor can invoke client confidentiality; workers confronting alleged misconduct must navigate both. Prosegur’s reported history of labor and human-rights allegations makes scrutiny appropriate, but allegations elsewhere do not prove misconduct in this deployment.

The political significance lies in the structure: public coercive power is being exercised through a company whose obligations are contractual and whose operations are less transparent than those of a public police force. Whether this particular deployment reflects election manipulation, union avoidance, bureaucratic incompetence, or some combination remains unproven.

Fear can misdirect accountability

By centering an unsubstantiated ballot-removal scenario, the coverage risks making the weakest claim carry the strongest outrage. That gives USPS an easy defense: deny an election connection and leave the underlying governance questions unanswered.

The necessary questions are more concrete. Who approved the contract? What powers do the guards possess? Who investigates force complaints? What are the deployment criteria? Why were unionized postal officers constrained while private guards expanded? How much is USPS paying amid reported multibillion-dollar losses?

Those questions attach responsibility to officials with decision-making power. Speculation about hidden “hijinks” attaches fear to guards and imagined future events while the agency’s documented choices recede from view.

The system hides in the contract

This story reveals a familiar pattern: government transfers a public function to a private actor, withholds operational details in the name of security, and then treats the resulting accountability gap as ordinary administration. Political distrust rushes into the vacuum because the institution refuses to make its chain of authority legible.

RawStory has grounds to scrutinize the deployment, especially amid Trump’s campaign against mail voting. It does not have evidence of an election operation. The sharper indictment is already visible: USPS placed hundreds of privately managed armed guards inside public facilities while workers and their representatives questioned supervision, safety, cost, and displacement of the agency’s own police. No conspiracy is required for that arrangement to deserve sustained scrutiny.
