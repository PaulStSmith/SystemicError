RFK Jr.'s strange claim befuddles the internet: 'Diagnosing children with fake diseases'

U.S. Health and Human Services (HHS) Secretary Robert F. Kennedy, Jr., speaks during a press conference at the Department of Health and Human Services in Washington, D.C., July 29, 2025. REUTERS/Jonathan Ernst/File Photo

The U.S. Secretary of Health and Human Services made a strange claim at a bill signing in Texas, and subsequently caused the internet to erupt in a flurry.

Robert F. Kennedy Jr. was in attendance as Texas Gov. Greg Abbott and Texas Republicans signed several bills into law that are modeled after Kennedy's "Make America Healthy Again" agenda. The bills revise state nutrition and fitness standards, increase food quality requirements, and prohibit people who receive food stamp benefits from using the funds to buy sweets and sodas.

During the ceremony, Kennedy said he could see the impact of America's health issues on young children.

"I'm looking at kids as I walk through the airport, or as I walk down the street," Kennedy said. "They are overburdened with mitochondrial challenges. You can tell from their faces and by their body movements, and their lack of social connection."

Several analysts replied to Kennedy's comments on social media.

"I, too, am overburdened with mitochondrial challenges," Irish sociologist Kieran Healy wrote on Bluesky.

"I can't imagine why kids are repelled by the leathery guy who looks them up and down in airports for 'mitochondrial challenges,'" journalist Mike Rothschild wrote on X.

"He’s diagnosing children from 30 yards away with fake diseases," military veteran John Jackson wrote on X.

"Horrors abound but a guy who will say 'overburdened with mitochondrial challenges' and somehow be in charge of the nation’s public health infrastructure is TOUGH TO BEAT," journalist Dave Levitan quipped on Bluesky.

"Serious question: What do the cuckoos mean by 'mitochondrial challenges?'" Dr. Melissa Johnson posted on Bluesky. "I'm deeply fascinated by whatever crunchy-granola clean-eating overnight-oats conspiracy theory is operating here."

"'Mitochondrial challenges usually means you're dead," Dr. Tessa Fisher wrote on Bluesky. "Best case, you have *severe* exercise intolerance."

Our Analysis:

The Bizarre Proclamations of Robert F. Kennedy Jr.: A Critical Analysis

In an era where political discourse frequently teeters on the brink of the absurd, the recent comments by Robert F. Kennedy Jr. at a bill signing in Texas have not merely approached this boundary but have vaulted over it with the agility of an Olympic pole vaulter. The occasion, graced by Kennedy's peculiar diagnostic acumen, aimed to herald the advent of several health-related legislative measures under the banner of his "Make America Healthy Again" agenda. Yet, the spectacle that unfolded has left many bewildered, prompting a tempest of reactions that oscillate between incredulity and outright derision.

The Claims

Kennedy's assertion—that he can discern children "overburdened with mitochondrial challenges" simply through casual observation at airports or on streets—is as startling as it is scientifically dubious. The human eye, even when attached to a brain steeped in health policy and advocacy, is not equipped with the molecular insight required to diagnose complex metabolic conditions on sight. This claim, seemingly plucked from the ether, not only undermines the credibility of Kennedy's broader health agenda but also trivializes the real struggles of those with mitochondrial diseases—a group of disorders that are, contrary to Kennedy's airport assessments, diagnosed through rigorous medical testing.

The Reaction

The internet's response, a blend of sarcasm and disbelief, illuminates the broader implications of Kennedy's remarks. Figures like Irish sociologist Kieran Healy and journalist Mike Rothschild have adeptly highlighted the absurdity of diagnosing "mitochondrial challenges" based on appearance alone, with Rothschild's commentary on the "leathery guy" casting judgment in airports striking a particular chord of dark humor. Moreover, the jests of military veteran John Jackson and journalist Dave Levitan underscore a growing concern over the suitability of Kennedy's leadership within the nation's public health sphere.

The Medical Community Weighs In

Dr. Melissa Johnson's inquiry into the "cuckoos" and their jargon, alongside Dr. Tessa Fisher's blunt clarification of the term's implications, reflect a medical community flabbergasted by Kennedy's foray into the realm of pseudo-diagnosis. The jest is clear: Kennedy's comments are not only unfounded but veer dangerously close to the propagation of misinformation, particularly in an age where the delineation between verified medical advice and "crunchy-granola clean-eating overnight-oats conspiracy theory" is increasingly blurred.

Concluding Thoughts

In the tapestry of political and health discourse, Kennedy's comments stand out as a vibrant thread of the absurd. They exemplify a troubling trend where personal convictions, untethered from scientific rigor, infiltrate public policy discussions. As observers and participants within this discourse, it is incumbent upon us to approach such claims with a critical eye, demanding evidence and eschewing the allure of baseless assertions.

The incident serves as a stark reminder of the vital need for scientific literacy in public health policy and the perils of conflating personal anecdote with universal truth. As the dust settles on this peculiar chapter, one can only hope that future discussions will pivot back towards a foundation of fact, rather than the whims of observational diagnoses made from afar.
