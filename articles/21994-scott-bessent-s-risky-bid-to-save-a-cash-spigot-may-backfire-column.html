Scott Bessent's risky bid to save a 'cash spigot' may backfire: column

Based on an article by Robert Davis

Bessent’s Yen Gambit Is a Subsidy for the Powerful

A Short Summary

The Guardian’s editorial board says Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent may be preparing a large yen-buying move that could stabilize markets in the short term while creating a much bigger crisis if it unravels. The column ties the risk to Donald Trump’s war in Iran, which is pushing inflation up and could drive Japan into tighter rates, making global borrowing more expensive and turning a currency correction into a Wall Street rout.

Who Actually Holds Power

This is not a story about “markets” in the abstract. The relevant power sits with Bessent in the Treasury, with Trump in the White House, and with the financial actors who depend on the yen as a cheap funding machine. The column’s own language makes the hierarchy clear: Bessent is not rescuing anything. He is trying to preserve a cash spigot that benefits the United States, and by that it means the parts of the U.S. financial system that profit from cheap leverage and buoyant asset prices.

That matters because the harms are not accidental. They follow from choices made by people with institutional authority. Trump’s war in Iran is pushing inflation. Japan may answer with aggressive rate hikes. Bessent is then left trying to paper over the consequences with another intervention. Policy creates the fire; Treasury reaches for a mop and calls it strategy.

The Real Game

The yen is described here as a global funding utility: borrow cheap yen, sell them for dollars, buy higher-return U.S. assets, especially tech shares, and let rising stock prices support more borrowing. That is not a neutral mechanism. It is a financial arrangement that turns Japanese monetary conditions into fuel for Wall Street.

So when Bessent moves to buy yen, the question is not whether he is “helping the market.” The question is which side of the leverage chain he is protecting. The answer is obvious. He is protecting the carry trade and the U.S. asset inflation it props up. That is a political decision dressed up as technical management.

Misdirection as Strategy

The Guardian is right to flag the danger, but its framing still leans too hard on market logic and too lightly on responsibility. This is not a mysterious unwind caused by impersonal forces. It is the predictable result of a government willing to intensify global instability and then improvise financial fixes for the people already positioned to profit from it.

Bessent’s history in the yen trade only sharpens the picture. The column notes that he once helped George Soros make more than $1 billion by trading against the yen. Now he is on the other side, trying to “rewrite the rules of the game.” That is not principled stewardship. It is the same speculative mindset moving from attack to defense, with public authority attached.

The messaging here also matters. “Rescue of the yen” sounds benign. It is not. If the real purpose is to preserve a U.S.-centered cash spigot, then the language is camouflage. The costs, if the scheme fails, are socialized across the global economy. The gains remain concentrated among the institutions close enough to profit before the unwind.

The Pattern Beneath It

This is the familiar structure of modern financial power: private actors capture upside in calm times, state power steps in when the system strains, and ordinary people inherit the losses when the intervention cannot outpace the damage. War, inflation, currency manipulation, and asset bubbles are not separate stories. They are parts of the same governing style.

Trump’s escalation creates the macroeconomic pressure. Bessent’s response tries to shield the financial system from the consequences without changing the policy that caused them. That is not stability. It is crisis management for the people least exposed to the crisis.

The System

The deeper lesson is that American power now treats global finance as a tool for domestic asset support, even when the underlying political decisions are destructive. Treasury does not correct the system; it defends the people who benefit from it. And when the structure wobbles, the state reaches for more intervention, not less, while pretending the problem is market complexity rather than the choices of those in charge.
