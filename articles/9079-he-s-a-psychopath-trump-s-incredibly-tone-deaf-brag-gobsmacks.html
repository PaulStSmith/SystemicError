'He's a psychopath': Trump's incredibly tone deaf brag gobsmacks

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 2, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Marble Facade of Indifference: Analyzing Trump’s Bathroom Renovation Amidst National Crisis

Former President Donald J. Trump kisses American flag at arriving on stage during CPAC Conference 2024 (Photo lev radin/Shutterstock)

As millions of families across the U.S. face loss of food aid over the weekend, President Donald Trump on Friday decided to show off photos of a White House bathroom that he boasted had been refurbished in “highly polished, statuary marble.”

Trump posted photos of the bathroom on his Truth Social platform, and explained that he decided to remodel it because he was dissatisfied with the “art deco green tile style” that had been implemented during a previous renovation — which he described as “totally inappropriate for the Lincoln Era.”

“I did it in black and white polished Statuary marble,” Trump continued. “This was very appropriate for the time of Abraham Lincoln and, in fact, could be the marble that was originally there!”

Trump’s critics were quick to pan the remodeled bathroom, especially since it came at a time when Americans are suffering from numerous policies the president and the Republican Party are enacting, including tariffs that are raising the cost of food and clothing; expiring subsidies for Americans who buy health insurance through Affordable Care Act exchanges; and cuts to Medicaid and Supplemental Nutrition Assistance (SNAP) programs in the One Big Beautiful Bill Act.

“Sure, you might not be able to eat or go to the doctor, but check out how nice Trump’s new marble s----er is,” remarked independent journalist Aaron Rupar on Bluesky.

Joe Walsh, a former Republican congressman who has become a critic of Trump, ripped the president for displaying such tone deafness in the middle of a federal government shutdown.

“Government still shutdown, Americans not getting paid, food assistance for low-income families and children about to be cut off, and this is what he cares about,” he wrote on X. “He’s a psychopath, humanly incapable of caring about anyone or anything but himself.”

Don Moynihan, a political scientist at the University of Michigan, expressed extreme skepticism that the White House bathroom during Abraham Lincoln’s tenure was decked out in marble and gold.

“Fact check based on no research but with a high degree of confidence: This is not the marble that was originally in the Lincoln Bedroom,” he wrote. “It is more likely to be retrieved from a Trump casino before it was demolished.”

Fashion critic Derek Guy, meanwhile, mostly left politics out of his criticisms of the remodeled bathroom, instead simply observing that “White House renovations are currently being spearheaded by someone with famously bad interior design taste.”

Earlier this month, Trump sparked outrage when he demolished the entire East Wing of the White House to make way for a massive White House ballroom financed by donations from some of America’s wealthiest corporations—including several with government contracts and interests in deregulation—such as Apple, Lockheed Martin, Microsoft, Meta, Google, Amazon, and Palantir.

Our Analysis:

The Marble Facade of Indifference: Analyzing Trump’s Bathroom Renovation Amidst National Crisis

In what can only be described as a staggering display of opulence and tone-deafness, President Donald Trump recently unveiled renovations to a White House bathroom, boasting of its “highly polished, statuary marble.” This revelation comes at a time when millions of American families face the grim prospect of losing food aid, a juxtaposition that lays bare the administration’s stark priorities.

Trump’s ostentatious reveal on his Truth Social platform—praising the switch from an “art deco green tile style” to a black and white polished marble, allegedly in homage to the Lincoln era—reeks of historical inaccuracies and ego-driven design choices. Trump’s claim that this aesthetic is “very appropriate for the time of Abraham Lincoln” not only stretches credulity but also underscores a profound misallocation of focus and resources.

Critics were swift and unsparing in their condemnation. Independent journalist Aaron Rupar’s scathing remark encapsulates the absurdity: “Sure, you might not be able to eat or go to the doctor, but check out how nice Trump’s new marble s----er is.” This sardonic observation highlights the grotesque contrast between the administration’s lavish expenditures on superficial projects and its aggressive dismantling of social safety nets, including food assistance and healthcare subsidies.

Former Republican congressman Joe Walsh called out the president’s “psychopathic” inability to empathize with struggling Americans, especially during a federal government shutdown that has left many without pay and on the brink of losing essential services. The administration’s actions, or rather inactions, speak volumes about its priorities, with the most vulnerable populations bearing the brunt of its callous policies.

Political scientist Don Moynihan expressed skepticism regarding the historical accuracy of Trump’s claims, suggesting the marble might be more suited to a Trump casino than the Lincoln era. This quip underscores the blending of Trump’s gaudy personal brand with the solemnity of the presidency, a merging that has frequently blurred the lines of propriety and respect for national symbols.

Fashion critic Derek Guy’s critique of the renovation’s taste—or lack thereof—while less politically charged, still points to a broader issue of the administration’s vanity projects overshadowing more pressing national concerns.

The demolition of the East Wing for a “massive White House ballroom,” financed by corporate donations from companies with clear vested interests in government decisions, is emblematic of the administration’s transactional view of power and influence. It’s a stark reminder of the quid pro quo nature of Trump’s relationships with big business, with policy implications that could further entrench the interests of the wealthy elite at the expense of the general populace.

In sum, the bathroom renovation, while seemingly trivial, is emblematic of a presidency marred by self-indulgence, historical revisionism, and a disturbing disconnection from the lives of ordinary Americans. As the nation grapples with pressing issues of economic inequality, healthcare access, and social welfare, the focus on marble and gold serves as a stark reminder of an administration more concerned with appearance than with the substance of governance.
