'Very dishonest': White House under fire over shifting story on lethal strikes

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 2, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

White House Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt holds a press briefing at the White House, in Washington, D.C. on Dec. 1, 2025. REUTERS/Evelyn Hockstein

The internet had a serious response to the new White House announcement on Monday, arguing that President Donald Trump and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth relied on orders from a senior military leader to authorize the two strikes on an alleged drug boat after reports that survivors were targeted after the first strike.

White House Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt said that Admiral Frank M. “Mitch” Bradley ordered the second strike on an alleged drug boat in the Caribbean, just off the coast of Venezuela, and that it was “well within his authority to do so.”

Social media users responded to the news and questioned the Trump administration over the new information.

“A straightforward progression from claiming ‘this entire narrative’ is ‘fabricated’ with ‘NO FACTS’ to admitting most of the report is accurate, shielding the top official involved, and saying that the strike was good, actually. Very dishonest people,” Matthew Gertz, senior fellow at Media Matters for America, wrote on X.

“So the thing the White House and the Secretary of Defense both attacked as ‘fake news’ did in fact happen. They were lying, and nobody should be buying this new version of the story either without asking a bunch more questions,” Aaron Fritschner, deputy chief of staff for Rep. Don Beyer (D-VA), wrote on X.

“Yesterday, Donald Trump said he would not have wanted a second strike. Today, the White House admitted the second strike did happen, but blamed Admiral Bradley. Either Trump has no control over his military, or Leavitt is trying to insulate Trump and Hegseth,” journalist Aaron Parnas wrote on X.

“Either Admiral Bradley went rogue, or was following @SecWar’s directiveDoesn’t matter who ordered it. It was unlawful. Operating lawfully is what gives an armed force their credibility. It’s what used to separate militaries in democracies from dictators. Not so much in America,” a user named Michael, who identified as a Royal Canadian Navy veteran, wrote on X.

“Oh so there was a second strike? lol weren’t they saying there wasn’t one?” Commentator Keith Edwards wrote on X.

“So either the Commander in Chief and Secretary of Defense did not know about the second strike that Republicans have even called a war crime, or the White House is just placing all the blame on Admiral Bradley to deflect,” podcast host Jessica Tarlov wrote on X.

“If he was ‘well within his authority,’ then release the unredacted video of the strike, the timeline of events, & the radio recordings of every order. Because based on the reporting we’ve seen, it appears @SecWar may have committed a war crime. The public deserves transparency,” Rep. Yassamin Ansari (D-AZ) wrote on X.

Our Analysis:

The White House’s Shifting Narrative: A Study in Evasion and Blame-Shifting

In a stunning revelation that should surprise precisely no one, the Trump administration has once again been caught in a web of contradictions and half-truths. This time, it revolves around the authorization of two strikes on what has been described as an “alleged drug boat” near Venezuela, a situation that has rapidly evolved from denial to grudging admission, accompanied by a desperate attempt to shield the highest echelons of power from the fallout.

First off, let’s dissect the audacity of the White House’s narrative flip-flop. Initially, the administration, in lockstep with Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, dismissed reports of the strikes as “fabricated” and devoid of “NO FACTS.” Yet, in a dizzying pivot, Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt confirmed the second strike, casually passing the buck to Admiral Frank M. “Mitch” Bradley. This is not just a mere admission but a glaring testament to the administration’s propensity for dishonesty. As Matthew Gertz of Media Matters for America succinctly puts it, this progression from outright denial to reluctant acknowledgment is emblematic of “very dishonest people.”

The inconsistency doesn’t just end there. Trump’s own comments have veered from disavowing the desire for a second strike to the White House’s subsequent admission that it did indeed occur, with the blame conveniently laid at Admiral Bradley’s feet. This raises a critical question: is the administration so chaotically disorganized that the left hand doesn’t know what the right hand is doing, or is this a calculated attempt by Leavitt to insulate Trump and Hegseth from accountability? Journalist Aaron Parnas highlights this pivotal dilemma, pointing to either a lack of control over the military or a deliberate strategy of deflection.

Furthermore, the legality of the strikes has been called into question, with individuals like Michael, a Royal Canadian Navy veteran, condemning the operation as “unlawful.” The distinction he draws between the credibility of armed forces in democracies versus those in dictatorships is particularly damning, suggesting that the United States, under this administration, is blurring these lines with reckless abandon.

The call for transparency by Rep. Yassamin Ansari is where the crux of this issue lies. If, as Leavitt claims, Admiral Bradley was “well within his authority,” then the administration should have no qualms about releasing unredacted footage of the strike, the timeline of events, and the radio recordings of every order given. The refusal to do so, coupled with the piecemeal and reluctant admissions, smells not just of a cover-up but of potential war crimes. The public’s demand for transparency is not just warranted—it’s essential.

In essence, the Trump administration’s handling of this situation is a microcosm of its broader approach to governance: evasion, inconsistency, and a blatant disregard for the truth. The shifting narratives and attempts to deflect blame onto Admiral Bradley do not just undermine the credibility of the White House; they raise serious questions about the legality and morality of its decisions.

In a functioning democracy, accountability is not an optional accessory but the very bedrock of governance. The administration’s response to this debacle is a stark reminder of how far we have strayed from this principle. It’s high time for a rigorous, transparent investigation into not just the strikes themselves, but the systemic failures that allowed them to happen.
