History repeating: What Nixon's secret tapes reveal about Trump

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 27, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet asked what Richard Nixon’s secret recordings reveal about concerns over Donald Trump’s fitness for office. Historian Luke Nichter’s answer was limited: Nixon’s tapes do not establish the alcoholism, violent rages, or alleged abuse described in the interview, and they provide no direct evidence about Trump’s health.

Nichter said the roughly 3,400 hours of recordings capture much of Nixon’s working life from 1971 until the taping system ended in July 1973. He acknowledged gaps—including Nixon’s final 13 or 14 months in office—but said the available audio contains little evidence that Nixon was routinely out of control.

The tapes do document something more concrete. According to Nichter, White House press secretary Ron Ziegler and doctors discussed how to describe Nixon’s temperature during his 1973 hospitalization for viral pneumonia: serious enough to justify hospitalization, but not so severe that the public might fear his death. Nichter suggested that administrations routinely manage presidential health disclosures, while stressing that he does not know the truth about Trump’s condition.

The headline outruns the evidence

The reporting begins with speculation about whether two presidents were “crazy,” then produces an expert who rejects the evidentiary premise about Nixon and declines to assess Trump. The secret tapes reveal no diagnosis of Trump, no verified parallel between the two men, and no reliable basis for remote medical judgment.

That does not make the interview useless. It makes the stated comparison the wrong story. The strongest evidence concerns information control inside the presidency—not mental illness. By organizing the piece around personal instability, AlterNet risks turning a documented exercise of institutional message management into an argument about individual psychology that its own interview cannot substantiate.

Power sits inside the disclosure machine

A president’s health is not merely private when it affects the exercise of executive authority. The presidency commands the armed forces, directs the executive branch, and can move markets through a single disclosure. That gives White House officials and presidential physicians extraordinary power over what the public learns and when.

Nichter’s account shows that Ziegler and the doctors were not passive observers struggling to describe Nixon’s pneumonia. They reportedly negotiated the presentation of his condition. The institutional advantage belonged to the people controlling the records, the medical access, and the briefing room—not to citizens or reporters trying to evaluate the president’s capacity from outside.

Speculation protects the gatekeepers

Debating whether a president is “crazy” invites sensational claims that are difficult to prove and easy for officials to dismiss. It also misdirects attention from conduct that can be documented: who released information, who withheld it, who shaped the official account, and whether those choices concealed facts relevant to presidential duties.

The interview’s unverified allegations about Nixon illustrate the problem. Nichter did not prove that misconduct never occurred; he said the extensive recordings he studied do not support the characterization presented to him, while acknowledging periods and places the tapes did not capture. The responsible conclusion is evidentiary restraint—not vindication, diagnosis, or analogy by insinuation.

The presidency grades its own condition

Nichter’s broader claim is that administrations build and defend narratives about presidential health. The Nixon example gives that claim a concrete mechanism: officials with privileged information calibrating a public statement to manage political and economic reactions.

That is the larger pattern. The executive branch possesses the facts, selects the messengers, defines the acceptable disclosure, and invokes privacy around the person whose condition may affect public power. The public then receives a curated account and is pushed toward guessing about symptoms instead of examining the officials who designed the information gap.

The lesson from Nixon’s tapes is therefore narrower—and more consequential—than the headline promises. They do not decode Trump. They show how presidential institutions can convert a matter of public capacity into a controlled communications problem, leaving outsiders to argue over rumors while insiders decide how much reality the country is permitted to see.
