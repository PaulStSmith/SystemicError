'Visceral hatred': CNN panel explains why Trump really backed off from Canada

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 28, 20254 min read

When Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney ran against Conservative Party leader Pierre Poilievre in April, the race became, in part, a referendum on U.S. President Donald Trump. And the Liberal Party's Carney, by positioning himself as the more anti-Trump of the two, enjoyed a decisive victory.

Carney subsequently met with Trump during a May 7 visit to the White House, politely emphasizing that while he values Canada's relationship with the United States, Canada will maintain its sovereignty and never become "the 51st state."

U.S./Canadian relations was the focus of a CNN panel hosted by Dana Bash early Tuesday afternoon, April 27.

Bash pointed out that Canadian anger toward Trump is quite real and that many Canadians deeply resent the trade war that Trump set off.

Bash told the panel, "The Canadians are pissed. And let me show you some data on that. Canadians boycotting U.S. goods 76 percent. So, three quarters of Canadians say that they're not buying U.S. goods, American goods. Now, here's another stat: Canadian travel from the U.S. — which is kind of a big deal for a lot of American hospitality, U.S. hospitality — by plane, down 20 percent. By car, down 35 percent. And these are actual statistics taken by Canada as they clock who comes back into their country."

But one of the panelists, CNN's Phil Mattingly, stressed that Carney has a better relationship with the Trump White House than Canada's former prime minister, Justin Trudeau, even though Carney and Trudeau are both members of Canada's Liberal Party.

Mattingly explained, "The president's relationship with Mark Carney is very, very different than it was with Justin Trudeau. I cannot overstate how personally people inside the White House, the president on down, viscerally loathed, on a personal level, Justin Trudeau. For whatever the reasons — we're going back to the first term — Carney is different. Even they're the same party. they are trying to establish a relationship. Both sides, I think, are actually making a good-faith effort. It will be interesting to see how that plays out."

Our Analysis:

The Political Chess Game: Carney vs. Poilievre and the Shadow of Trump

In the recent Canadian political saga, where Mark Carney emerged victorious over Conservative Party leader Pierre Poilievre, the undercurrents of U.S. influence, particularly that of former President Donald Trump, played a more significant role than many might admit. Carney, leveraging his anti-Trump stance, not only managed to clinch the title but also set a new precedent for Canadian-American relations. However, the implications of this dynamic extend far beyond a mere election victory.

Carney's Strategic Victory: It’s fascinating, albeit somewhat alarming, how the specter of Donald Trump loomed large over a Canadian election. Carney’s decisive win underscores a broader trend of global politics being increasingly influenced by figures who are not even on the ballot. By positioning himself as the antithesis to Trump, Carney tapped into a wellspring of resentment and concern among Canadians regarding Trump's policies and persona. This strategy, while effective, raises questions about the independence of Canadian politics. Are Canadians voting for their leaders, or against the influence of foreign ones?

The Aftermath and U.S.-Canada Relations: Carney's subsequent meeting with Trump, where he assured that Canada would not become "the 51st state," was a diplomatic tightrope walk. This statement, though seemingly obvious, was a necessary affirmation of sovereignty in light of the previous administration's overbearing tendencies. The CNN panel discussion hosted by Dana Bash further illuminated the tangible impact of Trump’s policies on Canadian sentiment and behavior, with significant shifts in consumption and travel habits. These figures aren’t just numbers; they’re a clear indication of a growing rift that could have long-term implications for cross-border relations.

The Shadow of Trump's Influence: The article touches on the distinct difference in the Trump administration's relationship with Carney as opposed to Justin Trudeau. While it's heartening to hear that efforts are being made to bridge the gap, one cannot help but ponder the personal dynamics at play. The visceral dislike for Trudeau as contrasted with the warmer reception of Carney suggests that personal affinities, rather than policy alignments, might be dictating international relationships. This is a precarious foundation for diplomacy, susceptible to whims rather than anchored in mutual respect and principles.

The Bigger Picture: Beyond the immediate political maneuvers and their outcomes, this article reflects a broader issue of the increasing polarization and personalization of politics. When elections become referendums on foreign leaders, and diplomatic relations swing on the pendulum of personal like or dislike, we find ourselves in a volatile and unpredictable global landscape. The Canadian election may be over, but the underlying currents it reveals are ongoing concerns that demand closer scrutiny.

In conclusion, while Carney's victory might be celebrated as a win against Trumpian politics, it also serves as a reminder of the intricate and often fragile web of international relations. The real challenge for Carney and future leaders lies not just in distancing themselves from contentious figures but in forging a path that prioritizes sovereignty, diplomacy, and principled leadership over reactionary politics. The world is watching, and the stakes are high.
