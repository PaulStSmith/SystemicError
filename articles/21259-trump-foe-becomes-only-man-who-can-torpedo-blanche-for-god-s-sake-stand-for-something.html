Trump foe becomes only man who can torpedo Blanche: 'For God's sake, stand for something'

Based on an article by Jake Johnson, Common Dreams

Paulo SantosAugust 15, 20263 min read

Trump Is Installing a Lawyer, Not an Attorney General

The Real Story

Republicans are treating Todd Blanche’s confirmation as a procedural drama about one wavering senator. That framing is convenient and wrong. The real fact is simpler: Donald Trump is trying to lock the Justice Department deeper into his personal control, and Blanche is not being selected to constrain that power but to serve it.

The source gives the game away. Blanche is Trump’s former personal lawyer, already leading the Justice Department in an acting role, while Senate Republicans scramble to supply the votes that would make that arrangement permanent.

Cassidy Is a Lever, Not the Plot

Bill Cassidy is the only interesting vote here because the Republican margin is so thin that one senator can momentarily interrupt the machine. But even that is overstated. Cassidy is not the center of the story; he is a stress point in a party that has already decided what it wants.

He voted to convict Trump after January 6, then later helped confirm Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and now faces pressure from the same political ecosystem that punishes independence and rewards obedience. That is not a profile in courage. It is the familiar Republican ritual of pretending to have a conscience after the damage has already been signed into law.

A Justice Department for Loyalists

Murkowski’s opposition is the clearest evidence of what is actually happening. Her stated objections are not abstract process complaints. They point to a department that has already been used to protect the president, his family, and their businesses; to make threats toward anti-abortion groups; and to target enemies from former staffers to sitting senators.

That is not a normal Justice Department with a few rough edges. That is a loyalty apparatus. Blanche’s confirmation would not create that problem, but it would ratify it.

The Senate’s Tiny Window

Murkowski also names the real legislative leverage: the pending nomination is the only thing standing in the way of a nearly $2 billion slush fund that she says could have rewarded January 6 protesters. That is the kind of sentence a functioning democracy should not have to read in plain English.

The point is not that the Senate is suddenly principled. The point is that senators are only useful to democracy when the confirmation process gives them a temporary choke point. Once that window closes, the same institutional actors who wring their hands about norms usually return to enabling the next outrage.

Misdirection as Strategy

The weak framing here is the attempt to turn this into a story about whether Cassidy “stands for something.” That is personality gossip dressed up as political analysis. The stronger question is who benefits from making the decision look personal instead of structural.

Trump benefits. Blanche benefits. The Republican Party benefits. The public gets a soap opera about one senator’s conscience while the administration tries to cement control over the Justice Department, preserve immunity, and keep the machinery of punishment pointed in the right direction.

The Pattern

This is what authoritarian drift looks like in American institutional language: not a dramatic seizure, but a chain of appointments, excuses, and procedural dependencies that normalize the conversion of public office into private protection. Republicans do not have to announce that they are building a captured state. They just have to keep voting for it while asking the country to confuse hesitation with resistance.

Blanche is not being confirmed because he is qualified to check Trump’s worst impulses. He is being advanced because he is close enough to them to be trusted.
