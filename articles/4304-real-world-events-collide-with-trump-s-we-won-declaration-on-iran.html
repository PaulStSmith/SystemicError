Real-world events collide with Trump's 'we won' declaration on Iran

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 13, 20265 min read

Donald Trump gestures during a visit to Hebron, Kentucky. REUTERS/Kevin Lamarque

The images of two oil tankers set ablaze Thursday in Iraqi waters offered a jarring split-screen to President Donald Trump’s declaration that “we won” the war against Iran.

The 79-year-old president held a campaign-style rally in Hebron, Kentucky, on Wednesday, where he insisted that joint U.S.-Israeli military operations had “virtually ⁠destroyed Iran,” but that statement was quickly punctured by stepped-up attacks in the Persian Gulf, reported Reuters.

“You never like to say too early you won. We won,” Trump told supporters at Verst Logistics. “In the first hour it was over.”

“We don’t want to leave early do we?” he added. “We got to finish the job.”

However, three sources familiar with the matter told Reuters that U.S. intelligence found evidence that Iran’s leadership was still mostly intact and the government was not at imminent risk of collapse, and Iran had escalated its attacks on cargo ships in the Straits of Hormuz.

“Undermining U.S. and Israeli claims to have knocked out ⁠much of Iran’s stock of long-range weapons, more drones were reported on Thursday flying into Kuwait, Iraq, the UAE, Bahrain and Oman,” Reuters said. “Iran has said it would not allow oil through the world’s most important energy trade route – the Strait of Hormuz that runs along its coast – until U.S. and Israeli attacks ceased, and it would not conduct any negotiations with Washington.”

Citibank closed its office in Dubai and Goldman Sachs Group Inc. told staffers there to stay away from their offices after Iran said it considered banks to be legitimate targets and warned Middle East residents not to go near them, and HSBC has closed branches in Qatar.

At least one person was killed after two oil tankers exploded in what is believed to be an Iranian attack, and an Iraqi-led investigation found evidence that explosive-laden boats linked to Iran rammed into the vessels transporting up to 400,000 barrels of Iraqi oil, reported International Business Times.

“ Video footage of the fires shows flames spreading across the surrounding water, suggesting that oil is leaking from the large tankers,” IB Times reported. “The targeted ships were identified as the Maltese-flagged Zefyros and the Marshall Islands-flagged Safesea Vishnu.”

Oil prices have surged above $100 despite the announcement that developed countries would release 400 million barrels of oil from their strategic reserves, with nearly half coming from the United States, but that unprecedented coordinated intervention would take months to carry out and provide just three weeks supply.

“The only way to see oil prices trade lower on a sustained basis is by getting oil flowing through the Strait of Hormuz,” ING analysts said. “Failing to do so means that the market highs are still ahead of ⁠us.”

Our Analysis:

Analysis of the Article’s Framing and Accountability

The article under scrutiny presents a complex scenario involving geopolitical conflict, economic repercussions, and environmental disasters. It juxtaposes President Donald Trump’s claims of victory over Iran with the reality of ongoing aggression and the tangible impacts of such conflicts on global commerce and security. To dissect the narrative effectively, we must scrutinize the institutional power dynamics, decision-making processes, and the framing of responsibility.

Institutional Power and Decision-Making

Who Holds Power?

  1. United States and Israel: These nations have formidable military and economic influence. President Donald Trump, representing the U.S., claims significant victory and influence over Iran’s operational capabilities.

  2. Iran: Despite claims of its weakened state, Iran demonstrates continued influence over regional stability and the global economy, particularly through control over the Strait of Hormuz.

  3. International Corporations: Entities like Citibank, Goldman Sachs Group Inc., and HSBC represent the economic dimension, with their operations directly affected by geopolitical tensions.

Who Made the Decisions?

  • President Trump’s declaration of victory reflects a high-level strategic narrative likely aimed at domestic and international political gains rather than an accurate assessment of military outcomes.

  • Iran’s decision to target oil tankers and potentially escalate military actions in response to U.S.-Israeli aggression points to a calculated attempt to leverage its geographical and strategic advantages.

  • Corporate decisions to close offices and branches in the region are reactionary measures rooted in concerns for safety and operational integrity.

Misdirection of Responsibility

The article frames President Trump’s assertions against the backdrop of ongoing and escalating violence, implying a direct linkage between his rhetoric and the complex series of events in the Persian Gulf. While presidential declarations have significant influence, they are not the sole drivers of regional dynamics. The framing risks oversimplifying the situation by attributing broad geopolitical movements and consequences to individual statements or actions.

Key Considerations:

  • Complexity of Geopolitical Conflicts: The situation involves multiple actors with varying degrees of power and objectives. While the U.S. and Israel hold significant military power, Iran’s control over the Strait of Hormuz and ability to conduct asymmetrical warfare presents a nuanced challenge that is not easily subdued by conventional military might.

  • Economic and Environmental Impacts: The targeting of oil tankers not only has immediate security implications but also affects global oil prices and environmental safety. These outcomes result from strategic decisions by Iran, influenced but not directly controlled by U.S. actions.

Conclusion

The article’s focus on President Trump’s declarations in the context of ongoing violence and instability in the Persian Gulf risks attributing too much causality to his rhetoric, potentially overlooking the broader context of institutional powers at play and the strategic decisions made by multiple actors. While it is critical to analyze and hold leaders accountable for their statements and their impacts on international relations, a nuanced understanding of all involved parties’ actions and motivations is essential.

Misplaced Criticism: Criticizing Trump for his optimistic declaration without acknowledging the limitations of his influence over Iran’s actions or the strategic calculations behind Iran’s military engagements presents an incomplete picture.

Lazy Narrative: By not fully exploring the strategic depth and the multiplicity of actors involved, the article leans towards a simpler, but less accurate narrative that centers primarily on U.S. presidential rhetoric rather than the complex interplay of international relations, military strategy, and economic considerations shaping the current crisis.

In summary, while President Trump’s declarations and their potential disconnection from reality merit scrutiny, a comprehensive analysis must consider the broader geopolitical chessboard, including the institutional powers and decision-making processes of all key actors.
