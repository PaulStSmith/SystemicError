Ken Paxton is losing votes in Texas — but not to James Talarico

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 2, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Ken Paxton may lose some disaffected Republican votes not to Democratic nominee James Talarico, but to Libertarian Ted Brown. AlterNet, citing The Bulwark’s Lauren Egan and a Texas Democratic strategist close to Talarico’s campaign, reports that Brown could receive 3 or 4 percent in the Texas Senate race as some John Cornyn voters recoil from both Paxton and, potentially, Talarico. That is campaign speculation, not a measured result.

The strategist contrasted that possibility with Texas’s 2018 Senate contest, when the Libertarian candidate received 0.8 percent and Beto O’Rourke lost to Ted Cruz by 2.6 percentage points. The comparison shows why Republicans are concerned, but it does not establish that Brown will draw those votes or determine the outcome.

AlterNet also reports that Paxton angered Republicans after saying at a private fundraiser that Donald Trump appeared to be hurting candidates’ numbers. Trump had endorsed Paxton over incumbent Senator John Cornyn. Patrick McHenry, whom AlterNet identifies as a former Texas House speaker pro tempore, responded on CNN by arguing that Trump had effectively created Paxton’s political fortunes and condemning Paxton for criticizing him.

The power is inside the Republican machine

The headline’s arithmetic can obscure the actual power structure. Talarico and Brown may receive votes, but neither selected Paxton as the Republican nominee. Republican primary voters did that after Trump intervened against Cornyn, an incumbent senator. Trump exercised the decisive political leverage described in the story; Paxton benefited from it.

Paxton is also not merely a polarizing personality asking voters to overlook private defects. He is the sitting Texas attorney general, an office with substantial state power. AlterNet describes allegations and controversies involving bribery, misuse of office, political retaliation, preferential treatment for a wealthy associate, infidelity, and advocacy for a lighter sentence for a convicted child sex offender. The article does not independently adjudicate those claims, and allegations are not convictions. But treating the resulting discomfort as an electoral branding problem strips the office—and its power—from the story.

Scandal is not a weather event

“Republican voters won’t feel comfortable” is bloodless language for a party confronting the candidate it nominated. Voters have agency. So do Trump, the party figures who supported Paxton, and the institutions that kept him politically viable through repeated controversy.

The reported Republican fracture was not inflicted by Democrats or Libertarians. Brown may provide an exit for conservatives unwilling to vote for Paxton but equally unwilling to support a Democrat. If that happens, he will be the beneficiary of the fracture, not its author.

Casting those voters as wandering pieces on a campaign chessboard also minimizes their choice. A third-party vote can function as ideological loyalty without accountability: rejecting the Republican nominee while refusing to help the only rival portrayed here as capable of defeating him.

Loyalty outranks judgment

McHenry’s reported response is revealing because it does not defend Trump’s electoral value or Paxton’s fitness. It enforces hierarchy. His argument is that Paxton owes Trump gratitude because Trump supplied the endorsement and protection that made Paxton’s ascent possible.

That is patronage logic stated without embarrassment. The offense is not that Paxton’s assessment was false; the story supplies no evidence resolving that. The offense is that a beneficiary criticized his patron. Even Trump’s reported ridicule of Paxton’s appearance becomes, in McHenry’s telling, something Paxton should accept gratefully.

This is how personal rule displaces institutional judgment: advancement produces obligation, obligation demands public submission, and dissent becomes betrayal. Candidate quality matters less than whether the candidate recognizes who owns the favor.

The convenient misdirection

The horse-race frame shifts attention toward Brown’s possible 3 or 4 percent, as though the Libertarian were siphoning votes from an otherwise sound candidacy. That reverses cause and effect. The reported vulnerability begins with Paxton’s record, Trump’s intervention and the Republican electorate’s nomination decision.

The 2018 comparison makes the stakes legible, but it can also manufacture certainty. A projected Libertarian share in one contest cannot simply be subtracted from a previous Republican margin in another. The candidates, coalitions and political conditions differ. The reporting establishes a plausible Republican concern, not a forecast.

A system built to evade responsibility

The larger pattern is a party converting accountability into factional management. Alleged abuses become “scandals.” A patron’s intervention becomes ordinary endorsement politics. Voter revulsion becomes a third-party spoiler problem. The language keeps moving responsibility away from the officials and institutions that exercised power.

If Paxton loses support, Brown will not have stolen it and Talarico will not have caused the rupture. The loss will reflect choices already made inside the Republican coalition: to elevate Paxton, to subordinate nomination politics to Trump’s favor, and to treat loyalty as the qualification that matters most.
