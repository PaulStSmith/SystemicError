Medicaid Cuts Were Designed to Hurt After the Vote

Power, Not Confusion

The reporting here is straightforward about the facts that matter: Donald Trump and the Republican Congress used their institutional power to pass a July 2025 law that slashes Medicaid by roughly $1 trillion over 10 years, while delaying the worst of the damage until 2027, after the midterms. That delay is not a policy detail. It is the political mechanism.

This is not a confused system producing accidental harm. It is elected power arranging harm on purpose, then scheduling it to avoid immediate electoral consequences.

The Paperwork Purge

The bill’s work and paperwork requirements are not neutral administrative reforms. They are a filter built to kick people off coverage without admitting that is what is happening. The source says the cuts are designed to force recipients off the rolls, with 10 million to 15 million people likely pushed out of Medicaid, on top of millions more losing coverage or facing higher ACA premiums.

That is not cost control. That is coverage sabotage dressed up as governance.

The Real Damage Hits the System

The usual trick in these fights is to describe Medicaid as if it were a line item that only affects poor people. That framing is useful to the people doing the cutting because it hides the structure of the damage. Medicaid dollars go to providers, not just recipients. Strip out a trillion dollars and the harm spreads through hospitals, nursing homes, emergency rooms, staffing, and pay.

Ornstein’s account makes clear what that means in practice: overcrowded ERs, unemployed health workers, shuttered facilities, thinner staffing, worse care, and more abuse risks in nursing homes. The cruelty is not abstract. It gets built into the daily operations of institutions people rely on to stay alive.

Blame the Architects

The article’s deeper value is that it names the real decision-makers instead of pretending this emerged from some faceless budget process. Republicans enacted the cuts. Republicans delayed them. Republicans designed the paperwork trap. Republicans made sure the pain would land after voters had already been managed.

That should end the fiction that this is merely “policy disagreement.” It is a deliberate strategy of concealment: take away care, postpone the bill, and hope the public notices too late.

Kakistocracy With a Budget

Ornstein’s use of “kakistocracy” is apt, but the term matters less as a flourish than as a diagnosis. This is what government looks like when the least responsible actors are rewarded for cruelty they can defer. The country is governed by people who know exactly where the pain will go and have decided that the timing is the only thing worth managing.

The larger pattern is simple: right-wing power in this era does not merely neglect public systems. It weaponizes them, starves them, and then hides the consequences behind procedure, delay, and electoral math. That is not failure at the edges. It is the operating logic.
