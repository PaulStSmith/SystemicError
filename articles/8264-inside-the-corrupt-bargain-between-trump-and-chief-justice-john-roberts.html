Inside the 'corrupt bargain' between Trump and Chief Justice John Roberts

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 29, 20257 min read

Mother Jones writers Pema Levy and Ari Berman say that President Donald Trump “owes his corrupt and abusive reign to one man” — Supreme Court Chief Justice John Roberts.

“Twenty years ago, John Roberts promised that as chief justice of the Supreme Court, he would be like an umpire, calling balls and strikes,” they write.

“His promise charmed senators and the media, who believed that his predilection for executive power and long-held antipathy for civil rights could be moderated by this commitment to faithfully apply the law,” they add.

But Roberts wasn’t what he seemed, they say.

“The delusion was so powerful that for two decades, the media defaulted to portraying him as a moderate institutionalist, pointing to high-profile decisions — to uphold parts of the Affordable Care Act or striking down President Donald Trump’s attempt to ask about citizenship in the 2020 census — in which he broke from conservative orthodoxy,” they write.

Roberts, they say, is the sole reason for Trump’s authoritarian streak.

“Today, as the Roberts court rewrites the Constitution in the image of Trumpian autocracy, it’s become clear that Roberts’ promise to be a neutral umpire was a lie. We are watching a rigged game, and Roberts set it up,” they write.

“The Roberts court has spent Trump’s second term not applying the law so much as clearing it out of his way,” they add.

Among the lawless actions Roberts has allowed: “firing independent agency commissioners, using racial profiling in immigration sweeps, disappearing immigrants to authoritarian and war-torn nations, and defying Congress’ power of the purse,” they note.

But Roberts’ “acquiescence to an anti-democratic America” isn’t new, noting he “has been embedding white-dominant authoritarianism into the country’s source code for two decades. It’s impossible to imagine today’s crisis without the Roberts court having first undermined the foundations of our democracy.”

Ryan Doerfler, a Harvard Law professor who studies the judiciary’s role in a democratic system, says that “You really can trace, in so many ways, the moment we’re in to critical decisions surrounding our law of democracy.”

And much of that began with Roberts, who, “over the last five decades, has taken aim at the law, beginning as a young lawyer in President Ronald Reagan’s Justice Department fighting its reauthorization, when he claimed it would ‘lead to a quota system in all areas,’” the Mother Jones writers explain.

“In 2019, Roberts toppled another pillar of democratic governance — if you don’t like a politician, you can vote them out — by writing in Rucho v. Common Cause that federal judges could not even review claims of partisan gerrymandering, deeming them ‘political questions beyond the reach of the federal courts,’” they explain.

The writers also note that “Roberts didn’t just strip political power from ordinary people — he handed it to billionaires.”

His decisive vote in 2010’s Citizens United v. FEC “lifted restrictions on political spending, while ludicrously insisting it would not ‘lead to, or create the appearance of, quid pro quo corruption.’ Political spending by billionaires has since increased 160-fold,” they note.

That decision is “a direct line between the ruling and Elon Musk buying Trump the White House with more than $290 million and being given free rein to fire his companies’ regulators in return,” they add.

In his nearly five decades in law, Roberts, they write, “didn’t wait till he was on the Supreme Court to empower Republican presidents — he auditioned for the job by showing his willingness to break the rules and come through for his team.”

And under Trump, “we are now operating under a Robertsian reimagining of the separation of powers, in which laws passed by Congress are mere suggestions for a monarchical president,” they explain.

“Trump needed Roberts to win, and Trump’s victory came just in time for Roberts,” they add. “His corrupt bargain has had an exorbitant cost, both for the nation and the court’s reputation.”

The writers agree, saying, today’s court under Roberts “will greenlight Trump’s corrupt, self-enriching behavior and unlawful power grabs. The majority will instinctively know that its fate is tied to the fate of Trump’s movement, and so it will protect it. The result will be a democracy in name only.”

Our Analysis:

The Roberts Court: An Architect of Autocracy

In a scathing critique, Mother Jones writers Pema Levy and Ari Berman lay bare the foundational role Supreme Court Chief Justice John Roberts has played in shaping the trajectory of American democracy—or, as they argue, its erosion. Their analysis is not merely an indictment of a single jurist but a broader lamentation of the Supreme Court’s evolution into an instrument of authoritarianism under Roberts’s stewardship. Let’s dissect their arguments, the evidence presented, and the implications of the Roberts Court on the fabric of American democracy.

Roberts’s Umpire Fallacy

Twenty years ago, John Roberts promised a judiciary tenure marked by impartiality, likening his role to that of an umpire. This analogy, as Levy and Berman point out, was a carefully crafted mirage. It was a charm offensive that successfully pacified senators and seduced the media into buying into the myth of Roberts as a moderate institutionalist. This mischaracterization has had dire consequences. By positioning himself as a moderate, Roberts has been able to facilitate a conservative agenda under the guise of neutrality, effectively manipulating the Court’s perceived legitimacy.

A Court Reimagined in Trump’s Image

The assertion that the Roberts Court has morphed the Constitution to mirror “Trumpian autocracy” is a bold claim, yet Levy and Berman back it up with a litany of the Court’s actions which have systematically dismantled the checks and balances essential for a functioning democracy. Their critique extends beyond mere disagreement with the Court’s decisions; it’s an alarm bell over a pattern of rulings that consistently benefit authoritarian tendencies—ranging from undermining Congress’s power of the purse to enabling racial profiling in immigration policies.

The Pillars of Democracy, Toppled

The writers highlight two landmark decisions to illustrate Roberts’s assault on democratic governance: the Citizens United ruling, which unleashed unfettered political spending by billionaires, and the Rucho v. Common Cause decision, which removed federal courts from adjudicating partisan gerrymandering cases. These decisions, according to Levy and Berman, are not isolated missteps but part of a concerted effort to concentrate political power away from the electorate and into the hands of a wealthy and influential few.

Roberts’s Legacy: A Democracy in Name Only

Perhaps the most damning conclusion drawn by Levy and Berman is the portrayal of the Roberts Court as an enabler of corruption and unlawful power grabs. By aligning the Court’s fate with that of Trump’s movement, Roberts has, in their view, transformed the Supreme Court into an entity that no longer serves as a bulwark against executive overreach but as its protector. The result, they argue, is a United States that operates as a democracy in name only, its core principles hollowed out by a Court more concerned with preserving power than protecting the rights of its citizens.

Levy and Berman’s analysis is a clarion call to those who hold the principles of democracy dear. It paints a picture of a Supreme Court Chief Justice whose legacy will not be measured by his adherence to the rule of law but by his complicity in its undoing. The article serves as a reminder that the health of a democracy can be gauged not just by the actions of its elected officials but by the vigilance of its judiciary. Under Roberts, they assert, that vigilance has not just waned—it has been weaponized against the very democracy it was meant to safeguard.

In sum, the Mother Jones article is not just a critique but a wake-up call. It challenges us to question the narratives we’ve been sold about the neutrality of the judiciary and to confront the reality of a Supreme Court that has strayed far from its foundational principles of impartiality and protection of democratic norms. Whether one agrees with every point Levy and Berman make, their argument compels us to scrutinize the role of the Roberts Court in shaping an America that, day by day, looks less like the democracy it professes to be.
