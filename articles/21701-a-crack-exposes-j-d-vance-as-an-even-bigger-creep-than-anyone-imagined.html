A crack exposes J.D. Vance as an even bigger creep than anyone imagined

Based on an article by John Casey

J.D. Vance Is Not the Problem. The Office Is.

This Is a Power Story, Not a Personality Story

The reporting here is simple enough: Secret Service agents assigned to J.D. Vance and his family are reportedly fed up with last-minute, expensive travel demands, including an aborted effort to move his son by Marine helicopter to a golf lesson and repeated no-notice trips for house-hunting. The source piece tries to make that irritation into a character study. That is the wrong frame.

The real question is not whether Vance is unpleasant. The real question is why a vice president can treat public protection resources like an on-demand concierge service while other people absorb the cost, the disruption, and the risk.

He Has the Power, They Carry the Weight

The institutional power sits with the vice president and his office. Secret Service agents do not decide the itinerary; they are forced to secure it. Marine Two does not exist to solve family logistics, and taxpayers do not pay tens of thousands of dollars an hour so a vice-presidential child can get to a golf lesson or a house tour on a whim.

The source notes that previous vice presidents had their kids driven in SUVs, which is the key fact buried inside all the gossip. This is not “the challenge of protecting young children.” It is the exercise of status without discipline, with public personnel drafted into private convenience.

The Leak Means the Norm Has Broken

When Secret Service agents start venting to reporters, the problem is no longer just the schedule. It means the usual code of professional silence has been strained enough to crack. That matters because these are not people who normally spend their energy complaining publicly about the families they protect.

But the leak should not be read as a referendum on Vance’s charisma. It is a signal that the internal machinery is being abused enough to produce resentment. The system is telling on itself: if the guardians are forced into improvisation and humiliation, the protectee is not the victim in that arrangement.

The Article Flattens Responsibility

The source piece leans hard on the idea that Vance is rude, awkward, or broadly disliked. Maybe he is. That is not the central issue, and it is not the most politically useful one. Personality gossip lets institutions off the hook by turning structural abuse into a rude-guy anecdote.

What actually matters is the pattern: a powerful official normalizing exceptional treatment, then hiding behind the sentimental language of family and security when the bill comes due. That is how elite entitlement survives. It dresses itself up as logistics, as parenting, as unavoidable complexity. It is none of those things.

The Pattern Is Older Than Vance

This is what bad political coverage does over and over: it treats deliberate choices by powerful people as friction, confusion, or bad vibes. But the choices are visible here. The office demanded the movement. The agencies were forced to comply or scramble. The public paid. The staff absorbed the chaos. The vice president kept the privilege.

The broader lesson is not about one obnoxious man. It is about a political class that assumes the state exists to absorb its convenience. That assumption is corrosive whether it is made by a president, a vice president, or the smaller aristocrats who learn to move through government as if rules are for other people.
