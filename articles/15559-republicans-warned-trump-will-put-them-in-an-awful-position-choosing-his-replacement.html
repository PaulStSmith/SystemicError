Republicans warned Trump will put them in an 'awful position' choosing his replacement

Looking far ahead at how the 2028 presidential nominations will line up, former Republican party campaign consultant Matthew Dowd predicted Donald Trump will, in all likelihood, put his party in an awful bind.

Reacting to accusations that the president is attempting to "freeze" the field as he flirts with running for a third term despite constitutional prohibitions, Dowd suggested Trump would wait to the last minute to pick his successor, thereby leaving the GOP leadership stuck with whomever he anoints.

Speaking with host Alex Witt on MSNBC, Dowd followed up comments that Rep. Alexandria Ocasio Cortez (D-NY) couldn't beat Trump but matched up well with Vice President J.D. Vance.

"It's primarily about the grift," he began. "That's the first point of it. But he is freezing the field, one, by virtue of his enormous personality and sway in getting media attention. But he does make other Republicans sort of stop and not do anything in the course of this."

"And I think that's part of his plan because Donald Trump doesn't want to be viewed as sort of the lame duck," he elaborated. "'We're going to to move on, we're going to have another leader emerge.' And I think he's going to try to push that as long as possible."

"I don't think there's any way in anybody's mind that he can constitutionally run, it's specifically forbidden in the Constitution," he claimed. " I think secretaries of states in Pennsylvania, Michigan, Wisconsin, Colorado, any number of states won't allow him on the ballot and will be supported by the courts in that. But i think it does put the Republicans in an awful position because they can't come up with their next leader."

He added. "And I think what allows Donald Trump to do is lay his hands on the last, on the last minute, on somebody, and then it puts them in an awful position of them having to rally around a one person that he lays his hands on in the final minute of this, and that's problematic."

Our Analysis:

The Trump Conundrum: A GOP Bind or a Calculated Freeze?

In an era where the boundaries between political strategy and outright grift blur, former Republican party campaign consultant Matthew Dowd's analysis of Donald Trump's potential impact on the 2028 presidential nominations offers a scathing indictment of not just one man's ego, but of a party's apparent paralysis in his wake.

Trump's Alleged Strategy: Dowd's assertion that Trump is effectively "freezing" the field to the detriment of the Republican Party is both a testament to Trump's enduring enormous personality and an indictment of a party's inability to move beyond a singular figure. The implications here are twofold: firstly, that Trump's sway within the GOP is so monumental that potential candidates are immobilized, and secondly, that the GOP leadership is seemingly incapable of orchestrating a succession plan that doesn't hinge on Trump's whims.

The Constitutional Roadblock: It's a point so glaring it should be obvious, yet it bears repeating - Trump's potential third term flirtation flies in the face of constitutional law. Dowd is spot on in highlighting the constitutional prohibition against a third term, and the expectation that state secretaries and courts would enforce this. The fact that this even needs to be stated is a bizarre commentary on the current state of political discourse. It's not just about Trump's ambitions; it's a reflection of a broader systemic failure to respect and uphold the foundational laws of governance.

The Grift and GOP Paralysis: Dowd's characterization of Trump's motives as primarily grift-centric is a brutal reflection of a political landscape marred by personal gain over public service. More alarming, however, is the portrayal of a GOP seemingly at the mercy of one man's ego-driven machinations. The notion that Trump could, at the last minute, anoint a successor, forcing the party to rally behind them, is emblematic of a deeper issue within the GOP - a lack of democratic process and a surrender to personality cultism.

AOC vs. Vance: Dowd's comments on potential matchups, like Alexandria Ocasio Cortez against J.D. Vance, while speculative, underscore a yearning for political contests based on policy and ideology rather than cults of personality. The contrast between AOC's policy-driven approach and Vance's vice-presidential gravitas (under the shadow of Trump) presents a stark choice for voters: substance over spectacle.

In Conclusion

Dowd's analysis, while focused on Trump's potential to disrupt the 2028 nominations, inadvertently shines a light on broader issues within American politics: a constitutional crisis in waiting, a major political party at risk of becoming a one-man show, and the ongoing battle between substantive political dialogue and personality-driven politics.

The GOP's "awful bind" isn't just about navigating Trump's influence; it's about rediscovering its identity and principles in a post-Trump era. Whether the Republican Party can or will attempt to extricate itself from Trump's shadow remains to be seen. But one thing is clear: the longer they wait, the harder it will be.
