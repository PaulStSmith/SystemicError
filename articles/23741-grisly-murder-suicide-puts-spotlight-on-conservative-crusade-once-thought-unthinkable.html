Grisly murder-suicide puts spotlight on conservative crusade once thought unthinkable

The “Family Values” Project Is a Cage

A Killing at the Exit

Cerina Fairfax spent nearly two years trying to leave her marriage while still sharing a Virginia home with her husband. State law required parents of minor children to wait a year before filing for divorce, and Justin Fairfax used additional legal tactics to prolong the process. After a judge awarded Cerina sole custody and ordered Justin to leave, police say he fatally shot her and then himself—days before his deadline to move out, with their teenagers inside the house.

That sequence does not prove Virginia’s waiting period caused the killing. It establishes something narrower and politically crucial: the state compelled prolonged legal and physical entanglement during a separation that ended in lethal violence. A law sold as protection for families increased the time one spouse remained exposed to another.

The State Chooses Who May Leave

Divorce restrictions are often discussed as if they merely encourage patience. They do something more coercive: they transfer power from the spouse seeking to leave to the spouse determined to prevent departure—and ultimately to judges and legislators.

Waiting periods, fault requirements and covenant marriages do not repair relationships. They regulate exit. When one spouse objects, the law can turn time, money, custody proceedings and evidentiary rules into leverage. That burden falls hardest on the person with fewer resources or greater exposure to violence, overwhelmingly women in domestic-abuse cases.

The central question is therefore not whether divorce is ugly. It is who receives institutional help when one partner says the marriage is over and the other says it is not.

Republicans Are Legislating Confinement

The renewed campaign against no-fault divorce is not an abstract cultural debate. Republican platforms in Texas and Nebraska call for ending or restricting it. Republican legislators in Iowa, Oklahoma, South Carolina and South Dakota have advanced measures to weaken access or make divorce harder. Oklahoma state Sen. Dusty Deevers has advocated public shaming for people deemed responsible for divorce.

Iowa state Sen. Jesse Green described no-fault divorce as a “circus” marked by chaos and insufficient accountability. But his proposed remedy would not have created mutual accountability. It would have allowed couples to surrender a future exit route at the moment of marriage, before either person could know whether the relationship would become dangerous.

This is authoritarian cruelty in domestic language: use state power to preserve the formal family even when the people inside it are unsafe. “Family values” supplies the branding. Compulsory dependence is the policy.

The Blame Is Pointed Downward

The anti-divorce argument blames individuals for allegedly abandoning marriage too easily. The evidence in the reporting cuts against that story. Divorce has declined nationally for more than four decades, and researchers say legal restrictions are not an effective way to determine divorce rates. Several states with the highest rates are Republican-led, while conservative and religious areas often contain demographic factors—particularly younger marriage—that make divorce more likely.

That leaves the campaign with a revealing target: not the economic and social conditions associated with marital instability, but the legal autonomy of the person trying to leave.

This is scapegoating by design. Legislators flatten complicated relationships into a morality play about selfish spouses, then give obstructive or abusive partners more procedural power. The weaker actor becomes responsible for the family’s collapse; the institution imposing barriers presents itself as the family’s defender.

Abuse Does Not Arrive With a Case File

Fault-based divorce assumes mistreatment can be packaged into evidence that satisfies a court. Domestic violence advocates know otherwise. Abuse may leave no police report, medical record, photograph or cooperative witness. Survivors may avoid authorities because intervention can escalate the danger. Separation itself is often the most dangerous period.

Requiring proof under those conditions does not simply identify wrongdoing. It forces a person seeking safety to persuade the state that the harm is sufficiently documented. The abusive spouse needs only to contest, delay and exploit the process.

The article’s framing is strongest when it connects divorce rules to that material danger. It weakens when it treats the conservative campaign mainly as the surprising return of an old debate. The relevant development is not that politicians are talking about divorce again. It is that Republican institutions are testing how much control the state can reclaim over intimate decisions after the destruction of federal abortion protections.

The Politics of Forced Dependency

Restrictions on abortion, contraception, fertility care and divorce do not operate identically, but they share a political structure: officials narrow women’s control over pregnancy, marriage and departure while calling the result protection.

The Fairfax case cannot tell us what every proposed divorce restriction would cause. It shows what lawmakers choose to disregard when they make exit slower and harder. Delay is not neutral. Procedure is not neutral. A legal barrier becomes power in the hands of whoever benefits from keeping another person trapped.

The larger pattern is a politics that treats the traditional family as an institution to be preserved even against the will—and sometimes the safety—of the people inside it. Its advocates speak of stability while redistributing power toward controlling spouses and the state. The family is the symbol. Enforced dependency is the governing project.
