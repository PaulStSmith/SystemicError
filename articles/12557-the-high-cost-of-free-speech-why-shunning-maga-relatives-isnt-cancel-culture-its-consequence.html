The High Cost of Free Speech: Why Shunning MAGA Relatives Isn’t Cancel Culture, It’s Consequence

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 20, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Freedom of speech is not freedom from consequence. That’s the line MAGA adherents never seem to grasp, a line that has become increasingly critical in the post-2016 political landscape. What Sarah Jones laid out in her recent analysis for New York Magazine was not only timely—it was a searing indictment of a collective gaslighting campaign by those who weaponize "family values" to excuse extremist ideologies. Her central thesis? Cutting off toxic family members isn't political censorship. It's self-preservation.

The article, shared on AlterNet under the headline "Move on: Americans urged to dump their MAGA relatives," cuts through the faux-outrage like a scalpel. The reaction from the right was predictable: performative pearl-clutching, accusations of leftist intolerance, and cries of "cancel culture!" But let’s get something straight: the people crying foul are often the same ones who cheer when a teacher gets fired for being gay or a book is banned for discussing race. The selective outrage is as hypocritical as it is hollow.

No, This Isn’t Just a Difference of Opinion

In a saner time, political disagreements might have centered on marginal tax rates or agricultural subsidies. But MAGA isn’t about policy. It’s about personality cults, manufactured grievances, and violent ethno-nationalism wrapped in the American flag. If you voted for Trump in 2016 and stuck with him after the Muslim ban, Charlottesville, the kids in cages, the pandemic death cultism, and January 6th, then congratulations: you're not just voicing an opinion. You're endorsing a worldview that sees your fellow citizens as enemies.

Jones points out that young people are going no-contact with MAGA relatives not for trivial reasons but for deeply personal and moral ones. A mother whose son won’t speak to her because she voted for Trump sees herself as a victim. Jones sees a narcissist who can't fathom the idea that her son might have principles. And she's right. This isn’t a political tiff. This is a moral rupture.

The Liberal Fetish for Neutral Ground

Jones also skewers the liberal obsession with civility and discourse, epitomized by former Obama speechwriter David Litt. Litt's call for reconciliation reads like a desperate plea from a bygone era, when politics was treated as a sport and not a fight for basic rights. He argues that shunning family members only deepens division. But here’s the thing: division isn't the disease. It’s the symptom of an untreated infection.

What Litt pines for—a neutral, apolitical Thanksgiving where everyone agrees to disagree—no longer exists, if it ever did. The idea that hate can be countered with hugs is not just naive; it's dangerously dismissive of the lived experiences of those targeted by MAGA ideology. Telling a queer teen to keep peace with a parent who believes they’re going to hell isn't unity. It's abuse.

Shunning as a Form of Moral Boundaries

Estrangement is painful. But sometimes it’s necessary. When someone repeatedly votes to hurt others—to erase trans rights, to strip bodily autonomy, to embolden racists and insurrectionists—they’re making a choice. And the people around them have the right to respond accordingly.

The right loves to act like the First Amendment is a shield against social fallout. It’s not. It never was. The Constitution guarantees you won’t be jailed for your speech. It doesn’t guarantee you won’t be blocked, unfriended, or cut off by your children. Actions have consequences. If your rhetoric contributes to harm, don’t be shocked when the people you harm walk away.

When Jones says, "If my parents liked Alligator Alcatraz, I’d no longer speak to them," she’s not joking. She's setting a boundary—one rooted not in intolerance, but in the refusal to normalize hate. This isn’t about echo chambers or ideological bubbles. It’s about refusing to keep toxic people in your life just because they share your DNA.

The Weaponization of Family

MAGA world thrives on turning every critique into an assault on traditional values. They love to paint themselves as victims—ostracized for "just having an opinion." But let’s be clear: voting for someone who called for political violence, endorsed white supremacy, and attempted to dismantle democracy isn't "just an opinion." It's a declaration of allegiance to a worldview that is incompatible with pluralism, decency, and yes—family.

What Jones captures so well is that sometimes, the act of knowing someone too well means you can’t unknow what they support. And once that line is crossed, the relationship isn't just strained. It's untenable. You can’t reason someone out of a position they were never reasoned into. If someone embraces fascism because it makes them feel empowered, they’re not looking for a debate. They’re looking for domination. And no amount of calm conversation is going to change that.

It's Not Cancel Culture. It's Grown-Up Accountability.

Here’s what makes the MAGA outrage over estrangement especially laughable: the same people who rage about being "canceled" for their views are the first to call for boycotts, bans, and censorship when someone expresses a progressive idea. They want their speech protected, but yours silenced. They want immunity, not equality. And when they don’t get it, they cry persecution.

This isn’t about cancel culture. This is about consequences. Speech is a two-way street. You can say what you want, but so can everyone else—including those who choose to shut the door in your face. That’s not censorship. That’s accountability.

Jones is absolutely right: the idea that discourse will bridge this chasm is a relic of another age. The American far right isn’t interested in dialogue. It wants submission. And if your dad, your sister, or your uncle has decided to side with that ideology, you have every right to decide they no longer get a seat at your table.

Final Thoughts: The Bridge Is Already Burned

What the MAGA crowd refuses to acknowledge is that the bridge isn’t being burned by those walking away. It was incinerated by the very rhetoric and policies they supported. You can only call someone a traitor, a pedophile, or a communist so many times before they take the hint and stop inviting you to dinner.

Estrangement is not political strategy. It’s not some tactical maneuver in a culture war. It’s the fallout from being told—again and again—that your existence, your rights, or your values are disposable. It’s not about echo chambers. It’s about sanity.

If your MAGA relatives feel shunned, maybe it’s time they look in the mirror. Not to mourn the lost connection, but to confront the reality that it was their words and actions that broke it.

And if you're someone who had to walk away from family to protect your peace, know this: you're not alone. You're not the problem. You're the consequence.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
