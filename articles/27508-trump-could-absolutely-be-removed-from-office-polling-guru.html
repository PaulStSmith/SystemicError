Trump ‘could absolutely’ be removed from office: polling guru

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 28, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

AlterNet’s headline overstates Nate Silver’s argument. Silver did not report that President Donald Trump is on a clear path to removal; he said Trump “could absolutely” be convicted by the Senate if his collapsing approval and the Nvidia scandal produced enough Republican defections. The obstacle is decisive: although Democrats won both chambers in the November 3 midterms, removing Trump would still require a two-thirds Senate vote, meaning a substantial bloc of Republican senators would have to abandon him.

Silver’s case, made Wednesday on Still Counting, rests on political deterioration rather than an announced impeachment drive. Prediction markets put the chance of Trump being impeached and removed before his term ends at roughly 25 percent—odds Silver called “entirely reasonable.” That is a market estimate, not a congressional count, and possibility is not momentum.

Nvidia turns party loyalty into a liability

The more consequential issue is the conduct Silver says could make removal plausible. On Monday, Nvidia’s board reinstated ousted CEO Jensen Huang after Trump pressured the company to restore him, according to AlterNet. Huang had reportedly had an affair with a woman widely believed to be a Chinese agent, while Nvidia faced questions about efforts to evade U.S. export controls.

Silver argued that if Trump was working to “profit off selling advanced chips to China,” removal would be warranted, calling such conduct “a profound betrayal of his base.” The conditional matters: the supplied reporting does not establish that Trump personally sought profit from chip sales. It does establish, as AlterNet reports it, that he used presidential pressure on behalf of Huang despite national-security concerns surrounding Huang and Nvidia.

That is where the story’s institutional stakes lie. A president can turn informal pressure into a material corporate outcome without waiting for Congress to legislate or an agency to act. Nvidia’s board made the reinstatement decision, but Trump reportedly applied the pressure that preceded it. Concentrating only on Huang’s personal exposure would misdirect attention from the public official wielding state power and the directors who yielded to it.

The political danger for Trump comes from his own coalition’s hostility toward China. An Economist/YouGov poll cited on the program found that 54 percent of Americans believed the affair definitely or probably compromised national security. Among Republicans, the figure was 61 percent. Those results measure public belief about the affair; they do not prove a security breach. But they show why Trump’s intervention could be unusually costly inside a party that has made confrontation with China central to its identity.

Republican criticism is not yet a removal coalition

Two Republican senators have publicly broken with the administration’s handling of the matter. Arkansas Sen. Tom Cotton called the scandal “just the tip of the iceberg.” North Dakota Sen. Kevin Cramer said the possibility that a Chinese asset could extort Huang was sufficient reason not to rehire him.

Those statements puncture the appearance of party unanimity, but criticism is cheaper than conviction. Nothing in AlterNet’s report indicates that Cotton, Cramer or other Republican senators have committed to removing Trump. Silver’s broader theory is that Republicans who survived the midterms and face voters again in 2028 may fear being dragged down by an increasingly unpopular president.

Trump’s approval rating was 35.9 percent in Silver Bulletin’s average. Silver said that, if the decline continued at the same pace, it could reach roughly 29 percent by the end of the following year. That is an extrapolation, not a forecast carved into the calendar. Approval can stabilize or recover. Its immediate significance is leverage: elected Republicans become more willing to defy a president when loyalty looks electorally dangerous rather than politically compulsory.

Silver said that, were he a Democratic leader such as Hakeem Jeffries or Chuck Schumer, he would already be counting votes for a veto-proof two-thirds majority to impose tougher China export controls. That would test Republican senators on policy before asking them to take the far more drastic step of ending a presidency. It would also force Trump’s allies to choose publicly between the president and the China restrictions their party’s voters say they favor.

A constitutional possibility sold as a probability

One of Silver’s co-hosts, Malone, rejected the removal scenario outright, saying it was more likely that Marco Rubio or JD Vance would resign than that Trump would be impeached and convicted. That disagreement is the clearest description of the present situation: Trump’s vulnerability is no longer unimaginable, but the reported facts do not show that removal is approaching.

The headline’s teaser therefore resolves to a narrower claim. Trump “could absolutely” be removed because the Constitution permits it, Democrats control Congress, his approval is weak, and the Nvidia affair has drawn criticism from Republican senators and voters. Yet Democrats cannot do it alone, prediction-market odds are not Senate votes, and rebukes from Cotton and Cramer are not commitments to convict.

What has changed is the cost structure around Republican obedience. Trump reportedly spent presidential influence to secure a corporate reinstatement that most Republican respondents in the cited poll viewed as compromising national security. Whether that becomes an impeachable rupture depends less on Democratic outrage than on how many Republican senators decide Trump has become more dangerous to their careers than defying him.
