White House shows 'symptoms of panic' in 'whack-a-mole' election dash: DC insiders

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

Paulo SantosOctober 1, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower made the problemA campaign fix, not an economic fixThe “whack-a-mole” dodgeGovernment by backlash

What happened

RawStory, citing a Washington Post report published five weeks before Election Day, says the Trump White House was rushing out a patchwork of proposals to address inflation that was outpacing wage growth. Economists quoted in the report said the measures were too limited—and too late—to meaningfully ease the affordability crisis before voters decided control of Congress.

The Post attributed some price pressure to administration choices: tariffs that raised prices on certain imports, an immigration crackdown that constrained low-cost labor, and Trump’s decision to join Israel’s war with Iran, which disrupted global oil supplies and elevated energy costs. The reporting does not specify the White House proposals, so their individual effects cannot be assessed from the supplied account.

Power made the problem

The decisive actors here are not consumers, workers, or an abstract “economy.” The president and his administration control tariff policy, immigration enforcement, and major national-security decisions. Congress also possesses substantial economic authority, although the supplied reporting focuses on the White House.

That distinction matters because affordability is often presented as a weather system that descends on presidents from nowhere. In this account, the administration exercised power in ways economists said increased costs, then confronted the political consequences as an election approached.

A campaign fix, not an economic fix

David Wessel of the Brookings Institution called the scramble a symptom of panic over problems the administration had partly created. Mark Zandi of Moody’s Analytics said the proposed fixes were small enough that they might not move the affordability crisis at all. Rebecca Patterson of the Council on Foreign Relations said an economy this large could not be repaired in a month.

Taken together, those assessments describe political triage. The immediate objective appears to be demonstrating activity before Election Day, not producing relief on a timeline when households could reliably feel it. That motive is an inference from the timing and expert analysis, not a stated White House admission.

The “whack-a-mole” dodge

Douglas Holtz-Eakin of the American Action Forum compared the approach to the Biden administration’s attempts to target particular prices: politically conspicuous interventions that neither solved the broader problem nor proved especially successful with voters.

That comparison is useful only if it does not flatten responsibility into ritualized equivalence. “Both administrations tried price fixes” is not a sufficient analysis. The relevant question is which government decisions added pressure in this instance. The reporting points specifically to Trump’s tariffs, immigration crackdown, and intervention in the Iran war. Treating the result as generic inflation would hide the policy chain behind the symptom.

Government by backlash

The larger pattern is government that treats consequences as messaging emergencies. Officials make far-reaching choices; predictable costs reach households; polling and elections make those costs urgent; and the administration searches for highly visible gestures that can outrun economic reality.

That is not confusion. The reporting depicts a mismatch between institutional power and political accountability: the White House could act quickly to impose tariffs, restrict labor supply, and enter a war, but could not quickly reverse the resulting price pressures. Voters were then offered evidence of effort because material relief could not arrive on the campaign calendar. The system rewards the exercise of power immediately and postpones its consequences—until those consequences become someone else’s bill and the governing party’s electoral problem.
