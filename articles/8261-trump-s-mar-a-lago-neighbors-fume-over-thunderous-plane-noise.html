Trump's Mar-a-Lago neighbors fume over 'thunderous' plane noise

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 29, 20254 min read

As of October, the U.S. Secret Service designated President Donald Trump’s Mar-a-Lago resort in Palm Beach, Florida, as a no-fly zone, forcing aircraft to divert around it even when he is not visiting. Speaking with the Washington Post, residents of the area planes must now fly over fumed over the noise and soot left behind.

As required by the no-fly zone, a significant number of lanes are now routed over the El Cid neighborhood, a few blocks north of Trump’s resort. However, it’s not just the noise from these planes leaving or arriving at Palm Beach International Airport causing a stir, as some area officials highlighted the abruptness with which the policy was put in place.

“There was no lead-up to this,” Nancy Pullum, chair of the Citizens’ Committee on Airport Noise, said. “It just happened. Literally nobody knew. The flight traffic controllers didn’t know. The airport, they didn’t know. Palm Beach County didn’t know.

“It’s thundering,” Pullum added. “It’s as if they’re accelerating when they’re right over me. You go take your trash out to your garbage can, and you realize there’s a plane right over your head, and you can see the belly of it.”

“We want to do everything to make sure we protect our president, and we understand that when he’s there, this is what needs to happen,” Palm Beach County Commissioner Gregg Weiss told the Post. “But when he’s not there, why? What’s the concern at that point?”

The Secret Service declined to go into much detail about the decision when pressed for comment by the Post.

“In order to ensure the highest levels of safety and security for the President, the U.S. Secret Service requested the FAA institute additional temporary flight restrictions over Mar-a-Lago,” a Secret Service spokesman said in a written statement. “We recognize that these changes could have an impact on the public and appreciate the Palm Beach community’s understanding as we work to keep the President safe.”

Since acquiring the Mar-a-Lago property in 1985, Trump has sued the county and the airport over plane noise. The first two cases were dismissed, as was the third, but only after he was elected president in 2016. This meant air traffic was diverted from over the resort, but unlike the present situation, it was only when he was visiting.

The current setup is set to remain in effect until at least October of next year.

Our Analysis:

The Unsettled Skies of Mar-a-Lago: A Tale of Noise, Nuisance, and No-Fly Zones

In a move that reeks of privilege and power misuse, the skies above Mar-a-Lago, President Donald Trump’s resort in Palm Beach, Florida, have been designated a no-fly zone by the U.S. Secret Service. This isn’t just about the high-flying antics of a president; it’s a clear-cut case of how the whims of one can disrupt the many. Here’s the breakdown:

The Impact: More Than Just Noise Pollution

Residents of the area, now under the frequent thunder of planes rerouted to avoid the skies above Mar-a-Lago, are experiencing firsthand the fallout from this decision. The aircraft, previously dispersed in their flight paths, are now concentrated over the El Cid neighborhood, a mere few blocks north of the Trump resort. The residents’ complaints of noise and soot are not just minor inconveniences but significant disruptions to their daily lives and environment. The abruptness of this change, as highlighted by Nancy Pullum of the Citizens’ Committee on Airport Noise, underscores a glaring lack of communication and consideration for the community.

The Rationale: Security or Overreach?

The Secret Service’s explanation for this imposition – ensuring the “highest levels of safety and security for the President” – is a well-worn refrain used to justify a broad swath of actions. However, as Palm Beach County Commissioner Gregg Weiss pointedly asks, “But when he’s not there, why? What’s the concern at that point?” This question pierces the heart of the issue: the no-fly zone remains in effect even in Trump’s absence, a policy that seems more like a permanent marker of privilege than a temporary security measure.

Historical Context: A Pattern of Privilege

Trump’s history with Mar-a-Lago and air traffic is not new. His lawsuits against the county and the airport over plane noise date back to before his presidency. These legal actions depict a man accustomed to bending the public sphere to suit his private desires. The current no-fly status of Mar-a-Lago, extending until at least October of the next year, is merely the latest chapter in this saga of entitlement.

Conclusion: The Cost of Convenience

The Secret Service’s brief statement does little to allay concerns, offering a vague nod to public impact without addressing the core issues of transparency, necessity, and proportionality. The residents of Palm Beach are left to bear the brunt of this decision, a testament to the power dynamics at play.

The situation at Mar-a-Lago is not just about air traffic; it’s about how decisions are made, who they benefit, and who is left dealing with the consequences. It’s a stark reminder of the disparities in influence and the ease with which those in power can impose their will on the environment and communities. As the skies above Mar-a-Lago remain clear for the convenience of one, the ground-level reality for many is a daily disruption that begs the question: At what point does security become overreach?
