Trump declares 2 new national holidays — as he bemoans 'we already have too many'

President Donald Trump took to his social media platform Monday evening to double down on his plans to celebrate the end of World War I and World War II in the United States by designating specific "Victory Days" for each conflict.

He wrote on his Truth Social app last week that he would rename May 8 as "Victory Day for World War II," aligning with the European observance of VE Day, and Nov. 11 as "Victory Day for World War I," the date of the armistice in 1918.

Trump has also floated renaming Veterans Day to "Victory Day for World War I," but the White House walked that back facing backlash from veterans' groups and others.

On Monday, he doubled down on the idea — but warned Americans not to expect more time away from the office.

"We won two World Wars, but we never took credit for it — Everyone else does! All over the World, the Allies are celebrating the Victory we had in World War II. The only Country that doesn’t celebrate is the United States of America, and the Victory was only accomplished because of us. Without the United States, the War would have been won by other Countries, and what a different World it would be," he wrote on his app.

Trump continued, "Therefore, I am hereby declaring a National Holiday in celebration of the Victories of World War I, where the Armistice was signed on November 11, 1918, and World War II, where the Victory date was May 8, 1945."

He clarified, however, "We will not be closing the Country for these two very important Holidays, November 11 and May 8, World War I and World War II, because we already have too many Holidays in America — There are not enough days left in the year. We were Workers then, and we are Workers now! Documentation to follow."

Our Analysis:

Trump's "Victory Days": A Misguided Attempt at Rewriting History?

In a move that is both bewildering and predictably egocentric, President Donald Trump has once again stirred the pot of national discourse, this time by proposing the establishment of "Victory Days" to commemorate the end of World War I and World War II. This proposal, articulated through a series of posts on his Truth Social app, not only showcases Trump's penchant for grandstanding but also underlines a profound misunderstanding of both history and the current socio-political climate.

A Misguided Celebration?

Trump's declaration to rename May 8 and November 11 as "Victory Day for World War II" and "Victory Day for World War I," respectively, reeks of a fundamental misreading of the room. To start, his assertion that the United States "never took credit" for its role in the World Wars is astonishingly inaccurate. The U.S. celebrates Veterans Day on November 11, a holiday that honors all U.S. veterans, coinciding with World War I's armistice date. Additionally, Memorial Day serves as a day of remembrance for those who have died in military service for the United States, a holiday that, while not specific to any single conflict, certainly encompasses the sacrifices made during both World Wars.

Moreover, Trump's proposal to align the U.S. with Europe by celebrating VE Day on May 8 ignores a crucial fact: the United States has its own history and traditions of remembrance that are deeply ingrained in the national consciousness. His suggestion not only shows a lack of respect for these traditions but also a startling disregard for the sensitivities surrounding national holidays that are meant to honor sacrifice, not merely "victory."

A Tone-Deaf Proposition

The former president's call to not give Americans additional time off because the country already has "too many Holidays" is perhaps the most tone-deaf aspect of this entire debacle. It belies a fundamental misunderstanding of what national holidays represent: a collective pause to remember, reflect, and honor. His characterization of Americans as "Workers then, and Workers now!" while invoking the spirit of wartime effort entirely misses the point of national remembrance days, which are as much about education and reflection as they are about honoring those who served.

Ignoring the Backlash

Trump's insistence on moving forward with this idea, despite backlash from veterans' groups and others, illustrates a characteristic stubbornness and disconnect from the populace he once served. His administration's quick backtrack on renaming Veterans Day — following widespread criticism — highlights a chaotic approach to policy announcements that seems more focused on garnering attention than on the substantive impact such changes would have on the nation's fabric.

Conclusion: A Distraction Rather Than Dedication

In essence, Trump's proposal for "Victory Days" comes across as a superficial attempt to rewrite history in a way that celebrates triumphalism over true sacrifice and dedication. It's a proposition that not only misunderstands America's complex relationship with its military history but also undermines the existing days of remembrance that hold deep meaning for countless Americans. As we navigate the ever-complex landscape of national identity and collective memory, it's imperative that we approach such subjects with the nuance and sensitivity they deserve — qualities notably absent from Trump's latest foray into the politics of commemoration.
