Trump is stopping you from getting richer: A renowned economist explains

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosSeptember 30, 20263 min read

What happened

AlterNet’s answer is that President Donald Trump is reducing Americans’ chances of becoming richer by weakening the institutions that support long-term growth. Economist Justin Wolfers pointed to erratic tariffs, favoritism, attacks on democratic accountability, tax cuts favoring wealthy people, and spending cuts affecting poorer people. He argued that the damage may emerge over decades rather than in current economic statistics.

Wolfers specifically rejected the interviewer’s claim that Trump is “keeping Americans poor.” His narrower conclusion was that Americans may inherit fewer jobs, inventions, and economic opportunities than they otherwise would. He also described Trump’s tariff regime as incoherent and said presidential control over exemptions can encourage businesses to seek political favor instead of competing through better products and lower prices.

Wolfers called the “One Big Beautiful Bill” the largest single redistribution from poor to rich in American history, crediting analyst Brendan Duke for the initial analysis. AlterNet’s interview, however, does not provide the underlying estimates or a comparison with other legislation, so readers are given Wolfers’s conclusion rather than enough evidence to independently assess its scale.

The headline outruns the evidence

“Trump is stopping you from getting richer” is a cleaner accusation than the interview supports. Wolfers did not claim that every American would lose wealth, nor that current statistics show economic collapse. He described a counterfactual: future prosperity may be lower than it could have been.

That distinction matters. Lost potential is real, but it is difficult to see precisely because the missing factory, invention, wage gain, or competitor never appears in the data. AlterNet turns that careful argument into a personalized certainty. The stronger formulation attracts attention; the economist’s qualified formulation carries the actual analytical weight.

Power sits in the exemption

Trump holds the institutional power at issue. Congress can write tax and spending law, but the presidency controls major parts of trade policy and the administration’s enforcement machinery. According to Wolfers, Trump’s shifting tariffs and ability to grant exceptions give the White House leverage over companies, industries, and trading partners.

That is not a free market. It is discretionary government power presented under the branding of economic nationalism. When corporate success may depend on access, flattery, or an exemption, executives have an incentive to cultivate the president rather than improve products. Wolfers uses Apple CEO Tim Cook as his illustration, though the interview does not establish that Apple displaced any specific innovation project with political lobbying. The broader incentive structure is the stronger point.

Redistribution is a decision

Tax cuts for wealthy people and reductions in programs serving people with fewer resources do not simply “happen” to produce inequality. Legislators pass them, presidents sign them, and administrations implement them. Calling the result an economic transformation can obscure the chain of agency.

Wolfers describes the legislation as an “anti-Robin Hood” measure because it combines benefits concentrated among the wealthy with large spending cuts affecting poorer Americans. If that distribution is accurate, the inequality is not an accidental side effect. It is embedded in who receives public support and who is required to absorb the losses.

The interview’s weakness is evidentiary: it asserts historic scale without showing the figures. “No one has disproved me” is not a substitute for presenting the calculation. A forceful indictment still needs the receipts.

Incompetence is not the whole story

Wolfers criticizes the administration’s weak preparation and constantly changing tariff policy. That may explain some damaging execution, but “incompetence” can also misdirect attention from the people exercising power. An unpredictable system may be economically irrational while remaining politically useful to the official who controls relief from it.

The reporting supports the existence of volatility and presidential discretion. It does not establish the motive behind every change or exemption. But the foreseeable consequence is clear: uncertainty punishes businesses without privileged access and increases the value of proximity to the White House. Whatever the intent, concentrated discretion creates fertile ground for crony capitalism.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is the conversion of economic policy from general rules into personalized permission. Tariffs, exemptions, tax advantages, and spending cuts determine who pays, who receives protection, and who must petition the government for relief. The governing question shifts from whether an enterprise serves the public efficiently to whether it can satisfy the official controlling the gate.

That is how institutional damage suppresses prosperity without producing an immediate statistical crater. The public sees an economy still operating; favored actors learn where power has moved. By the time lost competition and foregone investment become visible, the political hierarchy that caused them may already have hardened into the economic order.
