Trump proves he’s 'lost touch with the real world' once and for all

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 20, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A crisis met with fatalismWealth does not excuse failureThe administration still owes an explanation

President Donald Trump responded to a week of disruptive technology failures with a shrug. Asked during a trip through Asia whether he was concerned about outages affecting Microsoft, the Federal Aviation Administration, United Airlines, Chase Bank and other major institutions, Trump said: “It’s life. When you’re big and powerful and have all the money, things like that happen. It’s fine.”

The breakdowns described by AlterNet were not fine. They suspended air travel, disrupted communications and logistics, damaged economic activity and left some Americans stockpiling cash and supplies. Yet the official with the greatest political authority treated the failures as background noise—not as a governing problem requiring explanation, coordination or repair.

A crisis met with fatalism

MS NOW political columnist Zeeshan Aleem called Trump’s response evidence that the president is “staggeringly out of touch.” The more immediate problem is not simply Trump’s personal detachment. It is what his answer says about how he exercises power.

A president cannot personally repair a bank’s systems or diagnose an airline’s software. He can demand answers from federal agencies, direct officials to assess national-security risks, communicate what the government knows and mobilize resources against further disruption. Trump instead described systemic failure as an inevitable feature of life.

That fatalism is politically convenient. If severe outages merely “happen,” nobody has to answer for fragile infrastructure, weak safeguards or an inadequate government response. A failure demanding investigation becomes an inconvenience the public is expected to absorb.

The disruptions were unusual enough to provoke legitimate questions about whether they were connected, whether artificial intelligence or cyberattacks played a role and whether more failures might follow. According to the account, Friday’s FAA ground stop was only the second since the September 11 attacks. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy struggled to explain what had occurred, while the clustering of outages fueled public suspicion that coincidence might not be an adequate explanation.

Those suspicions are not proof of a common cause. They are, however, a reason for the administration to establish facts rather than wave away concern.

Wealth does not excuse failure

Trump’s claim that breakdowns are natural when an institution is “big and powerful” and has “all the money” reverses the ordinary logic of public capacity. Wealth and power provide the resources to build redundancy, employ specialists, maintain infrastructure and recover quickly. They create responsibility, not an exemption from it.

Aleem suggested that Trump’s “you” may have referred less to the affected institutions than to Trump himself: a wealthy and powerful man insulated from the consequences of failed services. That is an interpretation, but it fits the political hierarchy exposed by the remark. A grounded flight, inaccessible bank account or severed communications link can upend an ordinary person’s work, finances or safety. For someone surrounded by government transport, staff and security, the same breakdown can remain an abstraction.

Calling the outages “fine” transfers their costs downward. Executives and senior officials retain their mobility and institutional protection; travelers, workers and customers lose time, money and access. Trump’s language erased that distribution of harm while presenting elite insulation as evidence that no urgent problem existed.

The administration still owes an explanation

AlterNet’s account says determining what went wrong—and strengthening the country’s digital infrastructure against another wave of failures—will require substantial expertise and effort. That work begins with recognizing the event as a problem.

Aleem’s sharpest point was therefore less about tone than purpose: “‘Things happen’ is not what you say when you are looking for a solution.” Trump did not identify a cause, describe a federal response or tell the public when answers might come. He converted an unresolved infrastructure crisis into a fact of nature.

The outages may ultimately prove unrelated; the supplied reporting does not establish their cause. But the president’s response is already clear. At the moment when the government needed to distinguish coincidence from coordination, malfunction from attack and inconvenience from systemic vulnerability, Trump offered the governing philosophy of the insulated: the systems failed, other people suffered, and therefore everything was fine.
