Prominent Republican skewers MAGA as Trump’s approval continues death spiral

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosJune 24, 20263 min read

Polling Is Not the Problem. Power Is.

Power Has a Name

Donald Trump is not being misread by the public; he is being measured by it. The source story centers on a new poll showing Trump at 30 percent approval, with even worse numbers on the economy, his favorite self-myth. That matters because the actual institution here is not “public mood.” It is the presidency, and the person occupying it is Trump.

Rick Wilson’s snide prediction that Trump could sink into the 20s by late summer is a sideshow, but it points at the real fact: this is not some abstract drift. It is a response to decisions, performances, and consequences attached to a specific officeholder.

The Poll Is the Symptom

The article spends a lot of time on approval numbers because approval numbers are easy to narrate. They are also the least interesting part of the story. The more important fact is that disapproval is broadening across multiple pollsters, and the economy is a particular liability: just 26 percent approve of Trump’s handling of it, while 73 percent expect it to worsen and 65 percent already say it is in recession.

That is not confusion. That is judgment.

The article frames the numbers as if they are mostly a political weather report. They are better understood as a verdict on governance. When an administration can no longer sell competence on the one issue it claims as its strength, the problem is not messaging. The problem is the record.

The Economy Does Not Lie for Him

Trump has always treated the economy as a prop. The source notes the contrast with June 2018, when 41 percent approved of his handling of it. Now that figure has collapsed, and the downward trend is unmistakable.

The usual media habit is to treat this as a “challenge” for Trump, as if the presidency is an exam he might still study for. That softens responsibility. Economic pain is not an accident drifting toward him from nowhere. If the public sees worsening conditions and still rejects his stewardship, the political meaning is simple: his power is being judged by its consequences, not his claims.

Abroad, the Damage Is Bigger Than a Bad Headline

The second poll in the source widens the frame. Across 36 countries, just 23 percent express confidence in Trump, while 37 percent hold an unfavorable view of the United States and 50 percent say the country is not a reliable partner.

That is not just personal unpopularity. It is institutional degradation. Trump’s conduct is not being read abroad as a quirky American personality problem; it is being read as evidence that U.S. commitments are unstable. The report’s comparison to Pew’s earlier findings shows the decline in trust is sharp in multiple countries, including Sweden, Canada, and France. That is what happens when the presidency becomes a generator of volatility rather than a source of credibility.

What the Framing Hides

The weakness of the article is that it treats collapse as if it were mainly a polling story. Polls are not the engine. They are the receipt.

What the numbers actually expose is a familiar political pattern: powerful actors make damaging choices, then rely on the press to translate those choices into personality drama, horse-race decline, or generic “headwinds.” That framing shifts attention away from responsibility and toward optics. It turns deliberate governance into a popularity contest, which is useful only to the people trying to avoid accountability.

The Real Lesson

This story is not about Trump’s approval rating falling. It is about the public registering, in increasingly blunt terms, that the office is being used badly and the country’s standing is being traded down with it.

That is the recurring structure of authoritarian politics and institutional cowardice alike: harm is normalized, blame is diffused, and consequences are described as mood swings. The polling does not create the crisis. It merely records how far the damage has spread.
