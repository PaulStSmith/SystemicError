'An important word': JD Vance language slip may have caused legal nightmare for Trump

A CNN panel flagged Vice President JD Vance’s telling word choice in a recent NBC interview Monday morning, a slip they argued could create a major “legal” liability for the Trump administration as it pursues probes of the president’s critics.

Speaking with NBC News’ “Meet the Press” in an interview aired on Sunday, Vance remarked on the Friday FBI raid on the home of John Bolton, Trump’s former security advisor during his first term, now a vocal critic of the president. The raid was followed by President Donald Trump threatening to launch an investigation into former New Jersey Gov. Chris Christie, another former ally of Trump’s turned critic.

Vance was asked whether Bolton was being "targeted because he’s a critic of Trump,” to which he said “not at all.”

“If we were trying to do that, we would just throw out prosecutions willy-nilly like the Biden administration (Justice Department) did,” Vance said. “...We haven't (brought a case against Bolton) yet, the Department of Justice has not done that yet. We're investigating Ambassador Bolton, but if they ultimately bring a case, it will be because they determine that he has broken the law. We're going to be careful about that, we're going to be deliberate about that.”

Edward-Isaac Dovere, a senior CNN reporter, immediately singled out Vance’s language as being contradictory to the Trump administration’s claims that it is not engaging in political prosecutions of its political enemies.

“It's an important word that the vice president kept using in that clip, which is 'we,'” Dovere said. “He says 'we're' doing this investigation, 'we're' going after it; not the FBI, not the Justice Department, he is very clearly saying this is coming from the White House. The 'we' there is not separate.”

The panel’s host, CNN’s Audie Cornish, noted that Vance’s choice of language could “be a legal point to make later on.” Another guest of the panel, New York Times journalist Lulu Garcia-Navarro, also rebuked Vance for his denial of the Trump administration engaging in political prosecutions.

“Yeah, except just in the last 24 hours, President Trump started on Truth Social going after Chris Christie, another former ally of President Trump is a major critic,” Garcia-Navarro said. “The president hates to be criticized on television. Second of all, he really hates people who were former allies and have turned against him; that, to him, is a very, very sore spot.”

Vance's Verbal Slip: A Window into the Trump Administration's True Colors

In a recent interview that has tongues wagging and legal eagles perched on the edge of their seats, Vice President JD Vance may have inadvertently laid bare the inner machinations of the Trump administration with a simple pronoun: "we." During a segment on NBC News’ “Meet the Press,” Vance's choice of words was not just unfortunate but potentially revelatory, hinting at a sinister blend of political vendettas and prosecutorial power that could haunt the administration.

The Implications of "We"

The significance of Vance's linguistic slip-up cannot be overstated. By saying "we're investigating Ambassador Bolton," Vance didn't just blur the lines; he obliterated them. The traditional separation of powers and independence of the Department of Justice from the White House's political agenda is not just a matter of protocol; it's a cornerstone of American democracy. Yet, here we have the vice president, in a Freudian slip of seismic proportions, suggesting that the White House is directly involved in Justice Department affairs.

CNN's panel, led by the astute observations of Edward-Isaac Dovere, didn't miss a beat. Dovere's point that the use of "we" starkly contrasts the administration's claims of non-political prosecutions is a siren call, alerting us to the very real possibility of an executive branch overreach. This isn't just about semantics; it's about the fabric of democratic governance being potentially manipulated for political vendettas.

The Pattern of Political Prosecutions

What's equally troubling is the context in which this slip occurred. The Trump administration, as Vance's interview uncomfortably reminds us, appears to be on a warpath against its critics. The raid on John Bolton's home and the ominous threats against Chris Christie are not isolated incidents but part of a disturbing pattern. Vance's attempt to deflect by accusing the Biden administration of haphazard prosecutions does nothing but highlight the administration's own precarious stance on justice and legality.

Lulu Garcia-Navarro's observations further underscore the personal vendetta aspect of Trump's motivations. Trump's aversion to criticism, especially from former allies, is well-documented. However, the leap from personal grievance to legal persecution is a dangerous precedent. Garcia-Navarro rightly points out the pettiness of Trump's actions on social media, but the implications go far beyond petty; they hint at a tyrannical misuse of power.

The Legal and Democratic Quagmire

Audie Cornish's commentary on the potential legal ramifications of Vance's word choice is perhaps the most ominous aspect of this discussion. If the vice president's words are taken at face value, they could indeed form the basis of a legal challenge against the administration. The separation of powers is not just a political doctrine; it's enshrined in the constitution. Any evidence that the White House is directly influencing prosecutorial decisions could lead to serious legal consequences.

Conclusion

In his NBC interview, JD Vance may have given us more than just a glimpse behind the curtain; he may have shown us the playbook. The use of "we" is not just a slip; it's a signal, a worrying indication that the Trump administration views the Justice Department as an extension of its political will. This is not just about the potential legal jeopardy that the administration might face; it's about the integrity of American democracy itself. The implications of Vance's words are clear and troubling: the Trump administration's commitment to justice is not just questionable; it's potentially unconstitutional.
