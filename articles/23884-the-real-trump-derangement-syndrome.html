The real 'Trump derangement syndrome'

Nepotism Is Not a Foreign Policy

The Power Came From Trump

The reporting describes an Iran war without an exit, mounting damage concealed from the public, and failed diplomacy entrusted to Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff. But Kushner did not acquire state power through expertise, confirmation, or public accountability. Donald Trump gave it to his son-in-law. Any analysis that turns this catastrophe into a story about one arrogant amateur risks obscuring the president who selected him, empowered him, and accepted his judgment.

Ignorance Was Built Into the Assignment

Kushner’s alleged failure to understand what Iran was offering was not an unfortunate communications glitch. According to the source, Trump placed consequential negotiations in the hands of men who disparaged diplomatic experience while advertising their own superiority. That is governance by entitlement: access substitutes for competence, confidence impersonates knowledge, and entire populations absorb the cost of the impersonation.

Kushner’s contempt for the Iran nuclear agreement and the Oslo accords matters because it reveals the method. Complex agreements negotiated by specialists become “dumb” or failed; the unqualified insider then presents himself as the singular dealmaker capable of replacing them. Expertise must first be degraded so nepotism can masquerade as innovation.

The Scapegoat Is Convenient

Calling Kushner the person most responsible “aside from Donald Trump” correctly preserves Trump’s primacy, but the article still spends much of its energy cataloging Kushner’s vanity and stupidity. That framing is emotionally satisfying and politically incomplete. Kushner is not an independent center of constitutional authority. He is a beneficiary of delegated presidential power.

The stronger indictment is institutional. Trump chose loyalty and family proximity over competence. Cabinet officials converted that preference into policy. Congressional Republicans, as described in the source, avoided scrutiny by recessing rather than confronting an impeachment resolution against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. The system did not merely fail to restrain reckless personnel; powerful actors actively insulated them.

Concealment Is Part of the Policy

The source alleges that Hegseth and Trump tried to hide the war’s damage to troops, military infrastructure, and national security, while service members circulated clandestine images and messages. If accurate, that is not simply bad public relations following bad management. It is an attempt to preserve political freedom of action by denying the public an honest account of the consequences.

The same structure appears across the examples cited: agencies are weakened, expertise is displaced, and the resulting harm is treated as an unfortunate surprise. But when leaders deliberately remove institutional capacity and install loyalists hostile to professional knowledge, predictable failure is not confusion. It is policy with deniability.

Incompetence Serves an Authoritarian Order

The officials named in the source—Robert F. Kennedy Jr., Kash Patel, Todd Blanche, Hegseth, and Kushner—occupy different domains, but the alleged pattern is consistent. Personal loyalty outranks institutional duty. Expertise becomes an enemy because it can contradict the leader. Oversight becomes sabotage. Damage is hidden, blamed elsewhere, or converted into proof that government never worked.

That is authoritarian governance in an especially American form: not disciplined administration, but personalized rule enforced through patronage, sycophancy, and institutional vandalism. The chaos is real, yet it is also useful. Hollow agencies cannot resist. Discredited professionals cannot compete for public trust. Loyal amateurs owe their status entirely to the person who elevated them.

The System Rewards the Wreckers

The central political fact is not that Trump repeatedly finds unusually inept people. It is that incompetence becomes a qualification when power depends on obedience. Kushner’s role exposes the arrangement in its purest form: familial access translated into authority over war and peace, without the ordinary constraints meant to protect the country from private whim.

The broader failure belongs to every institution that treats this arrangement as merely eccentric—Congressional allies who block accountability, officials who execute reckless orders, and political coverage that personalizes structural abuse as a parade of buffoons. The buffoonery matters. But the systemic error is the machinery that gives it power, conceals its consequences, and keeps operating after the bodies begin to accumulate.
