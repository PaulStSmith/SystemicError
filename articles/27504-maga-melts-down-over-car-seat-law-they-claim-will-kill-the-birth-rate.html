MAGA melts down over car seat law they claim will 'kill the birth rate'

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 28, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A fit test became a blanket mandateThe burden is real; the demographic claim is notCulture-war arithmetic

California’s new child-passenger law does not require every teenager to ride in a booster seat until age 16. It requires children ages 8 through 15 to use one only when a standard seat belt fails a five-step fit test. Yet over Thanksgiving, prominent MAGA voices converted that safety standard into a lurid story about 16-year-old drivers trapped in booster seats—and then blamed it for suppressing the birth rate.

The uproar began with Katie Miller, wife of White House chief of staff Stephen Miller. “The fastest way to kill the birth rate is mandating more car seats,” she wrote on X, arguing that car seats complicate motherhood through vehicle-width constraints, anchors and the difficulty of traveling with them.

That complaint contains a recognizable burden: transporting several children safely can be expensive and inconvenient. But Miller inflated a conditional fit requirement into a sweeping mandate and leapt from inconvenience to demographic collapse. The target was not the cost of raising children, the design of family policy or the economic choices available to parents. It was a seat-belt rule.

A fit test became a blanket mandate

Under the law as AlterNet describes it, children between 8 and 15 may use an ordinary seat belt whenever it fits properly under the five-step test. Age alone does not determine whether a booster is necessary; height and belt placement matter. Many older children will therefore qualify for regular restraints.

That distinction dismantles the most provocative image circulated by the law’s critics. A 15-year-old with a learner’s permit would not automatically be “confined” to a booster seat, as a New York Post opinion article suggested. Nor does the law create a general rule that 16-year-olds must use boosters, despite repeated conservative claims built around that premise.

Robby Starbuck, an America First-aligned host, mocked the idea of a short 16-year-old being strapped into a booster while licensed to drive and asked Gov. Gavin Newsom whether “mommy” should buckle the teenager in. But the scenario he attacked does not match the law summarized in the reporting. The joke works only after replacing the actual policy with an easier target.

The New York Post similarly called the measure “nonsensical” and folded it into the familiar charge that California is a “nanny state.” Others repeated the label, including one self-described space industrialist who called the state a “literal nanny state.” Repetition did the work that accuracy could not.

The burden is real; the demographic claim is not

Some participants offered a more grounded point. Palmer Luckey, founder of Oculus VR and ModRetro, suggested that automakers could design vehicles and options specifically for new mothers. That response at least identifies a material constraint and proposes that companies serve families better.

Jeremy Carl, a senior fellow at the right-wing Claremont Institute and former Hoover Institution research fellow, attacked CNN’s S.E. Cupp for questioning whether Miller was serious. Carl said anyone who had tried to fit three car seats into a standard vehicle or travel with several restraints would understand the complaint.

But experience with cramped cars does not establish Miller’s claim that booster-seat requirements are “the fastest way to kill the birth rate.” It establishes that American transportation and consumer products often make family life needlessly cumbersome. Turning that structural problem into an attack on a conditional safety rule lets more powerful actors disappear from view: governments deciding whether to support families, employers determining pay and leave, and industries deciding what kinds of households their products will accommodate.

The misdirection became clearer in Gabriel Syme’s nostalgic account of six children piling into a station wagon without seat belts. He contrasted that childhood with the time modern parents spend securing children and warned that leaving them unattended could bring arrest or family separation. Whatever legitimate questions exist about overcriminalization or burdens on parents, riding unrestrained in a station wagon is not evidence that restraint standards are malicious. It is evidence that earlier generations routinely accepted risks now governed by safety rules.

Culture-war arithmetic

The political maneuver is familiar: take a narrow regulation, exaggerate its reach, attach it to a genuine social anxiety and declare the state the enemy. Here, collapsing birth rates supply the anxiety. California supplies the villain. Parents supply the emotional leverage.

The result is an argument that sounds pro-family while refusing to examine the conditions that shape family decisions. AlterNet reported that much of the online response pointed instead to affordability. Rep.-elect Sara McGee chose ridicule, writing that she was thankful Katie Miller was not her mother. Others argued that anyone unable to manage a car seat should reconsider having children.

That last response is glib in the opposite direction. Car seats do impose costs and logistical demands, especially on larger families and people without roomy vehicles. Dismissing those burdens concedes useful territory to demagogues who exploit them. The sharper answer is that a burden can be real while the political claim constructed from it remains false.

Miller and her defenders could have argued for cheaper restraints, better vehicle design or greater material support for parents. Instead, they turned a seat-belt fit test into demographic sabotage. The law’s actual terms were inconvenient to that story, so the story proceeded without them.
