The View stumps Sarah Huckabee Sanders with facts about her own state

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 10, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower without familiarityThe affordability dodgeA victim used to widen the frameLanguage as enforcementThe governing pattern

What happened

AlterNet reports that Arkansas Gov. Sarah Huckabee Sanders, appearing Monday on ABC’s “The View” to promote her book, could not correctly identify her state’s minimum wage. After presenting tax cuts and “maximum wage” as evidence of her affordability agenda, Sanders guessed $15 an hour; Arkansas’s minimum wage is $11, while the tipped minimum is $2.63.

The interview then turned to immigration. Sanders invoked the killing of an Arkansas nurse by an undocumented driver who, according to KATV as cited by AlterNet, was convicted and sentenced. Co-hosts Ana Navarro and Sunny Hostin challenged Sanders over deportations of people without violent criminal records and her use of “illegals” to describe undocumented people. Sanders acknowledged that enforcement should focus on violent offenders but defended broader border enforcement.

Power without familiarity

Sanders holds the institutional power here. She governs Arkansas, signs or rejects legislation, directs the executive branch and publicly defines her administration’s economic priorities. Hostin could expose a gap in her knowledge; she could not set Arkansas wage policy.

That distinction matters. The embarrassing moment was not merely that Sanders missed a trivia question. She promoted a labor-market slogan while apparently not knowing the wage floor governing workers in her own state. “Maximum wage” offers the language of aspiration without confronting the state’s actual minimum—or the dramatically lower tipped rate.

The affordability dodge

Sanders attributed Republican success to affordability and pointed to tax cuts that increase take-home pay. But tax cuts and wage standards distribute benefits through different mechanisms. A tax reduction helps only to the extent that a worker earns enough to owe the affected tax, while a wage floor directly constrains what employers may pay.

The branding does political work. “Maximum wage” shifts attention from enforceable protections toward the promise that workers might someday advance. It turns low pay into a temporary rung on an imagined ladder, leaving employers’ power over wages largely outside the frame.

A victim used to widen the frame

When challenged about immigration enforcement, Sanders cited a fatal crime committed by one undocumented person. The crime was real and severe as reported; the man was convicted and sentenced. But that case does not answer Hostin’s question about deporting people who have committed no crime beyond an immigration violation, nor Navarro’s point that many people swept up in enforcement lack violent records.

This is scapegoating by substitution: answer a question about a broad population with the most horrifying individual case available. The victim’s death becomes political cover for policies applied far beyond people convicted of violent offenses. Sanders herself said enforcement should focus on violent offenders, yet defended a crackdown the discussion described as reaching people without violent criminal histories.

Language as enforcement

The dispute over “illegal” was not cosmetic. Calling people “illegals” collapses a person into an alleged legal status and makes punishment sound self-justifying. Sanders argued that breaking the law supplies the definition; Hostin answered that people may commit offenses without becoming offenses themselves.

That rhetorical compression serves state power. It erases differences among unauthorized entry, disputed status, nonviolent immigration violations and violent crime. Once those categories are fused, the government’s choice to deport broadly can be presented as the automatic consequence of what people are, rather than a policy imposed on them.

The governing pattern

The interview revealed a familiar structure: weak command of material conditions, polished economic branding and punitive certainty toward people with less power. Arkansas workers are offered a slogan in place of a serious account of wages. Immigrants are reduced to a label and represented through an extreme criminal case.

The sharper political fact is not that a television host stumped a governor. It is that Sanders can misunderstand a basic wage standard and still retain the authority to shape workers’ lives, while people facing deportation can be rhetorically stripped of individuality before the state acts against them. The knowledge gap produced embarrassment. The power gap produces consequences.
