This rogue wave of outrage is enough to force Trump to resign

Based on an article by Jason Miciak

The President Who Turned His Party Into a Hostage

The Source’s Reality Check

The column’s core claim is not that Trump is suddenly vulnerable in some abstract sense. It is that his power has curdled into a narrower, more transactional thing: he can still command attention, but he no longer needs the party apparatus he once used as a shield. The economy is strained, Trump’s age and frailty are harder to hide, and Republican lawmakers are increasingly annoyed that he is acting like their problem rather than their patron.

That is the useful part of the story. The rest is the usual collapse fantasy dressed up as prophecy.

Who Actually Holds Power

The institutional power still sits with Trump. He controls the presidency, the executive branch, the pardons, the agenda, and the symbolic force that keeps much of the GOP in line. Even the column’s own evidence points there: congressional Republicans are complaining privately because they remain downstream from him. They are not directing events. They are reacting to them.

That matters because the piece briefly pretends the party is about to discipline him, as if embarrassment were a governing principle. It is not. A caucus that has spent years obeying him does not become principled because he has become inconvenient.

The Decision Was His

The article’s strongest accusation is also the simplest: Trump is using power for Trump. The ballroom obsession, the alleged slush-fund scheme, the reported appetite for insulating his tax issues from scrutiny, the fixation on wealth and spectacle, the indifference to grocery prices and farm costs all point to the same thing. He is not managing a coalition. He is monetizing office.

That is not confusion. That is deliberate conduct. The column’s claim that he has made himself less useful to Republican lawmakers does not soften the underlying fact that he put them in this position by governing as a personal enrichment project with a state seal attached.

Misdirection as Political Cover

The article gestures at economic pain, age, and congressional frustration as if they are separate waves crashing together. That framing is convenient because it makes the damage look accidental. It is not accidental. Inflation, war costs, elite enrichment, and intra-party revolt are being described as weather when they are really the result of political choice.

There is also a familiar evasive move here: blame floats toward “Capitol Hill,” toward staffers, toward voters, toward vague disarray. But the column itself says the White House has “gone rogue.” That is the cleaner truth. The problem is not that the system failed to contain Trump. The problem is that too many of its parts were built to accommodate him.

Congress Is Not Discovering Courage

The Republican caucus is not awakening morally. It is getting nervous because Trump’s priorities are now embarrassing them in public. They are being asked to defend a president who talks about ballrooms, personal gain, wars that raise fertilizer and diesel costs, and allies who make the whole operation look like a billionaire protection racket. Their irritation is real. Their innocence is not.

This is the oldest pattern in Trumpism: subordinates tolerate corruption until the bill arrives in their own districts. Then they call it concern for the midterms.

The Pattern Beneath the Noise

The larger story is not that Trump may be nearing a dramatic exit. It is that American right-wing power has been reorganized around one man’s appetite, and the party keeps mistaking dependency for control. When that appetite becomes too expensive, the same people who enabled it will call it dysfunction and pretend they only now noticed the fire.

That is the system’s signature move: endorse the wreckage while it is profitable, then narrate it as surprise when it starts costing them.
