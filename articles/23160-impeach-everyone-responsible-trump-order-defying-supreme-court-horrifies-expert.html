'Impeach everyone responsible': Trump order defying Supreme Court horrifies expert

Based on an article by David Edwards

Paulo SantosSeptember 6, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The MoveWho Actually Has PowerThe Euphemism Is the PointMisdirection by DesignThe Pattern

Bureaucracy as Defiance

The Move

The Trump administration has filed a new rule claiming that U.S.-born children of foreign government employees are not citizens unless one parent is already a U.S. citizen. DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin signed it, USCIS filed it late Friday, and the rule took effect immediately. It follows Trump’s second executive order on birthright citizenship, issued after the Supreme Court reaffirmed on June 30 that the Fourteenth Amendment guarantees citizenship to children born on American soil.

Who Actually Has Power

This is not some abstract legal misunderstanding. It is an executive branch choosing to test the boundaries of a Supreme Court ruling and a federal injunction, then wrapping that choice in regulatory language. The administration lost on the broad theory, got blocked again by Judge Deborah Boardman on Wednesday, and responded two days later with a narrower version aimed at foreign government employees.

That is what institutional power looks like when it is used aggressively: not persuasion, but repetition, filing, and administrative attrition. The point is not clarity. The point is to keep moving until something sticks.

The Euphemism Is the Point

The document says USCIS is amending regulations to permit these children to register for lawful permanent residence. That title is doing ideological work. It sounds procedural and benign, while the rule itself says the child is not a U.S. citizen under the Fourteenth Amendment. The claim is not merely narrow; it is camouflaged.

The fee structure makes the cruelty sharper. Registration is voluntary, and the government says the process could cost nearly $2,000 per family. That is not neutral administration. It is a toll booth placed on top of a legal claim to belonging.

Misdirection by Design

The rule tries to split hairs between foreign diplomats, who already have diplomatic immunity, and a broader category of foreign government employees, embassy staff, and international-organization workers with immunity. It also excludes chauffeurs, housekeepers, contractors, and third-country nationals. That taxonomy is supposed to make the policy look careful. In practice, it is a paperwork assault on children.

The administration is not solving a jurisdiction problem. It is manufacturing one, then pretending the Constitution contains an exception broad enough to accommodate it. The source’s own expert puts the central absurdity plainly: you do not get to say someone is subject to U.S. law except when citizenship becomes inconvenient.

The Pattern

What this story reveals is a familiar authoritarian habit: lose in court, narrow the target, rename the harm, and file again. The government is not confused about birthright citizenship. It is probing how much legal damage it can do before someone with standing, time, and resources can stop it.

That is why the real story is not the children of foreign officials. It is the executive branch’s willingness to use administrative process as a weapon against settled constitutional guarantees, then rely on technical language to hide the aggression. The law is being treated as an obstacle course, and citizenship as something the government can meter out case by case.
