Trump's 'madman theory' backfires

Based on an article by Alex Henderson

Paulo SantosJune 21, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Power CenterWhat the Source ShowsWho Enabled the OutcomeThe MisdirectionThe Larger Pattern

Unpredictability as Policy, Clumsiness as Cover

The Power Center

Donald Trump is not a consultant to this crisis; he is the institutional center of it. The source describes him threatening mass destruction, then pivoting to diplomacy, while U.S. and Iranian officials are expected to negotiate a permanent end to the war within 59 days. That is not a mystery of temperament. It is executive power behaving like a hazard.

What the Source Shows

The reporting gives the basic frame: Trump’s ceasefire with Iran exposes the limits of the “madman theory,” the idea that volatility scares adversaries into submission. Former diplomats quoted by NOTUS say Trump’s behavior is less strategy than sabotage-prone improvisation, and Wendy Sherman says the approach has proved “toothless” because the war did not end on his terms.

Who Enabled the Outcome

The relevant decision-maker is Trump, but the machinery around him matters too. The article notes that U.S. officials now have to negotiate while their boss may shift the goalposts at any moment. That is the real governance problem: not just one man’s unpredictability, but an administration built to absorb and normalize it. When the president treats policy as a series of public threats and reversals, subordinates become damage control staff, not diplomats.

The Misdirection

The “madman theory” label is useful mostly because it launderes responsibility. It turns deliberate coercion, public intimidation, and political improvisation into a theory of foreign-policy brilliance or psychological warfare. That framing flatters the chaos. It also obscures the simpler truth: if Trump is sabotaging negotiations by freelancing in the media and moving red lines, that is not unknowable madness. It is an exercise of power with consequences.

The Larger Pattern

This is the recurring logic of authoritarian-style politics in miniature: perform volatility, claim strategic genius, and let the institution clean up the wreckage. The myth says unpredictability is strength. The record in the source says something harsher: instability narrows diplomatic options, weakens credibility, and leaves the public to pay for a leader’s ego theater. The state does not become more formidable when its commander is erratic. It becomes more expensive, more dangerous, and easier to manipulate by his own impulses.
