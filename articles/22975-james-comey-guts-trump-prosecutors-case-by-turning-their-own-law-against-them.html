James Comey guts Trump prosecutors' case by turning their own law against them

Based on an article by Bennito L. Kelty

Paulo SantosSeptember 3, 20263 min read

When Power Fails, It Rebrands Itself as Law

The Real Actor

The source story is not really about a cryptic social-media post. It is about the Department of Justice, under the Trump administration, trying to convert a political grievance into a criminal case. James Comey’s lawyers now say prosecutors picked the wrong statute for the theory they want to sell: if the government thinks a post was a call for violence, then it is an incitement theory, not a threats case.

That distinction matters because law is supposed to constrain state power, not provide costumes for it.

A Charge Built Backward

The prosecution’s theory is already revealing. The government says an “objective viewer” could read the seashell post “86 47” as “Kill President Donald Trump.” But Comey’s defense argues that even if a reader reached that ugly interpretation, the indictment still fails because the charge is mismatched to the conduct alleged.

That is the tell. This is not a case where the law naturally fit the facts and prosecutors merely had to assemble evidence. This is a case where the desired punishment appears to have come first, and the legal theory was fitted around it after the fact. That is not rigor. It is institutional desperation dressed up as public safety.

“86” as Convenient Evidence

The government’s evidence on the meaning of “86” is laughably thin if the stakes were not so serious. According to the filing, prosecutors lean on a 2005 slang dictionary and a 1991 New York Times article that had to explain the term as military jargon for killed in action. That is not ordinary meaning. That is a scavenger hunt for a hostile interpretation.

The defense’s point is straightforward: the need to reach backward into dictionaries and old articles is itself evidence that the government does not have the plain-language case it wants. It is trying to make a political slogan into a violent threat by force of accusation, not by common understanding.

Speech, Threat, Incitement

Comey’s lawyers are also right to insist on the legal category. A call for others to commit violence is incitement, they say, and the indictment does not charge that. They cite Fourth Circuit precedent, including United States v. White, which recognized that broad public political statements can amount to protected hyperbole rather than a true threat.

That is the bigger problem with this prosecution: it treats ambiguity as guilt and legal nuance as an obstacle to be bulldozed. Once the state starts collapsing speech categories whenever it is politically convenient, it gains the power to criminalize opposition by interpretation. Today the target is a former FBI director. The method is larger than the man.

The Misdirection Is the Point

The weak framing here is not accidental. It asks the public to focus on the offensiveness of the post and not on the machinery assembled to punish it. That is how authoritarian-minded institutions operate when they want to look restrained while behaving aggressively. They present overreach as caution, and punishment as interpretation.

Nothing in the source suggests prosecutors discovered a clean, settled threat case and simply followed the law where it led. Quite the opposite: the defense says the government is trying to “broaden the possible bases for conviction” beyond the indictment itself. That is what happens when institutions start chasing symbolic victories instead of durable cases.

The Larger Pattern

This episode fits a familiar pattern in political policing: inflame the public, stretch the law, then act surprised when the record cannot support the charge. The state creates a scandal out of a post, then tries to turn that scandal into proof. It is a corruption of legal process into messaging.

The deeper issue is not whether “86 47” was clever, crude, or stupid. It is that a powerful administration is willing to use federal prosecution as a political instrument and then hide behind the language of objectivity. That is how democratic institutions get hollowed out from the inside: not only by open lawbreaking, but by the quieter habit of treating state power as a tool for punishing enemies first and justifying it later.
