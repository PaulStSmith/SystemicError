JD Vance slammed over ‘pathetic’ joke during 9/11 remarks: 'Worm of a man'

Based on an article by Erik De La Garza

Paulo SantosSeptember 4, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A Vice President Who Mistakes Cruelty for Wit

The Source Event

At a White House press briefing on September 3, 2026, Vice President JD Vance praised the 25th anniversary of the Sept. 11 attacks as an “amazing honor,” then immediately turned the moment into a joke about former President Joe Biden, who is undergoing cancer treatment. The backlash was instant. It should be. The episode is not a side note about bad manners; it is a small, clean demonstration of how power talks when it thinks decency is for people with less authority.

Who Actually Holds Power

Vance is not a random commentator throwing insults from the cheap seats. He is the vice president of the United States, speaking from the White House, in an official briefing. That matters. Institutional power is what makes the joke ugly rather than merely rude: he was not improvising in private, but performing for cameras inside the executive branch while cloaking himself in the language of unity and remembrance.

That is the pattern. The powerful announce solemnity, then use it as a platform for domination. They borrow the dignity of office, then spend it on contempt.

The Target Was Not Biden Alone

The immediate target was Biden, but the real target was the idea that public life should still be governed by minimum standards. Vance chose a 9/11 ceremony to make a cheap shot about a man with cancer. That is not wit. It is a test of what the audience will tolerate from a vice president who knows exactly how much protection status gives him.

The joke also works politically because it tries to recast cruelty as cleverness. If people object, they are supposed to look humorless. If they do not object, the vice president gets to normalize a style of politics where humiliation is treated as a sign of strength. This is not accidental. It is a technique.

The Framing Problem

The article captures the outrage well, but the larger frame is narrower than the behavior deserves. The issue is not that Vance “sparked outrage.” Outrage is the least important consequence here. The real story is that the vice president used a national remembrance event and a White House briefing to model contempt as a governing pose.

That matters because framing is never neutral. When political coverage stays focused on whether a comment was offensive, it can miss the more important question: what kind of officeholder uses state power to practice public sadism? The answer is not confusion, and it is not a lapse in judgment. It is a deliberate choice to speak in a way that degrades an opponent while signaling to supporters that empathy is weakness.

The Coalition He Is Building

Vance’s line was not meant for everyone. It was meant for a political culture that rewards mockery, cruelty, and the collapse of boundaries between official duty and campaign theater. He was not trying to unify Americans on a day devoted to memory and loss. He was demonstrating allegiance to a faction that treats every public setting as a stage for scoring points.

That is the deeper consequence. When senior officials make a habit of this, they lower the standards of the office and raise the value of humiliation. They teach their audience that power is the right to sneer from above. They also train the press to treat the performance as a personality story instead of what it is: an institutional signal.

What This Reveals

The political pattern is simple. There is no symmetry here, no shared decline into rudeness, no generic “tone problem” floating above the parties. One side is still trying to preserve democratic norms, however imperfectly; the other is normalizing governance by insult, grievance, and spectacle. Vance’s joke is small, but its meaning is not. It shows a vice president who thinks public office is a license to belittle a sick former president on the anniversary of a national catastrophe.

That is not leadership failing by accident. It is leadership choosing cruelty because cruelty is useful.
