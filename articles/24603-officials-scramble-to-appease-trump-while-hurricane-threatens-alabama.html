Officials scramble to appease Trump while hurricane threatens Alabama

Based on an article by Terry H. Schwadron

Paulo SantosOctober 10, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Alabama officials prioritized enforcing a state-mandated name for the Gulf while Hurricane Isaias approached the state’s coastline. House Bill 2, which took effect the week before the report, requires state and local entities and employees to use “Gulf of America” in newly created maps, educational materials, websites and official communications, following President Donald Trump’s preferred terminology.

The University of Alabama at Birmingham ordered journalists at WBHM, its NPR affiliate, to comply, according to emails obtained by the outlet. WBHM employees said they would follow the law but did not know how they were expected to police every on-air reference, particularly because NPR and the Associated Press continued using “Gulf of Mexico.” Public employees were also directed to make reasonable efforts to revise existing materials where practicable, unless doing so would create an operational or financial burden.

Faculty members raised concerns about academic freedom. Republican Gov. Kay Ivey’s spokesperson dismissed those objections as a “cover up” for bias against the Trump administration. The report does not establish that hurricane preparations were neglected; it shows officials enforcing the terminology mandate as the storm approached.

Power wrote the vocabulary

This was not a spontaneous dispute among people with equal standing. Trump supplied the preferred name. Alabama’s Republican lawmakers converted it into a legal requirement. Ivey’s administration defended it. UAB then applied it to a newsroom under the university’s authority.

Professors and journalists did not create this conflict. They were the regulated parties, facing instructions backed by employers and the state. Treating the episode as a quarrel over manners or word choice would conceal the decisive fact: government officials used institutional power to prescribe language across public education, communications and broadcasting.

Compliance is the point

The law’s practical value is difficult to locate. A hurricane does not change course according to political nomenclature, and emergency communication becomes no clearer when the state imposes a contested geographic label.

Its political value is more apparent, although the reporting does not establish lawmakers’ private motives. The mandate turns Trump’s preference into a test of obedience. Jason Senkbeil, a tenured geography professor, said he had already resorted to calling the body of water simply “the Gulf” because he did not know where the dispute was heading. That is a concrete consequence of state pressure: specialists begin editing ordinary speech to avoid institutional trouble.

The newsroom problem

Applying the law to WBHM sharpens the stakes. A public university’s authority over an affiliated broadcaster gives political officials an indirect route into editorial language. The university’s top lawyer reportedly suggested monitoring references, while the station receives material from news organizations that reject the imposed name.

The resulting burden falls on journalists, who must reconcile outside reporting with a state command. Alabama officials created that conflict and then presented compliance as simple. It is not simple when the government compels a newsroom to substitute official political terminology for its editorial judgment.

Manufactured grievance, real coercion

Ivey’s spokesperson recast free-speech objections as anti-Trump bias. That move avoids answering the underlying question: why should elected officials dictate the vocabulary used by professors and journalists in the first place?

The article itself also reaches beyond its evidence when it asks whether this issue is being elevated before the midterm election to divert attention from tariffs, war, deportation and corruption. The timing may invite that inference, but the report does not prove a coordinated diversion strategy. The documented abuse is sufficient without embellishment: state power was used to enforce a leader’s preferred language, and objections were attacked as partisan misconduct.

Politics by compelled repetition

This episode reveals a broader method of political control. A symbolic decree begins at the top, lawmakers institutionalize it, administrators transmit it and workers absorb the risk of noncompliance. Each layer can pretend it is merely following instructions, even though the coercive result depends on every institution choosing to enforce the demand.

The immediate consequence is not that Alabama residents forget the Gulf’s established name. It is that public institutions learn to treat political loyalty as an administrative standard. Once government can make a leader’s branding compulsory across classrooms, maps and news broadcasts, the argument is no longer about geography. It is about who gets to manufacture official reality—and who must repeat it to keep their job.
