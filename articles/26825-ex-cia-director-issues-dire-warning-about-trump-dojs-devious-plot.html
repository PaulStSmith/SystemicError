Ex-CIA director issues dire warning about Trump DOJ’s 'devious' plot

Based on an article by Brenda Alexander

Paulo SantosOctober 27, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedWhere the power sitsThe decision-makers need scrutinyEnforcement can become political theaterThe headline outruns the evidenceThe systemic error

What happened

AlterNet reports that former CIA Director John Brennan warned on MS NOW that President Donald Trump could use the Justice Department, ICE, and other executive-branch agencies to disrupt the midterm elections or challenge certification afterward. Brennan described this as a risk, not an established plot already proved by the reporting.

The immediate context was reported federal action involving Rep. Ilhan Omar: a raid on her home and an investigation into her citizenship application. Anchor Alicia Menendez said those actions raised concerns about Justice Department guidance cautioning against investigative steps that could influence voters during the 90 days before a federal election. The article does not establish whether that guidance was violated or provide the government’s stated basis for the actions.

Brennan also accused Trump of pursuing partisan control and called Attorney General Todd Blanche’s defense of the administration “devious” and “unprincipled.” Those are Brennan’s judgments. AlterNet supplies no operational evidence showing that the administration has adopted a plan to interfere with voting or certification.

Where the power sits

The relevant power does not belong to Brennan, television anchors, or voters expected to decipher ominous signals. It belongs to the president and the officials controlling federal law enforcement and immigration agencies.

A Justice Department investigation carries subpoenas, searches, prosecutions, and the prestige of federal authority. ICE and the Department of Homeland Security possess coercive powers that can alter public behavior even without changing a single ballot. If those institutions act near an election, the political effect can precede any courtroom finding. An investigation can stigmatize its target immediately; vindication, if it comes, arrives later.

The decision-makers need scrutiny

Trump commands the executive branch, while Blanche is responsible for how the Justice Department exercises and defends its authority. Any serious account must therefore examine identifiable decisions: who approved the reported raid, who authorized the citizenship investigation, what evidence supported them, and whether normal election-period safeguards were followed.

AlterNet does not answer those questions. That evidentiary gap matters because Brennan’s warning is broader than the facts presented. Calling something a “plot” suggests coordination and intent that this article does not demonstrate. The concern may warrant investigation, but a warning from a former intelligence official is not a substitute for documents, directives, or testimony.

Enforcement can become political theater

The danger in election-adjacent enforcement is not confined to an eventual prosecution. The spectacle itself can do political work. A raid supplies images of guilt before guilt has been adjudicated. A citizenship inquiry aimed at a sitting member of Congress can turn legal process into a public insinuation about belonging and legitimacy.

That does not prove the actions were ordered for electoral advantage. It does mean the Justice Department bears a heightened obligation to explain its legal basis and adherence to policy. When officials deploy coercive authority close to an election, “trust us” is not transparency. It is a demand that the public accept institutional force without examining its use.

The headline outruns the evidence

AlterNet packages Brennan’s allegation as a “dire warning” about a “devious plot,” but the article establishes neither a documented scheme nor the precise status and justification of the reported federal actions. It leans on Brennan’s biography and rhetoric where it should supply evidence about current decision-making.

That framing also personalizes a structural problem. Whether Blanche is a devious lawyer is less important than whether departmental rules constrain him, whether career officials can resist improper orders, and whether Congress or courts can obtain records quickly enough to matter. Fixating on temperament lets institutional machinery disappear behind a villainous adjective.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is the conversion of public authority into partisan leverage—and the media’s tendency to describe that possibility through personalities rather than chains of command. Executive power becomes most politically useful when formal actions can be presented as routine law enforcement while their immediate consequences fall on selected opponents.

The correct standard is exacting: establish who ordered what, under which authority, on what evidence, and with what electoral effect. Brennan identified a plausible avenue for abuse. AlterNet did not prove that abuse occurred. The systemic failure begins when powerful officials escape those concrete questions—and deepens when coverage replaces them with dramatic language.
