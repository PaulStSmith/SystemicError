Marine linked to 'six bullets' Hegseth death threat promoted to general

Promotion for the Connected, Purges for Everyone Else

The Real Gatekeepers

The source story is not really about one Marine colonel and a bad podcast. It is about who gets protected by institutional power and who gets punished by it.

Thomas Siverts was promoted to brigadier general after the Senate approved him unanimously and after Sen. Ron Wyden dropped his hold. That is the actual decision chain: the Senate cleared the promotion, the Pentagon executed it, and Pete Hegseth’s department had already signaled support. The machinery of approval mattered more than the obvious stink around the podcast network attached to Siverts’ family.

A Letter Beats the Record

Siverts’ defense was procedural and sterile: he said he condemned antisemitism and racism, and Wyden said that was enough to remove his concern. The letter became the shield; the surrounding context became an inconvenience.

That is how institutions launder reputational risk. A written denial passes for accountability even when the broader ecosystem remains ugly, public, and unresolved. Siverts did not need to explain why the show’s co-host later called for Hegseth to be shot over support for Israel. He only needed to say he personally disapproved, and the promotion rolled forward.

The Misdirection

The weak framing here would be to treat the story as a question of guilt by association. That is too easy, and it lets the real actors off the hook.

The more important fact is that the podcast ecosystem continued to circulate openly antisemitic material, including “ZOG” rhetoric and fantasies about government betrayal, while the officer tied to that world advanced inside the military hierarchy. The issue is not that every person adjacent to a vile podcast is equally responsible for its content. The issue is that the institution treated the problem as a paperwork issue instead of a power issue.

Hegseth’s Selective Cleanliness

Hegseth is not operating from a neutral standard. He has vowed to purge “woke garbage” from the military while deriding efforts to remove extremists from the ranks. That is not a contradiction to him; it is the method.

According to the source, he has fired or sidelined nearly three dozen senior military leaders, and nearly 60 percent of them were Black or female. At the same time, he backed Siverts’ promotion after the Marine Corps Inspector General declined to investigate. The pattern is plain: extremism is tolerated when it runs in the preferred direction, and discipline is applied with special force when the target is not.

Who Pays, Who Advances

Scott Siverts’ public victory lap says more than the official statements do. His boast that critics “lost” is a reminder that these networks understand power better than the people trying to excuse them. They do not need clean records. They need access, patronage, and a department willing to look away.

That is the broader political pattern: institutions will pretend they are enforcing standards while actually sorting people by loyalty, usefulness, and identity. They will punish the politically inconvenient and advance the politically protected. In that system, antisemitic rot becomes a reputational problem only when it threatens the wrong careers.
