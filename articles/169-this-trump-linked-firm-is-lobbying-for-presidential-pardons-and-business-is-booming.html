This Trump-linked firm is lobbying for presidential pardons — and business is booming

Based on an article by Alex Henderson

Paulo SantosJune 25, 20262 min read

Pardon for Sale

The Source of the Demand

CBS reports that a Trump-linked lobbying firm, Mo Strategies, is cashing in on the White House pardon pipeline. The firm, filled with former Trump campaign and administration hands, has already taken in $500,000 for work tied to “immigration and pardon-related discussions,” while the Trump DOJ says its pardon office is receiving a record number of clemency applications.

Who Actually Holds Power

The power is not in the hands of the applicants, their lawyers, or the lobbyists pretending to “navigate the process.” The power sits with Donald Trump, who has used the Justice Department to pursue enemies and pardon allies since returning to office. Everyone else is orbiting that fact, trying to convert access into a service business.

That is the real institution here: not law, not neutral review, but presidential discretion weaponized as a political instrument. Once that happens, the market does what markets do. It forms around the abuse.

The Access Industry

Mo Strategies is not selling legal analysis. It is selling proximity to a president who has made clemency a political favor with a price tag attached. Marty Obst’s language is revealing: “helping them navigate the landscape and process,” as though this were a bureaucratic maze rather than a power structure built around one man’s preferences.

The firm’s value lies in translation. It tells clients which cases might “appeal to this White House,” which means the question is no longer whether mercy is justified, but whether the applicant fits the political mood of the executive branch. That is not a justice system. It is a referral network for influence.

The Smokescreen of Procedure

The DOJ spokesperson’s pledge that recommendations will be “consistent, unbiased and uphold the rule of law” is the kind of institutional language that survives by ignoring the obvious. A pardon system flooded by political lobbying, while the president has already shown he rewards allies and punishes adversaries, does not become neutral because an anonymous official says so.

Obst’s effort to frame Trump-era prosecutions as unfair, “highly political” overreach is part of the same maneuver. It tries to recast the problem as one of excess and confusion, not deliberate selection. But the source material points in the other direction: the White House and DOJ are being used as instruments of factional power, and the pardon business is built to service that reality.

What This Story Really Shows

Blessinger Legal and its clients are not the scandal. They are the predictable consequence of a presidency that teaches everyone around it to monetize privilege. When mercy becomes a market, corruption no longer has to hide. It just needs a spokesperson.

The deeper pattern is not simply favoritism. It is institutional degradation through selective enforcement, selective forgiveness, and selective outrage. The administration creates the conditions, the consultants monetize them, and the bureaucracy dresses it up as process. That is how power protects itself: by turning abuse into a service economy and calling the bill “the landscape.”
