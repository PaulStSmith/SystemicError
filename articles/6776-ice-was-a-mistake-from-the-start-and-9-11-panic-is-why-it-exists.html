ICE Was a Mistake From the Start — and 9/11 Panic Is Why It Exists

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJanuary 10, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The fatal shooting of Renée Nicole Good in Minneapolis did not expose a flaw in the current form of Immigration and Customs Enforcement. It exposed something far more damning: ICE should never have existed in the first place.

To understand why, we have to rewind not to the Trump era, not even to the early years of ICE’s operational excesses, but to the fevered political moment after September 11, 2001 — when fear, grief, and rage were rapidly converted into permanent expansions of federal power.

What emerged from that moment was not careful governance. It was panic codified into law.

The Birth of Homeland Security: ‘Do Something’ as Statecraft

The Department of Homeland Security was created in 2002 as a direct response to the 9/11 attacks. It was sold to the American public as necessity: a reorganization meant to prevent intelligence failures, improve coordination, and keep the country safe.

In reality, it was a political artifact of crisis.

Rather than allowing existing law enforcement, intelligence, and judicial systems — which already numbered in the dozens at the federal level alone — to adapt and correct failures, Congress opted for spectacle. A massive new super-agency was assembled by stitching together 22 separate entities, many with wildly different cultures, mandates, and accountability structures.

ICE was one of the most visible products of that merger.

ICE Wasn’t About Security — It Was About Control

Immigration enforcement had existed long before DHS. Customs, border patrol, visa enforcement, and deportation processes were already embedded in federal law. What changed after 9/11 was not the need for immigration regulation — it was the framing.

Immigration was reclassified as a national security threat.

That rhetorical shift was catastrophic.

Once immigration enforcement was subsumed under “homeland security,” it inherited a war footing. Enforcement ceased to be civil administration and became quasi-military. Masks, tactical gear, secrecy, and aggressive posture were no longer aberrations — they were cultural signals.

ICE was designed to operate inside the country as an occupying force, not a regulatory body. Its incentives were never aligned with de-escalation or human outcomes. They were aligned with arrests, removals, and visible demonstrations of power.

The Bush Administration’s Legacy of Permanent Emergency

This architecture was not inevitable.

Had the presidency in 2001 been held by Al Gore rather than George W. Bush, it is entirely plausible that DHS — at least in its eventual form — would never have been created. That is not partisan fantasy; it reflects fundamentally different governing instincts.

A Gore administration, grounded in technocratic incrementalism, would almost certainly have pursued reforms within existing agencies: tighter intelligence-sharing protocols, clearer jurisdictional boundaries, and enhanced oversight — not the creation of a monolithic enforcement behemoth.

More importantly, the administration might not have framed 9/11 as justification for permanent domestic militarization.

History suggests something else, too: the political climate that produced DHS also produced the Iraq War, warrantless surveillance, torture memos, and the normalization of executive secrecy. ICE is not an outlier of that era. It is a sibling.

Panic Becomes Doctrine

The most dangerous thing about DHS was not its size — it was its logic.

DHS institutionalized the idea that extraordinary powers granted in response to an emergency should never fully recede. Temporary fear became permanent doctrine. Oversight became optional. Accountability became conditional.

ICE absorbed that logic whole.

Federal agents operating under ICE are routinely insulated from local authority. They override state and municipal policies, operate in secrecy, and face little consequence even when violence occurs. This is not accidental. It is the direct result of an agency built to answer upward to abstract national threats rather than outward to the communities it polices.

The Minneapolis shooting fits squarely inside that framework.

Why Reform Is a Lie

Calls to “reform ICE” miss the point entirely.

Reform assumes a legitimate core that can be corrected. ICE has none. Its mandate is coercive, its culture adversarial, and its operational model incompatible with democratic policing. You cannot train an agency out of the logic it was created to embody.

Even when administrations promise restraint, ICE’s internal incentives remain unchanged. Arrest quotas may fluctuate. Rhetoric may soften. But the underlying mission — enforcement through intimidation — persists.

That is why violence recurs regardless of who occupies the White House.

The Cost of Letting Fear Win

America did not become safer by creating ICE. It became harsher. More suspicious. More willing to accept domestic force in the name of abstract threats.

The tragedy in Minneapolis is not an aberration. It is a receipt.

It is what happens when a country allows trauma to rewrite its values and then refuses to revisit those choices once the fear subsides. Two decades later, the emergency has become routine, and the violence has become background noise.

The Only Honest Way Forward

If the United States is serious about preventing future killings like Renée Good’s, it must abandon the comforting fiction that ICE can be fixed.

It cannot.

The agency exists because politicians chose spectacle over governance, force over restraint, and fear over law. It was a mistake born of panic and sustained by inertia.

ICE must be abolished — not as a slogan, but as an act of democratic repair.

Until that happens, every claim of concern, every expression of belated empathy, and every call for patience rings hollow. The structure remains. And so will the violence.
