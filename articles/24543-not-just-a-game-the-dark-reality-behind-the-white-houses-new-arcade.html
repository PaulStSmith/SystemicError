Not just a game: The dark reality behind the White House’s new ‘arcade’

Based on an article by The Conversation

Paulo SantosOctober 8, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

The White House launched an online “arcade” whose games package administration messages as entertainment. AlterNet highlights “Rio Run,” a Snake-style game in which a border guard accumulates a growing trail of arrested migrants, alongside games promoting healthy school lunches and a “Trump savings account.”

Scott DeJong, a Concordia University communication-studies researcher and game designer, argues that these titles are built to advance simple political claims, not to explain policy. Unlike earlier government-funded games intended to teach users how disinformation works, the arcade offers little context, complexity, or invitation to question its premises.

Power disguised as play

This is official government communication. The White House controls the platform, selects the subjects and decides which people become protagonists, beneficiaries or objects inside the game. Calling the result an “arcade” does not diminish that institutional power. It decorates it.

“Rio Run” reduces immigration enforcement to an accumulating score mechanic. The player is not asked to consider legal status, due process, family separation or the personhood of those arrested. The government’s coercive authority becomes a gratifying loop: pursue, capture, grow, repeat.

The joke has an author

The article’s discussion of “ambivalence” identifies the political utility of this format. Supporters can receive the message sincerely, while officials and defenders can retreat into humor when challenged. The game communicates a position and simultaneously supplies its own alibi.

That is not public education. It is propaganda through plausible unseriousness: a state institution turns enforcement into amusement, then relies on the medium’s frivolity to make scrutiny look humorless. The ambiguity belongs to the presentation, not to the allocation of power.

Who disappears from the screen

The weakest actors in this story are migrants represented as collectible obstacles. They did not design the game, choose its rules or command the enforcement apparatus it celebrates. Yet the game makes them the source of action while hiding the institutions that determine immigration policy and carry out arrests.

That inversion matters. Political messaging often presents people subjected to state power as the problem and state coercion as the natural response. Here, the abstraction is especially efficient: erase individual lives, replace them with tokens and reward the player for producing an ever-longer line of captives.

A squandered public function

DeJong points to games such as “Cat Park,” “Harmony Square” and his own project, “The Feed,” as evidence that interactive design can expose manipulation and encourage reflection. The White House arcade reportedly chooses the opposite model: fixed conclusions embedded in repetitive actions.

The failure is therefore not that games are political. Politics enters every design through rules, incentives and omissions. The failure is that an institution with unmatched access to policy expertise uses those tools to flatten public questions into partisan cues. Complexity is available; the government chose caricature.

The governing pattern

The larger pattern is the conversion of official communication into meme warfare. Policy is stripped of history and consequence, state violence becomes content, and criticism is neutralized with “it’s just a joke.” The public is treated less as a citizenry capable of judgment than as an audience to be conditioned through repetition.

An arcade on a government website may look trivial. Its political function is not. It shows an executive institution using public authority to normalize a worldview while concealing that exercise of power beneath the visual language of play.
