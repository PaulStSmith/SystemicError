'Replace with what?’ CNN host corners MAGA rep Mike Lawler in clash over GOP's new pledge

CNN’s Brianna Keilar got into a heated exchange with Rep. Mike Lawler (R-NY), who is one of the few Republicans in Congress who has said he is open to a one-year extension of Affordable Care Act subsidies, but just as a temporary measure to allow time for Congress to create longer-term reform for health insurance.

When pressed, however, Lawler dodged what those longer-term reforms might look like, asserting the employer health insurance sector should be strengthened, and repeating a number of longtime GOP talking points like selling insurance “across state lines” — which has already been tried and failed.

“When you talk about small businesses and wanting employers to provide health coverage ... I hear your ideas, but, I mean, I’ve been covering — I covered the passage of Obamacare as a congressional correspondent, which sort of dates me,” said Keilar. “But these ideas, you know, a lot of them, they aren’t new. Congress has had a lot of time to tackle these. Republicans certainly have, this idea of repeal and replace. Replace with what? Speaker Johnson told CNN’s Kaitlan Collins a couple of weeks ago that Republicans have proposals ready to address health care subsidies. Have you seen anything of substance that is actually ready to go, rather than just kind of throwing out ideas? Because that’s really tough to make into action at this point in time.”

“Well, again, I was walking through a series of bills that we can actually bring forth immediately, and I — and I would support,” said Lawler. “I’m not advocating—”

“But those proposals — he’s talking about proposals,” said Keilar. “There’s a difference between bills—”

“Hold on, excuse me, excuse me,” Lawler cut in.

“No, excuse me, because I’ve let you talk a lot,” shot back Keilar. “So I want to ask you this. He’s talking about proposals that are ready to address subsidies. And this idea of proposals, is there anything like on paper that is, you know, ready to go, not just ideas of bills that could be introduced? I mean, is there, is there stuff that’s ready to go out the gate?”

“Well, again, we have multiple committees that deal with health, health care and health insurance, Energy and Commerce, and Ways and Means—” Lawler began.

“And how far away are they from a markup?” asked Keilar.

Lawler again deflected, saying health care is an “extremely complex” issue and that he acknowledges the GOP’s old mantra of “repeal and replace” would do “more harm than good” at this point. He suggested further “regulation” of insurers, including possibly a provision to ban insurers from also owning health care providers.

Our Analysis:

Analysis: The Dance of Avoidance in Political Discourse

In a recent heated exchange on CNN, Brianna Keilar’s interaction with Rep. Mike Lawler (R-NY) serves as a prime example of the frustrating dance of avoidance often witnessed in political discourse. Lawler, who has positioned himself as somewhat of a moderate by being open to a one-year extension of Affordable Care Act (ACA) subsidies, still falls into the familiar trap of vague promises and recycled talking points when pressed for concrete long-term solutions.

The Mirage of GOP Health Care Reform

Lawler’s proposition to strengthen the employer health insurance sector and his advocacy for selling insurance “across state lines” are as stale as last week’s bread. These ideas, trotted out as revolutionary by some GOP members, are neither innovative nor effective solutions to the complex issue of health care in America. The concept of selling insurance across state lines, in particular, has been tested and found wanting, failing to address the root problems of accessibility and affordability in health care.

The Eternal “Replace” in “Repeal and Replace”

Keilar rightly points out the elephant in the room: the GOP’s long-standing battle cry of “repeal and replace” Obamacare, which has yet to materialize into a coherent, actionable plan. The question “Replace with what?” hangs in the air, unanswered. Rep. Lawler’s hesitance to detail those long-term reforms further underscores the lack of substance behind the slogan. Despite claims from Speaker Johnson about having proposals ready to go, the reality appears to be a collection of half-baked ideas rather than a solid blueprint for health care reform.

The Complexity Excuse

Lawler’s admission that health care is an “extremely complex” issue and that outright repeal and replace would do “more harm than good” at this point is a rare moment of clarity. However, it also serves as a convenient shield against accountability. By acknowledging complexity, Lawler deflects from the fact that, despite years of criticism of the ACA, the GOP has not coalesced around a viable alternative that addresses the intricacies of the health care system.

The Regulatory Red Herring

Suggesting further regulation of insurers, including a possible provision to ban insurers from also owning health care providers, might sound like a substantive proposal. Yet, without the context of a broader, cohesive health care strategy, it feels like another red herring. The focus on this single aspect does little to reassure those concerned about the overall direction of GOP health care policy.

Conclusion: The Accountability Gap

What this exchange ultimately highlights is the accountability gap within the GOP’s approach to health care reform. The party’s inability or unwillingness to present a detailed, viable alternative to the ACA is not just a failure of policy but a failure of responsibility to the American people. As the debate over health care continues, one must wonder when, if ever, the GOP will move beyond the rhetoric of “repeal and replace” to offer real solutions that address the needs of the nation.
