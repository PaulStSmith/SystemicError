The day fear lost —and what Trump never saw coming

Based on an article by Joe Conason

Paulo SantosNovember 15, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The candidates cast as threats became officeholdersThe machinery of resentment met its limitA defeat, not an extinction

AlterNet’s account of the 2026 midterms presents the Democratic sweep as more than a partisan defeat for Donald Trump and congressional Republicans. The voters targeted by MAGA campaigns built around racial resentment, misogyny, nativism and anti-trans panic repeatedly refused the assigned role. The fear did not disappear. It was funded, broadcast and placed on ballots—and, in the contests AlterNet describes, it lost.

What Trump and his movement failed to anticipate was not merely Democratic turnout. It was a transfer of political power toward precisely the people MAGA propaganda had marked as suspect, inferior or dangerous: women, racial minorities, LGBTQ candidates, scientists and union workers. The attempted backlash became the setting for a rejection of the social hierarchy it was designed to protect.

The candidates cast as threats became officeholders

The clearest rebuke came from races in which identity itself had been turned into a Republican campaign weapon.

In Alabama, a Democratic software engineer became the first transgender woman elected to the state legislature, according to AlterNet. She flipped a state House seat despite a campaign that attacked her over her gender. Arizona elected its first Native American woman to statewide office. Nevada and Wisconsin each chose their first Black governor.

Georgia’s results concentrated several historic breakthroughs in one state. Voters elected their first Black woman governor, reelected their first Jewish senator and sent Emory University microbiologist Jasmine Clark to Congress. AlterNet identifies Clark as the first Black woman with a science doctorate elected to the House.

These victories matter because the candidates did not merely survive abstract “polarization.” They prevailed against a political project that made exclusion central to its appeal. Describing that result only as blue defeating red would erase the choice Republican strategists made: to spend heavily on messages intended to turn difference into danger.

The machinery of resentment met its limit

MAGA politics has long treated fear as a renewable electoral resource. Trump’s rise fused white grievance, hostility toward immigrants, contempt for women and theatrical religiosity into a coalition organized as much around cultural domination as conventional policy.

That strategy depends on political misdirection. Economic and institutional power remains concentrated among officeholders, donors and party organizations, but campaign anger is pushed downward—toward immigrants, transgender people, racial minorities and anyone else who can be portrayed as an intruder. Weaker groups become the alleged source of social disorder while the people writing laws and financing propaganda escape scrutiny.

AlterNet reports that Republicans poured millions of dollars into openly hateful advertising during the midterms. Yet the messages proved less effective than anticipated, including among white and male voters whom such campaigns typically sought to mobilize. That does not mean prejudice vanished. It means the Republican apparatus failed, in these races, to convert prejudice into enough votes to preserve power.

The distinction matters. Fear did not simply “lose” in some spontaneous national awakening. Candidates, organizers and voters defeated campaigns whose sponsors had money, party infrastructure and access to the presidency’s political megaphone.

A defeat, not an extinction

Roger Stone, a longtime Trump adviser, once summarized his political method with the claim that “hate is a stronger motivator than love.” The midterm results described by AlterNet exposed the limit of that proposition. Hate can command attention and enforce loyalty, but it is not invincible—and repeating it more loudly does not guarantee a majority.

Nor does one election permanently dismantle the movement that deployed it. Republican leaders retain institutions, donors and an experienced propaganda network. A future presidential campaign led by someone such as JD Vance could again try to assemble power from the resentments Trump exploited.

But the 2026 results deny MAGA one of its most useful claims: that its cultural cruelty represents an irresistible popular will. In race after race, voters handed authority to the people its campaigns told them to fear. The propaganda named the targets. The electorate gave them power.
