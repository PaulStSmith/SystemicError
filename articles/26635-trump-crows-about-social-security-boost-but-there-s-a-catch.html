Trump crows about Social Security boost —but there's a catch

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 15, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the Trump administration presented a 3.6 percent Social Security cost-of-living adjustment for 76 million Americans as an improvement in financial stability. The catch is that the increase reflects higher prices rather than a discretionary benefit expansion: prices were reportedly 3.7 percent above their year-earlier level.

Former Social Security Commissioner Martin O’Malley told AlterNet that the adjustment would not preserve retirees’ purchasing power, citing food costs and Medicare copays. He also argued that Social Security’s longer-term financing problem could be eased by applying the payroll tax more fully to high earners.

An inflation adjustment is not a gift

The administration did not create a 3.6 percent windfall. According to O’Malley’s account, the increase follows an inflation-linked formula and arrives after the costs it is meant to offset have already risen.

Calling that adjustment a “boost” converts partial compensation for lost purchasing power into presidential generosity. A benefit can rise in nominal dollars while recipients become less able to afford necessities. The administration’s statement foregrounds the larger check; AlterNet’s figures indicate that prices rose slightly faster.

Who holds the power

Social Security recipients do not control the inflation measure, the timing of the adjustment, Medicare charges, or the amount of earnings subject to Social Security taxes. Federal officials and lawmakers control those policy choices. Retirees absorb their consequences.

Commissioner Frank Bisignano’s statement supplied the administration’s favorable framing. But the article does not quote Trump personally, despite a headline attributing the boasting to him. That is imprecise political packaging: the reporting establishes an administration claim, not a presidential remark.

The convenient missing context

The central misdirection is the treatment of an automatic response to rising costs as an affirmative achievement. It asks readers to credit the officeholder for the adjustment without dwelling on whether the adjustment restores what inflation took.

O’Malley, meanwhile, blamed part of the recent pressure on prices on the “Iran War.” AlterNet presents that causal claim without evidence in the supplied report. It should remain attributed analysis, not established fact. His broader point is better grounded by the article’s own numbers: a 3.6 percent adjustment does not fully match a reported 3.7 percent annual price increase.

Solvency is a distributional choice

O’Malley argues that affluent earners are resisting higher Social Security taxes and that taxing earnings above the reported $184,500 threshold more fully could extend the program’s solvency. He predicts broad support for requiring the wealthiest Americans to pay the same rate as other workers, though the article provides no polling to establish that prediction.

The political conflict is therefore not simply whether Social Security can be “saved.” It is who will bear the cost: beneficiaries through weaker purchasing power and possible benefit restructuring, or high earners through a broader taxable earnings base. Describing that conflict as a technical solvency puzzle obscures the actors with authority to choose.

The larger pattern

This is a familiar governing maneuver: relabel damage control as progress, advertise the nominal number, and leave the diminished purchasing power in the fine print. The weaker actors—elderly and disabled beneficiaries—are then expected to treat incomplete protection from rising costs as benevolence.

The systemic error is not confusion about arithmetic. It is political communication that detaches a benefit increase from the price increases that caused it, while the more consequential distributional decision—whether wealthy earners should contribute more—remains unresolved.
