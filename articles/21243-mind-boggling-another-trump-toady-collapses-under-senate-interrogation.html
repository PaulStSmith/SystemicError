'Mind boggling': Another Trump toady collapses under Senate interrogation

Trump’s Intelligence Pick Pretended Not to Know the Crime

The Real Hearing

Cable news was busy with Todd Blanche, but the more revealing spectacle was Jay Clayton’s hearing for director of national intelligence. The nominee for one of the most sensitive posts in the federal government could not give a clean answer about Tulsi Gabbard’s presence in Fulton County, Georgia, when the FBI raided the county elections office. That is not trivia. It goes to the core of whether Trump’s intelligence apparatus is being used to surveil, pressure, or interfere with election administration.

Power Is the Point

The institutional power here sits with Trump, who chose the nominee, and with the Senate, which is supposed to test whether that nominee understands the limits of the office. Clayton’s evasions matter because the DNI is not a ceremonial job. It is part of the machinery that can either restrain or normalize presidential abuse. When the president has already ordered a predecessor to witness a raid tied to election records, the question is not whether the nominee is “comfortable” discussing it. The question is whether he grasps that the presidency does not own elections.

Evasion as Strategy

Clayton did not just stumble. He hedged, shifted, and repeatedly tried to retreat into vagueness until the exchange became farcical. That is a political technique, not a misunderstanding. Pretending not to remember a major episode involving an FBI raid in Fulton County is one way to launder a dangerous precedent into bland confusion. If the nominee cannot plainly acknowledge what happened, he is already practicing the habits that make abuse easier: delay, blur, deny, reframe.

The Senate’s Weak Spine

Jon Ossoff did the basic job here. He asked for a yes-or-no answer. That should not be treated as heroism. It should be the minimum standard for confirmation of someone asking to oversee intelligence. The more disturbing fact is that this still felt notable. The Senate routinely allows nominees to skate past direct accountability, then acts surprised when executive power comes armed with loyalists who “just learned” what everyone else already knows. That is how oversight becomes theater.

Misdirection by Design

The source’s own framing shows the problem. The attention economy was diverted to a different confirmation fight while a more consequential hearing unfolded in the background. That is how institutional decay gets managed: move the spotlight, blur the stakes, and let the public argue about the noisier piece of the day. Meanwhile the real question remains untouched: why is a Trump nominee for intelligence unable, or unwilling, to confront a story that speaks directly to election interference and constitutional boundaries?

The Pattern

This is not about one sweaty nominee failing a clip-friendly exchange. It is about a governing style that treats democratic guardrails as inconveniences and installs people who speak in fog when clarity would be dangerous to the project. The lesson is simple: when power moves toward election interference, the first casualty is candor. The second is institutions that are supposed to say no.
