Defeated Trump aides admit they've lost all control: 'Why bother?'

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

The Republican Party Has Chosen Obedience Over Government

“He’s the Boss” Is the Whole Ideology

The reported facts are straightforward: Donald Trump’s advisers and Republican allies increasingly believe his judgment is deteriorating, yet they have stopped trying to restrain him. They cite an Iran strike without a clear endgame, provocations aimed at Canada’s prime minister, an unwanted midterm convention, unpopular campaign messaging, and a grandiose White House renovation. Their explanation is even simpler: Trump wants these things, and Republicans comply.

This is not confusion inside the administration. It is a decision about power.

The Enablers Still Have Agency

Trump holds the presidency and dominates his party, but he does not govern alone. Senior aides execute orders. Elected Republicans provide legislative support and political cover. Donors finance the apparatus. Candidates repeat its messages. Advisers normalize each demand by presenting capitulation as realism.

“They know they can’t stop him” is an alibi, not an institutional fact. Members of Congress can oppose a president. Advisers can resign. Donors can withhold money. Candidates can refuse to attend spectacles designed around his ego. These people have options; they have calculated that resistance carries greater personal cost than obedience.

Their exhaustion may be genuine. It is also politically irrelevant. Being tired of enabling reckless conduct does not make the enabling involuntary.

Cowardice Disguised as Inevitability

The article’s weakest framing is its emphasis on aides being “worn down.” That language shifts attention from deliberate submission to emotional fatigue. It turns powerful adults into battered spectators of decisions they help implement.

The more revealing admission comes from the adviser who says, “He’s the boss.” That is the operating doctrine: formal authority is treated as entitlement to unconditional compliance. Even insiders who no longer trust Trump’s instincts decline to use their own power against him.

This is institutional cowardice presented as workplace malaise.

Recklessness Abroad, Spectacle at Home

An attack on Iran without a defined endgame is not merely another example of Trump “doing it his way.” It places military force under the same personalist logic governing social-media taunts, campaign staging, and architectural vanity. The consequences differ enormously, but the decision structure is consistent: presidential desire comes first; strategy, coalition interests, and public consequences follow if they are considered at all.

The ballroom renovation matters for the same reason. Critics’ comparison to Augustus may be rhetorical, but the political impulse is visible without the classical reference. Trump is reshaping public institutions around his personal image while subordinates explain that what he wants, he gets. The spectacle is not incidental to the power. It advertises the power.

The Party Is Protecting Its Future Submission

Republicans reportedly fear a difficult midterm election. Vulnerable candidates would prefer distance from an unpopular president, yet the party is building advertising and a convention around him anyway. That choice exposes the hierarchy: protecting individual seats matters less than avoiding Trump’s retaliation and preserving standing within the movement he controls.

The expectation that he will remain dominant after leaving office deepens the submission. Republicans are not merely obeying a sitting president. They are organizing themselves around his anticipated post-presidential authority. The lame-duck calendar may limit his constitutional tenure, but it does not dissolve a patronage system sustained by fear, ambition, and a base conditioned to treat personal loyalty as political principle.

Personal Rule Requires Many Volunteers

The systemic error is the comforting fiction that one domineering leader overwhelms everyone around him. Personalist power is more collaborative than that. It depends on legislators who will not confront it, advisers who translate impulse into policy, donors who keep paying, and candidates who accept humiliation as the price of advancement.

Trump is responsible for his decisions. The Republican officials enabling them are responsible for theirs. Their private doubts do not mitigate their public obedience; they make it more damning. They understand the risks, possess institutional power, and choose submission anyway.
