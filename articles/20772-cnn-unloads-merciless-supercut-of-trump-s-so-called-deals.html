CNN unloads merciless supercut of Trump's so-called 'deals'

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosJuly 14, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump’s Fake Deal, Real Damage

The Performance

CNN aired a supercut of Donald Trump repeating, over and over, that Iran “wants to make a deal.” The clips span months and show the same script on loop: imminent breakthrough, easy victory, total confidence. The reporting’s own premise is the point. Trump is not describing diplomacy. He is manufacturing the illusion of control after launching a war and then pretending the target is begging for mercy.

Who Actually Holds Power

The institutional power in this story sits with Trump and the Republican apparatus that keeps absorbing his claims as if they were governance. He is the one making the announcements, claiming secret calls, and setting the terms of public reality. The source notes that Iran has accepted more U.S. air strikes and that the Strait of Hormuz has been closed, which means the consequences are not rhetorical. They are material, geopolitical, and costly. This is executive power used as stagecraft.

The Lie Inside “Negotiation”

Trump’s language is not just puffery. It is a tactic. He repeats that Iran wants a deal “so badly” because he needs a narrative in which escalation becomes leverage and failure becomes proof of pressure working. But the source undercuts him completely: “There’s no deal,” Jonah Goldberg says, and even the memorandum of understanding was only a placeholder for a deal that was never going to hold. That is the actual structure here. Not diplomacy, but the performance of diplomacy by an administration that wants the optics of success without the burden of settlement.

Misdirection as Method

The article’s framing still soft-pedals the central political fact. It lingers on Kasie Hunt’s reaction, Goldberg’s commentary, and the embarrassment of the supercut. That is useful television, but it also risks turning deliberate state damage into a personality story. The public is not being misled by confusion. It is being managed through repetition. Trump says the same thing again and again until the lie sounds like background noise, and the press packages the contradiction as a clever segment instead of the consequence of a president who started the war.

Who Pays

The source says Trump is facing mounting rage from voters, legislators, and the international community because of a war he started and because energy prices are rising in the U.S. and globally. That is the real ledger. Ordinary people absorb the cost through fuel prices, instability, and political churn. The Republican Party absorbs the risk because it attached itself to a president who treats foreign policy like a branding exercise. The damage is not abstract. It is being paid for in public, and the bill keeps moving downward.

The Larger Pattern

This is the same authoritarian habit in miniature: declare victory, deny responsibility, and force everyone else to litigate the falsehood. Trump does not need a real deal if he can keep saying one is coming. He does not need accountable governance if he can keep producing enough noise to drown out the failure. The political lesson is blunt. When a government is run by a man who confuses assertion with authority, the lies are not decorative. They are operational.
