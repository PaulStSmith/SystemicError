Oath Broken: Venezuela, Regime Change, and the Corruption of Civil‑Military Power

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJanuary 3, 20265 min read

The Headline Says the Quiet Part Out Loud

When a U.S. president publicly declares that America will “run” another country after the capture of its leader, ambiguity evaporates. This is no longer a debate about “limited strikes,” “pressure campaigns,” or “counter‑narcotics operations.” It is an open admission of regime change — announced without shame, without lawful authority, and without the consent of the American people through their representatives.

You do not need to read past the headline to understand the gravity of the moment. The words alone place the United States on the wrong side of its own Constitution and squarely against the post‑war international order it once helped build.

This is not merely an illegal war. It is an attempted military coup in a foreign country, executed by executive fiat.

Congress Did Not Authorize This — Not Even Close

Congress did not declare war on Venezuela. Congress did not authorize the use of military force. Congress did not approve a mission to remove a foreign head of state, let alone to administer another country afterward.

Those facts are not disputed. They are conspicuous.

Even the most permissive reading of presidential war powers collapses here. There is no plausible self‑defense claim. Venezuela did not attack the United States. There is no imminent threat that could justify emergency action. And there is certainly no constitutional basis for occupying or “running” a sovereign nation absent explicit legislative authorization.

This is not pushing the envelope. This is ripping it up and setting it on fire.

Regime Change Is the Crime, Not the Consequence

For decades, U.S. officials have tried to pretend that regime change is something that just “happens” after military pressure. That fiction no longer holds.

If the president’s own words are taken seriously — and they must be — then regime change was not an unintended outcome. It was the objective.

Under both U.S. and international law, that matters.

Toppling a foreign government is not a policing action. It is not humanitarian aid. It is not a counter‑terror operation. It is an act of war, and one of the most destabilizing kinds. The U.N. Charter exists precisely to prevent powerful nations from deciding who governs weaker ones.

To announce that the United States will “run” Venezuela is to declare colonial administration in everything but name.

The President Is Not the Only Guilty Party

Fixating solely on Donald Trump lets others off the hook — and they do not deserve absolution.

Presidents do not plan invasions. They do not select targets. They do not move fleets, scramble aircraft, or draft operational orders. Those tasks are performed by civilian defense officials and uniformed military leadership.

Every Secretary of Defense, every four‑star general, every admiral who enabled this operation knew — or should have known — that it lacked legal authorization. They understood the absence of a declaration of war. They were aware that Congress had not approved regime change. They recognized the international law violations.

They proceeded anyway.

That is not obedience. It is complicity.

The Oath Was to the Constitution, Not the Commander

Members of the U.S. military swear an oath to defend the Constitution against all enemies, foreign and domestic. That oath is not symbolic. It is a binding moral and legal commitment.

When unlawful orders are issued, the oath does not dissolve. It becomes more urgent.

History offers no comfort to those who claim they were “just following orders.” That excuse has failed at Nuremberg, in My Lai, and in every serious accounting of state violence. An illegal order does not become legal through repetition or rank.

The refusal to draw a line here is not professionalism. It is cowardice.

Cowardice with Excellent Benefits

There is an uncomfortable pattern that repeats itself with numbing consistency. Senior military leaders speak eloquently about norms and restraint — right up until the moment restraint threatens their careers.

Then the briefings get vague. The legal opinions get creative. And the retirement plans remain intact.

The modern military‑industrial complex rewards compliance, not courage. Generals who keep quiet are welcomed into boardrooms, consulting firms, and defense contractors. Admirals who play along find themselves with seven‑figure compensation packages after retirement.

The Constitution, meanwhile, gets treated like a ceremonial document — saluted, not defended.

The Revolving Door Is the Root of the Rot

It should be illegal for senior military officers, defense officials, and legislators to transition directly into private‑sector roles that benefit from their insider knowledge, personal networks, or influence over government contracts.

This is not radical. It is basic anti‑corruption policy.

When decision‑makers know their future income depends on staying in the good graces of defense contractors, energy conglomerates, or lobbying firms, national security becomes a résumé‑building exercise.

Wars become career accelerators. Silence becomes strategy.

A republic cannot survive under those conditions.

Accountability Is Not Vengeance

Calling for accountability is not extremism. It is governance.

If laws exist only to restrain the powerless, then they are not laws at all — they are theater. A president who orders an illegal war must face consequences. So must the civilian and military officials who execute it.

Trials are not destabilizing. Impunity is.

The idea that holding leaders accountable would “damage morale” or “undermine institutions” is precisely backwards. Institutions rot when they are shielded from consequences, not when they are forced to live up to their stated principles.

This Is a Domestic Threat, Not Just a Foreign One

An executive branch that can unilaterally overthrow foreign governments can just as easily ignore domestic constraints. The logic is identical: power first, law later.

That is why the oath matters. That is why congressional authority matters. That is why silence from military leadership is so dangerous.

The enemy here is not only foreign. It is the normalization of lawlessness at the highest levels of power.

History Is Taking Notes

Future historians will not struggle to name this moment. The record will be clear: a president openly announced foreign domination without legal authority, and the institutions designed to stop him hesitated, equivocated, or complied.

Some will claim they had no choice. Some will cite precedent. Some will retreat into silence.

None of that will matter.

The Republic Is Not Self‑Enforcing

Constitutions do not defend themselves. Oaths do not enforce themselves. Laws do not punish themselves.

They require people — flawed, self‑interested, human people — to decide that the line matters more than their comfort.

In Venezuela, the line was crossed. In Washington, it was ignored.

The question now is not whether the order was illegal. It was.

The question is whether anyone still remembers what their oath actually meant.
