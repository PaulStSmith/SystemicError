‘Deeply uncomfortable’: National Guard troops reveal hidden toll of Trump’s deployments

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 18, 20255 min read

Members of the National Guard walk near the White House on the National Mall after U.S. President Donald Trump deployed the National Guard and ordered an increased presence of federal law enforcement to assist in crime prevention, in Washington, D.C., U.S., August 21, 2025. REUTERS/Al Drago

Ever since members of the National Guard have been deployed for President Donald Trump’s domestic law enforcement priorities around the country, they have faced “deeply uncomfortable” positions of explaining it to their family, friends, and coworkers, Slate reported on Monday.

This comes as, according to Slate, guardsmen have been “asking questions” more broadly about how they are being used and why.

The missions have so far taken place in Los Angeles, Washington, D.C., and Memphis, and litigation is ongoing to block Trump’s efforts to deploy similar operations to Chicago and Portland, Oregon. These missions are broadly voluntary, one person said in the interview captured by Slate, but many are afraid if they take the plunge, there’s no going back.

“It’s a legitimate thing that they’re thinking through, and the voluntary directive has come to their unit. They just haven’t taken it,” said National Public Radio’s Kat Lonsdorf. “But those deployments are voluntary until they’re not. If they don’t get enough people to voluntarily sign up to take one of these deployments, then they will start compelling people to take them.”

Those who go through with it, she added, have to face judgment of their peers back home — and many have opened up about their own stories.

“One of them told me that he doesn’t talk about his service anymore,” said Lonsdorf. “He basically, like, if he meets someone new, he will not share that he is part of the National Guard. He will not share that he’s part of the military. He feels deeply uncomfortable about it. Another one told me that in her civilian job, her coworkers were acting really differently toward her because they knew about her National Guard service and status, and they figured she must agree with what’s going on. And so they were kind of tiptoeing around her until she kind of confronted them a little bit and said, like, hey, I’m a singular person, and I’ll talk about it with you if you want.”

In a recent Supreme Court filing, Trump administration officials claimed they had the power to send “active duty” military rather than the National Guard to keep order in American cities — a seemingly blatant violation of the Posse Comitatus Act — and simply chose not to do so.

Our Analysis:

The National Guard’s Domestic Deployment: A Closer Look

In a recent article by Slate, the spotlight is cast on a deeply troubling development: members of the National Guard grappling with their role in President Donald Trump’s domestic law enforcement priorities. This isn’t just a simple assignment; it’s a profound ethical and moral quandary that places these individuals in the crosshairs of public scrutiny and personal doubt.

The Uncomfortable Position of Guardsmen

It’s distressingly ironic that those sworn to protect are now finding themselves in need of defense — not from external threats, but from the judgment and misunderstanding of their own communities. The article details how these guardsmen are becoming pariahs in their own right, silencing their service to avoid confrontation or judgment. Imagine the psychological toll this takes, serving your country only to feel you must hide that very service like a dark secret.

Voluntary Until It’s Not

The notion that these missions are “voluntary” is a thinly veiled facade. As the article points out, there’s an underlying threat: volunteer, or eventually, you will be compelled. This false choice is a manipulation, leveraging patriotism against personal autonomy. The guardsmen are trapped in a Catch-22, where the choice is an illusion, and the consequences, either way, are dire.

The Legal Quandary

Perhaps the most jarring revelation is the Trump administration’s claim of having the power to deploy active duty military to enforce domestic law, a clear affront to the Posse Comitatus Act. This act, for those unfamiliar, restricts the use of the U.S. military for domestic law enforcement. The administration’s brazen willingness to flirt with this legal boundary underscores a disturbing trend of testing the limits of executive power, regardless of legal or ethical considerations.

The Ethical Implications

The deployment of the National Guard in this manner raises profound ethical questions. What does it mean for a society when its protectors are used not just as shields but as swords against its own people? This isn’t merely about law enforcement; it’s about the erosion of civil liberties and the militarization of public spaces. The guardsmen, caught in the middle, are the human face of this policy — a policy that compels them to act against the very principles they swore to uphold.

Conclusion: A Reflection on Duty and Democracy

This article serves as a critical reminder of the complexities and consequences of using military forces in domestic roles. It’s a testament to the guardsmen’s integrity that they question and feel discomfort with these assignments. Yet, it’s a damning indictment of a policy that puts them in such a position. As we reflect on this situation, we must ask ourselves: What kind of society do we want to be? One that values civil liberties and democratic principles, or one that is willing to compromise these values for the illusion of security?

In the end, the use of the National Guard in this capacity isn’t just a systemic error; it’s a moral failure — a failure that we must confront with open eyes and a steadfast commitment to the principles of democracy and justice.
