'Everybody's funeral': Trump biographer smells panic in the White House

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 31, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower before panicAgency does not disappear downwardPrediction is not proofAccountability or partisan theaterThe system protects the apex

What happened

Michael Wolff told his podcast that a Republican defeat in the approaching midterm elections could trigger panic inside President Donald Trump’s White House. As reported by AlterNet via the Daily Beast, Wolff predicted that aides would face congressional investigations, legal bills, career damage, and blame from Trump—making the aftermath “everybody’s funeral.”

Wolff said Democrats gaining congressional power would probably pursue officials around Trump rather than prioritize another impeachment that Senate Republicans could block. He identified Attorney General Todd Blanche as a likely target and alleged that Blanche had enabled legally futile actions against people Trump resented. Co-host Joanna Coles also pointed to announced Democratic plans involving Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth.

These were predictions made before the reported election, not evidence that subpoenas had been issued, investigations completed, or crimes established. AlterNet offered no independent reporting showing that White House officials were actually searching for lawyers; its central evidence was Wolff’s commentary.

Power before panic

The story’s imagery reverses the real hierarchy. Trump’s aides are not merely anxious employees trapped in someone else’s storm. They occupy the executive branch, direct federal departments, and can use public authority against designated targets. Their possible exposure matters precisely because they currently hold extraordinary institutional power.

Trump sits at the top of that structure. Congress, however, controls its own formidable machinery: committees, hearings, subpoenas, and impeachment proceedings. The election matters because it could transfer investigative power—not because accountability suddenly materializes from partisan emotion.

Agency does not disappear downward

Wolff portrays Trump as the expected source of blame and Blanche as an enabler who acts because Trump instructs him. That distinction identifies command, but it does not erase subordinate agency. An appointed official who carries out a president’s demands still makes decisions, exercises discretion, and attaches government power to those choices.

The article’s “henchmen” language gestures toward responsibility while its panic narrative risks recasting powerful officials as impending victims. Losing insulation from scrutiny is not the same as being powerless. Fear of subpoenas is a foreseeable consequence of governing in ways that attract legitimate questions.

Prediction is not proof

The weakest part of the framing is its reliance on Wolff’s certainty. He alleges corruption, anticipates legal jeopardy, and predicts Democratic retaliation, but the article supplies no underlying records, court findings, or identified conduct sufficient to establish criminal wrongdoing. “Smells panic” is a mood presented as news.

That does not make congressional scrutiny improper. It means the case for scrutiny must rest on documented official acts, not a biographer’s metaphor or an opponent’s appetite. Otherwise, the coverage mimics the politics it claims to diagnose: personalities replace evidence, and anticipated spectacle replaces institutional analysis.

Accountability or partisan theater

Calling prospective investigations “the wrath of the Democrats” misdirects attention from the actual standard. The central question is not whether Democrats are angry. It is whether executive officials misused public authority and whether Congress can establish that through evidence, testimony, and lawful process.

That framing also gives Trump’s circle a ready-made defense. Officials can describe oversight as vengeance while avoiding the substance of what they authorized. Democrats would reinforce that defense if they began with promised targets and impeachment rhetoric instead of specific conduct and a disciplined evidentiary record.

The system protects the apex

The larger pattern is familiar: concentrated presidential power flows downward, while accountability struggles to travel upward. Wolff expects Trump to blame aides, Senate Republicans to shield him from removal, and congressional investigators to pursue more vulnerable subordinates. Each institution follows its incentives, leaving the central holder of power comparatively insulated.

That is the systemic error. Government officials can spend years exercising authority through a leader-centered chain of command, then become expendable when political control shifts. Coverage turns the resulting scramble into palace drama. The more consequential story is how institutions permit power to be personalized at the top and responsibility to be scattered beneath it.
