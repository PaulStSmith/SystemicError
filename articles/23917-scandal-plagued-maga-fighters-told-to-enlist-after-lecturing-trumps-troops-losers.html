Scandal-plagued MAGA 'fighters' told to enlist after lecturing Trump’s troops: 'Losers'

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

The Pentagon’s Influencer War Machine

Spectacle at the Center of Power

The Pentagon released a video of Jake and Logan Paul praising service members for their willingness to “live, die and fight” shortly after Donald Trump threatened to destroy Iran and amid reports that a ground invasion remained possible. The clip turned two wealthy internet celebrities into motivational props for people who may be ordered to bear the consequences of that policy.

The Paul Brothers Are Props, Not Principals

The backlash focused heavily on whether the Paul brothers should enlist. That response is understandable, but politically incomplete. Jake and Logan Paul did not command the Pentagon, publish its video, threaten Iran, or decide whether American troops would enter another war.

The institutional power belongs to the administration and the Defense Department. Officials chose to stage this visit, record it, and distribute it. Turning the episode into a referendum on influencer cowardice lets the officials operating the military’s publicity apparatus slip quietly out of frame.

The Paul brothers deserve contempt for selling sacrifice they have not undertaken. But they are the product being displayed. The Pentagon is the vendor.

Sacrifice for Them, Prosperity for Us

Logan Paul told service members that their willingness to fight allows Americans to “prosper” and be happy. The division of labor could hardly be clearer: troops accept death; celebrities enjoy prosperity; political leaders decide when the first group must protect the comfort of the second.

That rhetoric launders military obedience into a national gift rather than an institutional arrangement. It treats possible death as a noble personal choice while obscuring who selects the battlefield, defines the mission, and decides whether the mission is necessary at all.

The message is especially grotesque when delivered by men whose fame and wealth came through entertainment rather than public service. Their presence converts unequal exposure to violence into content.

Machismo as Military Public Relations

The choice of Jake and Logan Paul was not politically neutral. Both men have converted online notoriety into combat-sports brands, while facing criticism over the legitimacy of their professional advantages. Their usefulness to the Pentagon lies in the image they sell: aggression without policy, toughness without accountability, and domination as entertainment.

This is the manosphere pressed into government service. The administration does not need these influencers to explain Iran, justify military objectives, or describe likely costs. It needs them to make force look culturally familiar to an audience trained to consume conflict as spectacle.

That is propaganda in its cheapest modern form: not a coherent argument for war, but an aesthetic of strength designed to make argument feel unnecessary.

The Enlistment Joke Misses the Chain of Command

Calls to send the Paul brothers to the “frontline” expose the hypocrisy of civilian celebrities praising risks they do not share. But the joke also preserves a comforting fiction—that the scandal is insufficient personal courage.

It is not. The scandal is that state institutions enlisted celebrity branding to romanticize military sacrifice while the president escalated threats against an entire country. Whether the brothers enlist would not change the hierarchy. They would still not be the officials deciding if thousands of other people must fight.

Mocking the messengers is easy. Naming the decision-makers is more important.

War Packaged as Content

This episode reveals a governing culture in which the Pentagon can be treated as a creator studio and military personnel as scenery for influencer theater. Policy disappears behind masculinity, celebrity and patriotic sentiment. The people with authority become less visible precisely as the imagery of violence becomes more prominent.

That misdirection is politically useful. It encourages the public to argue about whether two performers are “fake tough guys” instead of scrutinizing the officials threatening escalation and the bureaucracy helping market it. The spectacle is ridiculous. The power behind it is not.
