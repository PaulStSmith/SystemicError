'Absolute immunity’ under fire as new plan hatches to hold Trump accountable

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that former deputy U.S. solicitor general Philip Allen Lacovara condemned the Supreme Court’s 2024 presidential-immunity ruling as constitutionally unsound and argued that it gives President Donald Trump “a virtual get-out-of-jail card.” He told New York Times contributor Thomas B. Edsall that the decision’s supposed exceptions for some official acts and all unofficial conduct are largely unusable while the Court’s current majority remains intact.

The report does not identify a new plan to hold Trump accountable. It presents Lacovara’s legal criticism and historian Luke Nichter’s broader argument that presidential policymaking, judicial intervention, and congressional passivity have scrambled the constitutional roles of the three branches. AlterNet also connects Trump’s interest in rehabilitating Richard Nixon to supporters’ portrayal of the Watergate prosecution as partisan “lawfare.”

The headline promises more than the reporting delivers

The headline advertises an emerging accountability mechanism. The article supplies none: no legislation, lawsuit, prosecutorial strategy, constitutional amendment, or pending institutional action. Lacovara describes why accountability may be exceptionally difficult; he does not unveil a way around the ruling.

That distinction matters. Turning a severe institutional barrier into the suggestion that some corrective plan is already “hatching” substitutes suspense for substance. The reporting’s actual conclusion is harsher: the institutions empowered to constrain presidential abuse have either fortified executive immunity or declined to exercise their own authority.

Power sits with the Court and the presidency

Trump holds the executive power at issue, but the Supreme Court’s majority established the legal protection surrounding its use. According to Lacovara, the 2024 ruling created an extraordinarily high barrier to prosecuting a president for official conduct and sharply limited the practical value of its nominal exceptions.

Congress also retains substantial constitutional authority. Nichter’s account depicts it surrendering initiative—holding hearings and talking while the president governs through executive action and courts increasingly determine the permissible boundaries. That is not institutional confusion. It is a distribution of choices: executive expansion, judicial permission and legislative retreat.

Accountability cannot survive as a slogan

Lacovara warns that the ruling may obstruct prosecution even where evidence appears to show presidential favors exchanged for financial benefits. His list of potentially insulated abuses includes corruption, election interference, pardon-selling and federal contracts awarded to donors. These are his legal conclusions, not findings that Trump committed each listed act.

The political consequence is nevertheless concrete. A legal system that recognizes presidential criminal liability in theory while making prosecution nearly impossible in practice preserves the language of accountability after draining it of force. “Absolute immunity” need not be formally declared if the evidentiary and procedural barriers produce nearly the same result.

Nixon’s rehabilitation serves executive power

The effort to recast Nixon’s prosecution as Democratic “lawfare” does more than polish one disgraced president’s reputation. It attacks the legitimacy of the historical precedent showing that presidential power can encounter criminal investigation and forced political accountability.

That revision serves the present. If Watergate becomes persecution rather than evidence of executive abuse, then investigation itself can be portrayed as illegitimate whenever it reaches a president. The stronger office becomes the aggrieved party; prosecutors, courts, legislators, and critics become the alleged aggressors. Power is rewritten as victimhood.

A system can fail through permission

The larger pattern is not simply an ambitious president overwhelming helpless institutions. AlterNet’s own evidence describes institutions with agency: Supreme Court justices made the immunity ruling, presidents choose executive action, and members of Congress choose whether to legislate, investigate effectively, or accept marginalization.

Constitutional breakdown rarely requires every formal safeguard to disappear. It can proceed through doctrines that preserve exceptions too narrow to use, hearings that replace consequences, and headlines that imply remedies the reporting cannot identify. The system still performs accountability rhetorically while its most powerful actors make accountability materially harder.
