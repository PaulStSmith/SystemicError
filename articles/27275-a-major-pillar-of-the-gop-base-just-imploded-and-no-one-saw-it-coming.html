A major pillar of the GOP base just imploded — and no one saw it coming

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 15, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Republican Party’s white evangelical turnout machine faltered in the November midterms, but calling that result an “implosion” outruns the evidence. An American Communities Project analysis reported by The Washington Post found sharply reduced participation and weaker Republican voting across 343 heavily evangelical counties in 15 states with Senate races. That is a serious breach in a coalition the GOP has relied on for decades. It is not yet proof that the coalition has collapsed.

The distinction matters because electoral power is measured in votes delivered repeatedly, not in dramatic post-election metaphors. These counties produced only 66 percent as many votes as they did in the 2024 presidential election. Surrounding counties in the same states reached 78 percent of their 2024 totals. According to ACP founder Dante Chinni, matching neighboring turnout rates could have supplied Republicans with hundreds of thousands of additional votes.

The missing votes came from Republican strongholds

ACP classifies the places it examined as “evangelical hub counties,” meaning they contain unusually high concentrations of people who identify as evangelical, particularly white evangelicals. Nearly all are in the Southeast, stretching from Texas to Virginia.

White evangelicals make up 52 percent of the population in those counties, compared with 13 percent nationwide, according to the Public Religion Research Institute’s 2023 Census of American Religion. These are not marginal constituencies or culturally mixed swing districts. They are communities situated inside one of the Republican Party’s most dependable sources of electoral power.

Their reduced participation therefore carried an asymmetrical cost. Chinni told the Post that enthusiasm in the evangelical hubs was lower than in other places. Because these counties normally vote heavily Republican, every missing voter was more damaging to the GOP than an equivalent turnout decline in politically balanced territory.

Researchers and pollsters were still examining the election results, according to the reporting, so the county analysis cannot by itself establish why participation fell. It does show where the Republican machine failed to produce its customary volume.

Trump’s transaction with evangelical voters is showing strain

Paul Binford, a 69-year-old Kentucky evangelical and regular Republican voter, offered one explanation for withdrawing support. He and his wife concluded that President Donald Trump was preoccupied with “enriching himself” rather than governing effectively.

“We thought he might not sell us out, but he was [the] worst offender of all,” Binford said.

One voter’s account cannot explain hundreds of counties. It does, however, expose the vulnerability in a political arrangement often presented as an alliance of shared conviction. Republican leaders received votes, institutional protection and moral cover from white evangelical communities. In exchange, those voters expected political power to serve their priorities. Binford’s complaint is not that Trump violated a standard imposed by his opponents; it is that he failed the bargain his own supporters believed they had made.

That is more politically consequential than a sudden change in theological doctrine. The available evidence points to demobilization and diminished Republican loyalty, not to a wholesale ideological realignment. Voters can stay home because they feel used without becoming Democrats. A weakened transaction is not the same thing as a transformed electorate.

A history too durable for one election to erase

The modern Christian right was built through institutions, not merely enthusiasm. Jerry Falwell Sr., the Liberty University founder and Moral Majority co-founder, helped turn conservative white evangelical identity into organized Republican power beginning in the late 1970s and 1980s.

That project carried the politics of the segregated South into a new partisan structure. Falwell had attacked Martin Luther King Jr. and defended segregation, declaring that “the true Negro does not want integration.” His later national prominence did not erase that record. The Christian right’s rise also overlapped with the Republican Southern Strategy, through which the party attracted white conservative voters alienated by civil rights and the Democratic Party’s changing direction.

But the supplied results do not establish that “even racism” failed to mobilize evangelical voters this year. That framing substitutes an asserted motive for an observed electoral result. The analysis measured turnout and Republican performance; it did not demonstrate that racial appeals were attempted, rejected or decisive. The history is relevant because it explains how this voting bloc was assembled and whom its politics often served. It does not relieve reporters of the obligation to distinguish that history from what the new data can prove.

A warning for the GOP, not an obituary

Republicans still hold the institutional advantages created by decades of evangelical organizing: churches, political networks, conservative media, donor relationships and communities accustomed to treating GOP participation as a cultural duty. One midterm turnout collapse does not make those structures disappear.

What it does reveal is that party leaders cannot treat those voters as an automatic resource. The reported deficit was large enough to matter, concentrated enough to identify and rooted in counties Republicans normally expect to carry their candidates. If disillusionment with Trump contributed to the decline, the party faces a problem of its own making: it concentrated authority and attention around a leader whose conduct at least some loyal evangelical voters now describe as self-serving.

The Republican evangelical pillar did not demonstrably implode. It failed under load. Whether that failure becomes a lasting fracture will depend on whether voters such as the Binfords return—and whether Republican leaders can persuade them that the transaction has anything left to offer besides another demand for obedience.
