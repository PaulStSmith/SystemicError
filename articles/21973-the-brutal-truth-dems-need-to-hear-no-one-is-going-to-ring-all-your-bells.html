The brutal truth Dems need to hear: No one is going to ring all your bells

Based on an article by D. Earl Stephens

Paulo SantosAugust 19, 20263 min read

Stop Selling Factional Theater as Strategy

The column’s real subject

The source piece is not really about centrists versus progressives. It is a plea to shut down intramural Democratic drama and line up behind whoever can beat Republicans. That instinct is correct as far as it goes. But the column also reveals how badly political discourse has been trained to treat internal branding fights as if they were the main event while the actual machinery of reaction keeps moving.

Power sits on the right

The institutional power in this story is not split evenly. The Republican Party, a disciplined vehicle for Trump, is the force with the White House wrecking ball, the anti-vote project, the trade chaos, the attacks on press and allies, and the Supreme Court alignment. The column names the core fact plainly: Republicans gave us Trump, Trump rewarded them, and the system now bends around that reward structure.

That is the correct place to start. Not with vibes. Not with factional purity. With power.

The distraction economy

The article rightly ridicules the media-generated horse race inside the Democratic coalition. That fight is useful mostly to commentators who need endless content and to operatives who need everyone else looking sideways. The more Democrats spend their oxygen rehearsing identity among themselves, the more the right gets to act with discipline and impunity.

But the column also slips into the same performance it condemns. “Centrist” versus “DSA” becomes a tribal shouting match instead of a strategic question: who can organize durable majorities, who can govern, and who can keep power from being handed back to an openly authoritarian Republican bloc. When that question is buried under label warfare, the press is not informing anyone. It is laundering paralysis.

Democrats need a spine, not a catechism

The piece is right that no candidate will “ring all your bells.” That is politics. The mistake is pretending that unity means erasing conflict rather than subordinating it to a common purpose. Democrats do not need sanctimony from the center or smug purity from the left. They need a coalition that understands the actual target.

That means no more treating non-endorsements, branding feuds, and self-protective posturing as signs of moral seriousness. Megan Romer’s line about protecting organizational credibility may sound principled in a vacuum, but in a moment when elections determine whether millions get hurt, it reads like luxury politics. Movements that cannot choose a side in a fight over survival are not protecting credibility. They are hoarding it.

What the column gets right and what it evades

The strongest part of the argument is its refusal to romanticize Republican discipline. The weakest part is that it stops at urging Democrats to “beat Republicans” without fully confronting the institutional rot that makes the stakes this high in the first place: donor power, the Supreme Court, and the structural incentives that keep rewarding authoritarian escalation.

Citizens United is mentioned, and rightly so, but it is not just a bad ruling in the abstract. It is a mechanism for converting wealth into political leverage and then pretending the resulting chaos is ordinary democratic disagreement. That is the larger pattern here: money hardens into law, law shields power, and media treats the damage as just another round of opinion.

The larger pattern

The real story is not that Democrats are divided. It is that American politics keeps trying to substitute self-expression for power analysis. The right has a machine, a leader, a court, and a long-running strategy of coercion. Democrats too often have commentary, branding, and internal grievance management. That is why so much of political journalism and so many inside-the-coalition fights feel unserious: they are arranged around personalities and posture instead of consequences.

The lesson is not that disagreement should disappear. It is that democratic politics fails when it mistakes factional self-regard for organizing and when it lets the most dangerous actors define the terms of the fight. The task is not to worship Democrats. It is to use them, critically and without illusion, as the only workable institutional barrier to an openly authoritarian Republican project.
