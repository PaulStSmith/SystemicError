The Crime of Holding Scissors While Foreign

That was one of the comments underneath a video discussing the arrest of a Chinese barber in Bangkok. Given Thailand’s international reputation for sex tourism, and the considerably more serious problem of child sexual exploitation, the comment practically writes itself.

But beneath the dark humour lies something considerably more interesting: a government creating the very conditions it subsequently uses to justify its restrictions.

And it all begins with a haircut.

The Great Bangkok Barbershop Bust

In November 2026, Thai immigration police conducted an undercover operation against a thirty-year-old Chinese national operating a private barbershop from a rented apartment in Bangkok.

His customers booked appointments through an application. They knocked on his door, were admitted, received their haircuts and paid for the service. A man’s haircut cost 800 baht.

The police sent an undercover officer posing as a customer and arrested the barber while he was cutting someone’s hair.

Apparently, Bangkok could sleep soundly that night.

The authorities had neutralized the threat of unauthorized hairstyling.

What particularly angered some Thai people, however, was that the barber served exclusively Chinese customers, refusing entry to Thai nationals.

On the surface, that sounds discriminatory. Imagine a business operating in your country and refusing to serve you because of your nationality.

Now imagine that your government prohibits the person operating that business from practising his profession because of his nationality.

Suddenly, the story becomes considerably more complicated.

Discrimination With a Government Seal of Approval

Thailand maintains a list of 27 occupations strictly reserved for Thai nationals, with another 13 subject to conditional restrictions.

Among the prohibited occupations are hairdressing, wood carving, gem polishing, street vending, tour guiding, secretarial work and traditional Thai massage.

This is not merely a licensing requirement.

A Chinese barber could possess thirty years of professional experience, internationally recognized qualifications, impeccable hygiene standards, sufficient capital to establish a business and legal permission to reside in Thailand.

None of those qualifications would entitle him to practise his profession under the ordinary occupational rules.

His exclusion is not based on competence, professional standards, public safety or consumer protection.

It is based on nationality.

Calling this economic protectionism does not change its underlying mechanism. Protectionism describes the intended economic outcome; discrimination describes the means through which that outcome is pursued.

The two are not mutually exclusive.

Manufacturing the Enemy

The video raises an interesting possibility regarding the barber’s refusal to serve Thai customers.

What if it wasn't motivated by contempt for Thai people?

What if it was motivated by self-preservation?

Every unfamiliar customer represented a potential informant. Opening his establishment to the general public increased the likelihood of being reported to immigration authorities.

Indeed, an undercover officer posing as a customer was precisely how the police caught him.

We cannot know the barber’s actual motivation without hearing from him. His exclusionary customer policy may have had other explanations. But the incentives created by the legislation are unmistakable.

The government prohibits foreigners from practising certain professions. Those who continue practising are driven underground. Underground businesses have an incentive to restrict access to trusted communities. Their secrecy and exclusionary practices subsequently reinforce public suspicions about foreigners.

Those suspicions can then be invoked to justify maintaining or expanding the original restrictions.

The government creates the conditions for segregation and then points to segregation as evidence that its restrictions are necessary.

It is a textbook self-fulfilling prophecy.

The Myth of the Zero-Baht Business

The video also discusses a phenomenon known in Thailand as the “zero-baht business.”

The term describes foreign-operated businesses whose economic activities supposedly remain entirely within their own national communities, leaving Thai citizens without any share of the proceeds.

There are legitimate concerns surrounding foreign investment, commercial rents, tax evasion and the displacement of local businesses. These deserve serious economic analysis.

But consider the supposedly isolated Chinese barbershop.

The barber rented premises in Bangkok. He presumably consumed electricity and water, purchased food, required transportation and incurred ordinary living expenses.

Unless he had somehow managed to establish an entirely self-sufficient Chinese sovereign territory inside a Bangkok apartment, the assertion that his business contributed absolutely nothing to the Thai economy requires considerably more evidence than the nationality of his customers.

Money does not acquire a nationality simply because of the passport held by the person spending it.

Furthermore, if the concern is that clandestine businesses fail to pay taxes, perhaps making their legal registration categorically impossible is not the most effective solution.

An underground business cannot readily declare its income, register its activities, advertise publicly or participate openly in the formal economy without exposing itself to prosecution.

The government has established an incentive against the very economic integration it ostensibly desires.

Protection From Competition

The conventional justification for reserving occupations for nationals is the protection of domestic workers.

That objective is understandable. Governments have legitimate interests in preventing labour exploitation, protecting wages and ensuring that local workers are not unfairly displaced.

But there is a fundamental difference between protecting workers from unfair competition and protecting them from competition altogether.

If a foreign barber is undercutting local businesses by violating minimum-wage legislation, enforce the wage legislation.

If he is evading taxes, enforce the tax legislation.

If his establishment fails to comply with hygiene regulations, enforce the hygiene regulations.

If wealthy foreign investors are distorting the commercial property market, address the mechanisms through which that distortion occurs.

None of these problems inherently requires determining the nationality of the person holding a pair of scissors.

There is also a neglected party in this discussion: the customer.

Chinese residents of Bangkok may prefer a Chinese-speaking barber for perfectly ordinary reasons. Communicating how one wants one's hair cut is considerably easier when both parties speak the same language.

By prohibiting that barber from operating legally, the government restricts not only his economic freedom but also the choices available to his prospective customers.

Imagine the Law in Reverse

Imagine a Thai national moving to another country.

She obtains legal residency and decides to establish a business providing traditional Thai massage, a skill she learned in Thailand and perfected over decades.

Now imagine that her adopted country declares that only its own nationals may provide that service.

Her qualifications are irrelevant. Her experience is irrelevant. Her willingness to pay taxes is irrelevant.

She is forbidden from practising a traditional art originating in her own country because she was born on the wrong side of a national border.

The discriminatory nature of such a restriction becomes considerably easier to recognize when the nationalities are reversed.

A consistent principle of fairness should survive that reversal.

The Difference Between Law Enforcement and Public Safety

This brings us back to the original comment.

Thailand has a documented history of commercial sexual exploitation of children associated with international tourism. This is not a victimless regulatory offence. It is a devastating violation of fundamental human rights.

Thai authorities do investigate and prosecute such crimes, and a single barbershop raid does not establish the country's overall law enforcement priorities.

Nevertheless, the juxtaposition illustrates an important distinction between illegality and harmfulness.

Something can be illegal without being inherently harmful. Something can be profoundly harmful while remaining inadequately investigated or prosecuted.

Legislation determines what constitutes an offence. It does not automatically establish the moral legitimacy of criminalizing that conduct.

The fact that the Chinese barber violated Thai law is not, in itself, a defence of that law.

Otherwise, every discriminatory statute ever enacted would become immune to criticism simply by virtue of having been enacted.

The Systemic Error

The fundamental problem here is not uniquely Thai.

Governments across the world frequently create artificial barriers to participation in society, punish people who circumvent those barriers and subsequently cite their clandestine behaviour as evidence that they cannot be trusted to participate openly.

The excluded become suspicious because they are excluded.

Their attempts to survive outside the system become the justification for keeping them outside it.

Meanwhile, the original policy escapes scrutiny because the consequences it produces are attributed exclusively to the people subjected to it.

Thailand’s Chinese barber offers a particularly mundane illustration of this mechanism.

There were scissors. There was a chair. There was a customer. There was a mutually agreed price.

There was no allegation in the reported arrest that the haircut was dangerous, fraudulent or involuntary.

The haircut wasn't illegal because of what was being done.

It wasn't illegal because of how it was being done.

It was illegal because of who was holding the scissors.
