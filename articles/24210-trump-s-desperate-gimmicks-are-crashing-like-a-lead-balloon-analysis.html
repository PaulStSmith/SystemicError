Trump's desperate 'gimmicks' are crashing 'like a lead balloon': analysis

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 1, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump’s proposed midterm inducements are failing to gain support from voters or Republican candidates. Drawing on an MS NOW analysis by Paul Waldman, it highlights Trump’s promise of a $5,000 payment to every American adult if Republicans retain Congress in November—a proposal that GOP candidates have largely declined to promote. A cited poll found only 13% of voters believed Trump genuinely intended to deliver the money.

Waldman also points to Trump’s interest in temporarily banning diesel exports as prices averaged more than $6.50 per gallon. Experts cited in the reporting warned that a ban would not lower diesel prices and could worsen global energy disruption. Other proposals, including an unspecified beef-import initiative, reportedly backfired or were shelved.

The analysis attributes these failures to diminished trust in Trump’s promises and intense dissatisfaction with his presidency. AlterNet cites approval ratings in the 30s and a Strength in Numbers/Verasight poll in which 50% strongly disapproved of his performance while 15% strongly approved.

Power disguised as a sales pitch

Trump is not an outsider tossing campaign ideas into the discourse. He is the president, backed by the executive branch and leading a party that controls Congress. That is the relevant power structure: the people presenting themselves as frustrated problem-solvers already possess the machinery to propose legislation, negotiate funding and administer policy.

The conditional promise of $5,000 checks—rewarding voters if they preserve Republican control—turns governing into a crude transaction. Yet the reporting offers no implementation details, funding mechanism or legislative path. Republican candidates’ refusal to advertise the proposal suggests they do not regard the president’s promise as dependable enough to stake their own campaigns on it.

The promise is not the policy

The gimmick framing is accurate but incomplete. A gimmick is merely shallow. These proposals also divert attention from accountability for governing results.

The diesel proposal illustrates the substitution. High fuel prices are a real material problem, but interest in an export ban is not evidence of an effective response. According to the experts cited by Waldman, it would fail at its stated purpose while risking broader energy turmoil. The administration’s political need for a visible move does not make that move economically sound.

Trump’s earlier promises to reduce prices immediately and cut energy and electricity costs in half matter because they establish the standard he chose for himself. If voters now discount another spectacular pledge, that is not confusion or excessive cynicism. It is a foreseeable response to the gap between his assurances and the conditions described in the reporting.

Republican silence is an institutional judgment

The clearest verdict may come from Trump’s own party. Candidates seeking votes usually advertise proposals they expect to be popular. Their silence about a universal $5,000 check—an offer with obvious surface appeal—signals that they see liability where Trump sees spectacle.

That silence is also an evasion. Republican lawmakers and candidates benefit from Trump’s leadership when it mobilizes the party, but they can quietly distance themselves when his promises become embarrassing. This arrangement lets the president generate attention while other Republicans avoid responsibility for defending the details.

Polling is evidence, not destiny

AlterNet’s language about an impending Republican wipeout runs ahead of what the evidence can establish. Polls can document current disapproval and voter intentions; they cannot certify the result of an election that has not occurred.

The figures cited are nevertheless politically severe. Approval in the 30s, a 35-point gap between strong disapproval and strong approval in one poll, and minimal belief in the promised checks describe a credibility problem that another announcement cannot easily repair. The electorate may change its mind before November, but the reported strategy assumes voters can be moved by an offer they largely do not believe.

The governing failure behind the gimmicks

The larger pattern is government by promotional stunt: identify public pain, announce a dramatic shortcut and leave the institutional work vague. When the proposal collapses under scrutiny, move to the next one.

That cycle protects power by redirecting attention from what officeholders have done to what they have just promised. Here, it appears to be failing because the messenger already controls the government and voters can compare his sales pitch with their lived conditions. The problem is not that Trump has run out of clever inducements. It is that spectacle cannot indefinitely substitute for credible policy, and even his own party appears to understand the difference.
