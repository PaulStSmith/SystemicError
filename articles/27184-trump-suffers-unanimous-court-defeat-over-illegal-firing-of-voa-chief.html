Trump suffers unanimous court defeat over illegal firing of VOA chief

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 11, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

A unanimous three-judge panel of the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit upheld an August 2025 ruling that Kari Lake unlawfully fired Voice of America director Michael Abramowitz. AlterNet reports that Lake dismissed him after he refused an ultimatum to accept a lower-level job in North Carolina or lose his position.

The appeals court left in place U.S. District Judge Royce Lamberth’s permanent injunction against Abramowitz’s removal. Lamberth concluded that firing authority belonged to the Senate-confirmed International Broadcasting Advisory Board, which President Donald Trump had eliminated early in his second term—not to Lake.

Abramowitz said he was considering his next steps and argued that the government had no reason to keep him from returning. Meanwhile, according to AlterNet, VOA’s broadcasting capacity has been sharply reduced and hundreds of employees have remained on paid administrative leave.

The power was never Lake’s

This was not an ordinary employment dispute. Congress placed control over the VOA director behind an institutional firewall intended to protect the broadcaster’s journalistic independence. Judge Cornelia Pillard’s opinion described that protection as a durable barrier, not a routine workplace rule.

Lake exercised authority the courts found she did not possess. Her ultimatum did not cure that defect; it merely dressed removal up as a choice between professional displacement and dismissal. The consequential decision was hers, enabled by an administration attempting to remake the agency before securing Senate confirmation for a lawful chief executive.

Abolishing the guardrail changes nothing

Trump eliminated the very advisory board empowered to remove the VOA director. But abolishing the authorized decision-maker did not automatically transfer its powers to a preferred subordinate. The administration behaved as though destroying a statutory constraint created a blank check.

That is the central power move: dismantle the body that can lawfully act, then claim practical necessity for actions the law assigns to that body. The court rejected that maneuver. Institutional vandalism does not manufacture legal authority.

The personnel story is a decoy

Framing this primarily as Abramowitz winning his job back would shrink the dispute to the size of one office. The reported damage extends well beyond him: reduced broadcasting capacity, hundreds of sidelined employees, and repeated judicial findings against Lake’s management of the U.S. Agency for Global Media.

Abramowitz was the immediate target, but VOA’s independence was the contested asset. Concentrating attention on his employment status risks obscuring who possessed the machinery of government and what that machinery was being used to weaken.

A court victory cannot restore capacity

The unanimous ruling blocks one unlawful removal. It does not automatically rebuild the broadcaster, return every employee to productive work, or reverse the operational losses AlterNet describes.

That distinction matters because demolition moves faster than adjudication. Officials can sideline personnel and constrict an institution immediately; courts arrive later, after capacity, expertise, and public reach have already been depleted. An injunction can restrain power without repairing everything power has damaged.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is executive control pursued through institutional hollowing: eliminate an independent authority, install or empower a loyal actor without the required legal mandate, and force courts to police the boundary after the disruption is underway.

Here, responsibility is not diffuse. Trump dismantled the board that held removal power. Lake issued the ultimatum and carried out the firing. The administration kept Abramowitz from performing the job despite the district court’s judgment. The courts identified the legal barrier clearly. What remains is the political consequence of a system in which unlawful action can produce lasting institutional damage even when it ultimately loses.
