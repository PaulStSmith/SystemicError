Embarrassing leaks reveal just how badly Trump 'lost': expert

Trump Started a War and Called the Chaos Strategy

The Decision Came From Washington

Donald Trump launched the war against Iran with Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s help. That is the central fact. Six and a half months later, neither Iran nor the United States has yielded, shipping remains obstructed, and intermittent American bombing has failed to resolve the conflict.

This is not a mysterious “situation” that befell the administration. Trump chose military escalation without securing the means to achieve a defined political outcome. Hegseth enabled that choice. The resulting paralysis belongs to them.

The “Forever War” Frame Lets Trump Off Easy

Calling this another “forever war” captures the danger of an open-ended conflict, but it also softens the indictment. Duration is not the only failure. The administration has created a war that inflicts economic damage, restricts navigation, exposes regional allies and produces no evident route to settlement.

Trump campaigned against indefinite wars, then initiated one. The contradiction is not merely rhetorical hypocrisy. It shows how antiwar branding can coexist comfortably with impulsive militarism when slogans substitute for strategy.

Saudi Arabia Exposes the Power Failure

Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman reportedly called Trump twice seeking American intervention after an Iran-backed militia attacked the kingdom’s east-west pipeline. The United States refused.

Washington is not obligated to enter every conflict requested by Riyadh. But Trump had already committed American force to a regional war while presiding over a security system built around promises of U.S. protection. Starting the confrontation and then withholding support when its consequences reached an allied state is not restraint. It is incoherence backed by bombs.

The Gulf states’ reported shock reflects a rational reassessment: American commitments mean less when the president who commands the military treats alliances as improvisational transactions.

Iran’s Leverage Was Not an Accident

The administration’s bombing has not forced Iran to capitulate. Meanwhile, Iran’s allies can impose costs on Saudi infrastructure, and mutual blockades continue to damage trade. The stronger military power has produced motion without control.

That distinction matters. The United States possesses bases across the region and vastly greater conventional capabilities. Iran and aligned militias possess asymmetric leverage: pipelines, shipping routes and vulnerable partners. Trump entered a conflict in which the adversary did not need to defeat the United States militarily. It needed only to demonstrate that Washington could not protect the order it claimed to manage.

“Lost the Gulf” Is a Verdict on Governance

The article’s podcast framing leans heavily on Trump’s damaged “credibility,” as though the primary casualty were his reputation for competent dealmaking. That is too personal and too shallow. The deeper damage comes from presidential power exercised without coherent objectives, reliable commitments or an exit mechanism.

Credibility is not a mystical reserve depleted by embarrassing headlines. It is the accumulated expectation that a government understands its own promises and can connect force to policy. Trump’s administration has instead shown that it can initiate violence, fail to compel its opponent and leave allied governments guessing about what American power is for.

Chaos Is the Policy’s Institutional Product

This episode reveals a larger pattern: concentrated executive authority makes it easy to begin a war and difficult for anyone else to impose strategic discipline afterward. Once the president acts, military operations acquire their own bureaucratic momentum, allies scramble to protect themselves, and critics debate whether electoral opinion will finally notice.

That sequence converts one leader’s decision into a regional condition for millions of people. Trump and Hegseth supplied the agency; Iran and its partners exploited the opening; Gulf states absorbed the insecurity. Calling the result “chaos” is accurate only if the word does not erase its authors. This disorder was not leaderless. It was produced from the top.
