Hey Boomers, About That Apology: Generations, Power, and the Convenient Myth of Blame

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 17, 20257 min read

The New York Times ran an opinion video that distilled a common frustration: younger Americans are staring down runaway housing costs, predatory tuition, climate collapse, and a political economy wired against them. The kicker was a simple demand to the Baby Boomers: how about an apology?

Cue the deluge. In came the replies — rueful, furious, defensive, sanctimonious, some honest, many boring. A sampler: “Stop dividing people.” “We just worked hard.” “College was cheap but mortgages were 12%!” “Kids don’t vote.” “It’s not generations, it’s class.” “We did civil rights.” “Put your phone down.” “We built the country.” And the ever-present sermon: if life is hard now, it’s your own fault.

Here’s the problem: generational blame is the laziest political analysis in America. It spares the powerful, scapegoats your neighbors, and keeps the cash register ringing for the same elite that’s been strip‑mining the future for fifty years.

The Escalator and the Trap

Two things can be true at once:

  1. Boomers benefitted from a once‑in‑a‑century public investment high tide — cheap public universities, strong unions, high marginal tax rates funding broad infrastructure, a housing market not yet turned into a speculative casino.

  2. Plenty of individual boomers struggled — layoffs, inflation spikes, predatory mortgages, and industries that chewed people up and spat them out.

What changed is not the moral fiber of any birth cohort. What changed are the rules. Deregulation, union‑busting, financialization, privatization, winner‑take‑all tax codes, and a politics addicted to donor money rewired the economy.

Generations don’t write legislation; coalitions do. Classes do. Lobbyists do. Courts do. The thumb on the scale isn’t “1946–1964” — it’s capital over labor, asset‑holders over renters, monopoly over markets, donor class over voters. If you aim your anger at a birth year, you’re bringing a water pistol to an oil fire.

The Greatest Trick: Turning Structural Theft into Personal Morality

Read the comments again and you’ll hear the catechism of American moral bookkeeping:

  • We worked hard; therefore we deserve what we have.

  • You want too much; therefore you don’t deserve what you demand.

  • We had 12% mortgages; therefore today’s prices are fine.

  • You don’t vote.

  • You look at TikTok too much.

It’s tidy, it’s comforting, and it’s wrong. “Hard work” is a virtue, not an economic model. Housing is not expensive because young people aren’t skipping lattes; it’s expensive because we turned shelter into a yield‑farm for investors and blocked supply with zoning written by incumbents. Tuition didn’t explode because Gen Z stopped mowing lawns; it exploded because states defunded public universities and then handed students a lifetime of non‑dischargeable debt.

And the climate? Spare me the sermon about character. Oil companies lied about warming for decades, politicians pocketed checks, newsrooms parroted “both sides”, and the tab now comes due with interest. You can recycle until your fingers bleed; if policy props up extraction, we cook.

“We Voted for Civil Rights” — and Then for Tax Cuts

Yes, many boomers marched for civil rights, women’s rights, gay rights. And then too many voted for politicians who kneecapped the public sector that makes rights real in practice: schools funded, clinics open, buses running, inspectors inspecting, courts accessible, neighborhoods clean, air breathable. Rights without the state capacity to administer them are museum pieces — pretty, inert, and useless when you need them.

If that stings, good. The honest boomers in the comments feel it: they remember when college was free or close to it; they know estate tax cuts and capital gains giveaways were a heist by and for the asset class; they see the climate math. Their contrition is real, and it points where anger belongs: upwards.

“Kids Don’t Vote” — A Lazy Half‑Truth

Another favorite scold: youth turnout. Convenient, because it offloads systemic sabotage onto teenagers. Youth turnout is a function of barriers (registration traps, ID laws, weekday voting, gerrymanders that make votes feel useless, closed primaries) and incentives (parties that actually deliver material gains). When politics whispers “nothing will change,” people hear it. When it shouts “we’re cancelling your debt, capping rents, taxing windfall profits, and making transit free,” turnout magically improves. Who knew.

It’s Not Generations. It’s Power.

The comments that cut through the fog say the quiet thing plainly: this is a class war masquerading as a food fight between age brackets. Plenty of millennials now sit on HOA boards strangling housing supply. Plenty of Gen Z hustle dropshipping scams while cheering union‑busting. Plenty of Gen X CEOs are writing the NDAs. And alongside them are boomers organizing tenants, funding abortion access, knocking doors, and getting cuffed at pipeline protests.

Birth year is a poor proxy for politics. Material position is a better one. Do you live on wages or on assets? Do you profit when rents rise? Do you make money when the planet burns? Do you fear a strike because it threatens your portfolio, or do you join it because it’s your paycheck? Those questions sort the world far better than “1963 vs. 1997.”

The Social Media Strawman and the Discipline of Policy

Nothing fogs a debate faster than culture‑war sneers. “Put down the phone.” “Dance videos.” “Entitlement.” Cute, but useless. If you want grown‑up politics, speak in policies and budgets:

  • Housing: End exclusionary zoning. Build social and public housing at scale. Ban corporate bulk buying of single‑family homes. Tax vacant units. Treat shelter as infrastructure, not an asset class.

  • Education: Restore public university funding to pre‑austerity levels. Cancel existing federal student debt. End the servitude of non‑dischargeable loans.

  • Labor: Card‑check recognition, sectoral bargaining, gig‑worker rights. End the misclassification racket.

  • Climate: Ban new fossil lease approvals; invest in grid, transit, and buildings at New Deal scale. Make polluters pay. Stop laundering oil profits through “offsets.”

  • Democracy: Automatic voter registration, universal vote‑by‑mail, weekend/holiday elections, independent redistricting, DC and Puerto Rico statehood, Supreme Court ethics and expansion.

  • Taxation: Progressive wealth taxes, close stepped‑up basis, end the carried‑interest charade, restore corporate rates with teeth and an enforcement budget that scares white‑shoe law firms.

This is the language of alignment — not finger‑wagging. You don’t need an apology to pass a bill. You need a coalition that can’t be ignored.

The Politics of Apology

So should boomers apologize? Some already have, and it’s decent as a gesture. But apology without transfer of power is performance art. Keep the apology if it lets you keep the zoning veto, the low assessment on the big house, the stock buyback windfalls, the donor hotline to your Senator.

A real apology looks like this: retirees showing up at zoning hearings to vote yes on housing; older homeowners championing land value taxes that hit them — and insisting the revenue build schools and clinics; long‑tenured managers bargaining in good faith with unions; investors demanding boards decarbonize on a timeline aligned with physics, not PR; grandparents telling their representatives: raise my taxes; I’ve eaten well.

What the Young Owe the Old

Honesty. Not deference, not spite. Honesty. To say: you were handed a different game, and some of you pulled the ladder up; others tried to bolt it down. We’ll stop pretending this is about vibes and start writing the law to match our lives.

You want reconciliation? Pass bills that change who wins and loses. If a policy makes Black renters, single parents, disabled workers, and low‑income students better off, it’s probably real. If it mainly soothes the portfolio class, it’s cosmetic. Pick.

The Aftertaste of the Comment Thread

The Times thread is a diorama of America’s favorite narcotic: grievance with none of the machinery. Nostalgia, scolding, mythology about bootstraps, moral parables about phones and lattes — anything to avoid naming the culprits written into statute and balance sheet.

There were bright spots — people who admit the heist and point to the vault. “It’s a class war.” Yes. “We need younger leaders.” Yes, if they govern like builders not brand managers. “We messed up. We’re sorry.” Good. Now act in ways that cost you something, not just your breath.

From Apology to Power

If this video jolts you, let it. If it offends you, good — offense is cheaper than rent. The only politics worth doing now rearranges property rights, rewrites budgets, and reins in the corporate capture that has colonized both parties. Everything else is theater.

Spare us the sepia photographs of your starter home and the sermons about character. The young aren’t asking for your stories; they’re demanding your votes — for housing, for schools, for air they can breathe and a planet they can inhabit. If you want absolution, put your shoulder to that wheel.

Not because the kids asked nicely. Because the math does not negotiate, and history does not accept IOUs.
