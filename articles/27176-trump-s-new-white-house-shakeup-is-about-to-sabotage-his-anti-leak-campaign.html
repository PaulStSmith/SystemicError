Trump's new White House shakeup is about to sabotage his anti-leak campaign

Based on an article by Terry H. Schwadron

Paulo SantosNovember 10, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump’s White House personnel shakeup is likely to undermine his campaign against unauthorized disclosures. The central change is the replacement of chief of staff Susie Wiles, who reportedly resisted proposals to arrest journalists to expose confidential sources, with Stephen Miller, who supported that approach. The outlet argues that departures across the White House, Justice Department, Health and Human Services, and Pentagon will create more disgruntled former officials with information to share.

The administration is reportedly pursuing leakers through criminal investigations, subpoenas, polygraph tests, litigation, and pressure on news organizations. Trump has demanded severe punishment for people who disclose military information, while Miller reportedly predicted that journalists would quickly surrender their sources under threat of arrest. AlterNet also describes the administration’s definition of a “leak” as selective: embarrassing disclosures are targeted, while improper disclosures by Trump or senior allies receive different treatment.

Power defines the offense

The decisive power here belongs to the president, the chief of staff, political appointees at the Justice Department, and agency leaders who can order investigations or restrict access. Journalists, civil servants, and departing officials do not control subpoenas, prosecutions, security clearances, press credentials, or federal law enforcement.

That imbalance clarifies what the anti-leak campaign is actually doing. Officials are not merely expressing frustration about confidential information. They are reportedly considering using state power against journalists to identify their sources. Calling this a dispute over White House “messaging” would conceal the coercive machinery behind it.

Loyalty is replacing restraint

Wiles reportedly pushed back against arresting journalists; Miller endorsed the idea. Replacing one with the other is therefore not an administrative shuffle without ideological content. It removes an internal restraint and elevates an adviser associated in AlterNet’s account with a more punitive response.

The wider departures matter for the same reason. Officials leaving Justice, Health and Human Services, and other offices take institutional memory with them. Some may speak to reporters; many may not. But an administration that treats internal disagreement as disloyalty produces the very incentives it claims to be suppressing: fear, grievance, and a preference for disclosure outside official channels.

That is not proof that every departing official will leak. It is evidence that the White House is choosing a management structure likely to deepen distrust while preparing harsher punishment for the consequences.

“Leak” is a political category

AlterNet’s strongest point is that the administration reportedly applies the label according to who is embarrassed, not simply according to whether classified information was mishandled. Trump cited supposedly leaked timing for B-2 operations even though the article says the information was publicly available. By contrast, Pete Hegseth’s distribution of military attack information in a Signal chat that included a journalist is treated differently in the administration’s political narrative.

The distinction is power, not secrecy. Disclosures about casualties, military conditions, or government conduct become intolerable when they weaken official claims. Information released or mishandled by powerful allies is excused, minimized, or placed outside the moral category of “leaking.”

“National security” then becomes less a consistent standard than a permission slip: broad enough to pursue critics, elastic enough to spare loyalists.

The press is the pressure point

The proposed arrests would target journalists because the government wants access to people it cannot easily identify. The reporter becomes leverage against the source. Miller’s reported confidence that journalists would surrender names quickly reveals the intended mechanism: intimidation first, information second.

The administration’s other reported tactics—denying press passes, ridiculing reporters, withholding information, issuing subpoenas, and pursuing expansive legal theories—fit the same pattern. The objective is not merely to rebut coverage. It is to raise the personal and institutional cost of obtaining information the government would prefer to control.

Congressional Republicans’ previous reliance on whistleblower protections during the Biden administration further exposes the partisan standard. Confidential disclosure is celebrated when it damages an opponent and criminalized when it exposes one’s own government.

Repression cannot manufacture trust

The larger pattern is a governing coalition trying to solve an internal legitimacy problem through external coercion. Personnel purges reduce dissent inside the institution, but they also remove officials capable of restraining reckless decisions or resolving disputes internally. The result is a smaller circle of loyalists, a larger population of alienated former insiders, and stronger incentives to communicate outside formal channels.

That is the structural failure in the anti-leak campaign. The White House can investigate employees, threaten reporters, and narrow access to information. It cannot compel confidence in leaders who treat accountability as sabotage and loyalty as the only acceptable form of truth.
