Former congressman cackles in Scott Jenning’s face

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 4, 20263 min read

What happened

Former South Carolina congressman Bakari Sellers laughed at Republican commentator Scott Jennings on CNN after Jennings blamed weak job growth and declining consumer sentiment largely on economic conditions President Donald Trump inherited from former President Joe Biden. Jennings argued that inflation had mostly normalized but that earlier price increases still shaped voters’ perceptions.

Anderson Cooper replied that Trump was already deep into the second half of his term and noted that Jennings’s defense resembled the Biden administration’s claim that economic conditions were better than voters felt. Sellers said household costs—not favorable indicators—were driving public anger, and pointed to competitive races in Republican strongholds. AlterNet also reported that some Republican candidates, including Leigh Wambsganss and Rep. Byron Donalds, were removing Trump branding from their websites; Wambsganss was reportedly removing the word “Republican” as well.

The people in charge own the result

The laughter supplied the clip, but Cooper’s timeline supplied the relevant fact: according to the report, Trump was no longer at the beginning of his term. A president can inherit economic conditions. A governing party cannot indefinitely treat inheritance as an exemption from accountability.

Republicans held the institutional power at issue. Their administration set federal policy, their candidates sought the benefits of party affiliation, and their media defenders tried to separate public dissatisfaction from the officials currently governing. Biden’s record may help explain how prices reached a given level. It does not erase the decisions made afterward by those empowered to respond.

Jennings recycled the excuse Republicans rejected

Jennings’s argument was not that households were imagining higher prices. It was that inflation had slowed while the accumulated cost increases remained painful. That distinction can be economically coherent. Politically, however, it reproduces the same defense Republicans used against Biden: the indicators are improving, but voters have not recognized it.

Cooper exposed that reversal directly. When Democrats offered this argument, Republicans treated household frustration as decisive evidence of failure. When the same frustration threatened Republicans, Jennings reframed it as a lagging perception caused by the previous administration. The standard changed when power changed hands.

The kitchen table is not a messaging problem

Sellers’s strongest point was not partisan but material. GDP, markets, and inflation rates do not pay a utility bill or fill a prescription. A slower rate of price growth means prices are rising less quickly; it does not restore what households lost in purchasing power.

Calling that gap a communications problem misdirects attention from governing choices to voter comprehension. People deciding which bill to postpone do not need a lecture about aggregate indicators. They need relief. Treating their hardship as a failure to appreciate the data protects officeholders from the consequences of conditions they now have power to address.

The disappearing brand tells the truth

AlterNet’s examples of Republican candidates stripping Trump—and, in Wambsganss’s case, reportedly even the Republican label—from campaign websites matter more than partisan mockery. If those changes were responses to electoral risk, they show politicians seeking distance from a governing brand without surrendering the party machinery, donor networks, or policy coalition attached to it.

That is not accountability. It is reputational laundering: preserve the institutional benefits while hiding the affiliation voters might punish. The candidates’ edits do not establish why particular races became competitive, and Sellers’s remarks do not prove an electoral outcome. But the retreat itself indicates that some Republicans saw their public identification as a liability.

Power changes the alibi

The larger pattern is familiar. Parties campaign by assigning full responsibility to the incumbent, then govern by distributing responsibility backward through time. Economic pain becomes decisive when it can be weaponized and complicated when it must be owned.

The central issue is therefore not whether Sellers laughed at Jennings. It is whether officials and their defenders will name who currently possesses the authority to act. Political accountability collapses when power remains in the present but responsibility is permanently reassigned to the past.
