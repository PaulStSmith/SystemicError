Trump ridiculed on nemesis network as montage of him sleeping plays: 'He is knocked out'

Based on an article by David McAfee

Paulo SantosOctober 5, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that MS NOW’s “The Weekend” mocked President Donald Trump after he described targets of U.S. strikes on Iran as “drug factories slash nuclear factories.” Jonathan Capehart compared Trump’s shifting explanations for the war to an overstuffed diner menu, while Jacqueline Alemany used the remark to introduce a montage of Trump with his eyes closed at public events.

The images did not establish that Trump was asleep, though Capehart said he was “knocked out.” The segment also showed Secretary of State Marco Rubio denying that he had ever seen Trump fall asleep in meetings. The hosts treated that denial—and the administration’s insistence that Trump does not sleep—as additional material for ridicule.

The consequential statement was not about sleep

Trump’s physical alertness is the flimsiest part of this story. A collection of photographs showing closed eyes cannot establish sleep, incapacity, or anything else about presidential performance. Mockery is not evidence.

The consequential event was a president publicly describing military targets in language so muddled that the distinction between nuclear facilities and drug factories became unclear. If the administration’s justification for striking Iran has changed, as Capehart asserted, that is the issue requiring documentation: what rationale officials gave, when they gave it, and whether the underlying evidence supports it.

Power disappears behind the punchline

Trump holds executive power and publicly described U.S. military action. His administration possesses the intelligence, command authority, and communications machinery relevant to explaining that action. The television hosts possess none of those powers.

Yet the segment converted a question about state violence into personality content. Instead of pressing the administration’s claims about the targets, it pivoted to Trump’s eyelids. That does not shift blame onto weaker political actors so much as drain accountability from the stronger one. The president becomes an object of ridicule rather than the official responsible for defending his government’s decisions.

Rubio answered a safer question

Rubio’s denial addressed whether he had personally witnessed Trump sleeping in meetings. It did not clarify what was struck in Iran, why those sites were selected, or why Trump conflated drugs and nuclear activity in his description.

That is how political communication evades consequence: answer the trivial allegation emphatically while the substantive claim goes untested. The available reporting does not establish that Rubio lied. It does show that the segment gave his denial more attention than the administration’s account of military targets.

Satire without accountability

The hosts correctly recognized that Trump’s explanation sounded incoherent. But recognition is not scrutiny. Calling the rhetoric absurd while leaving its factual basis unexplored turns journalism into reaction footage.

The larger pattern is an opposition media economy that treats presidential spectacle as sufficient political analysis. Trump supplies confusion; television supplies laughter; the machinery of war remains largely outside the frame. When officials exercise lethal institutional power, the central question is not whether they look tired. It is whether their stated justification is coherent, supported, and subject to meaningful challenge.
