Trump DOJ scrambles for new excuse after judge’s 'breathtaking' rebuke

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 12, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the Trump administration’s Justice Department offered a new argument in its effort to end New York City’s congestion-pricing program: the Federal Highway Administration may reverse its approval when federal priorities change. The claim appeared in the government’s June appeal of Judge Lewis Liman’s March ruling for the Metropolitan Transportation Authority.

The appeal also argues that Liman lacked jurisdiction because the dispute concerned a financial agreement. NYU law professor Roderick Hills Jr. told Streetsblog NYC that the new reversal theory is weak because neither the agreement authorizing congestion pricing nor the underlying law gives the agency an unrestricted right to revoke approval. He also said an appellate court could disregard the argument because the government did not raise it below.

The administration had previously advanced a broader claim that federal officials could cancel government agreements. At a January hearing, Liman called that position “breathtaking” and “wrong.” The program remained the status quo described in the reporting, while the federal government was asking the courts to stop it.

The power belongs to Washington

The institutional imbalance is plain. The MTA obtained federal approval, built its policy around that approval and defended the arrangement in court. The federal executive branch, controlling the Department of Transportation and the Justice Department, then attempted to withdraw the federal government’s commitment.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy is not a bystander seeking clarity. As Hills put it, Duffy is the official trying to change the status quo. The administration made the decision to target the program; federal lawyers supplied successive theories for doing so.

A permission slip that means nothing

The government’s new position would make federal approval radically unstable. If an agency may reverse itself whenever priorities change, without authority in the governing statute or agreement, then approval becomes temporary political indulgence rather than an administrable commitment.

Hills acknowledged that agencies can sometimes correct an original mistake. But the administration’s reported argument rests on changed priorities, not an identified error in the initial approval. That distinction matters: correcting an unlawful or mistaken decision is administration; nullifying a valid agreement because new officials dislike it is political control dressed as bureaucratic flexibility.

The procedural fog

The jurisdictional argument and the newly raised reversal theory shift attention from the decisive question: what legal authority permits the FHWA to terminate this approval? According to the reporting, the governing documents contain no such mechanism.

“Changing circumstances” sounds measured and technocratic. Here, it functions as a substitute for a missing grant of power. The language invites readers to treat the dispute as administrative uncertainty when the reported record shows an executive branch searching for a legally sustainable route to a chosen outcome.

The timing compounds the weakness. Because the government did not present this argument in the lower court, Hills said the appellate judges could deem it waived without reaching its merits. A theory introduced only on appeal is not evidence that the agency always possessed the power it now claims.

Courts as the restraint

Liman’s rebuke exposed the stakes of the earlier argument: whether executive officials may discard federal commitments simply because they control the executive branch. The revised argument is narrower in wording but similar in consequence. Both would enlarge federal discretion at the expense of statutory limits and institutional reliance.

The MTA’s lawsuit therefore concerns more than one Manhattan toll. It tests whether an administration must operate through powers actually provided by law, or whether policy preference can be retrofitted into legal authority after officials decide what result they want.

Government by revocable commitment

This is a recurring pattern of executive power: make the decision first, then cycle through rationales until one survives review. The misdirection lies in presenting that process as ordinary agency reconsideration. The reported facts instead describe federal officials seeking to undo an approved program without an express revocation provision.

If federal agreements endure only while the president’s appointees favor them, states and public agencies cannot reliably plan around federal action. The immediate consequence falls on congestion pricing. The systemic consequence is a government whose commitments function less like rules than permissions—binding on weaker institutions, optional for the officials powerful enough to reinterpret them.
