Trump loyalist breaks ranks over 'obvious corruption'

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 12, 20263 min read

What happened

Victor Davis Hanson, a prominent Trump ally and conservative commentator, accused figures around President Donald Trump of conduct he described as unethical and, more broadly, “obvious corruption.” AlterNet reports that Hanson urged Trump to impose restrictions on his administration and family comparable to a proposed congressional stock-trading ban.

Hanson cited Trump-family business activity in the Middle East, while saying he did not know whether it was illegal. He also raised an alleged pardon-related arrangement involving former Trump attorney Alina Habba Mildon, Steve Bannon’s acknowledged diversion of border-wall donations to other purposes, and unproven allegations involving the lobbyist daughter of former chief of staff Susie Wiles. Hanson presented these examples as a political liability exposed by the previous week’s midterm elections.

The break is narrower than advertised

Hanson did not abandon Trump or the MAGA project. He warned that apparent corruption was costing Republicans the “moral high ground” and weakening a movement built on denouncing governing elites. This was an argument for repairing the brand, not dismantling the power structure.

Even his criticism came padded with loyalty. Hanson said he respected Habba Mildon, personally liked Bannon and could not say whether some of the conduct he described was illegal. Those qualifications may be appropriate where allegations remain unproven, but politically they also reveal his position: an ally asking the leader to discipline his own circle before its behavior becomes electorally ruinous.

Power sits with the president

Trump holds the institutional power identified in this story. He controls executive-branch appointments, sets ethical expectations inside his administration and exercises the pardon power. His relatives, advisers and political associates benefit from access to a presidency organized around personal loyalty.

That makes “clean it up” an incomplete diagnosis. Ethical safeguards cannot depend on the discretion of the official whose family and associates create the conflicts at issue. Hanson proposes rules, but his appeal still casts Trump as the potential reformer of a system centered on Trump’s own authority.

Corruption as a partisan instrument

Hanson began with Hunter Biden, Nancy Pelosi and Ilhan Omar before examining his own coalition. That sequence exposes how corruption rhetoric usually functions in partisan politics: misconduct by opponents is treated as proof of a rotten order, while comparable concerns among allies become an internal housekeeping problem.

The central issue is not whether conservatives can remain “beyond suspicion.” It is whether public authority is being converted into private access, financial opportunity or preferential treatment. A movement does not earn ethical credibility by policing appearances. It earns it through enforceable rules that apply to relatives, donors, lobbyists and loyalists regardless of their usefulness.

The scandal is discretionary government

The examples Hanson selected share a structural feature: proximity to power may carry extraordinary value. Investors seek receptive channels, pardon seekers hire connected advocates, lobbyists sell access and political celebrities raise money from committed followers. Where formal restraints are weak, personal relationships become an unofficial currency.

That system does not require every allegation to be proved for the danger to become visible. The foreseeable consequence is a government in which outsiders must follow ordinary procedures while insiders can potentially purchase, broker or exploit proximity. Calling that merely a problem of “perception” misdirects attention from the arrangements producing the perception.

Loyalty cannot regulate itself

This episode reveals the limit of personality-based reform. Loyalists may object when misconduct becomes too conspicuous, but their remedy is often another request for the leader to police the network that sustains him.

The larger pattern is institutional cowardice disguised as moral advice. If ethical conduct depends on presidential initiative, partisan embarrassment or allies finally “breaking ranks,” then the safeguards have already failed. Public power needs binding constraints precisely because loyalty is not oversight, reputation is not accountability, and a leader’s promise to clean house is no substitute for rules he cannot selectively waive.
