'Sheesh': MAGA governor in red state faces 'full-blown devastating tsunami' as polls shift

Based on an article by David McAfee

Paulo SantosOctober 4, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower remains with the incumbentA poll made no decisionSpectacle replaces explanationThe systemic error

What happened

RawStory reports that a late-September and early-October 2026 New York Times/Siena poll put Democratic challenger Gina Hinojosa four points ahead of Texas Gov. Greg Abbott. Abbott had led by six points in June, making Texas the largest of several Democratic shifts across five Republican-leaning states.

The same comparison showed Democrats improving in Alaska’s Senate race, Iowa’s gubernatorial race and Ohio’s Senate race, while Republicans retained leads in several other contests. Political analyst Adam Carlson reacted “Sheesh,” while journalist Daniele Angrisani argued that an Abbott defeat would represent a “devastating tsunami” for Republicans. The reporting establishes a sharp polling movement, not an election result.

Power remains with the incumbent

Abbott is not merely a candidate caught in an unfavorable survey. He is the sitting governor of Texas and, as RawStory describes him, one of the country’s most powerful Republican governors. His institutional position gives him governing authority and the advantages of incumbency until voters decide otherwise.

Hinojosa’s four-point polling lead signals political opportunity, not equivalent institutional power. Treating the race primarily as a dramatic reversal risks obscuring the basic structure: Abbott holds the office; Hinojosa is trying to take it from him.

A poll made no decision

No actor in the story has produced the outcome promised by the “tsunami” metaphor. The Times/Siena poll measured voter preferences during a specified period. Carlson compiled the movement, commentators amplified it, and a progressive operative celebrated it. None of those actions elected Hinojosa or removed Abbott.

The shift deserves attention because it appears across several races, including Mary Peltola’s reported nine-point swing against Sen. Dan Sullivan and Rob Sand’s expansion of a two-point lead to 11 points in Iowa. But the Republican leads in Kansas, Alaska’s House race and Iowa’s Senate race complicate any claim of a uniform collapse. The evidence shows substantial movement with notable exceptions.

Spectacle replaces explanation

RawStory builds the piece around reactions—“Sheesh,” “wild,” “tsunami”—without explaining what changed voters’ preferences. The article identifies the polling movement but supplies no evidence about its cause. It therefore cannot tell readers whether policy, candidate performance, national conditions, turnout expectations or polling variation drove the numbers.

That omission matters. Horse-race coverage converts public judgment into weather: a wave arrives, candidates rise or sink, and political power appears to move by natural force. Voters, campaigns and officeholders disappear behind the metaphor. A ten-point swing is consequential data; calling it a natural disaster is not analysis.

The systemic error

The broader pattern is political journalism’s habit of treating polling volatility as an event in itself while leaving governing power and public consequences underexamined. Abbott’s authority does not weaken because commentators react dramatically to a chart. It weakens only if voters translate dissatisfaction into ballots and the electoral system converts those ballots into defeat.

The poll may be an early sign of a serious Republican problem across several red states. It may also change before Election Day. The responsible conclusion is narrower than the headline’s spectacle and more politically useful: Republican incumbents who once appeared secure were facing materially more competitive races, and Texas had become the clearest warning. Everything beyond that remained undecided.
