Multibillion-dollar obstacles stand between the new Congress and your wallet

Based on an article by Terry Schwadron

Paulo SantosNovember 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that a newly elected Democratic majority in the House and Senate would face immediate limits on its promise to lower household costs while Donald Trump remained president during the lame-duck period. Democrats could pass bills addressing health coverage, food assistance, wages, tax credits, tariffs, or the war with Iran, but Trump could veto them unless enough Republicans joined an override.

The article attributes rising household pressure to several administration policies and broader economic conditions. It cites researcher estimates that higher gasoline costs are costing the average household $1,100 annually and tariffs another $2,000, while Affordable Care Act coverage has risen an average of 13 percent per month. It also reports increases in heating oil, groceries, and airfare, with inflation outpacing wage adjustments.

AlterNet says the incoming majority could block further Trump appointments and conduct investigations, but it might struggle to produce rapid price reductions. Reversing spending cuts or restoring services would require new revenue or reallocating money from administration priorities, while oil and mortgage prices would remain partly beyond Congress’s direct control.

Power did not change hands completely

Winning Congress is not the same as controlling the federal government. Trump would retain the veto, command the executive branch, and continue directing foreign policy and federal agencies. Democrats would gain legislative power, subpoena authority, and control over confirmations, but not unilateral authority to reverse his program.

That distinction matters because “Can Democrats lower prices?” is too convenient a question. It places responsibility for immediate economic relief on the incoming majority while the president who imposed tariffs, approved spending priorities, and pursued the reported conflict with Iran still controls crucial levers. Democrats would own their choices, but they would not inherit Trump’s powers merely by inheriting Congress.

Scarcity was politically constructed

The article describes the central obstacle as finding money for health care, food benefits, student debt relief, or childcare assistance. But the reported fiscal constraint did not simply materialize. The administration cut social programs, reduced taxes collected from corporations and wealthy people, and redirected resources toward immigration enforcement and the military.

Congress would therefore confront an allocation fight disguised as an accounting problem. Restoring public support would require lawmakers to identify whose benefits should be reduced, whose taxes should rise, or which enforcement priorities should lose funding. “The money must come from somewhere” is true but incomplete: the money already went somewhere, through decisions made by identifiable officials.

Affordability can become a performance

Some proposed actions could relieve specific costs. Others would mainly demonstrate opposition because Trump could veto them or because Congress cannot legislate a guaranteed oil price, mortgage rate, or grocery bill. AlterNet is right to distinguish passing a message from delivering a material result.

But its suggestion that investigations might distract from practical legislation creates its own misdirection. Oversight and economic policy are not inherently competing duties. If executive misconduct, unlawful defiance, or misuse of agencies affects how public money and authority are deployed, investigation is part of governing. The failure would be conducting hearings as spectacle while declining to use legislative power where it can change budgets, appointments, and legal authority.

Obstruction has an author

The article sometimes describes the coming struggle as generalized “political ugliness,” competing Democratic strains, or institutional difficulty. That language blurs the asymmetry in its own reporting. The decisive obstruction would be a presidential veto, reinforced by legislators choosing to sustain it.

Republicans would have agency, too. They could support relief legislation, oppose it, or protect Trump’s priorities despite the election result described by AlterNet. Calling that outcome gridlock would conceal the actual chain of responsibility: Congress acts, the president blocks, and enough lawmakers enable the block.

The system protects policy from elections

The larger pattern is not merely that divided government moves slowly. It is that voters can reject an agenda without obtaining its prompt reversal. Staggered terms, the veto, fragmented fiscal authority, and executive control allow an outgoing political order to preserve decisions that keep extracting money from households.

That structure makes accountability easy to scramble. The administration responsible for the reported tariffs, cuts, and reallocations can blame inherited conditions; the new majority can blame the veto; industries benefiting from protection can call higher prices necessary; and every institution can describe the result as complexity. The complexity is real. So is the agency inside it.
