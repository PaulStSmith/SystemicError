Inside one agency's terrible, horrible, no good, very bad year — at the hands of Trump

As 2025 draws to a close, the departure of the beleaguered acting director of the Federal Emergency Management Agency, David Richardson, caps a tumultuous year for FEMA. In January, President Donald Trump took office and vowed to abolish the department. Though the administration subsequently slow-walked that proposal, its government-wide staffing cuts have led to a nearly 10 percent reduction in FEMA’s workforce since January. Now it faces a long-awaited report issued by a review council, commissioned by the president and Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem, just as a new interim FEMA chief prepares to take the reins in December.

Although some expected the review council to recommend further cuts or try to fulfill the president’s suggestion of disbanding FEMA entirely, a leaked draft of the report, obtained by the New York Times, recommends preserving the agency. “There’s been a need for emergency management reform for a while,” said Jeffrey Schlegelmilch, a professor at the Columbia Climate School and the director of its National Center for Disaster Preparedness. “But the wrecking balls came in before there was a blueprint for what to do.”

The Trump administration’s first pick to lead FEMA, Cameron Hamilton, was fired after telling Congress that the agency should not be eliminated. Richardson was tapped to replace him, despite a lack of emergency management experience; he reportedly told staff members he had been unaware the United States had a hurricane season, although he later claimed to be joking.

“There’s been a lot of mistrust with expertise in this administration,” said Schlegelmilch, when asked why Richardson was chosen as FEMA administrator.

Richardson’s first test in the national spotlight came in early July, when devastating floods struck Central Texas, killing 135 people. A month earlier, Noem had instituted a new rule requiring her personal sign-off on any FEMA expenditures over $100,000. That meant that, in order to get aid to the region, FEMA officials needed Richardson to get Noem’s approval. But according to reporting from the Washington Post, Richardson made a habit of not checking his phone outside of traditional working hours. This made it a challenge to contact him when the floods hit over the July 4 holiday weekend. As a result, it took over three days for Noem to sign off on expenses for swiftwater rescue teams. It was also later reported that nearly two-thirds of calls to FEMA’s emergency assistance line went unanswered during the floods, because a critical call center was severely understaffed.

A final recommendation on suggested FEMA reforms will arrive by the end of the year, but a leaked draft report supports preserving the agency and restoring it to a cabinet-level agency that reports directly to the president, rather than to the Department of Homeland Security, where it’s been housed since 2003. This has been a longtime goal pursued by emergency management experts, according to Schlegelmilch, because it would give the department more autonomy, reduce red tape, and hopefully improve the speed and efficacy of disaster response in general. A bipartisan bill called the FEMA Act of 2025, which would elevate the department to a cabinet-level agency, was introduced in Congress in July, but it’s stalled in committee.

How the administration will receive the final report from the task force is uncertain, but FEMA’s new interim director, Karen Evans, may not bring much stability to the agency. Although Evans has some emergency management experience, it is largely in cybersecurity rather than disaster response, and the Trump administration’s disinterest in appointing a permanent director may bode poorly for the agency’s long-term future.

“This is the third acting FEMA administrator within a year,” said Shana Udvardy, senior climate resilience policy analyst at the Union of Concerned Scientists. “What the Trump administration is doing is side-stepping the Senate confirmation process for a FEMA administrator, someone we just desperately need in place, given how turbulent it’s been over the past year.”

Our Analysis:

The Chaotic Carousel of FEMA Leadership Under Trump: A Deep Dive

In a move that is as bewildering as it is distressing, the Trump administration’s handling of the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) has been nothing short of a bureaucratic rollercoaster ride—with sharp turns, steep drops, and, unfortunately, real casualties along the way. As 2025 nears its end, the departure of the beleaguered acting director, David Richardson, marks yet another low point in a year fraught with calamity for FEMA.

Trump’s Initial Vow: A Recipe for Disaster

Let’s start with President Donald Trump’s initial vow to abolish FEMA. This proposition, as ludicrous as it is dangerous, was thankfully slow-walked. However, the administration’s appetite for destruction manifested through government-wide staffing cuts, culminating in a nearly 10 percent reduction in FEMA’s workforce. In the face of escalating climate emergencies, this move is akin to pulling firefighters away from a blaze.

The Review Council’s Surprising Sanity

Against all odds, sanity prevailed—at least momentarily. A leaked draft of a long-awaited report by a review council, commissioned by Trump and Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem, recommended preserving FEMA. Jeffrey Schlegelmilch’s metaphor of “wrecking balls” coming in without a “blueprint” poignantly captures the recklessness at play. Yet, the draft report’s suggestion to restore FEMA as a cabinet-level agency directly reporting to the president offers a glimmer of hope amid the chaos.

Leadership Limbo and Lapses in Judgment

The saga of FEMA’s leadership under Trump reads like a tragicomic play. Cameron Hamilton, Trump’s first pick, was ousted for the heretical suggestion that FEMA should, in fact, not be eliminated. Enter David Richardson, a figure so perplexingly out of depth that he joked (or so he claims) about being unaware of the United States’ hurricane season. This level of ignorance, whether feigned or real, is not just embarrassing—it’s dangerous.

Richardson’s tenure was highlighted by a fatal delay in response to catastrophic floods in Central Texas, a debacle exacerbated by Noem’s bureaucratic red tape and Richardson’s apparent aversion to checking his phone during crises. The result? A delayed disaster response that cost lives and highlighted a gross mismanagement of FEMA’s emergency assistance capabilities.

The Quest for Stability and Expertise

Now, as Karen Evans steps in as the new interim director, there’s little to suggest a departure from the status quo. Evans, with a background in cybersecurity rather than disaster response, is yet another example of the Trump administration’s puzzling disinterest in appointing qualified, permanent leadership for an agency that is quite literally on the front lines of America’s battle against natural disasters.

The Trump administration’s tactic of sidestepping the Senate confirmation process for a FEMA administrator is not just a political maneuver—it’s a dereliction of duty. As Shana Udvardy poignantly notes, the necessity for a stable, confirmed leader for FEMA cannot be overstated, especially in an era where climate change is intensifying the frequency and severity of natural disasters.

In Conclusion: A Call to Action

The article from Grist lays bare a stark reality: FEMA, an agency critical to the nation’s emergency management infrastructure, has been left adrift in a sea of political gamesmanship and administrative incompetence. The preservation of FEMA and its elevation to a cabinet-level agency should not be mired in political quagmire; it is an essential step towards ensuring that the United States is prepared to meet the challenges posed by an increasingly unstable climate.

In the end, the saga of FEMA under Trump serves as a cautionary tale of what happens when political vendettas are prioritized over public safety and expert advice is disregarded in favor of political loyalty. As the clock ticks down on 2025, one can only hope that reason will prevail, and FEMA will be restored to its rightful place as a cornerstone of America’s emergency response apparatus.
