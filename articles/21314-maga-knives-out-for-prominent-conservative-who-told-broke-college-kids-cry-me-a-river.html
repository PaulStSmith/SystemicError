MAGA knives out for prominent conservative who told broke college kids 'cry me a river'

When the Burrito Costs $20, the Lie Costs More

Power, Not Piety

Marc Thiessen tried to sneer a college student out of an affordability complaint and got dragged by his own side, with JD Vance joining the pile-on. The immediate spectacle is trivial. The political meaning is not: the people with institutional power in this story are not the students eating cafeteria burritos. They are the vice president, the GOP’s media ecosystem, and the think-tank class that has spent years laundering elite economic doctrine as common sense.

The Student Was Not the Problem

A student said a burrito should not cost $20. Thiessen replied with contempt, as if that settled the matter. It didn’t. It exposed the central fraud of the right’s current economic posture: when ordinary people complain about prices, the response is not policy but ridicule. The burden gets shifted downward, onto the person noticing the cost, never onto the system producing it.

That is not confusion. It is misdirection.

Vance’s Populism Has a Ceiling

Vance’s jab at Thiessen was funny only in the way factional cannibalism is funny. He was not defending affordability as a governing priority. He was policing the boundaries of MAGA’s self-image. The vice president can attack a think-tank conservative for sounding dismissive, but he cannot escape the record the source itself points to: Trump called the affordability crisis a hoax, and Vance has been scrambling to explain that away ever since.

That is the problem with performative populism. It can mock elites. It cannot govern against them.

The Right Wants the Credit Without the Costs

The article notes that affordability has become the GOP’s Achilles’ heel, and that is not because conservatives lack better messaging. It is because material reality keeps intruding on a coalition built on grievance, denial, and loyalty theater. Andrew Kolvet and the other MAGA voices were happy to defend the student once the complaint became a weapon against Thiessen. That is not solidarity. It is opportunism.

Even the right’s internal critics understand the trap. CJ Pearson warned that dismissiveness will not hold young voters. Gabe Guidarini pointed at the think-tank class and the market-worshipping sermonizing that helps make room for actual socialists. The message is plain: when conservatives tell young people to eat contempt with their overpriced lunch, they should not be surprised when those voters look elsewhere.

What Thiessen Really Represented

Thiessen was not being attacked for a single bad line. He was serving as a useful symbol of the old conservative habit of treating material pain as a personal failing. That habit is not accidental. It is how elites protect themselves from accountability. If the burrito is too expensive, the student is indulgent. If the economy is punishing people, the complaint is unserious. If the politics fail, blame the messenger, not the policy order.

And yet the right keeps running into the same wall: the cost of living is not a vibe. It is a daily verdict on who gets protected and who gets squeezed.

The Pattern Beneath the Punchline

This story is about a party that wants the populist aesthetic while defending the institutional arrangements that make life more expensive and less secure. It wants to posture against elites while relying on them. It wants young voters without offering them material relief. It wants to laugh at a $20 burrito while insisting its own economic agenda is serious.

That is the deeper political pattern here: authoritarian politics thrives on contempt for ordinary pain until that pain becomes too visible to deny. Then it turns defensive, splits into factions, and starts blaming its own mouthpieces for saying out loud what the coalition depends on.
