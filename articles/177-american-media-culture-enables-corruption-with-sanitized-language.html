American media culture enables corruption with sanitized language

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosJune 25, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Problem Is Not Trump’s Vulgarity. It Is the Machinery That Keeps Normalizing It.

Power Is the Story

A right-wing Italian newspaper did what much of the American press still refuses to do: it looked at Donald Trump’s behavior and named it as contemptible. The immediate trigger was petty and revealing enough. Trump invented a humiliating lie about Giorgia Meloni “begging” him for a photograph, and Meloni’s government answered with public anger. But the insult matters less than the structure behind it: a president who can fabricate nonsense about a foreign leader, then expect the story to be treated as a colorful anecdote instead of a display of raw dominance and ego.

The central fact is not that Trump is rude. It is that he exercises institutional power while treating truth as optional, and too many institutions keep adapting to that fact instead of confronting it.

Who Enabled the Outcome

Trump did not stumble into these outcomes. He made them, or benefited from them, through deliberate acts: the sexual abuse verdict in Manhattan; the recorded bragging about grabbing women; the Eric Trump Foundation’s diversion of charity money toward Trump-linked golf courses and groups; the fake “university” that ended in a $25 million fraud settlement; the memecoin enrichments that pumped money into his family and insiders; the war against Iran launched without a congressional vote, which the Brennan Center called unconstitutional.

These are not foggy “controversies.” They are decisions, schemes, and abuses. The people with actual power are the ones who set them in motion, profited from them, or failed to stop them. The weaker actors are the donors, buyers, students, immigrants, and the public left to absorb the damage.

Sanewashing Is Not Neutrality

The article’s best target is the press habit of laundering obvious misconduct through sterile language. “Controversial.” “Polarizing.” “Made claims.” “Stoked tensions.” That vocabulary does not describe reality; it protects power from description.

This is where “objectivity” becomes a mask for cowardice. No serious newsroom should confuse emotional restraint with accuracy. If a president lies, steals, abuses, and enriches himself, then the plain language is not editorializing. It is reporting. The refusal to use it is a choice, and it always leans upward, toward the powerful person whose feelings, lawsuits, access, and wrath matter more than the public’s right to clarity.

The Misdirection Is the Point

The article also identifies the mechanism behind this drift: fear, legal pressure, ownership concentration, and a long political campaign to intimidate the press into flinching. Trump sues. Corporations settle. Editors learn. The language softens. The story blurs. The behavior survives.

That is not accidental. It is a system that trains institutions to treat the aggressor’s aggression as a problem of tone. The same pattern appears in the way Trump’s abuses get translated into procedural gossip, while the people harmed by them are made secondary to the spectacle of his latest lie. The result is a press that reports consequences while blurring authorship.

What This Pattern Really Shows

The larger pattern is simple and ugly: concentrated power now gets to write its own atmosphere. It can corrupt, litigate, brand itself, and still be described in public as merely “unconventional.” It can damage immigrants, donors, students, voters, women, and even foreign allies, and still be folded into the language of politics as usual.

The Italian newspaper understood what much of the American press still dodges: sometimes the responsible word is the blunt one. A free press is not supposed to protect the powerful from accurate description. It is supposed to deny them the comfort of euphemism. When institutions refuse to do that, they are not preserving civility. They are helping power hide in plain sight.
