'Bang up work': Republican shamed for glaring error in 9/11 memorial message

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Paulo SantosSeptember 19, 20262 min read

The Wrong Date Is the Least Revealing Part

A Memorial by Template

Michael Whatley, Donald Trump’s endorsed candidate, posted a Sept. 11 memorial graphic dated “Sept. 1, 2001,” then deleted it and reposted a corrected version. The mistake drew ridicule while Whatley trailed Democrat Roy Cooper by 11 points in recent polling.

That is the event. Its political meaning lies less in the typo than in the machinery that produced it.

Power Without Attention

Whatley is not merely a careless social media user. He is a candidate backed by a president, seeking institutional power. His campaign uses national trauma as campaign material, with staff, graphics, messaging and distribution all organized to manufacture public identification with solemnity and patriotism.

The product failed because nobody with authority bothered to verify the defining fact. “Never forget” became branding assembled by people who did not check what they were telling others to remember.

The Candidate Owns the Campaign

The article’s focus on social media derision risks shrinking a political failure into an internet blooper. Aaron Rupar captured the deleted post; Democratic accounts and other users mocked it. But they did not create the error. Whatley’s campaign did.

Responsibility does not disappear into an unnamed staffer or a rushed posting schedule. A candidate benefits from the polished output of a campaign operation and owns its negligence when that operation exposes how little attention sits behind the performance.

Deletion Is Not Accountability

The campaign’s apparent response was to erase the evidence and upload the corrected graphic. That repaired the date, not the underlying contradiction.

A sincere commemoration requires attention to the people and event being commemorated. A disposable graphic requires only that the branding look correct long enough to circulate. The delete-and-repost maneuver suggests damage control, not reflection: remove the defective content, preserve the message and continue as though the failure were merely cosmetic.

Patriotism as Campaign Inventory

The familiar phrases were all present: first responders, national unity, divine blessing. Their function was political recognition, not historical insight. The mistake matters because it punctured that formula and exposed how easily solemn language can be mass-produced without care.

This is not proof that Whatley personally feels no respect for those killed or affected by Sept. 11. It is evidence that his campaign treated their memory casually enough to publish the wrong date while claiming permanent remembrance. The distinction is important—and hardly exculpatory.

The Politics of Empty Ritual

American campaigns routinely convert grief, military service, faith and national catastrophe into symbolic inventory. Candidates borrow the moral gravity of events they did not shape, attach their names to approved sentiments and collect the reputational benefit.

When the performance breaks, coverage often concentrates on mockery because mockery is easy to package. The deeper failure is institutional: political organizations seek authority while demonstrating that even their most solemn declarations may be hollow products moving through an inattentive content pipeline.

The wrong date was embarrassing. The automatic patriotism behind it is the systemic error.
