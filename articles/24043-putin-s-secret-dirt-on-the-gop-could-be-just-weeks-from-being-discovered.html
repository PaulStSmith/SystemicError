Putin's secret dirt on the GOP could be just weeks from being discovered

Based on an article by Tara Dublin

A Theory of Total Control With Almost No Evidence

The Claim Outruns the Record

The article’s thesis is sweeping: Vladimir Putin has “owned” Donald Trump since the late 1980s, Russian leverage explains Republican submission, and this hidden control accounts for much of the present political order. But the evidence presented does not establish that thesis. It gestures toward reporting, allegations, suggestive timing and unanswered questions, then converts them into declarations of treason and blackmail.

That is not forensic political analysis. It is certainty manufactured from insinuation.

Republican Submission Needs No Secret Tape

The strongest institutional fact in the piece is also the one buried beneath espionage melodrama: Republican officials once dismissed Trump, then accepted his domination of their party. Matt Schlapp’s September 2015 prediction that Trump would not become the nominee illustrates how badly party insiders misunderstood the forces reshaping their coalition. It does not prove they were later blackmailed.

Republican leaders had visible power throughout this transformation. They could oppose Trump, deny him institutional protection or refuse to treat personal loyalty as a condition of political survival. The article itself says they failed to challenge him until too late. That account points toward opportunism, cowardice and incentives—not necessarily clandestine Russian control.

A theory of kompromat can become a convenient absolution. It recasts powerful officials making advantageous choices as helpless victims of an unseen hand.

Suspicion Is Not Proof

Trump’s July 27, 2016 statement—“Russia, if you’re listening”—was public and politically consequential. The article also says Russian hackers accessed Republican and Democratic email systems but released Democratic material. Those details can support scrutiny of Russian interference, Trump’s rhetoric and the media’s response.

They do not, by themselves, establish that Trump knew what hackers were doing. The article’s phrase “It’s almost as if” performs the work of an allegation while avoiding the burden of proving it.

The same problem infects its use of the Steele dossier. The article describes the dossier as having “revealed” Kremlin cultivation, although the text supplies no corroboration for that claim and does not distinguish allegation from established fact. It similarly invokes a 2017 Politico report about Soviet interest in cultivating Trump after his July 1987 Moscow visit, but leaps from reported interest to successful ownership. Identifying a target is not proof of controlling one.

The Media Critique Is Stronger Than the Spy Story

The article is on firmer ground when it describes a media system unsure how to handle Trump’s statements and drawn toward spectacle. Its contention that ratings incentives encouraged equivocation is an interpretation, but at least it identifies a recognizable power structure: news organizations decide what to amplify, whom to normalize and when provocation becomes programming.

Yet the piece reproduces the same defect it condemns. Sensationalism displaces disciplined judgment. Nicknames, unsupported accusations and speculative links generate heat while weakening the case against actors whose documented conduct should be examined with precision.

Propaganda is not defeated by adopting its appetite for total explanations.

Accusation as Misdirection

The article calls Republicans traitors, suggests undisclosed crimes may have been captured on video, speculates about names in “Epstein Files,” and claims that House Speaker Mike Johnson shut down the House until after the midterms. It offers no supporting evidence for those assertions within the supplied text. It also links Trump, Benjamin Netanyahu, Gaza, NATO, Washington and Robert F. Kennedy Jr. to Putin’s supposed design without establishing the necessary causal chain.

These are not minor rhetorical flourishes. They move responsibility away from observable decisions and into an all-purpose conspiracy. Netanyahu’s actions, congressional choices, Trump’s statements and Republican obedience each involve identifiable actors exercising power. Explaining them all through Putin risks erasing their agency.

When officials choose cruelty, impunity or institutional surrender because those choices serve their interests, inventing a puppet master can obscure more than it reveals.

The System Does Not Require a Kremlin Remote Control

The larger pattern is less cinematic and more damning. Republican officials underestimated Trump, then accommodated him. Media institutions amplified him while struggling to distinguish spectacle from consequence. Political actors discovered that loyalty could be rewarded and resistance punished. None of that requires secret blackmail to be politically dangerous.

Investigating foreign influence is legitimate. Declaring treason without evidence is not analysis; it is a substitute for it. The systemic error here is the belief that institutional collapse must have an exotic explanation. Powerful people often enable destructive outcomes openly, for ordinary reasons: ambition, fear, access and the refusal to surrender power.
