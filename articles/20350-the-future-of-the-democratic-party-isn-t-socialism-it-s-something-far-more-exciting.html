The future of the Democratic Party isn't socialism — it's something far more exciting

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosJuly 2, 20262 min read

The Vote Was Against Stagnation, Not a Slogan

The Easy Story

The piece wants you to believe this is about the rise of Democratic Socialism. That is the cleanest available headline, and also the least honest one. The actual story in the source is more basic and more damaging to the old guard: long-entrenched Democrats lost to younger challengers who sold competence, energy, and an end to political drift.

Where Power Actually Sat

The power in these races was not with the insurgents. It was with incumbents like Diana DeGette, who held her seat for nearly 30 years, and with the broader Democratic establishment that normalizes that kind of permanence. The source keeps circling the DSA as if the label itself explains the outcomes. It does not. What explains the outcomes is that voters were done rewarding people who had turned office into tenure.

What the Winners Sold

The successful challengers did not win by offering a theory seminar. Melat Kiros talked about getting money out of politics and treating Trump as a symptom, not the disease. Manny Rutinel won by looking younger, sharper, and more responsive than a moderate who argued she had “broader appeal.” Zohran Mamdani, Katie Wilson, and Janeese Lewis George all centered housing, childcare, transit, public services, and the plain fact that government is supposed to function.

That is the substance. The ideology label is garnish.

The Misdirection

The source keeps leaning on youth, immigrant background, gender, and DSA membership as if those are the central political facts. They matter, but the piece uses them to obscure the deeper conflict: people are rejecting an exhausted political class that has not earned trust. It is convenient to frame this as a generational and demographic narrative because it sounds hopeful. It is less convenient to say the quiet part: the old political order failed at basic delivery and then asked for gratitude.

The article also repeats the familiar trick of treating the energy around these campaigns as proof of a new movement, when it may be proof of disgust with incumbency, big corporate money, and hollow management. That is not the same thing.

The Pattern Beneath the Wins

These victories point to a recurring pattern in American politics: when institutions stop delivering, voters look for people who sound like they mean to break something. Not because voters have become doctrinaire, but because they have become impatient with ceremonial politics. They want builders and fighters, not custodians of failure.

The source thinks the headline is “Democratic Socialism is rising.” The more serious reading is uglier: when a party protects stale power for too long, it hands insurgents a lane. The brand changes. The machinery stays broken until someone forces it open.
