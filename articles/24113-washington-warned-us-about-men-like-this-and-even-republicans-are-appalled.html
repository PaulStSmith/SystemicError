The Presidency Is Not Trump’s Advertising Account

Public Money, Private Glorification

The reporting describes a federally funded television ad aired during three Fox programs that features Donald Trump, vows to defeat “communism, socialism and Marxism,” includes praise from UFC executive Dana White, and ends with the disclosure: “Paid for by the U.S. government.” The White House calls it a nonpolitical public-service announcement because Trump is not on the ballot. That defense avoids the central issue: public resources were reportedly used to promote the sitting president as a singular national champion.

The White House Made the Choice

The institutional power here belongs to the executive branch. Television viewers did not appropriate the money, Fox’s audience did not commission the ad, and the musician whose work appeared in it says he authorized no political use. Administration officials decided that government-funded communication should center Trump’s image, voice and personal mythology.

Calling the result “educational” does not answer what the public was supposedly being taught. The reported content does not merely explain a program or tell citizens how to obtain a service. It presents Trump as uniquely strong and resilient, then attaches the federal government’s authority and money to that presentation. Whether this spending violated federal law is a legal question the article does not resolve. Its political function, as described, is much easier to see.

“Not on the Ballot” Is a Semantic Escape Hatch

The White House’s defense treats politics as something that exists only during an election campaign. That is convenient nonsense. Presidents accumulate power, define enemies and cultivate loyalty while governing. A message does not become apolitical simply because no vote is scheduled beside the president’s name.

The article reports that Democratic Representative Jamie Raskin called the ad unethical under federal provisions including the Hatch Act. Republican Senators John Kennedy and Thom Tillis also objected to spending public money on what they regarded as personal promotion; Tillis compared the practice to Viktor Orbán’s. Those statements establish bipartisan criticism, not a judicial finding. But they expose the weakness of the administration’s excuse: the dispute concerns control of state resources, not campaign-calendar technicalities.

The Contradiction Is the Message

The ad reportedly promises victory over communism while Trump, on the same day, welcomed Chinese President Xi Jinping on a red carpet. That juxtaposition does not prove a hidden allegiance or motive. It demonstrates something more mundane and politically useful: “communism” functions in the ad as a domestic label of condemnation, not as a coherent standard governing presidential conduct.

The enemy words are aimed at an American audience. They sort citizens into loyalists and suspects while the president’s own diplomacy proceeds according to other calculations. The inconsistency is not explained in the government-funded message because explanation is not its apparent purpose. The message supplies a hero, a collection of ideological villains and the prestige of the state.

Congress and Enforcement Agencies Are Not Spectators

The article’s weakest framing appears when it approaches this conduct as Trump’s personal appetite for flattery. Vanity may describe the aesthetic; it does not explain the machinery. Federal money must be appropriated, contracts administered, legal objections handled or ignored, and government property placed under official control. A personality cult cannot become state practice without officials who approve it and institutions that decline to stop it.

The reporting lists other alleged or documented acts of presidential branding, including large Trump banners on federal buildings and administration initiatives carrying his name. It also says federal appropriations measures have long restricted spending on “publicity or propaganda.” But the decisive missing element, as the article itself recognizes, is enforcement. Congress possesses oversight and spending powers. Executive-branch lawyers and the Justice Department possess investigative or enforcement authority within their lawful jurisdictions. If those institutions refuse scrutiny, their passivity enables the result.

The Systemic Error Is Personal Government

A republic distinguishes the office from its temporary occupant. Government information serves a public function; personal advertising serves the official featured in it. Once those categories are deliberately blurred, taxpayer funding becomes a tool for teaching citizens that loyalty to the leader is loyalty to the country.

That is the larger pattern revealed by the reported ad. The danger lies not only in one broadcast or one politician’s self-regard, but in an institutional class willing to convert an abuse of public resources into an argument over branding etiquette. The White House supplies the euphemism, allied media supply the audience, and inactive oversight supplies the permission. Personal government is built through precisely that division of labor.
