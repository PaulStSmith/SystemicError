The verdict is in —and it’s time for MAGA to beg for forgiveness

Based on an article by D. Earl Stephens

Paulo SantosNovember 17, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

An unfinished election becomes a blank checkCollective contempt obscures institutional responsibilityAccountability cannot mean “extermination”Reconciliation is not the immediate question

AlterNet columnist D. Earl Stephens says MAGA must beg forgiveness before any political reconciliation can begin. But the “verdict” promised by his headline never arrives. His column supplies no completed election result and identifies no actual judgment rendered against Trump’s movement. Instead, it converts an election still being finished — with an Alaska Senate contest described only as hanging in the balance — into a sweeping moral mandate, then substitutes collective abuse for an account of who exercised power, through which institutions, and with what consequences.

That is not accountability. It is catharsis dressed as a news judgment.

An unfinished election becomes a blank check

Stephens writes as Alaska appears to lean toward electing a Native American woman to the U.S. Senate and as the country completes what he calls extraordinarily consequential midterm elections. The column does not name the candidate, provide results or establish that the outcome produced the repudiation he claims. Nevertheless, he declares that “the vast majority of the American people have spoken” and are ready to leave Trump behind.

The supplied reporting does not support that conclusion. An election can weaken a political movement, deny it offices or expose the limits of its appeal. It cannot establish that every voter shares one motive, much less authorize a columnist to speak for “tens of millions” without evidence presented here.

Stephens is right to reject the familiar ritual in which powerful officials invoke reconciliation before investigating misconduct or repairing harm. Calls to “move on” have often protected officeholders, donors and institutions from consequences. But his column does not distinguish reconciliation from impunity. It demands abasement from a mass of voters while leaving the machinery of power largely offstage.

Collective contempt obscures institutional responsibility

The column’s bill of particulars includes support for Trump, participation by some supporters in the Capitol attack, resistance to masks during the pandemic, tolerance of corruption, hostility to science and book bans. It also accuses MAGA supporters of celebrating lethal government violence and accepting the return of measles. These are serious claims, but Stephens assigns them indiscriminately to “you” — a political population treated as a single culpable body.

That framing misdirects scrutiny. Voters can enable political harm, and those who joined an attack bear responsibility for their own conduct. But voters did not all possess the same authority as the president, lawmakers, party officials, agency heads, wealthy donors or corporate interests that Stephens mentions only near the end. The people with institutional power made decisions, financed campaigns, controlled government machinery and shaped the information environment. Collapsing them into one despised crowd blurs crucial differences between architects, beneficiaries, operatives and followers.

Stephens ultimately gestures toward “billionaires” and “crooked corporations” that he says have polluted Congress and taken government from the public. Yet his fury remains concentrated on ordinary MAGA adherents, especially white men. The result is politically convenient for the powerful: structural decisions disappear into a morality play about the personal depravity of millions of people.

Accountability cannot mean “extermination”

Stephens repeatedly says he wants justice rather than accommodation. He calls for defending science, books, equal rights and truth, and for confronting corruption instead of ignoring it. Those are recognizable political objectives. But he sabotages them with language that treats opponents not as people subject to law, organizing and democratic defeat, but as contamination to be removed.

He writes that Trump’s movement must be “dead and gone,” threatens to cut its members into “tiny bite-sized pieces” with words, warns Democrats against appeasing rather than “exterminating” evil, compares supporters to Confederates and Nazis, and tells them it is “time to meet your maker.” In context, some of this is plainly figurative invective. It is still reckless. A demand for accountability should specify investigations, prosecutions where evidence warrants them, electoral defeat, removal from office, institutional reform or restitution. Stephens instead offers humiliation and vaguely described “punishment.”

That choice matters because dehumanization is not redeemed by the speaker’s preferred policy agenda. Calling people “worthless,” describing them as diseased and imagining them scrubbed clean with a steel brush does nothing to establish legal culpability. It replaces standards with tribal permission: the righteous may speak without restraint because the condemned deserve whatever follows.

The most striking contradiction is Stephens’s insistence that truth must displace lies while his own argument outruns the facts he presents. He calls Trump a rapist, felon and thief without providing the legal or factual context necessary to distinguish adjudication, conviction and accusation. He describes the Capitol attack as the worst assault on that building since the War of 1812, but uses the conduct of participants to condemn an entire voting bloc. He demands precision from his enemies and exempts himself from it.

Reconciliation is not the immediate question

Stephens frames the choice as appeasement or vengeance. That is a false constraint. A government can investigate crimes without assigning hereditary guilt to a political identity. It can protect public health without declaring dissenters subhuman. It can defeat an authoritarian movement without adopting that movement’s habit of dividing the population into the pure and the disposable.

Nor does forgiveness belong chiefly to a columnist. People directly harmed by violence, discrimination, official neglect or corruption decide for themselves whether forgiveness is possible. Public institutions have a different obligation: establish facts, apply law consistently, constrain abusive power and prevent recurrence. An apology from a MAGA voter cannot substitute for any of those tasks, just as refusing an apology cannot accomplish them.

Stephens’s anger points toward real political conflicts over democratic government, public health, racial equality, corruption and truth. His column then evacuates those conflicts of institutional substance. Who authorized abuses? Who funded the officials involved? Which laws failed, which agencies complied and which officeholders escaped consequences? Those questions would locate power. Demanding that millions crawl forward and beg instead locates a target.

The supposed verdict, then, is not an election result established by the material AlterNet published. It is Stephens’s own sentence: collective guilt, compulsory contrition and indefinite contempt. That may satisfy a desire to reverse the humiliation Trumpism imposed on its targets. It does not explain how the people who wielded power will be made to surrender it.
