‘Outright worse’: Expert warns Trump’s tax bill crosses line even Bush avoided

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 22, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The GOP's Tax Plan: A Masterclass in Fiscal Inequity

FILE PHOTO: U.S. President Donald Trump and Speaker of the House Mike Johnson shake hands during a House Republican members conference meeting in Trump National Doral resort, in Miami, Florida, U.S. January 27, 2025. REUTERS/Elizabeth Frantz/File Photo

President Donald Trump's "big, beautiful bill" on tax cuts, energy deregulation, and border security is in many ways a repeat of past enormous GOP tax cuts for the wealthy — but there's something different about this one compared to recent similar bills, wrote Mike Konczal of the Economic Security Project.

Specifically, he wrote, the George W. Bush tax cuts, and Trump's own first major tax cut bill in 2017, cut taxes across the board, giving the overwhelming majority of savings to the highest-income earners but at least giving some small tax relief to lower-income Americans as well. This time, however, the bill raises taxes on the lowest-income earners, with the bottom quintile seeing their take-home pay fall by $1,000.

"Even though conservatives are still blowing out the deficit, this time they aren’t even bothering to bring everyone along," Konczal wrote. "Instead, those at the bottom are outright worse off. The cuts to spending programs, especially Medicaid and SNAP, are severe. Estimates suggest at least the bottom two quintiles, 40%+ of Americans, will experience a clear loss in income. This isn't a subtle debate over which basis to judge the proportionality of tax cuts: millions will simply have less money, even as the bill adds $3.8 trillion to the deficit."

Moreover, this doesn't even factor in the effect of Trump's tariffs, which, even after he has eased off his most draconian plans, amount to a significant tax increase on purchasing goods and services, that will heavily impact lower-income households.

In essence, Konczal wrote, Republicans have brought back their obsession with what they call the "Lucky Duckies" — people who are so poor they don't pay income taxes, which in their view means they aren't contributing to society. Such lower-income people pay a much higher share of their income on things like sales taxes, and often struggle to afford basic necessities.

In 2002, amid the debate over Bush's tax cuts, following a conservative op-ed using the term, Konczal wrote, "'Lucky Ducky' became a meme ... popularized by the cartoonist Ruben Bolling. The notion that the bottom half of income earners were politically suspect because they paid no income taxes, and they needed to be paying more in taxes even as taxes came down for the top half, showed up from time to time, but was rarely spoken out loud outside conservative think tanks and private donor meetings."

This persisted in the GOP for a long time. Famously, Mitt Romney complained behind closed doors on a hot mic that he'd never be able to win the "47 percent" who don't pay income taxes, and it contributed to the failure of his 2012 presidential campaign. However, for many years, this attitude was less emphasized under Trump, who, at least in his rhetoric, glorified certain struggling working-class groups and sought to scapegoat their problems.

"I expected it to be buried in the Trump years, with his right-wing populism and appeals to working people," wrote Konczal. "But apparently not."

Our Analysis:

The GOP's Tax Plan: A Masterclass in Fiscal Inequity

In the latest iteration of what can only be deemed a grotesque fiscal policy, President Donald Trump's touted "big, beautiful bill" on tax cuts, alongside energy deregulation and border security, emerges not merely as a continuation but as an exacerbation of the GOP's long-standing tradition of fattening the wealthy at the expense of the impoverished. Mike Konczal of the Economic Security Project adeptly points out the stark deviation this bill makes from its predecessors — a deviation that plunges the lowest-income earners into deeper financial distress.

Historical Echoes, Amplified Inequities

The George W. Bush tax cuts, and Trump's inaugural major tax cut bill in 2017, while disproportionately benefiting the highest-income earners, at least feigned a semblance of universal benefit by providing minimal tax relief across all income levels. This facade of benevolence has been unceremoniously stripped away in Trump's latest proposal. Here, we witness a bill that unabashedly raises taxes on the lowest-income earners, with the bottom quintile predicted to see their take-home pay slashed by $1,000. Konczal's analysis sheds light on a bill that not only fails to "bring everyone along" but actively worsens the financial state of those at the bottom.

A Cruel Irony: Cutting from the Poor to Sustain the Deficit

The bill's cuts to essential spending programs such as Medicaid and SNAP are described as severe, a characterization that might still fall short of capturing the full impact on the bottom two quintiles of Americans. These individuals, representing over 40% of the population, stand to experience a clear loss in income. It's a perverse twist of fiscal policy — millions will have less money in their pockets, even as the bill contributes an additional $3.8 trillion to the federal deficit. This isn't merely a policy misstep; it's a structured demolition of financial stability for the most vulnerable, all in the service of tax cuts that balloon the wealth of the richest.

The "Lucky Duckies" Fallacy Revisited

Konczal points to the GOP's revived disdain for the so-called "Lucky Duckies" — those too impoverished to owe income taxes and thus deemed by some conservatives as not contributing their "fair share" to society. This perspective grotesquely overlooks the disproportionate burden these individuals bear in terms of sales taxes and the struggle to afford basic necessities. The term "Lucky Duckies," once a meme, reveals a deep-seated ideological cancer within conservative fiscal policy, marking the poor not as citizens to be uplifted but as drains on resources.

Romney's Echo and the GOP's Unchanged Tune

The article invokes Mitt Romney's infamous "47 percent" gaffe, highlighting a continuity in GOP rhetoric that views a significant portion of the American populace with disdain. This attitude, seemingly dormant during the Trump era's veneer of right-wing populism, has resurfaced with a vengeance, laying bare the Republican Party's unwavering commitment to policies that exacerbate inequality.

Conclusion: A Path Forward or a Deepening Chasm?

The analysis provided by Mike Konczal is not just a critique but a clarion call to recognize the GOP's latest tax plan for what it is — a deliberate act of economic warfare against the nation's most vulnerable. As this bill moves through the legislative process, its provisions must be scrutinized and opposed by all who believe in a fair and equitable society. The question now is whether this call will be heeded, or if the chasm between the wealthy and the poor will be allowed to widen further. The GOP's strategy is clear; the response of the American people, and their representatives, will reveal the true character of the nation.
