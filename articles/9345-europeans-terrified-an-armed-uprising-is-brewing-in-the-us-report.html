Europeans terrified 'an armed uprising' is brewing in the US: report

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 23, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Correspondent Andrew Buncombe tells The I Paper that our neighbors across the ocean are half-expecting a second Civil War to break out in the U.S. if tempers continue to flare.

The assassination of MAGA activist Charlie Kirk prompted many to demand an end to political violence and the rhetoric that led to it. But President Donald Trump blamed the “radical left” for the majority of political violence: “[Kirk] did not hate his opponents, he wanted the best for them. That’s where I disagreed with Charlie. I hate my opponents, and I don’t want the best for them, I’m sorry.”

Buncombe says British readers were already aware of violence escalating in the U.S., including an attack on Minnesota legislators and an April arson incident at the home of Pennsylvania’s Gov. Josh Shapiro. Then Trump seized on Kirk’s killing to justify sending troops into cities such as Chicago, Portland, and threatened to invade New York.

His invasion has spurred local outcry from state governors like Illinois Gov. JB Pritzker, who accused Trump of following a playbook to “cause chaos, create fear and confusion, make it seem like peaceful protesters are a mob by firing gas pellets and tear gas canisters at them … to create the pretext for invoking the Insurrection Act so that he can send the military to our city.”

The Insurrection Act allows Trump to call up federal troops to put down domestic rebellion or insurrection, and U.S. courts could allow Trump to paint anything he wants as an “insurrection.”

“An armed uprising,” reports Buncombe, “is entirely possible.”

Benjamin Jensen, a senior fellow at the Centre for Strategic and International Studies, tells The i Paper that the definition for a civil war used by modern scholars is a conflict in which at least 1,000 people are killed. But what’s more likely is “something similar to the 1791 armed uprising known as the Whiskey Rebellion,” which lasted two years, with hundreds of rebels taking up arms, with casualties.

Jensen said the rebellion could take the form of “sporadic acts of violence that will not be neatly defined by right or left but that stop short of becoming formal, organized parties locked in civil conflict.”

When asked to name a circumstance in which a violent conflict might take place, Coventry University political sociology professor Joel Busher proffered Republicans refusing to step down after losing “the next election.”

“Would they accept the result and support the process of democratic transition?” asked Busher. “I hope so, but if they did not, it is not beyond the realms of possibility that decentralized networks of armed actors would seek to mete out violence against their political enemies – Democratic lawmakers, minorities identified as being somehow less ‘American’.”

“If such violence were not condemned by senior Republicans, we would be looking at a potential civil war scenario,” Busher said.

Our Analysis:

Dissecting the Precipice of Civil Unrest: A Critical Analysis

In an article that could easily be mistaken for a script from a dystopian thriller, Andrew Buncombe of The I Paper sketches a United States teetering on the brink of internal collapse. The assassination of MAGA activist Charlie Kirk is the pivot around which current fears of a second Civil War orbit. But as we peel back the layers of Buncombe’s reporting, a more complex and damning picture of American politics and society emerges, one marked by political violence, inflammatory rhetoric, and an alarming readiness to escalate tensions.

The Spark in the Powder Keg

The killing of Charlie Kirk is not just a tragic event but a catalyst that exposes the deep fissures running through the American political landscape. President Donald Trump’s response, as reported, is nothing short of fuel to the fire. By blaming the “radical left” for the majority of political violence and openly admitting to hating his opponents, Trump not only deepens the divide but also sanctions a dangerous us-versus-them mentality.

This isn’t merely about the loss of civility in political discourse; it’s the deliberate construction of a narrative where political opponents are not just wrong but are to be despised and, implicitly, targeted. Trump’s remarks, as noted in the article, are not just reckless; they are irresponsible and potentially incendiary.

The Insurrection Act Gambit

Trump’s threat to send troops into cities like Chicago, Portland, and New York under the guise of protecting public order is a stark reminder of the thin line between law enforcement and military rule. Illinois Governor JB Pritzker’s accusation that Trump is aiming to “cause chaos, create fear and confusion” is a chilling interpretation of the situation, suggesting a strategy not of governance but of tactical manipulation.

The invocation of the Insurrection Act as a potential tool to quell dissent reveals a disturbing readiness to militarize political disagreements. This isn’t about ensuring safety; it’s about asserting control through intimidation.

The Shadow of Civil War

The suggestion of an armed uprising, as discussed by Benjamin Jensen and Joel Busher, moves the conversation from the realm of political hyperbole to a tangible, if still distant, possibility. The comparison to the Whiskey Rebellion and the focus on “sporadic acts of violence” point to a scenario where the fabric of American society is torn, not by organized military conflict, but by widespread civil unrest and guerrilla tactics.

The critical question raised by Busher regarding the potential refusal of Republicans to accept an electoral defeat is a stark one. It’s not just about the outcome of one election but about the fundamental principles of democratic governance. The prospect of “decentralized networks of armed actors” engaging in violence against perceived enemies is a dire warning of what happens when political leaders not only fail to condemn violence but implicitly encourage it.

Conclusion: A Systemic Failure

The article from The I Paper, while focusing on the immediate aftermath of Charlie Kirk’s assassination, touches on issues that go far beyond a single event. This is a narrative of a nation at war with itself, where political violence is not just a threat but a reality, where the rhetoric of hate is not just tolerated but propagated by those in power, and where the mechanisms designed to protect democracy are turned against it.

The potential for civil war, whether it manifests as organized conflict or as widespread unrest, is a symptom of a deeper malady: a systemic failure to address the root causes of division and to uphold the principles of democracy and civil discourse. If the current trajectory continues, the United States risks not just a political crisis but a fundamental breakdown of the social order.
