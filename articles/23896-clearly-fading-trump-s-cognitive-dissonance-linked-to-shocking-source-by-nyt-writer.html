'Clearly fading': Trump's 'cognitive dissonance' linked to shocking source by NYT writer

The Bubble Is Not the Government

A Diagnosis That Lets Power Off the Hook

The reported conversation offers a seductive explanation for Donald Trump’s conduct: he lied so persistently that he may have entered an alternate reality of his own construction. That is speculation, not an established medical or psychological fact. More importantly, it converts a political problem into a personal pathology.

Trump is not merely an aging performer trapped inside his mythology. He is the president. The relevant question is not whether he believes his falsehoods, but what he does with the institutional power attached to them.

The President Has Agency

David French’s theory blurs the distinction between deception and delusion. Politically, that blur is convenient. A liar can be held responsible for manipulating others; a confused man becomes an object of diagnosis, pity, or palace intrigue.

Whether Trump privately believes any particular claim does not erase agency. He chooses what to say, whom to reward, and which version of reality the presidency will promote. Falling asleep at a memorial may illustrate fatigue or indifference, but it does not explain the decisions made while he is awake.

A Bubble Built by Enablers

Michelle Goldberg points to Trump’s increasingly narrow world and fading political instincts. But presidential isolation is not weather. A political bubble requires people willing to maintain it: advisers who filter information, allies who repeat falsehoods, and a movement that made loyalty to one man more valuable than contact with reality.

The source does not identify those enablers, and that omission matters. “He’s in a bubble” makes the structure sound self-created and self-contained. In practice, powerful institutions and ambitious subordinates benefit from keeping a leader insulated from contradiction.

MAGA’s Crisis Is Transactional

The reported erosion of trust within Trump’s base is presented as evidence that his salesmanship is failing. That framing mistakes political loyalty for consumer confidence.

The larger problem is not that Trump can no longer sell the myth as effectively. It is that a mass political movement accepted serial deception while it delivered identity, enemies, and power. Distrust emerging only when the performance becomes less convincing is not accountability. It is dissatisfaction with the product.

Failure Was Never Disqualifying

Goldberg correctly locates failure inside Trump’s established persona, invoking his casino bankruptcies and the thinning mythology surrounding him as present catastrophes accumulate. But failure alone does not remove a president. Institutions do—or refuse to.

Poll numbers can weaken political leverage, but they are not a substitute for institutional restraint. Treating Trump’s decline as a branding collapse centers the durability of his image when the consequential issue is the durability of the machinery available to him.

The Comfort of Calling It Confusion

The cognitive-decline framing offers elites an escape from a harsher conclusion: sustained falsehood can be an instrument of rule even when it becomes erratic, repetitive, or self-defeating. A leader does not need a coherent grasp of reality to impose consequences on people who lack comparable power.

The systemic pattern is institutional cowardice disguised as psychological curiosity. Commentators inspect the president’s inner world while the presidency remains an engine of material decisions. The bubble is interesting. The power operating from inside it is what matters.
