‘Fundamentally ignorant:’ Ex-Treasury chief torches Trump's 'downright scary' math skills

Paulo SantosAugust 6, 20254 min read

Former Treasury Department official Steven Rattner ripped into President Donald Trump for his “economic ignorance” Tuesday in a scathing op-ed published in The New York Times, pointing to what he described as the president’s “foolhardy tariffs.”

“Now we have a president who is fundamentally ignorant of the most basic and incontrovertible economic principles, as evidenced in his latest round of foolhardy tariffs (and in so many other ways),” Rattner wrote.

Trump’s so-called ‘reciprocal tariffs’ are set to resume on Aug. 7 following a 90-day pause, with the economic impacts already being felt across the American economy — prices have increased, job growth has slowed dramatically and inflation has ticked up. And yet, despite being “told over and over again by economists” that his tariff policy would harm American consumers, Rattner argues that Trump’s actions “reflect no rhyme or reason.”

“Mr. Trump’s ignorance goes far beyond the tariffs-are-a-tax concept,” Rattner wrote. “He believes trade deficits are tantamount to ‘losing’ money to other countries. Losing money is what happens when $100 falls out of your wallet. When you spend $100 to buy new earbuds made in China, you haven’t lost it; you’ve spent it on earbuds.”

Trump’s “economic ignorance,” Rattner argued, extended well beyond his tariff policy. On inflation, which Rattner says Trump “seems not to understand,” the president continues to proclaim there to be “no inflation,” despite inflation ticking up by 2.6% over the previous year.

The federal deficit, which Trump has regularly pledged to reduce, is only projected to increase by a projected $3.4 trillion over the next decade as a result of his signature budget reconciliation bill, known as the One Big Beautiful Bill Act.

And on interest rates, Trump continues to “demonstrate his economic ignorance,” Rattner argued, by continuing to threaten to remove Federal Reserve Chair Jerome Powell, a move that Rattner characterized as the president’s “potentially most dangerous transgression.”

“Mr. Trump insists that our interest rates are too high and should be as low as Europe’s (2% versus our 4.5%),” Rattner wrote.

“Yet when he pronounces our economy ‘the strongest in the world,’ as he regularly does, he is unconsciously citing one of the reasons for our higher interest rates: Robust economies need higher interest rates to restrain inflation… Mr. Powell’s term is coming to an end next year, and the prospect of Mr. Trump picking his successor is downright scary.”

Our Analysis:

Trump's Economic Follies: A Scathing Review by Steven Rattner

Steven Rattner's op-ed in The New York Times doesn't just scratch the surface of President Donald Trump's economic policies; it takes a deep dive into the abyss of what he describes as Trump's "economic ignorance." Rattner, a former Treasury Department official, zeroes in on Trump's tariff policies, which he rightly labels as "foolhardy." But the criticism doesn't stop there. Rattner's analysis cuts through the façade of economic prowess often projected by the Trump administration, laying bare a troubling lack of understanding of basic economic principles.

Foolhardy Tariffs and Their Consequences

Let's start with the so-called 'reciprocal tariffs.' Despite a 90-day pause, their resumption on August 7 is already causing ripples through the American economy. Prices are climbing, job growth is faltering, and inflation has seen an uptick. Yet, Trump seems impervious to the economic consensus that tariffs, in essence, are a tax on American consumers. This stubborn insistence on policies that clearly harm the very people he claims to champion is perplexing at best and disastrous at worst.

The Misconception of Trade Deficits

Rattner points out Trump's misunderstanding of trade deficits, a fundamental concept that the president equates to "losing" money. This analogy of dropping $100 from your wallet versus spending it on goods, like earbuds, illustrates the transactional nature of trade that Trump seems to miss. It's not about losing but exchanging money for value. This basic misconception underpins a flawed approach to international trade and economics at large.

Inflation, Deficits, and Interest Rates: A Trio of Misunderstandings

Trump's pronouncements on inflation, federal deficits, and interest rates further display a disconcerting disconnect from economic realities. Despite evidence to the contrary, Trump's claims of "no inflation" fly in the face of a 2.6% increase over the previous year. His pledges to reduce the federal deficit seem hollow against projections of a $3.4 trillion increase due to his One Big Beautiful Bill Act.

Perhaps most concerning is Trump's stance on interest rates and his threats against Federal Reserve Chair Jerome Powell. Rattner points out the contradiction in Trump's desire for lower interest rates akin to Europe's while boasting of the U.S. economy's strength — a factor that, in a healthy economy, necessitates higher rates to curb inflation. Trump's lack of understanding here isn't just a faux pas; it's a potential threat to the stability of the U.S. and global economy, especially with Powell's term ending and Trump's hand poised to pick his successor.

Conclusion: A Dangerous Blend of Ignorance and Power

Rattner's critique of Trump's economic policies isn't just an academic exercise; it's a stark warning. The combination of significant power in the hands of someone with a fundamental misunderstanding of basic economic principles is more than troubling—it's potentially catastrophic. The tariffs, the misconceptions about trade deficits, inflation denial, misguided fiscal policies, and the cavalier attitude toward interest rates and Federal Reserve leadership all paint a picture of a presidency dangerously detached from economic reality.

In this light, Rattner's op-ed isn't just scathing; it's a clarion call for a return to informed, evidence-based economic policymaking. Ignorance, especially when wielded from a position of power, is not just a personal failing; it's a systemic threat.
