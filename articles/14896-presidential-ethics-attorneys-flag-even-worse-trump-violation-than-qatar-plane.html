Norman Eisen, Virginia Canter, and Richard W. Painter, served as ethics counsels in the Clinton, George W. Bush, and Obama White Houses.

They aren’t just talking about the $400 million from Qatar. The three believe “his crypto entanglements are just as bad — perhaps even worse.”

First, they addressed the plane: “This arrangement has been blessed by his White House counsel, David Warrington, and his attorney general, Pam Bondi, on the theory that Mr. Trump is not getting the plane, the United States is.”

Claiming “Mr. Trump would be personally benefiting from the use of the plane while in office,” the ethics experts say, “this is a gift to him, notwithstanding the rationalizations offered by administration lawyers.”

Perhaps more concerning is “Trump’s other crypto ventures, like his Trump meme coin.” They said this move can “provide a virtually limitless opportunity for foreign governments to pump emoluments to Mr. Trump.”

The venture makes it easy for any foreign government “to put cash in World Liberty Financial.” The cryptocurrency was founded by Trump 2024 and managed by Trump’s sons, Don Jr. and Eric.”

“This also runs afoul of the foreign emoluments clause,” the writers said. Later adding, “and it captures why we have these rules in the first place. The conflict of interest is clear. How can we trust someone who is in charge of regulating crypto if he could benefit from lax regulation?”

This isn’t their only crypto concern. “Stunts like giving the top buyers of his $Trump meme coin personal access to him at a private dinner” are also a reason for pause.

Lastly, the writers believe Trump has “put White House entry up for sale, with the very largest purchasers offered a tour.”

They do admit he's not the first president to give donors access to the White House. “Donor sleepovers in the Lincoln Bedroom during the Clinton administration come to mind,” they said.

“The American people should make their disgust known,” they concluded, “There is no room for this kind of flagrant disregard of the Constitution in the skies above or in digital wallets down below.”

Our Analysis:

Trump's Constitutional Contempt: A Deep Dive into the Allegations

The recent guest essay in the New York Times by three former ethics attorneys—Norman Eisen, Virginia Canter, and Richard W. Painter—throws a harsh spotlight on President Donald Trump's latest ventures, painting a picture of a presidency marred by "flagrant disregard of the Constitution" in ways that are both brazen and disturbing. These attorneys, having served under Clinton, George W. Bush, and Obama, bring a wealth of experience and a bipartisan perspective to the table, making their critique all the more damning.

The $400 Million Qatar Quandary and Beyond

At the heart of their argument is not just the eyebrow-raising $400 million deal with Qatar but an intricate web of "crypto entanglements" that might be even more problematic. Let's dissect these accusations piece by piece, starting with the plane.

The Plane Problem

The claim here is as audacious as it is simple: Trump is benefiting personally from the use of a plane, an arrangement ostensibly for the United States but, in reality, a veiled gift to Trump himself. The legal justifications provided by White House counsel David Warrington and Attorney General Pam Bondi are dismissed as mere "rationalizations" by the ethics experts. The underlying issue? A direct benefit to Trump at the expense of constitutional integrity.

Crypto Controversies

Then there's the murky world of Trump's crypto ventures, notably the Trump meme coin. This isn't just a tale of digital currency; it's a narrative of potentially unlimited foreign influence, with foreign governments having a backdoor to funnel emoluments directly to Trump via World Liberty Financial. The involvement of Trump's sons, Don Jr. and Eric, in managing the cryptocurrency only thickens the plot, drawing a stark line under the conflict of interest inherent in Trump's position on crypto regulation.

The Emoluments Entanglement

What these former ethics counsels underscore is a blatant breach of the foreign emoluments clause, a cornerstone of America's constitutional framework designed to prevent precisely this kind of conflict of interest. The essence of their argument? How can Trump be trusted to regulate an industry from which he stands to benefit personally?

More Than Just Money

But wait, there's more. The essay also highlights how Trump's crypto ventures are intrinsically linked to personal access—pay for play on a disturbingly blatant level. The top buyers of his Trump meme coin are rewarded not just with digital currency, but with personal access to Trump himself. This commodification of presidential access is nothing new (the Clinton administration's "donor sleepovers" in the Lincoln Bedroom come to mind) but the directness and digital nature of Trump's approach bring a new level of transparency to the transactional nature of politics.

Conclusion: A Call to Arms

The conclusion drawn by Eisen, Canter, and Painter is clear and unequivocal: "There is no room for this kind of flagrant disregard of the Constitution". This isn't just a critique; it's a clarion call for public outrage and action, a reminder that the integrity of the presidency and the constitutional safeguards that protect it are not just abstract concepts but the bedrock of American democracy.

In the end, the article doesn't just invite scrutiny; it demands it. It's a piercing examination of the ways in which power, if left unchecked, can erode the foundational principles of governance. As observers, participants, and defenders of democracy, the American people are called upon not just to witness but to act. The skies above and the digital wallets below, as the authors poignantly note, must be safeguarded against the encroachments of those who would put personal gain above constitutional fidelity.
