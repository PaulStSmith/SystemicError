Harvard teacher reveals how Trump breaks reality

By Paulo Santos

Donald Trump and his MAGA allies repeatedly make claims that abuse basic math and break from reality, according to one mathematician and Harvard, who wrote on Thursday that the administration’s lofty claims shatter both truth and “mathematical possibility.”

Aubrey Clayton is a PhD mathematician who teaches subjects like probability, statistics and philosophy at Harvard University. Writing in an op-ed for the New York Times, he called out Trump and his allies for their use of numbers and statistics, which he claimed were divorced from reality and used only to make their arguments sound bigger.

As a prime example, Clayton cited Trump’s oft-repeated claims about how much he has brought down prescription drug prices. While the reality of those price drops is debatable on their own, the president has claimed multiple times to have them down by percentages well over 100. Most recently, he claimed a 600 percent drop during his State of the Union address. As Clayton pointed out, since a 100 percent drop in price would make the drugs free, Trump’s claims “would require a pharmaceutical company to pay you five times over to take a medication.”

“From the perspective of a math educator, this is not a minor abuse of statistics but a failure of epistemic responsibility, one that undermines the possibility of public reasoning itself,” Clayton wrote, later adding that these claims were meant to “short-circuit” public debate about his policies.

Clayton further highlighted Attorney General Pam Bondi’s claim that Trump’s anti-fentanyl policies had “saved 119 million American lives (later revised to 258 million),” a number only matched by the deadliest plagues in human history. He contested Trump’s claim that his tariffs had created $18 trillion in new investments for the U.S., “which would represent a rate of economic growth that dwarfed even the greatest periods of post-World War II expansion.” He also noted Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem‘s claim that the agency could deport up to 100 million people, despite that being roughly double the actual number of immigrants in the U.S.

“Elementary number sense and some understanding of the world reveal that these numbers are grossly out of scale,” Clayton argued, later adding, “These numbers go beyond plausibly being true to being inconsistent with known mathematical axioms and definitions.”

Clayton concluded his arguments by suggesting that Trump’s use of “absurd” statistical claims shows his disdain for the intelligence of Americans, since he does not expect them to be smart enough to debunk them.

“We can and must hold our political leaders to a higher standard,” he wrote. “When officials use absurd numbers, it shows that they believe that Americans are incapable of critiquing them.”

Our Analysis:

The Mathematical Malpractice of MAGA Misinformation

In a realm where numbers should illuminate truth, the Trump administration and its MAGA allies have chosen a path of mathematical mockery. Aubrey Clayton, a mathematician with the credentials to back his critique, has thrown a spotlight on this egregious trend in a recent op-ed for the New York Times. His analysis isn’t just an academic rebuke; it’s a clarion call to recognize the abuse of statistics as a deliberate strategy to befuddle and mislead.

The Art of the Impossible Claim

Trump’s assertion of reducing prescription drug prices by 600% isn’t just an error; it’s an impossibility that insults basic arithmetic. In a world governed by the laws of mathematics, a 100% reduction makes anything free. Trump’s fantasy of a 600% reduction would require pharmaceutical companies to pay consumers in a manner so absurd, it transcends the boundaries of rational discourse. Clayton’s critique here isn’t merely about the numbers; it’s about the blatant disregard for factual accuracy in public statements.

The Tall Tales of Tariffs and Triumphs

The claim that Trump’s tariffs have spurred $18 trillion in new investments is another flight of fancy. Such a figure not only distorts reality but also suggests a level of economic growth that is not just unprecedented but laughably infeasible. Similarly, Homeland Security’s outlandish claim of being able to deport up to 100 million people—a figure that doubles the actual number of immigrants in the U.S.—is not just an exaggeration. It’s a numerical nightmare, showcasing a detachment from demographic realities.

The Epistemic Erosion

Clayton’s most damning accusation is perhaps the intentional undermining of public reasoning. By spouting figures that are not just wrong but mathematically nonsensical, the MAGA machinery isn’t just lying. They’re engaging in an assault on epistemology itself, attempting to short-circuit debate by making the terrain of discussion utterly unrecognizable to reasoned argument.

An Insult to Intelligence

The underlying insinuation of these statistical stunts is as clear as it is insidious: a belittlement of the American public’s intelligence. Clayton posits that such absurdities are predicated on the belief that the populace won’t be critical enough to question them. This isn’t just a failure of factual representation; it’s a cynical manipulation of public discourse.

The Call to Mathematical Arms

Clayton’s closing argument is a rallying cry for accountability. In a landscape littered with falsehoods, the onus falls on us to demand more from our political leaders. The use of “absurd” statistics isn’t just a harmless quirk; it’s a deliberate strategy to demean and disorient the public consciousness.

In Conclusion

The article paints a damning picture of an administration comfortable with distorting reality to an extent that defies basic mathematics. It’s not just about the misuse of numbers; it’s about what this practice signifies—a contempt for truth and an attempt to erode the foundation of public debate. The MAGA approach to information is not just a political strategy; it’s an ethical failure that challenges the very premise of informed democracy. As Clayton argues, we must not let this slide. The integrity of our public discourse—and the respect owed to the intelligence of the American people—demands better.
