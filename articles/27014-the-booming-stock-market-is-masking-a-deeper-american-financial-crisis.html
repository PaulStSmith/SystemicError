The booming stock market is masking a deeper American financial crisis

Based on an article by The Conversation

Paulo SantosNovember 1, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the booming stock market and strong headline indicators are concealing widespread economic strain. Political scientist Erin Kay Lockwood argues that consumer spending, business investment, low unemployment and GDP growth do not capture how unevenly prosperity is distributed—or why consumer sentiment in 2026 remained 10% below its level a year earlier.

Inflation fell from 9.1% in June 2022 to 3.7% in September 2026, yet sentiment was lower than at the inflation peak. Inflation-adjusted wages rose between 2022 and the first half of 2026 before beginning to fall, while the richest 10% of Americans generated half of consumer spending and spending by the bottom 80% failed to keep pace with inflation.

That divide carries immediate political consequences. An October 2026 Pew Research survey found that 84% of registered voters considered the economy very important to their midterm vote. AlterNet reports that Republicans faced grim polling ahead of the November 3 election and that Democrats were expected to gain substantially, although those projections did not establish the election’s outcome.

The averages serve the winners

The apparent contradiction disappears once the economy is treated as a distribution of power rather than a national average. If affluent households can sustain aggregate spending while most earners lose purchasing power, “strong consumer spending” describes the volume of transactions without identifying who can afford them.

The stock market creates the same distortion. The top 0.1% of households hold five times as much wealth as the bottom half of the country. Rising asset prices therefore register as broad economic strength while delivering their largest gains to a narrow ownership class. The metric is accurate. The political use of it is misleading.

Power sits above the consumer

Ordinary households do not set tariff policy, determine the tax treatment of wealth or decide whether economic management will prioritize asset values over purchasing power. Those choices belong to elected officials and institutions with authority over trade, taxation and monetary policy.

In 2026, President Donald Trump and a Republican-controlled Congress held the most immediate electoral responsibility for federal economic policy. But the inequality described in the reporting grew over roughly two decades, narrowing briefly during the crises of 2008 and 2020 before widening again. That record implicates a durable governing structure, not a single election cycle.

“Vibes” is an evasion

Calling public pessimism “vibes” shifts scrutiny from economic arrangements to the supposed irrationality of the people living under them. The reporting supplies a more concrete explanation: falling real wages in recent months, spending that has failed to match inflation for the bottom 80%, and wealth gains concentrated among households that already own most financial assets.

Consumer sentiment may also reflect judgments about the country as a whole, not merely respondents’ personal finances. That is not confusion. Voters can recognize that an economy is producing impressive totals while distributing security, ownership and opportunity upward.

The ballot assigns responsibility

The governing party possesses the institutional power and therefore bears the immediate political exposure. If voters punish Republicans in the 2026 midterms, that would not prove any single economic theory, but it would show the limits of campaigning on aggregate growth while household insecurity remains widespread.

Democrats gaining seats would not itself repair the structure. Electoral backlash can change who administers an unequal economy without changing who benefits from it. The consequential question is whether lawmakers alter the distribution of income, wealth and risk—or simply wait for improving sentiment to rescue the same policies.

The crisis is political measurement

The deeper failure is not that conventional indicators are false. It is that officials routinely present them as sufficient. Aggregate growth can coexist with shrinking purchasing power for most earners; record markets can coexist with extreme wealth concentration; low unemployment can coexist with profound insecurity.

That framing protects the institutions and asset owners doing well under the current arrangement. It converts distributional conflict into a communications problem and material hardship into public misunderstanding. The system is not failing to produce wealth. It is producing wealth, concentrating it, and then citing the total as evidence that everyone else should feel better.
