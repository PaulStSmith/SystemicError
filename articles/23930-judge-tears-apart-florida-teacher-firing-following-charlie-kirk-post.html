Judge tears apart Florida teacher firing following Charlie Kirk post

Florida Tried to Turn Off-Hours Speech Into Professional Misconduct

Punishment Without Disruption

Florida sought professional sanctions against Duval County teacher Hope McMath over Facebook posts she made after Charlie Kirk was killed in September 2025. Yet the state’s case, as described in the administrative record, lacked evidence of the workplace harm it invoked: no student saw the posts, no teacher, administrator, parent, or student complained, and a district investigation found no disruption of school operations.

Administrative Law Judge Garnett Chisenhall therefore recommended dismissing charges that included gross immorality and conduct seriously reducing McMath’s effectiveness. His conclusion was blunt: the state presented no competent, substantial evidence that her posts impaired her service to the community.

The State Held the Power

McMath did not control anyone’s teaching certificate or employment. Florida education officials did. Shortly after Kirk’s killing, then-Education Commissioner Anastasios Kamoutsas reminded superintendents that he could discipline educators and sanction their certificates for qualifying personal conduct.

The district, meanwhile, gave McMath a verbal warning and reassigned her to an art resource center without teaching duties. According to the record, she has not returned to the classroom since the incident. Chisenhall said he saw no persuasive explanation for removing her from students.

That distinction matters. The central injury described here was not caused by adolescent exposure to disturbing material; the evidence indicated that no student encountered the posts. It arose from institutions exercising disciplinary power despite an absence of demonstrated educational harm.

An Imagined Audience Became the Pretext

The state argued that material trivializing a homicide and depicting the decapitation of a national figure could harm adolescent students who encountered it. That formulation quietly substitutes a hypothetical for the record. The case was not about students who actually encountered McMath’s posts. None did, according to the evidence presented.

The same substitution appears in the school board policy adopted that September. It declared that publicly accessible “inappropriate or unbecoming” material has the potential to disrupt education and damage the district’s reputation. Potential harm is an elastic standard: officials can invoke what speech might cause while avoiding the harder burden of showing what it did cause.

Chisenhall rejected that maneuver. He found no evidence that anyone could reasonably attribute McMath’s views to her employers and no evidence that the posts disrupted the school community.

Offensive Speech Is Not a Violent Act

McMath’s “Karma” post connected Kirk’s killing to his own remarks accepting some annual gun deaths as the cost of protecting Second Amendment rights. She testified that she was not celebrating his death. The state also cited an artist’s depiction of a figure holding a severed head intended to resemble President Donald Trump.

Those posts were plainly provocative. But provocation did not establish the charges. Chisenhall found that they showed no use, attempted use, or threatened use of violence by McMath, and noted that the state did not contend the image was a genuine threat to decapitate Trump.

The weak framing is the state’s, not the judge’s. Officials attempted to convert distasteful political expression into evidence of professional incapacity without producing evidence that McMath’s teaching, students, or school had been harmed.

Retaliation Is Alleged, Not Yet Proved

McMath has separately sued the school district, the Florida Department of Education, Moms for Liberty, and its Duval chapter in federal court. Her complaint alleges retaliation, viewpoint discrimination, malicious prosecution, defamation, and tortious interference, and claims the organization significantly influenced individual defendants. Those remain allegations in that litigation; the administrative recommendation does not establish them.

Still, the documented sequence requires no speculation: McMath posted outside work, the district found no operational disruption, her school community produced no complainants, and she was nevertheless removed from teaching while the state pursued serious professional charges.

Discipline First, Evidence Later

This episode exposes a broader political method: define reputational discomfort as institutional harm, treat hypothetical exposure as actual injury, and force an employee to defend her livelihood against allegations untethered to measurable job performance.

The administrative judge’s recommendation may halt the charges, though the Education Practices Commission must act on it. It cannot erase the consequence already imposed. McMath’s absence from the classroom demonstrates how state power can punish before it proves anything—and how a failed case can still accomplish much of the work of intimidation.
