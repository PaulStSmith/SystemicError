Senator sounds alarm as ICE expands surveillance tech to 'hack into smartphones'

By Paulo Santos

Sen. Ron Wyden (D-OR) is sounding the alarm after a review of federal spending disclosures revealed that Immigrations and Customs Enforcement is spending billions of dollars to expand its mass surveillance capabilities, which include contracts with companies that provide iris scanning, facial-recognition and phone-hacking technology.

“I’m extremely concerned about how ICE will use spyware, facial recognition and other technology to further trample on the rights of Americans and anyone who Donald Trump labels as an enemy,” Wyden said, speaking with The Washington Post in a report published Friday.

According to the Post, which reviewed what it called a recent “blitz of surveillance purchases,” ICE has purchased technology that would allow it to remotely “hack into smartphones” within the past few weeks, allowing the agency to monitor a user’s communications and track their movements, and without a warrant from a court.

ICE has also contracted the company Penlink for digital surveillance technologies to help the agency “build detailed portraits of individuals” using social media and online data, announced a plan to establish a team of analysts to sift through social media platforms to generate leads, and spent $3.75 million for facial recognition software to be used in public spaces.

Wyden was not the only Democratic lawmaker raising concern over ICE’s push to expand its mass surveillance capabilities. Last week, three Democratic House members wrote a letter to the Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem – who oversees ICE – over concerns of a new ICE contract with the company Pradagon for Graphite, an Israeli-made spyware that allows for remote phone-hacking.

“Graphite can gain unauthorized access into mobile phones without the owner’s knowledge or consent, allowing access to encrypted applications, the phone’s location data, as well as messages and photographs saved to the phone,” wrote Reps. Summer Lee (D-PA), Shontel Brown (D-OH) and Yassamin Ansari (D-AZ).

“Given the Trump Administration’s disregard for constitutional rights and civil liberties in pursuit of rapid mass deportation, we are seriously concerned that ICE will abuse Graphite software to target immigrants, people of color, and individuals who express opposition to ICE’s repeated attacks on the rule of law.”

The three lawmakers never received a response, according to the Post.

Our Analysis:

ICE’s Surveillance Expansion: A Grave Threat to Civil Liberties

In what reads like a dystopian fiction, Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) has alarmingly expanded its surveillance capabilities. This isn’t a subplot from George Orwell’s 1984; it’s the chilling reality of today’s America. According to a recent investigation by The Washington Post, ICE is spending billions to amass a high-tech arsenal designed for mass surveillance. This includes contracts for iris scanning, facial recognition, and even phone-hacking technology. Senator Ron Wyden (D-OR) has voiced his concern, encapsulating the fears of many: “I’m extremely concerned about how ICE will use spyware, facial recognition and other technology to further trample on the rights of Americans and anyone who Donald Trump labels as an enemy.”

The Terrifying Details

Remote Smartphone Hacking: ICE has acquired the capability to “hack into smartphones” remotely, a power that grants them access to monitor communications and track movements without a court warrant. Imagine the implications: the government can now snoop on anyone’s personal life, without any judicial oversight.

Digital Surveillance Technologies: Through its contract with Penlink, ICE aims to “build detailed portraits of individuals” using data mined from social media and online activities. This isn’t just about tracking; it’s about compiling comprehensive profiles on individuals, reducing privacy to a quaint concept of the past.

Facial Recognition in Public Spaces: With a $3.75 million investment in facial recognition software, ICE can now recognize and track individuals in public spaces. The very act of stepping outside transforms citizens into potential targets for government surveillance.

Social Media Monitoring: The establishment of an analyst team to scour social media for leads is a stark reminder of the extent to which surveillance has penetrated daily life. Your tweets, your Facebook posts, even your TikTok videos could now serve as fodder for ICE’s insidious agenda.

Bipartisan Concerns

It’s not just Senator Wyden who’s alarmed. Three Democratic House members—Summer Lee (D-PA), Shontel Brown (D-OH), and Yassamin Ansari (D-AZ)—expressed serious concerns over ICE’s contract with Pradagon for Graphite, an Israeli-made spyware. Graphite’s capabilities are the stuff of nightmares: unauthorized access to mobile phones, decryption of encrypted apps, and theft of location data, messages, and photographs. The lawmakers’ fear is palpable: “Given the Trump Administration’s disregard for constitutional rights and civil liberties in pursuit of rapid mass deportation, we are seriously concerned that ICE will abuse Graphite software to target immigrants, people of color, and individuals who express opposition to ICE’s repeated attacks on the rule of law.”

The Silence That Speaks Volumes

The most telling part of this saga? The silence from Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem. The three lawmakers who raised the alarm never received a response. This non-answer is a deafening affirmation of the administration’s indifference to the grave implications of its actions.

Conclusion: A Call to Vigilance

The expansion of ICE’s surveillance capabilities is not just an affront to privacy; it’s a direct threat to the civil liberties that form the bedrock of American democracy. This isn’t about enhancing national security; it’s about wielding unchecked power. The Orwellian state, it seems, is not a warning of what could be—it’s a description of what is. As citizens, our response must be one of vigilance and vocal opposition. The very essence of freedom is at stake.
