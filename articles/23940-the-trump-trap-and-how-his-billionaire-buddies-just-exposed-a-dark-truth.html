The Trump trap —and how his billionaire buddies just exposed a dark truth

Based on an article by Robert Reich

AI Is the Bait. Corporate Power Is the Trap.

A Strange Alliance, a Familiar Anger

Robert Reich reports that Bernie Sanders and Steve Bannon appeared at the same Washington conference to attack AI, data centers, and the corporate interests behind them. Their convergence does not amount to a shared politics. It shows that public anger over AI has become too potent for either left-wing economic populists or right-wing opportunists to ignore.

AI is the immediate controversy. The deeper issue is who controls investment, employment, natural resources, and political access—and who absorbs the consequences.

The Power Sits Above the Machines

AI does not build data centers, seek subsidies, weaken labor protections, or lobby for favorable regulation. Corporations and their executives make those decisions. Elected officials decide whether to accommodate them. Political consultants advise candidates when silence is safer for fundraising.

Reich cites Bureau of Labor Statistics data showing labor’s share of the economy at 52.9 percent in mid-2026, down from more than 65 percent when the series began in 1947. Whatever AI’s eventual effect on employment, that decline predates the current boom. The technology is entering an economy already structured to direct gains toward owners while exposing workers to displacement and wage pressure.

Claims that AI will necessarily eliminate jobs or worsen inequality remain forecasts, not established outcomes. But the distribution of decision-making power is already visible: investors choose the deployments, executives capture the upside, and workers rarely receive a meaningful veto.

Scapegoats Protect the Owners

Reich correctly identifies the central fraud of right-wing populism. Trump-aligned politics has often directed resentment toward immigrants, racial and religious minorities, LGBTQ+ people, and vaguely defined “woke” enemies. Those groups do not control corporate investment or federal economic policy. Blaming them gives concentrated wealth a costume change, recasting economic domination as cultural grievance.

Bannon’s criticism of “corporate oligarchs” does not erase that machinery. A politics built around weaker targets can condemn elites rhetorically while preserving the hierarchy that makes those elites powerful. The anger is real; its destination is engineered.

Democratic leaders have practiced a less vicious but still useful form of misdirection. According to Reich, most have made Trump the primary object of populist outrage while avoiding a sustained confrontation with wealthy corporate donors. Trump is an actor with enormous political agency, but opposition to one politician is not an economic program. It does not redistribute bargaining power, restrain monopolies, or protect workers from decisions made in corporate boardrooms.

Silence Has a Sponsor

The most revealing detail is not a poll or a conference speech. It is Politico’s report, cited by Reich, that some Democratic consultants were privately advising candidates in difficult midterm races to avoid discussing AI because well-funded pro-AI groups might retaliate with campaign spending.

That is institutional cowardice reduced to a campaign memo. The consultants’ concern, as reported, is not that candidates lack evidence or that voters are indifferent. It is that organized money may punish political speech.

This does not prove a direct purchase of policy, much less criminal bribery. It demonstrates something more routine: concentrated wealth can narrow debate before voters ever get a choice. Candidates learn which subjects carry financial penalties, consultants call retreat “strategy,” and corporate influence operates without requiring an explicit order.

Polling Is Evidence of Estrangement, Not Consent

Reich cites an NBC poll in which 54 percent agreed that powerful people will always do whatever they want. He also reports broad opposition to data centers and widespread fear that AI could destroy humanity. Those findings do not establish why respondents answered as they did, nor do they prove that AI will produce the outcomes they fear.

They do show profound estrangement from institutions that claim to represent the public. When people expect power to ignore them, corporate expansion stops looking like innovation and starts looking like another decision imposed from above.

A Times–Siena poll cited by Reich found that 43 percent of likely voters associated “elitist” with Republicans, compared with 33 percent who chose Democrats. That shift does not make Democrats a workers’ party. It merely suggests that Republicans’ alliance with wealth has become harder to disguise.

The System Rewards Managed Anger

The political struggle over AI is not fundamentally between people who love technology and people who fear it. It is between those who can authorize economic transformation and those expected to endure it.

Republican scapegoating protects ownership by redirecting anger downward. Democratic timidity protects it by treating donor retaliation as an immovable fact. Corporate leaders benefit from both arrangements: one supplies false enemies, while the other suppresses confrontation.

AI may intensify this pattern, but it did not create it. The systemic error is a political order in which private capital can reorganize work, consume public resources, and discipline candidates while the public is offered culture-war targets and carefully managed silence.
