'Indefensible' revelation emerges in Samuel Alito's long-withheld disclosures

Based on an article by David Edwards

Samuel Alito’s Conflict Is the Point

The Power Is His

Samuel Alito is not some peripheral actor caught in a technicality. He is a Supreme Court justice deciding whether fossil fuel companies can escape climate liability altogether. That is institutional power at the highest level, exercised inside a court that lets each justice police his own conflicts and then calls that ethics.

The disclosure posted this week shows he still held oil and gas investments going into an October climate case. He signed the filing on August 11, but the public did not get it until late. That delay matters because delay is how accountability is laundered: keep the disclosure offstage until the damage is done, then call the process transparent.

The Conflict Was Already Known

The basic facts are not subtle. Alito held shares in energy companies, including ConocoPhillips and Phillips 66, along with other fossil-linked firms and a mineral interest in Oklahoma. No other justice directly holds oil and gas stock. The question was never whether the court could see the conflict. The question was whether the court would let it matter.

Watchdogs asked the Senate Judiciary Committee to investigate whether he violated the court’s ethics code by staying on the case. Their position is straightforward: if a justice’s financial position overlaps with a case that could benefit that sector, recusal is not optional theater. It is the minimum safeguard against self-dealing.

The Court’s Ethics Regime Is a Shield

The Supreme Court adopted a formal ethics code in 2023 after public reporting on undisclosed luxury travel and other abuses. That sounds like reform until you read the fine print: each justice still gets to decide recusals for himself. That is not enforcement. That is permission.

Lisa Graves was blunt for a reason: a code with no enforceable mechanism is basically decorative. The court wants the appearance of restraint without the inconvenience of outside control. It gets to say it has standards while preserving the real standard, which is discretion protected from consequences.

The Real Misdirection

The court’s defenders lean on a cramped argument: Alito owns no stock in the specific companies before the court, so nothing to see here. That is the kind of formalism that treats ethics as a spreadsheet instead of a governing principle. The companies themselves have argued that the Colorado case is linked to others in a way that affects recusal. If the industry understands the cases as connected, Alito does not get to pretend the connection disappears when it becomes inconvenient.

This is how elite institutions misdirect blame. They narrow the frame until the wrongdoing looks accidental, technical, or “inadvertent.” But the relevant fact is not confusion. The relevant fact is that a justice with financial exposure is participating in a case that could reshape fossil fuel liability nationwide.

What The Case Is Really About

The case began as Boulder’s attempt to make fossil fuel producers pay climate costs. The companies want the justices to shut down those suits at the federal level. If they win, dozens of similar cases could be blocked. So this is not a narrow procedural dispute. It is a fight over whether local governments can use the courts to force the industry to absorb the damage it helped create.

Alito’s investments matter because they sit on the same side of that legal and political project. His stock holdings are not a side note to the case. They are part of the case’s moral architecture: a judiciary that can be nudged, insulated, and left to judge its own financial entanglements while climate accountability is narrowed out of existence.

The Pattern

This is what institutional decay looks like in a refined suit. The court delays disclosure, self-certifies ethics, and hides behind formal distinctions while fossil fuel power seeks legal immunity. Congress can complain, watchdogs can document, reporters can lay out the record, but the structure still leaves the decisive power where it began: in the hands of the justice with the conflict.

That is the larger lesson. American democratic institutions are not failing only when rules are broken. They are failing when the rules are written so that powerful actors can keep deciding whether the rules apply to them at all.
