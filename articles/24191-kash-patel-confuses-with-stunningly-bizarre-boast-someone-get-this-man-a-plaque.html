Kash Patel confuses with stunningly bizarre boast: 'Someone get this man a plaque'

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosSeptember 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower disguised as performanceThe jokes are not the storyAccountability requires evidenceThe institution behind the manPropaganda by measurement

What happened

RawStory reports that FBI Director Kash Patel posted a campaign-style infographic on September 27, 2026, boasting that he had made 101 calls to “foreign partners” during his first 18 months in office. Commentators noted that this amounted to roughly 1.3 calls per week. Progressive writer Alex Cole supplied the headline’s punch line: “Somebody get this man a commemorative plaque.”

The outlet places the post amid calls for Patel’s resignation and scrutiny of his conduct. It cites FBI records reportedly showing attention to online critics, allegations that he used government resources for trips connected to his girlfriend Alexis Wilkins, and criticism of his handling of Epstein-related files. RawStory also reports allegations involving FBI protection and transportation for Wilkins and a friend, as well as Patel’s firing of an FBI veteran he accused of leaking information about his travel.

Power disguised as performance

The number of calls is not a meaningful measure of FBI leadership. It reveals nothing about the substance of those conversations, the decisions they produced, or whether they advanced legitimate law-enforcement objectives. Patel offered activity as achievement and branding as accountability.

That distinction matters because he does not occupy a ceremonial job. The FBI director controls an institution with enormous investigative, surveillance, personnel, and security powers. A self-congratulatory graphic about telephone use trivializes the standard by which that power should be judged.

The jokes are not the story

The ridicule is justified, but it can also shrink the scandal. Mocking Patel for celebrating basic office work is easier than examining what he allegedly did with public aircraft, armed personnel, disciplinary authority, and internal records.

The central issue is not that Patel’s boast was embarrassing. It is that an official facing serious questions about his stewardship answered with public relations theater. Counting phone calls directs attention toward a harmless metric and away from allegations involving state resources and retaliation against an employee accused of exposing controversial travel.

Accountability requires evidence

RawStory’s article bundles several allegations together but supplies limited underlying detail about most of them. Claims that Patel used an FBI jet to attend Wilkins’s performances, assigned a SWAT team to protect her, or had agents transport her reportedly intoxicated friend require documentary evidence, dates, costs, authorization records, and Patel’s response. The article does not establish those allegations merely by repeating them.

That evidentiary gap does not excuse institutional silence. It identifies where scrutiny belongs: official travel logs, protection decisions, personnel records, applicable FBI policies, and the authority under which resources were deployed. Sarcasm cannot substitute for that accounting.

The institution behind the man

Patel made the boast, and according to the reporting, he made or enabled the disputed personnel and resource decisions. But senior officials rarely act without subordinates approving logistics, processing expenses, accepting assignments, or declining to challenge dubious orders.

That is the larger power structure: a director can turn personal priorities into institutional action because the bureaucracy beneath him translates preference into policy. If safeguards exist only until the boss applies pressure, they are not safeguards. They are etiquette.

Propaganda by measurement

The infographic reflects a broader political habit: choose a number that sounds productive, detach it from any useful benchmark, and present it as proof of competence. The metric is designed to preempt judgment, not enable it.

Patel’s critics treated the graphic as an absurdity. More precisely, it is an attempt to define accountability downward. When powerful officials advertise routine motions while withholding the information needed to assess consequential decisions, the comedy serves the same function as the graphic: it risks making institutional failure look merely ridiculous.
