Trump biographer assumes everything being said publicly is probably the opposite

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 15, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Reassurance without an accountingA crisis becomes a funding pitchModernization entrusted to a troubled contractorDistrust is the result, not the evidence

Michael Wolff’s answer to the federal government’s explanation of a nationwide aviation breakdown was blunt: assume the opposite. Speaking Saturday with Joanna Coles on a Daily Beast podcast, the Trump biographer argued that officials’ public assurances about a Microsoft outage and an FAA failure were so untrustworthy that their denial of a connection made him suspect one.

That is a judgment about institutional credibility, not evidence that the failures were connected. AlterNet’s report establishes no such link. What it does reveal is a government asking the public to trust its crisis management while offering shifting spending demands, blaming inadequate technology and confronting reported concerns about the contractor entrusted with modernizing critical air-traffic systems.

Reassurance without an accounting

According to AlterNet, Markwayne Mullin opened a Friday news conference by saying the Microsoft and FAA failures were unrelated, assuring the public that the incident was not another September 11 and urging people not to panic. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy attributed the disruption to poor technology and used the breakdown to press for additional FAA funding.

Wolff treated the denial itself as incriminating. “It’s a fair chance the opposite is the truth,” he said, arguing that the officials had exhausted their credibility. After a clip in which the government claimed, “We’re built to handle crisis,” Wolff replied: “We’re built to create crises.”

The line is effective because it captures the governing contradiction on display: officials responsible for the system presented themselves as reassuring bystanders to its failure. But Wolff’s suspicion does not establish causation. His theory—that officials either did not understand what happened or understood and would not admit it—remained a theory. The stronger indictment lies in what the government’s own response reportedly showed: no clear public accounting of why the aviation system failed, paired with an immediate demand for more money.

A crisis becomes a funding pitch

AlterNet reports that the administration’s proposed additional FAA funding moved through a series of figures. An initial request for $15 billion became $19 billion. President Donald Trump then sought another $30 billion, and Duffy said he wanted the total increased to $50 billion, on top of $12.5 billion already provided.

A failing system may genuinely require major investment. Air-traffic infrastructure is not repaired through austerity or slogans. But rapidly changing price tags do not explain what failed, who was responsible or how new spending would prevent a recurrence. Duffy’s appeal converted operational chaos into leverage before the public received a coherent diagnosis.

That is not proof that the request was illegitimate. It is evidence of an administration asking Congress and the public to accept urgency in place of specificity. The people controlling the agency, its contracts and its budget proposal hold the power to produce that specificity. Travelers stranded by a ground stop do not.

Modernization entrusted to a troubled contractor

The funding argument also runs through Peraton, the contractor selected to take over FAA modernization. AlterNet cites an August Politico report in which three FAA officials expressed serious concerns about the company’s performance.

One person familiar with internal discussions said Peraton had “basically not done any work since they got in,” faulting it for failing to manage subcontractors and allowing an Alaska project to slip behind schedule. An industry official offered a more vivid account: at one radar work site, a Peraton manager allegedly brought doughnuts and sat in a corner instead of ensuring that other contractors performed their duties.

The criticism was not one-directional. The officials also reportedly accused the FAA itself of obstructing Peraton’s work. That points to an institutional failure broader than one inattentive manager: a federal agency and its chosen contractor blaming each other while the infrastructure they are supposed to modernize remains vulnerable.

The reported contractor problems do not prove that Peraton caused the Microsoft outage, the FAA failure or the ground stop. AlterNet does not establish that connection. They do, however, complicate Duffy’s attempt to package the episode simply as a technology problem requiring a larger appropriation. Technology does not neglect schedules, mismanage contractors or stonewall modernization. People and institutions do.

Distrust is the result, not the evidence

Coles called the officials a “ship of fools.” Wolff suggested that disrupted staffing, underfunding and unfilled jobs may have left them unable to understand the crisis—or that they knew more than they disclosed. The supplied reporting does not resolve that choice.

Nor does it justify Wolff’s shortcut that official statements should be inverted automatically. Governments can destroy their credibility through evasions, contradictions and visible incompetence, but disbelief is not an investigative method. If officials say two failures were unrelated, the responsible challenge is to demand the technical basis for that claim, not declare the reverse true by reflex.

Still, Wolff’s cynicism did not arise in a vacuum. Officials reportedly met a nationwide disruption with reassurance, blame and an expanding funding request, while earlier warnings described paralysis between the FAA and a major contractor. The administration may eventually provide a convincing explanation. In the account AlterNet published, it had instead offered the public an assurance of competence from the same institutions whose conduct made that assurance difficult to credit.
