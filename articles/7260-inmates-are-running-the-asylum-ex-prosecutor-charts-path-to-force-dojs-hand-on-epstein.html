‘Inmates are running the asylum!’ Ex-prosecutor charts path to force DOJ’s hand on Epstein

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 28, 20255 min read

Legal analyst and former federal prosecutor Kristy Greenberg appears on MS NOW, Dec. 27, 2025. (Screengrab / MS NOW)

As the Trump administration faces growing scrutiny over its failure to release files on Jeffrey Epstein with limited redactions – as it’s required to by law – former federal prosecutor Kristy Greenberg charted a path Saturday for Democratic lawmakers to force the administration’s hand.

Trump’s Justice Department was hammered last week after failing to comply with the newly enacted Epstein Files Transparency Act, a bill that required the DOJ to release all of its files on Epstein by Dec. 19, and with redactions limited to protecting the identities of victims and minors.

Instead, the DOJ released only a small fraction of its files on Epstein by the deadline, and with redactions outside the scope of what the new law permitted. One file in particular drew scrutiny; a 2020 email indicating the DOJ had “anticipated” filing charges against 10 potential Epstein co-conspirators, though sections that presumably listed the names of those individuals were mostly blacked out.

Appearing Saturday on MS Now, where she also serves as a legal analyst, Greenberg pointed to the 2020 email as a potential lever to force the DOJ to release its trove of Epstein documents, and in full.

“That whole memo is redacted, other than saying ‘anticipated charges’ and that a meeting with the deputy attorney general took place,” Greenberg said.

“That is exactly the kind of information that the American people want to see. If [the Southern District of New York] did want to bring charges against other people, who stopped it, and what was the evidence that would have allowed them to bring such a case? Those kinds of things are what should be seen.”

As to what course of actions Democratic lawmakers should take, Greenberg said that filing a lawsuit was the only path forward.

“If the DOJ fails to comply, then they are in contempt of a court order; any other avenue here doesn’t really work,” she continued.

“Sure, you could find them in contempt of Congress, but are they really going to put these guys in jail or detain them? No. You can refer them for criminal prosecution to the DOJ, but the inmates are running the asylum, so that’s not going to do any good! This has to be handled in court. That’s what they’re next move should be.”

Our Analysis:

Analysis of DOJ’s Non-compliance with Epstein Files Transparency Act

In an era where the American public’s trust in its institutions continues to wane, the Trump administration’s latest debacle over the Jeffrey Epstein files only adds fuel to the fire of skepticism and disillusionment. The Department of Justice (DOJ), under Trump’s watch, has failed to comply with the clear mandates of the newly enacted Epstein Files Transparency Act. This act required the DOJ to release all its files on Epstein by December 19, with limited redactions solely for the purpose of protecting the identities of victims and minors. Instead, what we’ve witnessed is a blatant disregard for the law, with only a fraction of the files released and redactions far exceeding the scope permitted.

DOJ’s Evasion: A Scathing Review

The revelation of a 2020 email detailing “anticipated” charges against 10 potential Epstein co-conspirators, heavily redacted to obscure crucial details, is particularly egregious. This document potentially holds the keys to understanding the breadth and depth of Epstein’s network and the DOJ’s handling (or mishandling) of the case. The redactions seem to serve not the law or the public interest but the interests of those who wish to keep these details shrouded in mystery.

Former federal prosecutor Kristy Greenberg’s commentary sheds light on the gravity of the situation. Her assertion that the American people deserve to know the extent of Epstein’s network, the evidence that could have supported charges against co-conspirators, and the reasons behind the DOJ’s apparent reluctance to pursue these charges is spot-on. This isn’t just about bureaucratic oversight; it’s a glaring example of the potential cover-up and accountability evasion within one of the nation’s most powerful law enforcement agencies.

The Path Forward: Legal Action

Greenberg’s recommendation for Democratic lawmakers to take legal action against the DOJ is not just appropriate; it’s essential. The notion that the DOJ can essentially ignore a law designed to enhance transparency and accountability without facing substantial consequences is absurd. The suggestion that other avenues, such as finding the DOJ in contempt of Congress, would be ineffective is a stark reminder of the limitations of our current checks and balances when facing an entity that operates with apparent impunity.

The inmates are running the asylum metaphor used by Greenberg is a powerful indictment of the current state of affairs within the DOJ. It underscores the perverse situation where the agency tasked with upholding the law is itself accused of flouting it. For the American people to have any hope of seeing justice served in the Epstein saga, it seems clear that the judiciary must step in where other institutions have faltered or failed.

Conclusion: A Test of Our Democratic Principles

The DOJ’s failure to fully release the Epstein files as mandated by law is a litmus test for the strength of our democratic institutions and principles. It’s a matter of public interest and the pursuit of justice for Epstein’s victims. The administration’s actions—or lack thereof—underscore a disturbing trend of opacity and a disdain for accountability that should concern all Americans, regardless of political affiliation.

The call for legal action is not just about the Epstein files; it’s a call for the restoration of faith in our justice system and the principles of transparency and accountability that underpin a healthy democracy. It’s high time the DOJ is held to the standard it’s supposed to uphold. Failure to do so not only undermines the Epstein case but signals a broader threat to the rule of law in America.
