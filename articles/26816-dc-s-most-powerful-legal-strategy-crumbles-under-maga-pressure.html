DC's most powerful legal strategy crumbles under MAGA pressure

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 26, 20264 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that Washington attorney Beth Wilkinson has represented clients across sharp political and commercial divides, including Donald Trump, Jimmy Kimmel, Disney, Brett Kavanaugh, major corporations, and media mogul David Ellison. The central claim is not that her legal strategy has collapsed, but that mounting partisan pressure may make this kind of flexible, elite representation harder to sustain.

Citing New York Times reporting, AlterNet highlights Wilkinson’s work for Ellison in defending his proposed $111 billion purchase of Warner Bros. Discovery from a challenge by state attorneys general. It also quotes American Economic Liberties Project executive director Nidhi Hegde, who argues that defending ABC against the Trump administration does not erase Wilkinson’s role in facilitating media consolidation.

The article revisits Wilkinson’s defense of Kavanaugh during his 2018 Supreme Court confirmation fight. In an MSNBC interview, she questioned why attorney Michael Avenatti had not taken one accuser’s allegation to police, while declining to discuss Trump’s own accusers or make broader political arguments.

The headline outruns the evidence

Nothing in AlterNet’s account establishes that Wilkinson’s strategy has “crumbled.” No client is reported to have fired her. No court defeat is identified as the breaking point. No professional sanction or measurable collapse appears in the story.

What the reporting actually describes is a reputational conflict: Wilkinson’s ability to represent ideologically opposed clients has become harder to package as politically neutral expertise. That is meaningful, but it is not institutional ruin. The dramatic framing converts a dispute over elite accountability into a premature obituary for one lawyer’s business model.

Power belongs to the clients—and the system serving them

Wilkinson has substantial influence, but the institutional power described here rests mainly with presidents, Supreme Court nominees and justices, corporate owners, regulators, state attorneys general, and companies seeking control over major communications infrastructure. They can appoint judges, approve mergers, challenge transactions, shape markets, and absorb enormous legal costs.

Reducing this arrangement to Wilkinson’s “juggling act” risks making the intermediary more important than the institutions employing her. She did not create corporate consolidation or presidential pressure on media companies. She chose to sell formidable legal representation within that system, including to actors advancing those outcomes. Agency attaches to that choice; governing power still belongs to the officials and corporate principals directing the underlying fights.

Neutrality is a service, not an alibi

A lawyer may represent unpopular or contradictory clients without personally endorsing them. But professional role morality does not make the consequences of that work disappear. Wilkinson’s clients seek concrete outcomes: confirmation, merger approval, dismissal of claims, defeat of regulation, or protection from government pressure.

Her 2018 MSNBC appearance showed the limits of procedural compartmentalization. Wilkinson narrowed the discussion to what Kavanaugh believed and rejected questions about Trump as outside her assigned purpose. That was a defensible boundary for an advocate, but also a useful political maneuver: isolate the client’s immediate problem from the wider power structure that made the defense consequential.

The distinction matters. Wilkinson’s conduct does not prove ideological loyalty to every client. It does demonstrate how elite advocacy turns political conflicts into discrete technical assignments, allowing the advocate to claim distance while still helping powerful clients secure material victories.

Partisanship is not the deepest contradiction

The article frames the pressure as a problem of loyalty in a polarized era: can one lawyer continue moving between MAGA figures and their opponents? Hegde identifies the more important fracture. The contradiction is not simply representing rival political camps; it is defending a media company from government pressure while also assisting a transaction critics say would further consolidate media ownership.

That conflict cannot be resolved by sorting clients into Democratic and Republican columns. A corporation can resist abusive state power in one case and pursue greater private power in another. Supporting its legal rights does not require pretending its market ambitions serve workers or the public.

If Democrats later celebrate Wilkinson solely for defending ABC while ignoring her corporate consolidation work, they will be choosing partisan symbolism over a material account of power. Hegde’s criticism targets that selective memory, not merely Wilkinson’s résumé.

The durable Washington bargain

The larger pattern is an elite legal market built to make immense power portable. The same professional credibility can defend a nominee against misconduct allegations, help corporations resist liability, challenge antitrust enforcement, protect media companies, and advance ownership consolidation. Political antagonists rotate through the client list; the class of people able to purchase this access remains remarkably stable.

MAGA pressure may narrow the space for such cross-faction practice, but AlterNet does not show that it has destroyed it. The sharper conclusion is that partisan conflict is exposing a longstanding Washington arrangement: powerful actors treat ideology as situational, expertise as purchasable, and accountability as somebody else’s portfolio.
