The Housing Market Gerontocracy: When Time Locks the Door

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 11, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Boomers Bought the Dream—and Changed the LocksMarket or Museum?The Generational Tectonic ShiftHousing Is a Right, Not a Reward

In a blink-and-you're-priced-out America, the housing market has become less a ladder and more a drawbridge—raised high and held by the same hands that have clutched the deed for decades. A short but savage video circulating online recently laid this bare. Using a simple animated conversation between two cartoon characters, Mushie and Birdie, it drives a stake into the heart of American housing policy with one brutal statistic: the median age of homebuyers in 2007 was 39. Today, in 2025, it is 56.

Pause and let that sink in. Seventeen years have passed, and the median age of homebuyers has risen by exactly seventeen years. That’s not just a data point—it’s a red flag, a siren, and a damning indictment of a market that has completely stopped renewing itself. The same generation that bought homes before the Great Recession is the one still buying them now. And if you're under 40? Good luck. The market isn't interested in you.

The National Association of Realtors confirms it. In 2007, the average buyer was born around 1968. In 2023, that same cohort—still born in 1968—dominates the market. The story isn’t about individual financial choices anymore. It's about structural exclusion. What we’re witnessing is a housing gerontocracy: a system where those who bought early continue to benefit, and those who didn’t are increasingly shut out.

Boomers Bought the Dream—and Changed the Locks

In the golden post-war years, homeownership was sold as the American dream. And for Boomers, it largely delivered. They bought homes when interest rates were low, wages were high (relative to cost of living), and supply was ample. Today, they sit atop a mountain of equity—and they’re buying again, either as investment properties or downsized retirement pads, often outbidding younger would-be buyers who are still struggling to scrape together a down payment.

Meanwhile, Millennials and Gen Zers have been fed a diet of bootstraps and avocado toast shaming, while home prices have skyrocketed, interest rates have doubled, and student debt has reached stratospheric levels. The average starter home—if you can even find one—is now a luxury good. In 2024, the U.S. median home price was over $400,000. Pair that with a 7% mortgage rate, and you’ve got a cruel joke disguised as fiscal policy.

This isn’t about financial literacy. It’s about financial access. And that access has calcified.

Market or Museum?

A functioning market requires churn. Buyers need to become sellers, and new buyers need to enter. But when the entry point is gated by generational wealth and institutional investors hoarding single-family homes, the market stops being a marketplace and starts becoming a museum—populated by exhibits from a bygone era of economic mobility.

It’s worth noting that institutional investors now account for nearly 20% of all single-family home purchases in some markets. These are not families buying homes to live in. These are corporations buying homes to rent out, further driving up prices and locking out ownership. In this game, the ladder isn’t just pulled up—it’s being rented back to you at 3x the cost.

The Generational Tectonic Shift

Let’s be clear: Boomers didn’t single-handedly ruin the housing market. They merely played the game as it was designed—one that rewarded early entry and punished delay. But they also lobbied to keep that game rigged. Zoning laws that prevent dense housing, tax breaks for mortgage interest, policies that protect property values at all costs—these are not economic necessities. They’re political choices. And they’ve overwhelmingly benefited the old at the expense of the young.

And now the numbers speak for themselves: fewer than 30% of Millennials own homes. Gen Z? Even less. The average age of first-time buyers has crossed 36. That's after college, after marriage, and deep into parenthood for many. Compare that to Boomers, many of whom bought homes in their 20s, on single incomes, with high school diplomas and no student debt.

Housing Is a Right, Not a Reward

If we continue treating housing as a reward for wealth instead of a basic human necessity, we are signing the death warrant for a functioning democracy. You cannot build a stable society on a foundation that only exists for the old and the rich. Intergenerational wealth is fine—intergenerational monopolies are not.

The cartoon video may be funny, but the joke is over. The housing market doesn’t need reform. It needs a reset. We need to abolish exclusionary zoning. We need to penalize property hoarding by corporate landlords. We need aggressive public housing investment, and yes, we need to start taxing the hell out of unoccupied investment properties. Because a home isn’t just an asset—it’s where life happens.

The generation that locked the doors behind them may not give up the keys willingly. But we can't wait until we're 56 to find out.
