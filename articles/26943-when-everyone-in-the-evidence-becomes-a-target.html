When Everyone in the Evidence Becomes a Target

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 2, 202613 min read

In another post, we looked at Senator Bill Hagerty’s attempt to turn the Constitution’s Speech or Debate Clause from a shield protecting legislative acts into something much larger: an investigative moat surrounding legislators themselves.

The distinction mattered.

A vote is protected.

Legislative deliberation may be protected.

The content of certain legislative communications may be protected.

But if the mere existence of a telephone call becomes untouchable because the call might have involved legislative activity — and investigators must notify the legislator before even obtaining metadata — the privilege has ceased merely protecting legislation. It has begun protecting the legislator from investigation itself.

Now comes the next stage of the argument.

And this one depends upon another word quietly changing meaning:

target.

On October 1, the Daily Caller published an article under the remarkable headline:

“Investigation Into Jack Smith Suggests He Was The Real Threat To Democracy.”

The article recounts disclosures from the Senate Judiciary Committee concerning former Special Counsel Jack Smith’s investigation into Donald Trump and efforts to overturn the result of the 2020 presidential election. It points to telephone records involving Republican members of Congress, financial records containing information about thousands of Trump donors, communications involving journalists and hundreds of subpoenas concerning Republican individuals and organizations.

Those facts warrant scrutiny.

Some of them warrant serious scrutiny.

But the article performs a remarkably simple transformation.

Someone appears in evidence.

Therefore that person was investigated.

Therefore that person was targeted.

Therefore Republicans were targeted.

Therefore the investigation itself was partisan.

And from there:

Jack Smith was the threat to democracy.

That progression works only if we quietly erase the distinction between a subject of an investigation and someone whose information appears in evidence gathered during that investigation.

That distinction is not a technicality.

It is how investigations work.

The Evidence Is Larger Than the Defendant

Imagine investigators suspect that Alice and Bob committed a crime together.

They obtain Alice’s telephone records.

Those records show calls to Charlie.

Charlie is not therefore accused of committing the crime.

Charlie is not necessarily suspected of committing the crime.

Charlie may not even know a crime occurred.

Charlie is nevertheless in the evidence.

Investigators obtain Alice’s bank records.

They contain transactions involving David, Ellen and Frank.

David, Ellen and Frank have now appeared in investigative material.

That does not transform all three into criminal suspects.

Investigators obtain Alice’s emails.

Some contain messages from journalists.

Some contain messages from politicians.

Some contain messages from employees.

Some contain messages from family members.

None of those people automatically becomes a “target” because investigators lawfully acquired Alice’s mailbox.

This is elementary.

Yet remove Alice and Bob, replace them with Donald Trump and his associates, replace Charlie with a Republican senator, and suddenly the vocabulary changes.

People whose information was obtained become people who were targeted.

Organizations whose records were subpoenaed become organizations that were spied upon.

Journalists whose messages existed inside records obtained from government sources become journalists whose communications Smith obtained.

Trump donors whose checks were contained within financial records become thousands of Americans supposedly swept into a political investigation.

Then somebody counts them.

And the number becomes the argument.

4,782 Victims?

The most emotionally effective number is 4,782.

According to material released by Senate Judiciary Committee Chairman Chuck Grassley, Smith’s investigation acquired at least 4,782 checks from people who had donated to Trump or Trump-aligned organizations. The committee says those checks contained names, addresses, bank routing and account numbers, and signatures. Some represented donations as small as a few dollars.

That deserves an explanation.

Why were the checks relevant?

Why was that date range necessary?

Could the same investigative purpose have been accomplished with less personal information?

What minimization procedures were used?

Who had access to the information?

How long was it retained?

Those are excellent oversight questions.

Here is a very different question:

Why was Jack Smith investigating 4,782 ordinary Americans for donating to Donald Trump?

Because that question already contains an answer that has not been established.

The Daily Caller itself illustrates the problem beautifully.

After describing the checks, it writes:

“It remains unclear if Smith was attempting to charge Americans…”

Unclear?

That sentence smuggles an astonishing premise into the story.

There is no evidence presented in the article that Smith was considering prosecuting 4,782 small-dollar donors.

The article does not establish that those donors were suspects.

It does not establish that they were investigative targets.

It does not establish that prosecutors believed making a political contribution was criminal.

It establishes that their checks appeared in material obtained during a criminal investigation.

Those are not remotely the same proposition.

Suppose investigators subpoena the accounting records of a corporation suspected of fraud.

The records include 20,000 customer payments.

Did investigators just “target” 20,000 customers?

Of course not.

Perhaps the subpoena was too broad.

Perhaps investigators collected information they did not need.

Perhaps privacy safeguards were inadequate.

Perhaps the government should have required redaction.

All legitimate questions.

But the existence of Customer Number 17,432’s bank details in the return does not prove that prosecutors were preparing a case against Customer Number 17,432.

Yet place a Trump campaign logo on the check and suddenly ordinary rules of inference disappear.

Fifty Journalists Were “Targeted”

Then come the journalists.

Grassley announced that communications involving nearly 50 journalists from organizations including CNN, Fox News, CBS, NBC, NPR, The Washington Post, The Wall Street Journal, Reuters and the Associated Press had been “swept up” during the investigation.

That sounds alarming.

And government acquisition of journalists’ communications should receive scrutiny.

Source confidentiality matters.

Press freedom matters.

Justice Department rules governing investigative steps involving journalists exist for good reason.

But again:

How were those communications obtained?

Contemporary reporting on the disclosures says the messages were contained in records produced by the National Archives in response to a subpoena seeking records associated with phones used by Trump White House officials.

That distinction changes the story considerably.

If prosecutors subpoena a reporter’s telephone account because they want to identify her sources, that is one kind of government action.

If prosecutors subpoena the records of a government official under investigation and discover that the official exchanged messages with a reporter, that is another.

The journalist exists in both datasets.

But only one scenario requires investigators to have targeted the journalist.

This is why verbs matter.

“Obtained.”

“Swept up.”

“Collected.”

“Surveilled.”

“Spied on.”

“Targeted.”

They are not synonyms.

Political rhetoric becomes considerably easier when we pretend they are.

Then There Are the Senators

We already examined the congressional telephone records in detail.

For several lawmakers, investigators obtained non-content toll records covering the four days surrounding January 6, 2021 — records indicating things such as when calls occurred, with whom and for how long.

Smith testified that the records were metadata rather than recordings and that his office consulted the Justice Department’s Public Integrity Section before obtaining them. He said communications involving members of Congress were relevant because investigators were examining whether Trump and others attempted to exploit the violence of January 6 in furtherance of the alleged election scheme.

That does not prove every subpoena was proper.

Grassley has raised a considerably more serious allegation: that subpoena returns also contained the content of messages from 44 members of Congress and that some material allegedly reached investigators before appropriate privilege filtering occurred. If established, that presents a much stronger Speech or Debate Clause question than the mere acquisition of toll metadata.

That is exactly the sort of allegation Congress should investigate.

Notice what happens when we stay precise.

We can ask whether particular communications were privileged.

We can ask whether filter procedures failed.

We can ask whether prosecutors obtained excessive material.

We can ask whether Smith properly supervised his team.

We can ask whether individual subpoenas were appropriately scoped.

No conspiracy theory is required.

No one has to transform metadata into wiretapping.

No one has to transform incidental collection into partisan targeting.

No one has to transform every Republican appearing in evidence into a criminal suspect.

Facts are quite capable of being serious without assistance.

“Every Name on It Is a Republican”

Senator Ted Cruz supplied perhaps the purest version of the argument.

According to the Daily Caller, Cruz told Smith:

“In all, you issued 197 subpoenas to 34 individuals at least, and 163 businesses, and you sought records on at least 430 Republican individuals and entities.”

Then came the rhetorical flourish:

“Every name on it is a Republican.”

Well.

Yes.

The investigation concerned Donald Trump, his campaign, people surrounding him, the alternate-elector effort, attempts to influence government officials, communications surrounding certification of the election and an alleged effort to keep Trump in power after he lost.

One would expect an investigation of such conduct to encounter a rather large number of Republicans.

That is not exculpatory.

It is not inculpatory.

It is simply unsurprising.

Imagine investigating the Watergate burglary and emerging triumphantly from the evidence room:

Every senior campaign official we found was working for Nixon!

Imagine investigating corruption inside a labor union:

Almost everyone whose records we subpoenaed was connected to the union!

Imagine investigating accounting fraud at Enron:

An astonishing number of people in these documents worked for Enron!

Yes.

That is how subject matter works.

An investigation develops a natural population.

You cannot infer discriminatory selection merely by observing that the resulting sample resembles the population being investigated.

To establish partisan targeting, one needs something more.

Show that investigators selected people because they were Republican rather than because of evidence connecting them to conduct under investigation.

Show internal instructions directing investigators to map Republican political activity unrelated to potential crimes.

Show subpoenas with no plausible investigative nexus.

Show comparable Democratic conduct deliberately ignored under materially similar circumstances.

Show that political affiliation, rather than evidentiary relevance, was the selection criterion.

Those would be evidence of political targeting.

“Look how many Republicans are here” is not.

The Calendar Is Supposed to Be Suspicious Too

Grassley also argues that some subpoenas reached from 2019 through 2023 even though the central events occurred around the 2020 election and January 6, 2021.

That too is worth examining.

Why 2019?

Why 2023?

What did investigators expect to establish?

Were those ranges justified by particular theories of the case?

Those questions have answers somewhere in investigative records.

But a date range extending beyond the date of a crime is not itself evidence of misconduct.

Criminal schemes have origins.

They have preparation.

They have financing.

They have relationships established beforehand.

They have consequences afterward.

Investigators examining whether an organization participated in unlawful activity do not ordinarily restrict themselves to records stamped with the exact date on which the alleged offense culminated.

January 6 was an event.

The alleged effort to overturn the election was a process.

Smith’s federal indictment did not allege that a criminal conspiracy magically materialized at noon on January 6 and evaporated before midnight. It alleged conduct developing after the November 2020 election and continuing through certification.

One may dispute that case.

One may challenge Smith’s legal theory.

One may challenge particular subpoenas.

But “the records extend beyond January 6” does not establish that investigators were secretly mapping the Republican Party.

It establishes that investigations possess timelines.

The Trick Is Aggregation

The rhetorical device becomes obvious once the pieces are placed together.

Take:

  • congressional metadata,

  • donor checks,

  • White House communications with journalists,

  • business records,

  • campaign organizations,

  • fundraising data,

  • political consultants,

  • government officials,

  • Trump associates.

Now remove the reason each category entered the investigation.

Remove the source of each record.

Remove the distinction between content and metadata.

Remove the distinction between subpoena recipient and person mentioned in subpoenaed material.

Remove the distinction between investigative subject and third party.

Remove the alleged criminal conduct connecting the records.

Then pile everything into one enormous number.

Hundreds of Republicans.

Thousands of donors.

Dozens of journalists.

Members of Congress.

The scale itself begins to feel incriminating.

And once scale substitutes for causation, the conclusion almost writes itself:

This was not an investigation of an alleged effort to overturn an election.

It was an investigation of Republicans.

Except that conclusion has not been demonstrated.

It has been produced by aggregation.

Oversight Without Alchemy

There is a particularly frustrating aspect to all of this.

Congress does not need rhetorical alchemy to investigate Jack Smith.

Grassley has identified questions worth answering.

If privileged congressional communications bypassed required filtering, investigate it.

If subpoenas unnecessarily exposed thousands of donors’ banking information, investigate it.

If prosecutorial requests were broader than their investigative justification, investigate them.

If Justice Department rules protecting journalists were violated, investigate that.

If prosecutors misled courts when requesting nondisclosure orders, investigate that.

If Smith gave inaccurate testimony, establish exactly what he said and exactly what contradicts it.

Those are concrete propositions.

They can be tested against documents.

They can be evaluated against statutes.

They can be compared with departmental policy.

They can be adjudicated without ever asking anyone to trust Jack Smith.

That is oversight.

But oversight becomes something else when the conclusion is embedded in the vocabulary.

A subpoena becomes spying.

Evidence becomes surveillance.

A person mentioned in records becomes a target.

A donor whose check appears in financial data becomes a political victim.

A reporter corresponding with a government official becomes a journalist investigated by the state.

A Republican appearing in an investigation centered on a Republican president becomes evidence that Republicans were selected because they were Republicans.

And eventually the prosecutor investigating an alleged attempt to overturn an election becomes:

“the real threat to democracy.”

That is not a finding.

It is a narrative.

The Perfect Immunity Machine

Our previous article ended with a warning.

The Speech or Debate Clause must remain a shield protecting legislative independence without becoming a moat protecting legislators from ordinary investigative scrutiny.

This new argument reveals how the moat can become even wider.

First, congressional communications become presumptively untouchable.

Then people connected to politicians become presumptive targets whenever their records are examined.

Then donors become presumptive victims whenever financial records contain their information.

Then journalists become presumptive subjects whenever communications with officials appear in subpoena returns.

Then every person connected to a political movement becomes evidence that the movement itself was being investigated.

Follow that principle far enough and something remarkable happens.

Political organizations become nearly impossible to investigate.

Because any serious investigation of political misconduct will inevitably encounter:

politicians,

campaign employees,

donors,

activists,

lawyers,

consultants,

journalists,

businesses,

government officials,

and advocacy organizations.

The investigation cannot follow those connections because following them proves political targeting.

The investigation cannot collect records because innocent people will appear inside them.

It cannot obtain communications because some participant may be constitutionally privileged.

It cannot do so secretly because secrecy prevents those people from objecting.

And if investigators nevertheless proceed, the breadth of the resulting evidence becomes proof that the investigation was abusive.

It is a magnificent machine.

The wider the alleged conspiracy, the less investigable it becomes.

The more politically connected its participants, the more dangerous investigation becomes.

The more evidence investigators collect, the stronger the accusation that they collected too much.

And the more people the evidence touches, the easier it becomes to declare all of them victims.

Eventually, the investigation itself becomes the crime.

Evidence Is Not Guilt

There is a principle here considerably larger than Jack Smith.

Government investigations are intrusive.

That is why they require limits.

Subpoenas should have relevance.

Privileges should be respected.

Sensitive information should be minimized.

Judicial supervision should be meaningful.

Nondisclosure should require justification.

Investigators should be accountable when they exceed lawful authority.

None of that requires pretending investigations can somehow collect only information belonging to people who will ultimately be charged.

Evidence does not arrange itself that neatly.

Innocent people appear in telephone records.

They appear in bank statements.

They appear in emails.

They appear in photographs.

They appear in calendars.

They appear in witness interviews.

They appear because human beings exist in networks.

The question is not whether investigators encounter innocent people.

They inevitably will.

The question is what investigators do with that information, why they obtained it, whether obtaining it was reasonably related to the investigation, and what protections governed its use.

That is where oversight belongs.

Because once merely appearing in evidence becomes synonymous with being targeted by the state, we have created an impossible standard.

And curiously enough, the people who will benefit most from that impossible standard are people surrounded by large networks of political power.

A constitutional shield can become a moat.

A subpoena can become “spying.”

Evidence can become “targeting.”

And an investigation into whether political power was unlawfully used can, through careful rearrangement of vocabulary, become evidence that investigating political power was itself unlawful.

That is a very convenient doctrine.

Especially for political power.
