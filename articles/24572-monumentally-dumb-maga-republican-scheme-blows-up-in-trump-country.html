'Monumentally dumb': MAGA Republican scheme blows up in Trump country

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 9, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Missouri Republicans’ attempt to engineer a seventh GOP-held U.S. House seat was blocked before the November midterm election. Acting on President Donald Trump’s advice, Republican lawmakers approved new boundaries in September 2025 that weakened the Kansas City-based district represented by Democrat Emanuel Cleaver. Courts ultimately required the state to use its previous congressional map.

People Not Politicians collected more than 300,000 signatures seeking a referendum. Republican Secretary of State Denny Hoskins initially refused to certify it, arguing that Missouri’s petition process did not apply to redistricting, but the Missouri Supreme Court disagreed. On September 25, the U.S. Supreme Court left that result in place.

The discarded map had already governed the August primaries, leaving more than a million voters facing conflicting instructions about their districts as absentee voting began, according to the Associated Press. Democratic candidate Fred Wellman told The Guardian that the reversal strengthened fundraising and volunteer activity in his race against Republican Representative Ann Wagner. The election result remained undecided in the reporting.

Power drew the map

This was not an electoral misunderstanding. Trump urged the change, Missouri’s Republican legislators enacted it, and Hoskins tried to keep the referendum from proceeding. These officials possessed the authority to redraw districts, administer elections, and determine whether citizens could challenge their work.

Their objective was equally concrete: convert a six-to-two Republican advantage in Missouri’s House delegation into seven-to-one. The confusion came afterward. It was the consequence of officials changing the rules for partisan gain, not an explanation that dissolves responsibility.

Voters did not create the chaos

More than a million voters were reportedly affected after one map governed the primaries and another was ordered for November. A St. Louis-area voter told The Guardian that he no longer knew which district he lived in. That uncertainty is not evidence of a fickle or inattentive electorate. It is administrative damage inflicted from above.

Coverage that centers “confusion among voters” risks making ordinary people sound like the problem. The decisive sequence ran in the opposite direction: Republican officials imposed new boundaries, citizens organized a petition, the secretary of state resisted certification, and courts intervened. Voters were navigating the wreckage.

The check came from organized resistance

The courts blocked the map, but they did not spontaneously discover the dispute. A petition campaign gathered more than 300,000 signatures and forced the legitimacy of the plan into litigation. Institutional restraint arrived because people created the conditions for it.

That distinction matters. Judicial review checked this attempt, but only after the contested map had already been used in primaries. A remedy delivered that late can prevent one outcome while leaving damaged election administration, disrupted campaigns, and public distrust behind.

A setback is not accountability

Wellman called the scheme “monumentally dumb” because the reversal brought his campaign money, volunteers, and attention. Cleaver said some Republican voters were newly willing to support him and described the redistricting as “pure, undistilled cheating.” Those are the candidates’ assessments, not settled election results.

Calling the effort merely foolish, however, understates its political meaning. The plan failed in court and may have produced electoral backlash, but failure does not make the underlying use of power accidental. Republican officials pursued an additional House seat through lines designed to diminish a Democratic district. They have also indicated that further redistricting attempts may follow the midterms.

The system rewards repeated attempts

Missouri voters were set to decide Proposition A in November: a “no” vote would reject the legislature’s 2025 map, while a “yes” vote would approve it for future use. The reporting did not provide that vote’s outcome.

The larger pattern is a governing party treating electoral boundaries as instruments for insulating itself from voters, then leaving voters to absorb the procedural disorder when the maneuver is challenged. One map was blocked. The power, incentive, and announced willingness to try again remained.
