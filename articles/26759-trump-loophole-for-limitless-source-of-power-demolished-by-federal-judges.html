Trump loophole for 'limitless source of power' demolished by federal judges

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 22, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower without a legal foundationAn election lawsuit looking for a conflictThe courts blocked a strategy, not a tendencyThe systemic error

What happened

AlterNet reports that federal judges rejected key parts of the Trump administration’s attempt to use the Constitution’s supremacy clause to expand federal authority over immigration enforcement and voting. In New York, U.S. District Judge Ramón Reyes dismissed a Justice Department challenge to New York City’s sanctuary policies on October 15, finding that the federal government lacked standing and could not force local officials to carry out federal immigration policy.

The administration also sued in California to prevent ballots from roughly 20,000 people it considers likely noncitizens from being counted, despite California already prohibiting noncitizen voting in federal elections. Separately, on October 1, U.S. District Judge David Leibowitz dismissed the prosecution of a Jamaican-born woman who had unlawfully registered to vote, ruling that the federal provision was unconstitutional as applied to her. That decision neither invalidated the statute nationwide nor ended the California case.

Power without a legal foundation

The supremacy clause establishes that valid federal law prevails when it conflicts with state law. It does not manufacture powers the Constitution withholds from the federal government.

That distinction is fatal to the administration’s position as described here. New York City was not overriding federal immigration law; it was declining to provide local personnel and resources for federal enforcement. Reyes called the Justice Department’s preemption theory a “backdoor around the Tenth Amendment.” In plain terms, the administration tried to convert federal priority into federal command.

The federal government holds immense enforcement and litigation power. New York City did not create the underlying immigration regime, and local officials do not control the Justice Department. Casting their refusal to serve as federal auxiliaries as obstruction obscures who is demanding additional power from whom.

An election lawsuit looking for a conflict

The California case exposes an even thinner theory. The state already bans noncitizen voting in federal elections, so the conflict supposedly triggering federal supremacy is not apparent from AlterNet’s account.

What remains is an effort to stop the counting of ballots from about 20,000 people the administration labels likely noncitizens. “Likely” is doing dangerous work. The reporting does not establish that those people are noncitizens, that their ballots are unlawful, or that the government’s classification is accurate.

The proposed remedy therefore reaches beyond enforcing an agreed legal prohibition. It puts thousands of ballots at risk based on a federal allegation about voter eligibility. The foreseeable consequence is disenfranchisement if eligible voters are swept into the government’s category.

The courts blocked a strategy, not a tendency

These rulings matter, but “demolished” overstates the result. Reyes dismissed the New York suit, while Leibowitz’s Florida ruling applied to one prosecution and did not erase the statute nationwide or terminate the California litigation. The administration still has institutional resources to continue pressing its claims.

That asymmetry is part of the strategy’s power. Governments can lose individual cases while forcing cities, states, and defendants to spend time and money resisting theories that test the outer boundary of federal authority. A courtroom defeat constrains a claim; it does not undo the coercive effect of bringing it.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is executive expansion through deliberate constitutional inversion: a clause limiting state resistance to valid federal law is recast as an independent reservoir of federal power. The New Republic’s Matt Ford describes the administration as treating the clause as “nearly limitless.” The rulings reported by AlterNet show judges refusing that reading where other constitutional limits still control.

The misdirection is to portray local noncooperation or contested voter eligibility as the central abuse. The more consequential act is the Justice Department’s attempt to turn federal supremacy into authority to commandeer local government and interfere with ballot counting without an established conflict in state law. When constitutional boundaries are treated as obstacles to be litigated around, institutional restraint depends on judges repeatedly saying no—and on that refusal arriving before the government’s allegation becomes someone else’s lost vote or surrendered autonomy.
