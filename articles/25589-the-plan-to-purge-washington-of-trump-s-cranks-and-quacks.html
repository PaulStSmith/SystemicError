The plan to purge Washington of Trump's 'cranks and quacks'

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 11, 20263 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that New Republic writer Jason Linkins wants a future Democratic administration to vet and remove Trump-aligned federal employees who engaged in extremism or tried to undermine democratic government. Drawing on recent reporting, he argues that restoring the agencies would require more than rehiring experienced professionals.

The examples include Justice Department hires accused of serious ethical or legal misconduct, Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.’s declaration that his changes to HHS would outlast him, and what Linkins describes as extremist propaganda from the Department of Homeland Security. He endorses “lustration,” a term Yale Law professor Dina Francesca Haynes uses for removing public servants who support extremism or attacks on democracy.

AlterNet does not describe an official Democratic proposal, identify a participating campaign or present a governing blueprint. The “plan” in the headline is Linkins’s recommendation, not an adopted program.

The headline promises too much

Calling this a plan disguises how preliminary it is. Linkins has supplied an argument and a label. He has not supplied the legal standards, evidentiary rules, appeals process or institutional sponsor that would turn “lustration” into policy.

Those omissions are central. A competent government may discipline misconduct, remove unlawful appointees and screen candidates for relevant qualifications. A political purge based on affiliation or broadly defined beliefs is another matter. Without enforceable distinctions, the proposed cure risks borrowing the patronage logic it claims to reverse.

Trump’s appointees hold the power

The weaker actors here are not the officials running federal departments. Trump controls executive appointments; Kennedy runs HHS; Justice Department leadership determines hiring and enforcement priorities; Homeland Security controls personnel, communications and coercive machinery. These officials possess authority over public money, institutional expertise and, in DHS’s case, armed enforcement.

That hierarchy matters more than Linkins’s catalogue of “cranks and quacks.” Mocking an employee who cannot spell “north” may illustrate incompetence, but it can also shrink a structural assault into a freak show. The consequential decisions are made by leaders who select loyalists, relax professional standards and redirect agencies toward political objectives.

Degradation is a governing method

The reported pattern is not merely that eccentric people entered government. It is that loyalty can displace expertise while institutional leaders claim permanence for the resulting changes. Kennedy’s statement that HHS would never return to its previous form, even if he died that night, presents bureaucratic transformation as an accomplished legacy rather than a temporary personnel choice.

If the Justice Department’s Civil Rights Division has been hollowed out and filled with ethically compromised hires, the political consequence is larger than embarrassment. A division cannot reliably enforce civil-rights law when professional capacity and ethical standards are subordinated to factional loyalty. Administrative incompetence becomes politically useful when it disables protections that those in power do not value.

Accountability needs rules

Linkins is right to focus on durability: political appointees leave, but damaged hiring systems, lost expertise and embedded personnel can survive an election. He is less persuasive when he substitutes exterminator metaphors for institutional design. “Fumigation” and “taking out the garbage” erase the distinction between documented misconduct and ideological contamination.

A legitimate repair program would start with conduct: unlawful orders, ethical violations, falsified qualifications, abuse of authority and participation in prohibited political activity. It would require evidence and review. Otherwise, “rooting out” becomes an invitation for each administration to define the civil service as enemy territory.

The deeper contamination

The larger pattern is the conversion of public administration into personal rule. Agencies cease to be durable institutions governed by law and become prizes to be staffed, weakened and branded by whoever controls the presidency.

Reversing that project cannot mean installing a more agreeable class of loyalists. It requires rebuilding professional capacity while holding identifiable decision-makers responsible under clear rules. The systemic error is not simply that unqualified people obtained government jobs. It is that powerful officials made qualification secondary to obedience—and that their critics may answer an institutional problem with the seductive language of another purge.
