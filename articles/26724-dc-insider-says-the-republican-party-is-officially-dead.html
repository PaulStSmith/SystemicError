DC insider says the Republican Party is officially dead

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosOctober 20, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet published an essay by former Labor Secretary Robert Reich arguing that the Republican Party is “dead” as a governing institution because it has subordinated policy, principle, and internal dissent to Donald Trump. Reich’s claim is political, not literal: the party still exists and contests power, but he says loyalty to Trump has displaced its former commitments.

Reich points to Republican officials who supported Trump despite acknowledging his conduct surrounding January 6 and his false claims about the 2020 election. He highlights then–New Hampshire Governor Chris Sununu’s April 14, 2024, statement that backing Trump was ultimately about securing “a Republican administration.”

He also cites the Gerald R. Ford Presidential Foundation’s reported refusal in 2024 to honor former Representative Liz Cheney. According to former board member David Hume Kennerly, who resigned over the decision, its leaders feared a future Trump administration might retaliate against the foundation’s tax-exempt status.

Power survived the party

Calling the GOP dead risks obscuring the more consequential reality: an institution can abandon its governing identity without surrendering any of its machinery. Republican officeholders still possess votes, offices, donor networks, legislative authority, and access to executive power. What Reich describes is not political extinction but institutional capture.

That distinction matters. A dead party cannot govern. A captured party can govern aggressively while shedding the restraints that once made internal disagreement possible. The party’s weakness before Trump became Trump’s strength over the party.

The officials made a choice

Sununu’s explanation strips away the comforting fiction that Republican leaders were merely confused or overwhelmed. He acknowledged Trump’s role in the events surrounding January 6 and his election falsehoods, yet endorsed him because party control took precedence.

That is agency, not bewilderment. Republican officials were not powerless spectators watching a personality cult consume their organization. As Reich recounts it, they expelled or marginalized lawmakers who rejected Trump’s false election narrative and rewarded those willing to accommodate it. The outcome was enabled through endorsements, nominations, silence, and continued institutional support.

Fear became advance compliance

The Ford Foundation episode reveals how power operates before any order is issued. According to Kennerly, the organization withheld an honor from Cheney because its leaders feared possible retaliation under a second Trump administration. The essay does not establish that Trump directed the decision or had actually threatened the foundation’s tax status. It establishes something more limited but still corrosive: decision-makers reportedly changed their conduct in anticipation of what he might do.

Calling this timidity is accurate but incomplete. Institutional cowardice is not politically neutral. When prominent organizations protect themselves by avoiding symbolic opposition, they transfer the cost of resistance to people with less money, prestige, and insulation.

The blame slides downward

Reich partly weakens his own indictment when he turns from Republican leaders to an “angry, anxious working class” craving a strongman. Voters have agency, and racial resentment is not an innocent misunderstanding. But party officials, donors, media operators, and institutional gatekeepers possess far greater power to select candidates, normalize lies, enforce loyalty, and convert grievance into government.

The central story is not that distressed citizens mysteriously destroyed a party. It is that powerful Republicans repeatedly decided that Trump’s coalition was useful, then reorganized the party around preserving it. Blaming mass cynicism without maintaining that hierarchy of responsibility lets the architects hide among their audience.

The system rewards surrender

Reich traces the party’s collapse to Nixon’s pardon and the lesson that presidents can escape accountability. His broader point is stronger than the obituary metaphor: impunity compounds. Each institution that declines to impose a cost makes the next surrender easier to justify.

The systemic error is treating a party’s moral disintegration as though it also means political irrelevance. An institution emptied of principle but stocked with power is not dead. It is available—to the leader who commands loyalty, to officials who prefer victory to restraint, and to every organization willing to comply before being ordered.
