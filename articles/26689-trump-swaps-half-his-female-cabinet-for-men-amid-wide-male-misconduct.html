Trump swaps half his female cabinet for men amid wide male misconduct

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 18, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet, citing the Guardian, reports that four of the Trump administration’s eight female cabinet officials left or were removed within several months, and that men replaced all four. They were Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem, Attorney General Pam Bondi, Labor Secretary Lori Chavez-DeRemer and Director of National Intelligence Tulsi Gabbard. Trump separately denied that he was considering replacing White House chief of staff Susie Wiles.

The reported departures followed distinct events. Noem was fired on March 5 amid uproar over immigration operations that killed two U.S. citizens in Minneapolis. Bondi was ousted in April after failing to prosecute Trump’s political opponents aggressively enough, according to the Guardian, and after political fallout from her handling of the Epstein-file release. Chavez-DeRemer resigned amid inspector-general allegations of workplace misconduct. Gabbard was pushed out after telling Congress that Iran was not building a nuclear weapon, contradicting Trump’s case for attacking Iran.

AlterNet contrasts those exits with the continued employment of senior men facing misconduct or competence allegations, including Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and FBI Director Kash Patel. The report also notes other women’s departures, including press secretary Katie Zacharia after five days and an ambassador identified only as Kimberly after allegations that she sought money from a donor in exchange for administration access.

The power belongs to Trump

The decisive fact is not that women happened to leave. It is that Trump controlled who remained, who was dismissed and who replaced them. The pattern described by AlterNet is therefore a record of presidential personnel decisions, not an unexplained demographic drift.

Each cabinet official’s conduct still has to be judged on its own evidence. Chavez-DeRemer faced serious allegations; Bondi and Noem presided over consequential controversies. Gender does not immunize an official from accountability. But accountability becomes patronage when punishment depends less on conduct than on usefulness to the president.

Loyalty is the operating standard

The clearest examples are Bondi and Gabbard. According to the reporting, Bondi’s failure was not institutional negligence alone but insufficient aggression against Trump’s political enemies. Gabbard’s offense was giving Congress an intelligence assessment that conflicted with the president’s argument for war.

That is an authoritarian personnel logic: officials are retained for obedience and discarded when professional judgment obstructs presidential preference. Gabbard’s replacement—first by loyalist Bill Pulte, who reportedly lacked intelligence experience, and later by Jay Clayton—underscores the priority. Expertise was subordinate to alignment.

Misconduct is not applied evenly

The headline’s emphasis on “male misconduct” risks flattening important distinctions: the allegations against these officials vary in seriousness, substantiation and relevance to their jobs. But the underlying comparison is legitimate. The administration reportedly tolerated grave concerns surrounding powerful men while rapidly removing women, sometimes for disloyalty rather than misconduct.

Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez described that as rewarding male misconduct. The evidence presented cannot establish that every personnel decision was caused by sexism. It does establish a gendered outcome produced by discretionary choices: half the women in the cabinet departed, every replacement was a man, and senior male officials remained despite reported controversies.

The women were never the power

Trump’s appointment of women functioned as a defense against accusations of misogyny. That argument was always cosmetic. Giving individual women prestigious jobs does not redistribute presidential authority, constrain abusive policy or create neutral standards of accountability.

Noem’s role makes the point especially starkly. As the public face of mass deportation, she exercised substantial power over people with far less institutional protection. Her eventual dismissal does not turn her into the primary victim of that system. The relevant hierarchy runs downward from the president through cabinet officials to those subjected to state force.

Patronage consumes institutions

This story is larger than a cabinet gender count. It describes an administration in which continued employment appears tied to personal loyalty, inconvenient professional judgments carry penalties, and alleged misconduct matters unevenly depending on who commits it and whether that person remains useful.

The result is institutional decay with a personnel chart. Competence becomes optional, candor becomes dangerous, and accountability becomes selective. Women can be displayed as evidence of inclusion and discarded when they cease serving the president’s political needs. The governing principle is not equality or even consistent discipline. It is proximity to power on power’s terms.
