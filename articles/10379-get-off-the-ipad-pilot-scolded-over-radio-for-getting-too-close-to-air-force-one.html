'Get off the iPad!' Pilot scolded over radio for getting too close to Air Force One

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 18, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Skies of Discontent: A Closer Look at the Air Traffic SnafuThe IncidentThe ExplanationThe FAA's ResponseThe Bigger Picture

An air traffic controller scolded a Spirit Airlines pilot in a stern message after the pilot got too close to Air Force One with President Donald Trump on board.

"Pay attention, Spirit 1300. Turn 20 degrees right now..." the air traffic controller in New York Center says to the Spirit Flight 1300 pilot in audio played live on CNN on Wednesday. "Pay attention. Spirit 1300, traffic is off of your left wing by eight miles. 747. I'm sure you can see who it is. Keep an eye out for him, he's white and blue. Pay attention, get off the iPad."

The iPad reference, CNN aviation correspondent Pete Muntean reports, is important. The pilot was not looking at memes, Muntean joked, but instead pilots use the technology to navigate and for other functions in the air.

Muntean also added that air traffic controllers are under a lot of stress with mandatory six to eight weeks of 12-hour shifts.

The FAA issued a statement:

"The FAA is aware of social media posts about Air Force One and a Spirit Airlines flight in Boston airspace on Tuesday, Sept. 16. Required separation was maintained between the aircraft."

Trump was heading to the U.K. on Air Force One ahead of a state visit.

Our Analysis:

The Skies of Discontent: A Closer Look at the Air Traffic Snafu

In an incident that could easily serve as a metaphor for the state of our nation's aviation safety and oversight, a Spirit Airlines pilot found himself on the receiving end of a stern reprimand from an air traffic controller. This wasn't just any flight path mishap; this was a close shave with Air Force One, carrying none other than President Donald Trump. The event, which unfolded in the airspace over Boston, has sparked significant concerns, discussions, and, of course, a statement from the FAA. Let's dive into the facts, the failures, and the farcical nature of the explanations offered.

The Incident

"Pay attention, Spirit 1300. Turn 20 degrees right now..." These words, delivered with an urgency that's rare yet necessary in air traffic communications, were directed at a Spirit Airlines pilot who had apparently allowed his aircraft to get uncomfortably close to the world's most recognizable Boeing 747. The air traffic controller's admonition was broadcasted live, adding a public dimension to what is usually a private exchange. The controller's message didn't mince words: "Pay attention. Get off the iPad."

The Explanation

CNN's aviation correspondent, Pete Muntean, offered some context that might initially elicit a chuckle. The pilot wasn't browsing memes; rather, he was using the iPad as part of the modern cockpit's arsenal of tools for navigation and other essential functions. Yet, this attempt at lightening the narrative does little to assuage the underlying concerns. It's indicative of a broader issue within our skies: the reliance on technology without adequate safeguards or attention.

Furthermore, Muntean shines a light on the immense pressure air traffic controllers face, with mandatory six to eight weeks of 12-hour shifts. This detail is not just a footnote; it's a critical piece of understanding the environment in which these guardians of the sky operate. The stress, the fatigue, and the relentless focus required cannot be overstated. Yet, it's treated almost as an occupational hazard, a given in a field where there's no room for error.

The FAA's Response

The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA)'s statement is a masterpiece of noncommittal language, noting merely that "required separation was maintained" between the aircraft. This bureaucratic brush-off does little to address the real issues at hand or to reassure the public that measures are in place to prevent future occurrences. It's the verbal equivalent of shrugging one's shoulders and moving on, a posture that's all too common in the aftermath of incidents that could have had catastrophic consequences.

The Bigger Picture

This incident, while resolved without physical harm, serves as a stark reminder of the vulnerabilities in our air traffic control system. The reliance on overworked controllers, the integration of technology that can distract as much as it aids, and the FAA's tepid responses to potential disasters are all symptoms of a systemic problem.

The skies are a shared space, one that requires vigilance, strict adherence to protocols, and a culture that prioritizes safety above all. When a commercial pilot and Air Force One can come within a proverbial stone's throw of each other, it's a clear sign that our current systems and attitudes are not up to the task.

In the end, this incident should not just fade into the news cycle as an oddity or a close call. It should be a wake-up call. The skies, much like the country below, are in desperate need of attention, investment, and reform. The next time a stern warning is issued, it may not be enough to avert disaster.
