The song Trump loves gives tune to his spreading rot

Based on an article by Paul Rogat Loeb

Patriotism as a Blank Check

The Concert, the Message, the Damage

The immediate story is small and revealing: after musician after musician pulled out of Trump’s “Freedom 250” concert, the event was left with Lee Greenwood, an opera tenor, a few military bands, and Kash Patel’s girlfriend. The deeper story is bigger. The article treats that lineup not as an embarrassment but as a symptom of how Trump-style politics keeps trying to convert patriotism into spectacle, loyalty, and permission.

Who Actually Holds Power

Trump holds the institutional power here. Not Greenwood. Not the singers who backed out. Not the military bands drafted into the tableau. The concert is not about music; it is about presidential branding. Trump uses patriotic ritual to launder political authority, turning the flag, the anthem, and the armed forces into props for his own rule.

That matters because the article identifies the real function of this kind of performance: not to honor the country, but to naturalize whatever the powerful are already doing. If the anthem becomes a shield, then accountability disappears behind sentiment.

The Misdirection

The article is right to reject the lazy framing that treats this as harmless celebration. “God Bless the USA” is not presented here as a neutral singalong. It is a political technology. Its message is simple: America is free, therefore whatever leaders do in America inherits legitimacy. That is not patriotism. That is consent manufacturing.

The article also names the trick Trump depends on: redirecting attention from power to ritual. While ICE agents “grab innocent people off America’s streets,” while Trump cozies up to Vladimir Putin, Recep Erdoğan, and Mohammed bin Salman, the patriotic soundtrack tells viewers to feel proud instead of asking who is being harmed. That is how state violence gets wrapped in flags and applause.

Greenwood’s Anthem, Reagan’s Politics

Greenwood did not invent the larger machine; he gave it a hook. The article places “God Bless the USA” in the Reagan era for a reason. Reagan used patriotic media to sell an economic program that broke unions and enriched the wealthy. The song’s emotional innocence helped furnish cover for a political project of redistribution upward.

That connection is the point. The anthem’s vagueness is its usefulness. It blesses the nation without defining justice, duty, or limits. If freedom means nothing more than living under the American flag, then every abuse can be recast as a patriotic act. That is not an accident. It is the operating logic.

Better Patriotism Exists

The article does something important here: it refuses the false choice between cynicism and cheerleading. It points to other songs that name obligations instead of demanding obedience. Waylon Jennings asks America to “promise and then follow through.” Katherine Lee Bates wanted “liberty in law.” Springsteen’s work stays with ordinary people, not abstract power.

That contrast is the heart of the piece. A serious patriotism does not excuse the country from its crimes. It measures the country against its own promises. It asks whether the people in charge have earned the symbols they are using.

The Larger Pattern

This is the recurring grammar of modern authoritarian politics: take a public virtue, empty it of content, then use the shell to shield power from scrutiny. Patriotism becomes applause. Security becomes cruelty. Freedom becomes a slogan attached to censorship, deportation, dictatorship, and inequality.

That is why the article’s framing is strongest when it names the real issue directly: not that Trump staged a concert with a thin roster, but that he depends on a political culture where symbolic loyalty can substitute for moral limits. Once that substitution is accepted, the rest follows naturally. The song ends up defending the power that is eroding the country it claims to love.
