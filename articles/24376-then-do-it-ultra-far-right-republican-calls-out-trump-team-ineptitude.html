'Then do it': Ultra far-right Republican calls out Trump team ineptitude

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 3, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power is in the administrationWorkers become the targetEnforcement as spectacleThe article’s framing falls shortThe radical flank sets the clock

What happened

AlterNet reports that James Fishback, a far-right Republican who lost Florida’s 2026 gubernatorial primary to Byron Donalds, mocked the Trump administration for failing to act on its own hostility toward H-1B visas. After Vice President JD Vance called the program “completely broken” and suggested abolishing it, Fishback replied on X: “Then do it. What are you waiting for?”

H-1B visas provide temporary work authorization to foreign professionals in specialized occupations and are widely used by technology companies. During his campaign, Fishback demanded that 1,900 Florida employers fire H-1B workers and hire Floridians instead, threatening businesses with what he called the “ultimate wrath” of state government.

AlterNet does not report a new administration decision on the program. The event is a dispute over action: Vance denounced the system, while Fishback accused the administration of substituting rhetoric for policy.

The power is in the administration

Fishback currently holds no public office. His leverage is ideological and rhetorical, not institutional. The officials with actual power are Vance, President Trump, and an administration capable of proposing legislation, changing executive policy within legal limits, and directing immigration enforcement.

That distinction matters because Fishback’s post is less an act of opposition than an attempt to enforce discipline from the administration’s right. He is demanding that officials convert a shared political target into government action.

Workers become the target

Vance framed H-1B hiring through a hypothetical in which a lower-paid foreign accountant replaces an American worker. AlterNet’s report does not establish how representative that example is, nor does it provide evidence that abolishing the entire program would restore particular jobs or raise wages.

Fishback goes further. His threat against Florida employers casts foreign employees—not the companies making hiring and wage decisions—as disposable intruders. Employers control recruitment, compensation, and staffing. Migrant workers do not design the visa system or set corporate labor policy. Yet Fishback’s proposed punishment begins with firing them.

Enforcement as spectacle

The phrase “ultimate wrath of the state” reveals the political appeal. Fishback did not merely advocate different visa rules; he advertised state retaliation against businesses that employed legally authorized workers.

The underlying labor question—who suppresses wages, who profits, and which rules permit abuse—gets displaced by a spectacle of expulsion. That is scapegoating with an administrative costume: concentrate public anger on workers with less political power while leaving corporate control of employment largely intact.

The article’s framing falls short

AlterNet correctly identifies Fishback’s complaint as an attack on the administration’s failure to move quickly. But describing the exchange chiefly as “ineptitude” risks accepting Fishback’s premise. The reported facts do not establish bureaucratic incompetence. They establish a gap between Vance’s rhetoric and government action.

That gap could reflect legal constraints, political disagreement, competing business interests, or simple inaction; the article does not determine which. Calling it ineptitude turns an unresolved policy choice into a management failure and makes faster coercion sound like competence.

The radical flank sets the clock

This episode shows how authoritarian policy pressure can operate without electoral success. A defeated candidate can still police the governing coalition by treating denunciation as insufficient and delay as betrayal. The demand is always for quicker punishment, broader executive force, and fewer distinctions between regulating employers and purging workers.

Fishback’s post is therefore not important because he controls the state. It is important because he is telling those who do control it what increasingly counts as proof of loyalty: not merely attacking immigrants in speeches, but making identifiable people bear the consequences.
