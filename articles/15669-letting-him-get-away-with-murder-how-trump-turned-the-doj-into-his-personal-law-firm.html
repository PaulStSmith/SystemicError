'Letting him get away with murder': How Trump turned the DOJ into his personal law firm

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 25, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The DOJ's Disturbing Transformation: Trump's Strategy of Chaos

FILE PHOTO: U.S. President Donald Trump gestures beside U.S. first lady Melania Trump as they leave the U.S. Capitol building on the inauguration day of Donald Trump’s second presidential term in Washington, U.S. January 20, 2025. REUTERS/Carlos Barria/File Photo

NOTUS reports President Donald Trump has remade the U.S. Dept. of Justice in his own image, and that image is: "Delay, obstruct, confuse."

Justice Department attorneys have adopted the same litigation strategies Trump’s defense team often used when battling tax charges, sexual abuse and stolen classified documents cases. Currently, for example, the DOJ is “ignoring federal judges, claiming privileges to refuse turning over records and toying with the clock to disrupt the normal pattern of court cases” — all straight from the playbook Trump’s personal defense team used between Trump’s first and second presidencies.

“We know that Trump views the DOJ as his lawyers,” George Washington University Law School professor emeritus Catherine J. Ross told NOTUS. “And it almost seems as if theclient is telling them, ‘Do what I did in the civil cases and earlier criminal cases: delay, obstruct, confuse, be obtuse, appeal every little possibility and just stall forever.’ That’s the strategy.”

The new strategy coincides with Trump hiring two personal lawyers to man the Justice Department, including deputy attorney general Todd Blanche and principal associate deputy attorney general Emil Bove.

Judges are now having to adapt to these unfamiliar new stances. This week U.S. District Judge Paula Xinis chided the DOJ for refusing to hand over documents proving the administration was following Supreme Court orders to “facilitate” the return of Kilmar Armando Abrego Garcia from a Salvadoran prison. Suddenly judges are having to tell the attorneys of a 150-year-old agency not to “mischaracterize” a Supreme Court’s order.

“For weeks, defendants have sought refuge behind vague and unsubstantiated assertions of privilege, using them as a shield to obstruct discovery and evade compliance with this court’s orders. Defendants have known, at least since last week, that this court requires specific legal and factual showings to support any claim of privilege. Yet they have continued to rely on boilerplate assertions,” she wrote.

What administration lawyers are delivering in lieu of court-mandated updates are snide comments, say critics.

“Please refer to my prior declarations for the foundation of my personal knowledge,” Department of Homeland Security acting general counsel Joseph N. Mazzara wrote in response to Xinis’ demand for updates on Garecia’s return, “There are no further updates.”

“The judges are letting Trump get away with murder,” said Pamela Keith, a lawyer who represents current FBI agents and support personnel in their effort to stop the DOJ from retaliating against them over their work in the aftermath of Jan. 6. “The judges are going to extremes to give the DOJ chances to explain the inexplicable. And this is just more indication of how successful the Trump team has been at perverting our legal system.”

Our Analysis:

The DOJ's Disturbing Transformation: Trump's Strategy of Chaos

In a recent exposé by NOTUS, a stark and troubling picture has been painted of the U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ), one that should raise alarms for anyone with a shred of concern for the integrity of American judicial processes. Under President Donald Trump, the DOJ has been accused of morphing into a mirror image of Trump's personal litigation tactics - a strategy succinctly summarized as "Delay, obstruct, confuse."

The Playbook of Obstruction

The NOTUS report outlines how DOJ attorneys have seemingly adopted the dubious litigation strategies that were hallmarks of Trump's defense in various legal battles, ranging from tax charges to accusations of sexual abuse and mishandling classified documents. This approach, characterized by ignoring federal judges, claiming undue privileges, and manipulating timelines, is alarmingly reminiscent of tactics employed by Trump's personal defense team.

Professor emeritus Catherine J. Ross from George Washington University Law School didn't mince words when she highlighted the apparent client-lawyer dynamic between Trump and the DOJ, suggesting that the department is being directed to employ a strategy of delay, obstruction, and confusion. This is a damning indictment of how far the DOJ has strayed from its mission.

Judicial Frustration and Legal Anarchy

The consequences of this transformation are profound, with judges forced to navigate uncharted waters. U.S. District Judge Paula Xinis' criticism of the DOJ's refusal to comply with court orders and its reliance on "vague and unsubstantiated assertions of privilege" lays bare the extent of the department's recalcitrance. The DOJ's response, or lack thereof, to demands for updates on specific cases, such as the return of Kilmar Armando Abrego Garcia from a Salvadoran prison, exemplifies this defiance.

Moreover, the appointment of Todd Blanche and Emil Bove, two of Trump's personal lawyers, to pivotal roles within the DOJ raises significant concerns about the impartiality and independence of the department. It's a move that blurs the lines between personal loyalty and public duty, casting a long shadow over the DOJ's actions and intentions.

The Fallout: A Compromised Legal System

The ramifications of this shift in DOJ strategy extend far beyond the immediate legal battles. Critics, including Pamela Keith, a lawyer representing FBI agents and support personnel, argue that the judiciary's leniency towards the DOJ's tactics is indicative of a broader erosion of legal norms and accountability. This "perversion of our legal system," as Keith puts it, undermines the very foundations of justice and the rule of law.

In essence, the NOTUS report sheds light on a deeply troubling trend within the Department of Justice under Trump's administration. The adoption of a legal strategy designed to delay, obstruct, and confuse not only compromises the integrity of the DOJ but also poses a direct threat to the principles of justice and accountability that underpin the American legal system. As this situation unfolds, one must ask: at what point does the pursuit of personal defense strategies threaten the very structure of our judicial processes? And more importantly, what can be done to reverse this dangerous course?

The answers to these questions are critical, as the implications of this transformation reach far beyond the courtroom. They touch on the very essence of democracy and the rule of law. It's a moment of reckoning for the American legal system, and the path chosen will have lasting impacts on the fabric of the nation.
