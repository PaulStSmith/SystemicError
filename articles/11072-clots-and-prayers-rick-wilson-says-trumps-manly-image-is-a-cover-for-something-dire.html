‘Clots and prayers’: Rick Wilson says Trump’s ‘manly image’ is a cover for something dire

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 30, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A view of the hand of U.S. President Donald Trump at the White House on Friday. REUTERS/Annabelle Gordon

Rick Wilson, political strategist and co-founder of the Lincoln Project, suggested Friday that President Donald Trump’s “tough guy” persona, one fed into by many of his most loyal supporters, is in many ways a cover for something far more dire.

“This idea that a lot of folks have in their head, that Donald Trump is ill, appears to be correct,” Wilson said, speaking on an episode of the Lincoln Project’s “Elephant in the Room” podcast in an episode titled “Sending Clots and Prayers.”

“I’m not going to pretend that I'm a doctor and can make a diagnosis of Trump, but I can make a diagnosis about why they're doing the way they're doing it.”

Wilson went on to recount the “obvious signs of Trump’s mental and physical decline,” including his bruised and discolored hands, his swollen ankles linked to vascular issues, and the increased instances of rambling during speeches.

“These aren't negotiable, these aren't debatable, these aren't hypothetical, they're real,” Wilson said.

“You've seen the giant cankles that Trump is rocking from the edema that's in his body. You've seen his inability to stay on task or on target while he's speaking. You've seen the bruising on his hands from whatever treatment they're giving him.”

Trump has fiercely denied claims that he is in poor health, proclaiming himself a “very stable genius,” and bragging about getting “every answer right” on an April cognitive test. He’s also said that physically, he was “in very good shape” with a “good heart” and a “good soul.”

Were Trump’s health to be worse than he claims, Wilson proposed, his “manly man” persona could be tarnished, and it’s for that reason, Wilson argued, that Trump’s administration feels so compelled to hide details as to Trump’s physical and mental health.

“Why is it that they're hiding this? The first is, Trump always commits to the bit, he always commits to the image-making flim flam of Trump,” Wilson said.

“A larger number of men bought into the 'Trump's the most virile manly man to ever be in the White House' – yea, Teddy Roosevelt would like to have a talk mother f------.”

Our Analysis:

The Masquerade of Masculinity: Trump's Health Under the Microscope

Rick Wilson, a political strategist and a name synonymous with the anti-Trump movement through his association with the Lincoln Project, has once again stirred the pot with his commentary on President Donald Trump's health. In a recent episode of the Lincoln Project’s podcast, Wilson pulls no punches in suggesting that Trump's "tough guy" facade is not just a political strategy but a desperate cover-up for a deteriorating physical and mental state. Let's dissect this assertion, shall we?

The Allegations: Wilson points to "obvious signs" of Trump's decline, including bruised and discolored hands, swollen ankles linked to vascular issues, and increased instances of rambling during speeches. These are serious claims, and if true, they paint a picture of a leader not at the peak of his powers as he would have us believe. Trump's self-proclaimed status as a "very stable genius" and his boasts about acing a cognitive test only add layers of irony to this unfolding drama.

The Reality Check: It's crucial to note here that Wilson, by his own admission, is not a medical professional. His observations, while concerning, are not a substitute for a thorough medical examination. However, the physical signs he describes are not mere fabrications—they have been visible to the public eye, raising legitimate questions about the state of Trump's health.

The "Manly Man" Persona: The crux of Wilson's argument lies in the perceived necessity for Trump to maintain his "manly man" image—a persona that has endeared him to a significant segment of his base. This image, characterized by toughness and an unyielding nature, is antithetical to the vulnerabilities implied by serious health issues. Wilson's scathing critique of this facade is not just about health but about the authenticity of leadership. To quote Wilson, a direct challenge to Trump's virility, "Teddy Roosevelt would like to have a talk mother f------."

The Secrecy Surrounding Trump's Health: The allegation that Trump's administration might be concealing the true extent of his health issues is perhaps the most damning if proven true. It suggests a level of manipulation and deceit that goes beyond personal vanity—it speaks to a systemic effort to mislead the public about the capability of the person occupying the highest office in the land.

Conclusion: Rick Wilson's commentary, while provocative, raises valid concerns about transparency, authenticity, and the physical fitness of political leaders. The insistence on projecting an invulnerable image at the expense of honesty not only misleads the public but also sets dangerous precedents for future leaders. Whether Trump's health is as dire as Wilson suggests remains to be seen, but the conversation about the physical and mental fitness of our leaders is one that we should not shy away from.

In the theater of politics where perception often trumps reality, the health of a President should not be treated as another pawn in the game of image management. The American public deserves transparency, not a performance. And if the allegations hold any water, then it's not just Trump's health that's in decline—it's the integrity of the office he holds.
