'Why did you do this?' Patel put on the spot by baffled GOP senator on bestiality move

The FBI Hearing Became a Tutorial in Republican Misdirection

The Exchange

At a Senate Judiciary Committee hearing, FBI Director Kash Patel tried to explain why bestiality appeared in agency screening guidance: some applicants may have been trafficking victims forced into degrading acts. Senator John Kennedy repeatedly interrupted, conflating coerced victims with willing participants and turning a question about culpability into a spectacle about standards.

Power Was Sitting at the Microphone

Patel runs the FBI. Kennedy helps oversee it. Neither man was powerless, and neither was discussing an abstraction.

The agency decides whom to disqualify. Senators decide whether its policies receive serious scrutiny or theatrical distortion. Patel’s relevant claim was that an automatic ban could punish people compelled into sexual abuse by traffickers. Kennedy’s response was to keep asking whether a hypothetical man who “participated” should be hired, stripping coercion from the scenario Patel was attempting to describe.

That omission did the political work. Once force disappears, a trafficking victim can be recast as a deviant applicant asking the FBI to lower its standards.

A Victim Is Not an Accomplice

The distinction Kennedy resisted is elementary: committing an act voluntarily is not the same as being forced to endure it. An institution capable of investigating trafficking should be capable of recognizing that difference in its hiring rules.

Kennedy treated the word “bestiality” as if its mere appearance proved bureaucratic depravity. But the policy question was not whether the FBI approves of the act. It was whether an automatic exclusion should erase coercion, victimization, and individual responsibility.

His interruptions prevented that question from being answered clearly. The confusion was not generated by the policy alone; it was actively maintained by the hearing.

“Fairly or Unfairly” Is an Alibi

Kennedy said people believe political views affect who can become an FBI agent, “fairly or unfairly,” then argued that discussing this screening issue had further damaged the bureau. That framing launders a political campaign against the FBI into ambient public distrust for which nobody is accountable.

Public suspicion does not materialize independently of the officials and media figures who cultivate it. Kennedy acknowledged the partisan “game” of trying to make agency leaders look bad, then continued playing it: invoke institutional mistrust, sensationalize a narrow rule, interrupt the explanation, and cite the resulting confusion as evidence that the institution has lost credibility.

The hearing did not merely examine reputational damage. It manufactured another installment of it.

Oversight as Content Production

Congressional oversight is supposed to clarify who made a decision, what the rule says, and what consequences follow. Kennedy instead produced a clip. The grotesque subject supplied the hook; the hypothetical applicant supplied the caricature; the trafficking context became disposable.

The source article’s framing also indulges the spectacle. Describing Kennedy as unable to “get his head around” the issue makes the encounter sound like personal bewilderment. That is too gentle. Whatever Kennedy privately understood, his questioning publicly collapsed the distinction between perpetrator and victim after Patel introduced coercion. The political consequence matters more than the senator’s state of mind.

The System Rewards the Distortion

This is a familiar pattern: officials weaken institutional legitimacy, cite the weakness as a public concern, and then use oversight proceedings to generate more distrust. Victims become suspicious. Nuance becomes evidence of corruption. The official creating the distortion poses as the man bravely trying to “get to the bottom” of it.

The larger failure is not that Washington briefly became confused about an obscene subject. It is that institutional power was used to make moral and legal distinctions harder to see—and the manufactured confusion was then presented as proof that the institution itself cannot be trusted.
