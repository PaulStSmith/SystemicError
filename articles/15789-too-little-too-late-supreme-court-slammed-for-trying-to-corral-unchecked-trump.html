'Too little too late': Supreme Court slammed for trying to corral 'unchecked' Trump

U.S. Supreme Court Justice Samuel Alito (from Creative Commons)

The U.S. Supreme Court issued a7-2 ruling Saturday that orders theTrump Administration totemporarily cease the deportations of Venezuelan nationals using the Alien Enemies Act of 1798. Thetwo dissenters were far-right GOP-appointed Justices Clarence Thomas andSamuel Alito.

The ruling comes at a time when human rights activists are decrying the deportation of Kilmar Abrego García, a Salvadoran man who was living in Maryland legally and is now being held in a Salvadoran prison. President Donald Trump and his allies are claiming that García was a member of the MS-13 gang, but García's defenders and relatives maintain that there is no evidence linking him to MS-13 and stress that he was never charged with anything.

Human rights attorney Jesselyn Radack analyzes the High Court's ruling in anarticle published by Salon on Monday, arguing that it is "too little too late" — especially in light of the Court's 2024 decision in Trump v. the United States, which said that U.S. presidents are immune from criminal prosecution for "official" acts committed in office but not for unofficial acts.

In her Salon article, Radack argues that human rights abuses under former President George W. Bush helped pave the way for the García case — but that President Donald Trump is taking human rights abuses to another level.

"As a human rights attorney," Radack explains, "I've seen some of the worst conduct by government employees, military officials, and private contractors — often done under the weighty guise of protecting the country from mythical ticking time bombs. My unfortunate niche is innocent Americans who were mistreated, maimed, or killed in the name of elastic, expansive, nebulous, and incendiary words like 'terrorists,' 'insider threats,' 'enemies within,' 'illegals,' and 'traitors.' I am a first-hand witness to our nation's decades-long descent into lawlessness. I know exactly how we got here."

Radack continues, "President Obama's decision to 'look forward, not backwards' — an ethos also embraced by predecessors and successors alike — past the architects who carried out and covered up torture and other human rights atrocities paved the way for today's lawless incursions on people's fundamental constitutional due process rights and political freedoms. The shocking circumstances of Kilmar Abrego García's detention in an El Salvadoran gulag is the logical conclusion stemming from impunity for egregious government conduct."

Trump, Radack warns, isn't being discouraged by the United States' federal courts.

"The Supreme Court's recent grant of presumptive presidential immunity from prosecution for all of a president's official acts just further insulated unchecked extralegal conduct," Radack observes. "Their 'middle of the night' 7-2 decision this weekend granting the request in an emergency appeal to block further extradition flights may be too little too late."

Radack adds, "The same Supreme Court effectively insulated extralegal conduct as long as it has the imprimatur of being 'official.' The Abrego García case seems to have crossed that line. But instead of line-crossing and then backtracking, Trump is doubling down on lawlessness and enjoying the bubbling constitutional crisis. History will justifiably excoriate the United States for this, whether our democratic republic survives or not."

Our Analysis:

The Supreme Court's Late Conscience and the Unchecked Presidential Power: A Dangerous Precedent

The recent 7-2 ruling by the U.S. Supreme Court, ordering a temporary halt on the deportations of Venezuelan nationals under the archaic Alien Enemies Act of 1798, shines a dim light in the otherwise dark corridors of the Trump Administration's immigration policies. However, this flicker of judicial intervention does little to obscure the larger, more disturbing trend of expanding presidential immunity and disregard for human rights—a trend sharply criticized by human rights attorney Jesselyn Radack in her Salon article.

The Dissenting Shadows of Thomas and Alito

The ruling's dissenters, Justices Clarence Thomas and Samuel Alito, represent the far-right fringe of the GOP-appointed justices, whose dissent underscores a troubling alignment with the administration's hardline stance against immigrants and asylum seekers. Their dissent is not just a disagreement with the majority's decision; it is indicative of a judicial philosophy that seems all too willing to rubber-stamp policies that trample on basic human rights, under the guise of national security and executive authority.

The Case of Kilmar Abrego García: A Symptom of a Larger Disease

At the heart of the controversy is the case of Kilmar Abrego García, a Salvadoran man whose life has been upended by baseless allegations and the U.S.'s relentless deportation machine. The Trump Administration's claims of García's association with MS-13, despite a glaring lack of evidence, is a stark example of how narratives are manipulated to justify inhumane policies. This is not an isolated incident but a symptom of a systemic issue where individuals are stripped of their rights and dignity based on unfounded suspicions.

Presidential Immunity: A Blank Check for Abuse

Radack's critique of the Supreme Court's 2024 decision, which effectively grants U.S. presidents immunity from criminal prosecution for "official" acts, hits the nail on the head. This ruling doesn't just protect the president; it insulates a wide range of executive actions from scrutiny and accountability, paving the way for unchecked abuses of power. The Trump v. the United States decision is a dangerous precedent, granting a level of invincibility to the presidency that is antithetical to the very principles of a democratic republic.

A Legacy of Impunity

Radack eloquently traces the lineage of today's abuses to past administrations, highlighting the continuity of impunity from George W. Bush's era to the present. The decision by President Obama to "look forward, not backwards" has only perpetuated a cycle of unaccountability, allowing human rights abuses to fester and grow. The case of García is not an anomaly but a direct result of this legacy of impunity, where the architects of torture and violations of due process have been allowed to walk free.

Too Little, Too Late

The Supreme Court's decision to block further extradition flights is a mere drop in the ocean of necessary judicial intervention. As Radack points out, this "middle of the night" decision, while welcome, cannot undo the damage already inflicted by policies that have torn apart families and destroyed lives. It is a band-aid on a gaping wound, offering little solace to those who have already suffered at the hands of a lawless administration.

Conclusion: A Call for Accountability

The narrative spun by the Trump Administration and its enablers in the judiciary is one of fear and division, painting immigrants and asylum seekers as threats to be neutralized at all costs. This narrative, however, is a smokescreen for the erosion of democratic norms and human rights. It is imperative that we, as a society, demand accountability and justice for the victims of these policies. History will indeed judge the United States for its actions—and inactions—in the face of such blatant disregard for the rule of law and human dignity. The question remains: will we continue to stand by as our democratic republic is undermined, or will we fight for the principles upon which it was founded?
