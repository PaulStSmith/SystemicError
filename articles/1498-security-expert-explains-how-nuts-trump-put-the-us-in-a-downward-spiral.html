Security expert explains how 'nuts' Trump put the US in a downward spiral

By Paulo Santos

Trump’s Weakness Is Not an Accident. It Is the Policy.

Phillips P. O’Brien’s argument is not really about Iran. It is about a president whose threats no longer reliably move allies, rivals, or even his own government. The source text says Trump joined Israel’s bombing campaign, then tried to stop it, then watched both Israel and Iran behave as if his wishes were optional. That is the context. The political meaning is uglier: the United States is being run as a stage set for one man’s leverage problem.

Power Has Already Shifted

Actual institutional power in this story sits where it always does in a functioning state: in control of military force, diplomacy, and federal appointments. Trump holds the presidency, but the article’s central point is that formal office and real command are no longer the same thing. Israel is acting on its own incentives. Iran is walking away from talks. Trump is left shouting, threatening, and posting reassurance like a man trying to narrate authority back into existence.

That is not strength. It is the visible decay of it.

The Real Decision Was the War

The source makes clear that Trump helped trigger this condition by deciding to join Israel’s attack on Iran. That decision matters more than the bluster afterward. Once he entered the war, he inherited the consequences: a conflict he could not fully control, a ceasefire he could not stabilize, and allies who no longer treat his orders as binding.

O’Brien’s point is blunt: the president launched a war and then discovered that war is not a press release. The escalation was chosen. The loss of control followed from that choice.

Misdirection by Design

The most revealing part of the source is the effort to keep up the fiction that negotiations are “going well.” That is not confusion. It is messaging under strain. The administration, according to the article, wants the public to believe Iran is making concessions and a deal is close even as Iran says talks are off. That is not diplomacy. It is an attempt to preserve the image of competence after the substance has already slipped away.

This is where the article’s framing is weakest and most honest at the same time: it treats Trump’s instability as a personal trait, when the deeper issue is that his government is built to conceal failure until concealment becomes the only operating principle left.

Loyalty as a Survival System

The source links foreign-policy weakness to domestic rule for a reason. Trump is not merely rewarding loyalists; he is building a protective structure around himself. The article describes a GOP under his total sway, a federal government staffed by “uber loyalists,” an electoral revenge campaign, and a corrupt slush fund. Read together, that is not scattershot misconduct. It is a system for insulating power from accountability.

The point of demanding absolute loyalty is not just obedience. It is to ensure that when institutions are needed to constrain him, they instead absorb the damage and keep functioning on his behalf.

Scorched Earth, Then Denial

O’Brien’s most important claim is that Trump is willing to damage the country to preserve himself. That is the correct frame. The article’s domestic examples are not separate scandals. They are the same strategy in different rooms: weaken competence, punish dissent, pack the bureaucracy with dependents, and leave behind enough institutional wreckage that no one can easily reverse course.

If that sounds like decline, it is. But decline here is not passive erosion. It is deliberate sabotage dressed up as command.

The Pattern: Personal Rule Eats State Capacity

This story is not just about a failed negotiation or a volatile president. It is about what happens when a party, a bureaucracy, and a foreign policy apparatus are rearranged around one man’s security needs. The result is a government that cannot tell the truth about its own power because telling the truth would expose how little power remains.

That is the larger political pattern: authoritarian loyalty culture does not merely distort decisions. It hollows out the state until the state can no longer reliably do anything except protect the person who hollowed it out.
