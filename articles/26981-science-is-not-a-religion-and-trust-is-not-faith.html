Science Is Not a Religion — And Trust Is Not Faith

By Paulo Santos

There is a superficially compelling argument that appears periodically in discussions about science:

Most people believe what scientists tell them without personally verifying it. Most religious people believe what religious authorities tell them without personally verifying it. Therefore, for the average person, belief in science functions much like religious faith.

It sounds clever.

It is also built around a category error.

The first observation is perfectly correct. Almost none of us personally verify more than a microscopic fraction of the scientific knowledge we accept.

I have never measured the speed of light.

I have never sequenced DNA.

I have never personally observed Neptune through a telescope and calculated its orbit.

I have never performed the experiments establishing the charge of an electron. I have not reproduced the evidence for plate tectonics. I cannot manufacture a semiconductor from raw silicon. I certainly haven’t independently verified every piece of physics necessary to explain why the computer on which I’m writing this works.

And yet I accept all of those things as true.

So, superficially, there seems to be a problem.

If I criticize someone for accepting a religious proposition without personally verifying it, while simultaneously accepting thousands of scientific propositions that I have never personally verified, aren’t I doing essentially the same thing?

No.

Because trust is not faith.

And understanding the difference requires asking a much more interesting question than whether we personally performed the experiment.

Civilization Runs on Delegated Trust

Modern civilization would be impossible if everyone were required to personally verify everything they believed.

Knowledge has become far too specialized.

An astrophysicist studying black holes probably hasn’t personally reproduced the experiments establishing modern molecular genetics. A geneticist probably hasn’t independently verified the equations used to design a suspension bridge. A structural engineer probably hasn’t personally confirmed the mechanisms underlying mRNA transcription.

Even scientists spend most of their lives trusting scientific work performed by other scientists.

There is nothing irrational about this.

It is delegated epistemic trust.

We distribute intellectual labor because no human being has enough time, equipment, expertise, or cognitive capacity to independently reconstruct humanity’s accumulated knowledge.

When I board an airplane, I am trusting an extraordinary chain of people I have never met.

I trust the engineers who designed the aircraft.

I trust the metallurgists who developed its alloys.

I trust the technicians who inspected it.

I trust the physicists whose understanding of fluid dynamics underlies its wings.

I trust the people who designed its engines.

I trust the mathematicians whose work ultimately underpins its navigation systems.

I haven’t checked their calculations.

Neither have you.

That isn’t faith.

It is reliance upon a system of distributed expertise.

The interesting question therefore isn’t:

“Did you personally verify this?”

The interesting question is:

“What happens if the people we trusted were wrong?”

And that is where science and religion separate dramatically.

The Most Important Feature of Science Is That It Can Lose

Science isn’t valuable because scientists are unusually intelligent.

It isn’t valuable because scientists are unusually virtuous.

It certainly isn’t valuable because scientists are immune to ego, greed, ideology, careerism, tribalism, corruption, or stupidity.

Scientists are people.

And people are perfectly capable of being assholes.

The genius of science is that, when properly practiced, it does not require scientists to be saints.

It creates mechanisms through which their mistakes can be discovered.

Measurements can be repeated.

Experiments can be reproduced.

Predictions can fail.

Methodologies can be criticized.

Statistical methods can be examined.

Datasets can be challenged.

Alternative explanations can be proposed.

Other researchers can attempt to reproduce the result.

And ultimately, reality gets a vote.

That last part is enormously important.

A scientific proposition must, somehow, remain accountable to the universe it claims to describe.

If a theory predicts X and sufficiently good observations repeatedly produce Y, the universe does not care about the researcher’s résumé.

It doesn’t care how prestigious the university is.

It doesn’t care how many citations the paper has.

It doesn’t care whether the researcher has tenure.

It doesn’t care whether questioning the theory is considered impolite.

Eventually there is a problem that has to be explained.

That doesn’t mean scientific institutions immediately abandon incorrect ideas. History provides plenty of examples of resistance, professional rivalry, institutional inertia and simple human stubbornness.

But the error remains vulnerable.

There exists, at least in principle, a mechanism through which evidence can force the claim to lose.

That is fundamentally different from faith.

“Science Has Been Wrong Before”

Of course it has.

This sentence is frequently deployed as though it were some devastating indictment of science.

“Scientists used to believe X!”

Yes.

And how exactly did we discover they were wrong?

Science.

That’s the part mysteriously omitted from the accusation.

Science being wrong is not an embarrassing exception to the scientific process.

Finding out that we were wrong is one of its principal functions.

Newtonian mechanics wasn’t thrown into a furnace when Einstein arrived. Newton’s equations remain extraordinarily useful within the conditions where their approximations hold. But relativity demonstrated that Newtonian mechanics was not a complete description of reality.

Our model improved.

Medicine has repeatedly discarded treatments that were ineffective or harmful.

Astronomy discarded geocentrism.

Geology abandoned explanations that could not survive accumulating evidence.

Biology transformed under evolution, genetics and molecular biology.

The history of science is littered with discarded explanations.

That’s not a bug.

That’s the garbage collection system running.

The alternative would be considerably worse: a knowledge system incapable of acknowledging that its previous conclusions were mistaken.

Scientific knowledge is powerful precisely because its conclusions are provisional.

Not “provisional” in the intellectually dishonest sense that therefore everything is equally uncertain.

The Earth being approximately spherical is not epistemically equivalent to a hypothesis proposed yesterday.

Evolution isn’t hanging by a thread waiting for somebody on Facebook to type “just asking questions.”

Relativity isn’t merely Einstein’s opinion competing on equal terms with something your cousin heard on a podcast.

Evidence accumulates.

Confidence changes.

Some conclusions become overwhelmingly established.

But none acquire supernatural protection from contradictory evidence.

The Problem With “Planes Fly”

One defense of science deserves more scrutiny than it usually receives:

“Science works. Planes fly. Bridges stand. Computers work.”

This is a strong demonstration that enormous bodies of scientific knowledge successfully describe reality.

Aircraft are particularly unforgiving philosophical instruments.

You can argue about aerodynamics all you want, but eventually the runway ends.

Reality conducts the peer review.

But there is an important limitation to this argument.

A Boeing 747 flying successfully today demonstrates that the physics and engineering underlying that aircraft are sufficiently accurate.

It does not demonstrate that every scientific institution operating today is healthy.

A bridge standing doesn’t prove that a psychology paper published last Tuesday has a sound methodology.

GPS working doesn’t prove that a pharmaceutical trial wasn’t distorted by publication bias.

Your CPU successfully executing billions of operations every second doesn’t prove that a nutrition study involving 34 participants deserves a headline announcing that chocolate prevents dementia.

Past scientific success does not grant present scientific institutions perpetual immunity from scrutiny.

The scientific method can be extraordinarily reliable while scientific institutions can simultaneously become dysfunctional.

That distinction matters enormously.

Science Is a Method. Scientific Institutions Are Human Organizations.

This is where conversations about “trusting science” often become unnecessarily stupid.

Science gets anthropomorphized into some enormous white-coated oracle called SCIENCE™, standing on a mountain and periodically handing humanity tablets containing Objective Truth.

That’s not science.

Science is a collection of methods for systematically interrogating reality.

Scientific institutions are organizations created by human beings attempting to practice those methods.

And human institutions develop incentives.

Researchers need jobs.

Universities want prestige.

Journals want citations.

Companies want profitable products.

Governments want policies justified.

Funding agencies have priorities.

Researchers need grants.

Careers depend on publications.

Editors prefer interesting results.

Media organizations prefer spectacular conclusions.

“Study finds nothing interesting” isn’t exactly clickbait.

These incentives can distort the scientific process without anyone secretly meeting in a volcano lair to fabricate science.

Consider publication bias.

Suppose twenty teams independently investigate whether Substance X improves memory.

Nineteen find no statistically significant effect.

One obtains a statistically significant positive result.

If the nineteen boring results disappear into filing cabinets while the exciting result gets published, the scientific literature now gives a profoundly misleading picture of the evidence.

Nobody necessarily committed fraud.

Nobody necessarily lied.

The institutional incentive structure simply selected the interesting result.

Reality didn’t change.

Our representation of reality did.

The same applies to p-hacking, underpowered studies, selective reporting, inadequate replication and methodological fashions.

These are not arguments for abandoning science.

They are arguments for defending science from the institutions supposedly practicing it badly.

Skepticism Is Not Anti-Science

One of the strangest developments of recent years has been the transformation of “trust science” into something resembling a demand for institutional obedience.

That’s dangerous.

Scientists should be trusted because their claims survive scrutiny, not protected from scrutiny because they are scientists.

Scientific authority is earned epistemically.

It is not granted ceremonially.

Questioning methodology isn’t anti-scientific.

Asking for evidence isn’t anti-scientific.

Examining conflicts of interest isn’t anti-scientific.

Demanding replication isn’t anti-scientific.

Criticizing statistical methodology isn’t anti-scientific.

Investigating whether institutional incentives distorted research isn’t anti-scientific.

Those things are science.

But there is an equally important warning on the other side.

“Do Your Own Research” Is Usually Bullshit

Institutional skepticism does not mean every opinion deserves equal epistemic weight.

This is where legitimate criticism of scientific institutions gets hijacked by cranks.

Scientists can be wrong.

Therefore scientists might be wrong about this particular subject.

So far, perfectly reasonable.

Then comes the spectacular leap:

Therefore the scientific consensus and something a guy discovered after watching three YouTube videos deserve equal consideration.

No.

The fallibility of experts does not establish the expertise of amateurs.

If my mechanic occasionally makes mistakes, that doesn’t qualify me to rebuild my transmission.

If doctors occasionally misdiagnose patients, it doesn’t follow that somebody selling crystals on Instagram has developed a superior medical system.

If scientific institutions sometimes produce bad research, that doesn’t transform every contrarian claim into Galileo.

This particular fantasy is enormously popular.

Everyone wants to be Galileo.

Nobody wants to consider the statistically much more probable possibility that they’re the guy yelling nonsense at Galileo.

Real skepticism requires considerably more work than disbelief.

It requires applying scrutiny to claims you want to believe as aggressively as you apply it to claims you dislike.

Otherwise it isn’t skepticism.

It’s shopping.

You browse the epistemological supermarket until you find the conclusion you wanted and then declare that you’ve “done your research.”

Consensus Matters — But It Isn’t Sacred

Scientific consensus is another concept that gets mangled by both sides.

Consensus isn’t proof.

Scientists voting on gravity wouldn’t determine whether objects fall.

But consensus among relevant experts is useful evidence for someone who lacks the expertise necessary to evaluate the underlying research.

If 98 structural engineers inspect a bridge and say it is unsafe while two insist it’s perfectly fine, I am not going to climb onto the railing and announce that I’m keeping an open mind.

That isn’t intellectual independence.

It’s stupidity wearing glasses.

Expert consensus matters because independent specialists examining overlapping evidence and converging upon the same conclusion increases our reason for trusting that conclusion.

But consensus remains downstream from evidence.

The moment “consensus” becomes a substitute for examining evidence rather than a description of what examination has produced, something has gone wrong.

The hierarchy should be:

Reality → evidence → analysis → scientific conclusions → consensus.

Not:

Consensus → therefore reality.

That distinction protects us simultaneously from institutional dogmatism and contrarian idiocy.

Trust Should Be Conditional

So I don’t particularly like saying that I “believe in science.”

Science isn’t asking for my belief.

And scientific institutions certainly shouldn’t be asking for my loyalty.

What I offer is conditional trust.

I trust scientific conclusions in proportion to the quality of the evidence supporting them.

I trust institutions in proportion to their transparency, methodological rigor and willingness to expose their conclusions to criticism.

I trust results more when they have been independently replicated.

I trust extraordinary claims less when they emerge from tiny datasets, weak statistical effects or suspiciously convenient methodologies.

And I become considerably more suspicious whenever criticism is answered with authority rather than evidence.

“Trust us” is not a scientific argument.

“Here is the evidence” is.

The distinction matters because blind institutional trust is every bit as intellectually lazy as reflexive institutional distrust.

One says:

“They’re scientists, therefore they’re right.”

The other says:

“They’re scientists, therefore they’re lying.”

Neither position requires thinking.

Science Doesn’t Need Believers

This ultimately brings us back to the claim that science functions like religion for ordinary people.

There is one superficial similarity.

Both require most people to rely upon information produced by others.

But that describes practically every sophisticated human institution.

I haven’t personally audited my bank’s accounting system.

I haven’t inspected the municipal water treatment plant.

I haven’t reviewed every line of code in my operating system.

I haven’t personally tested whether every ingredient listed on a food package is actually present in the stated quantity.

Delegation is not faith.

The epistemological difference lies in how claims are justified and what happens when they fail.

Science, properly practiced, institutionalizes doubt.

It assumes observations may be mistaken.

It assumes measurements contain error.

It assumes hypotheses may fail.

It assumes researchers can be wrong.

It builds procedures intended to expose those failures.

Religion may contain philosophical inquiry, textual criticism and enormous intellectual traditions, but religious faith ultimately permits propositions whose truth does not depend upon their surviving empirical falsification.

Science cannot do that without ceasing to be science.

And that is why the most important defense of science isn’t:

“Trust scientists.”

It is:

“Protect the machinery that makes scientists prove themselves.”

Fund replication.

Publish negative results.

Reward methodological rigor.

Expose conflicts of interest.

Make data available whenever reasonably possible.

Encourage adversarial review.

Punish fraud.

Stop treating retractions as institutional humiliation and start treating successful correction as evidence that the system worked.

And never confuse defending scientific institutions with defending science itself.

Because institutions can fail.

Scientists can fail.

Journals can fail.

Universities can fail.

Consensus can change.

The scientific method remains valuable because it gives us something far more useful than an institution that promises never to be wrong.

It gives us a mechanism for discovering how we were wrong.

Science doesn’t deserve trust because planes fly.

It deserves trust when its institutions maintain the conditions that allowed us to discover how to make planes fly in the first place.

And if those institutions stop maintaining those conditions, skepticism isn’t betrayal.

It’s maintenance.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
