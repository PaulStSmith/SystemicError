SYSTEMIC ERROR / COMMENTARY

Alina Habba's new husband made fortune in controversial deals tied to romantic partners

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedWhere the power sitsA headline outrunning its evidenceHabba is context, not proofAccess is the system

What happened

RawStory reports that former New Jersey U.S. Attorney Alina Habba married Turkish businessman Turhan Mildon at New York City’s Waldorf Astoria after a relationship of several months. The article links Mildon’s companies to major government-backed construction ventures in Congo and Venezuela, but it does not establish that his romantic partners obtained those contracts for him.

Mildon’s Milvest reportedly secured roughly $1 billion in Congolese construction contracts after Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan introduced him to Congolese President Félix Tshisekedi. Projects later faced allegations involving mismanaged funds and improper import exemptions; Mildon denied the latter accusation, while former Congolese finance minister Nicolas Kazadi denied wrongdoing. Mildon subsequently received a reported $67 million contract to build a drone base.

Middle East Eye also reported that, after Mildon’s 2024 divorce, he dated the daughter of Venezuelan admiral Remigio Ceballos. In 2025, Mildon’s Vencement announced a profit-sharing agreement with Venezuela to revive 10 state-owned cement factories. Mildon denied allegations that the company owed contractors money and blamed extreme cement markups on resale markets the government had failed to control.

Where the power sits

The decisive actors here are not the women Mildon married or dated. They are presidents, ministers, state companies and procurement authorities capable of arranging introductions, granting exemptions and awarding enormous contracts.

The clearest reported connection is political rather than romantic: Erdoğan introduced Mildon to Tshisekedi, and Mildon’s company subsequently obtained extensive Congolese work. That sequence warrants scrutiny, but it does not by itself prove favoritism or illegality. The unanswered questions concern tender procedures, contract terms, oversight and public value—the machinery through which government power becomes private revenue.

In Venezuela, the same principle applies. A profit-sharing deal involving public factories necessarily depends on state authority. If contractors went unpaid or cement reached consumers at grossly inflated prices, responsibility must be traced through company accounts, distribution systems and government enforcement. A businessman’s dating history cannot substitute for that reporting.

A headline outrunning its evidence

RawStory’s framing claims a fortune built through controversial deals “tied to romantic partners.” The reporting supplies proximity and chronology, not a demonstrated causal chain. One former wife’s father had a company associated with an Erdoğan-requested mosque project. Another former partner was the daughter of a Maduro-aligned admiral. Those facts may justify further investigation, but they do not establish that either woman delivered contracts or that Mildon’s wealth came through them.

That gap matters. Turning women into narrative conduits between powerful men gives the story a tabloid charge while allowing the institutions that actually control public money to fade into the scenery. It also risks converting association into evidence: politically connected relatives become insinuations of misconduct without documentation showing what they did.

Habba is context, not proof

Habba’s own political position makes the marriage newsworthy. She represented Donald Trump in his civil fraud case and later resigned as New Jersey’s U.S. attorney after, according to RawStory, her appointment was found improper. Her proximity to presidential power deserves rigorous scrutiny—particularly if it intersects with Mildon’s business interests.

But the article reports no contract, intervention or policy decision involving Habba on Mildon’s behalf. A large diamond, an expensive handbag and the timing of her divorce are celebrity details, not evidence of public corruption. Treating them as substitutes for financial records or official acts weakens the case for accountability.

Access is the system

The larger pattern is access capitalism: private firms pursue public fortunes through relationships with officials who control introductions, contracts and regulatory privileges. That system thrives when scrutiny stops at personal intrigue.

Serious political reporting would follow the money through procurement records, beneficial ownership, exemption decisions, project performance and payments. Romantic genealogy may point reporters toward a network, but it cannot prove how that network operated. When coverage foregrounds marriages and ex-girlfriends while leaving state decision-making largely unexplained, power receives camouflage disguised as scandal.
