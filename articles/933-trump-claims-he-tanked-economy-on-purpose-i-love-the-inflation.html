Trump claims he tanked economy on purpose: 'I love the inflation'

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Trump Treats Economic Damage as Proof of Power

The Real Story

Trump says he loves inflation and says he tanked the stock market on purpose as part of his war on Iran. In the same remarks, he ties the rise in energy prices to U.S. strikes, claims senior officials agreed the cost was acceptable, and presents the damage as an intentional tradeoff for stopping Iran from getting a nuclear weapon.

Who Actually Holds Power

The power in this story sits where it always does in an executive war machine: with the president and the officials who execute his decisions. Trump names Scott Bessent, Howard Lutnick, Pete Hegseth, and Todd Blanche as people in the room, but he also makes clear who set the terms. He did. They did not overrule him. They were there to ratify the choice.

That matters because the article’s framing risks treating this as a chaotic presidential outburst. It is more accurate to read it as an admissions statement from the top of the chain of command. The consequences were not accidental side effects of policy drift. Trump describes them as features.

Misdirection by Design

The soft language of “risks,” “predictions of experts,” and “the cost was worth it” helps launder responsibility. Trump does not speak like a leader accounting for harm. He speaks like someone turning harm into proof of seriousness.

That is the trick. Inflation becomes “love.” Stock-market damage becomes courage. Oil spikes become evidence of effectiveness. If the economy suffers, the suffering is recoded as strategic virtue. This is not candor in any meaningful democratic sense. It is damage control dressed up as dominance.

The Pattern Beneath the Bombs

The larger pattern is a familiar one: executive power externalizes costs downward and then claims moral authority for having imposed them. Consumers pay higher energy prices. Investors absorb the market shock. Workers and households live with the inflation. The president gets to narrate the pain as national strength.

This is the politics of unilateral sacrifice, where the public is expected to absorb the bill after the decision has already been made. The people asked to pay are never the ones consulted.

Institutional Cowardice

Trump says his cabinet agreed. That detail is not a defense; it is part of the indictment. If these officials really accepted the premise, then they helped normalize economic pain as a sign of toughness. If they did not object, they served as décor for a decision already made. Either way, the institution around him looks less like a check on power than a stage set for it.

That is how accountability disappears in modern executive politics. The president boasts. The aides sit in the room. The harm lands somewhere else.

The Point

This story is not about one reckless quote. It is about a governing style that treats public loss as collateral branding. Trump is not hiding the consequences of his war. He is advertising them as proof that he can impose them.

That is the system at work: power concentrated at the top, blame scattered below, and real-world damage repackaged as leadership.
