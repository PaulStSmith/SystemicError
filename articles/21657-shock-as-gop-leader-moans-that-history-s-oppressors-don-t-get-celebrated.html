Shock as GOP leader moans that history's 'oppressors' don't get celebrated

Based on an article by David Edwards

Congress Isn’t Correcting Museums. It’s Policing Memory.

The Hearing Was the Point

A House Republican used a congressional hearing to stage a public reprimand of the Smithsonian’s history director, Anthea Hartig, after Trump’s order to purge “improper ideology” from museums. That is the real story: not a neutral inquiry into public programming, but an exercise in state power aimed at disciplining how Americans are allowed to remember themselves.

Rep. Tim Burchett, chairing the Oversight subcommittee on Delivering on Government Efficiency, did not present a governing critique. He performed a purge. He framed the National Museum of American History as politically contaminated and treated historical complexity as a disciplinary offense.

Who Has Power Here

The power in this story sits with Congress, the presidency, and the machinery they command over federal institutions. Burchett was not speaking as a disappointed visitor. He was wielding oversight authority in the wake of a presidential directive. That matters.

The museum director was not operating from equal footing. Hartig was answering to a hostile committee under political orders already set from above. This was not a debate among peers. It was a subordinate being hauled in front of authority for failing to flatter it.

Manufactured Offense, Real Control

Burchett’s attack relied on a familiar trick: turn representation into corruption, then call censorship “accountability.” He attacked exhibits on race, identity, gender, and sexuality, complained about “bondage” and “overtly sexual things,” and fixated on a temporary exhibit, Girlhood: It’s Complicated, which he tried to recast as sexualized indecency.

Hartig’s explanation was plain enough from the source: the exhibit included a 13-year-old’s diary, with parental advisory signage at the entrance. That is not the behavior of a museum hiding its content. It is a museum making context visible. Burchett’s tactic was to ignore context entirely because context is what exposes the fraud.

The Blame Is Pointed Downward

This is the standard authoritarian move: upstream decisions are made by elected officials, then the blame is pushed onto cultural workers, curators, educators, and institutions expected to absorb the damage quietly. The museum is accused of “woke ideology,” while the political apparatus that ordered the attack pretends to be correcting excess.

Burchett even tried to make Hartig defend statements he attributed to her, including claims about America being founded on “the supremacy of white men” and the 250th anniversary being worth “problematizing.” Whether those lines were faithful, distorted, or weaponized in context is not established by the hearing itself. What is established is the method: isolate a phrase, strip away nuance, and use congressional theater to manufacture guilt.

What This Is Really About

This is not about curatorial taste. It is about who gets to define national identity when the facts are inconvenient to power. Museums become targets when they refuse to convert patriotic mythology into state doctrine. Historical accuracy is rebranded as ideological hostility because the people demanding obedience cannot tolerate a public record that includes hierarchy, exclusion, and violence.

The pattern is older than this hearing. Conservatives in power do not merely want institutions to be neutral; they want them to be compliant. They want public memory sanitized into reassurance. They want the nation's past stripped of conflict unless the conflict can be blamed on the usual targets: immigrants, queer people, racial justice, anyone outside the approved hierarchy.

The Larger Pattern

When a Republican-led committee summons a museum official to answer for telling the truth too clearly, the issue is not excess realism in the exhibition hall. The issue is a political project that needs history to lie flatter. Burchett’s hearing shows how authoritarian politics works in practice: seize institutional leverage, stage moral panic, and call the suppression of knowledge public service.

That is the danger here. Not just one hostile hearing, but the normalization of federal power being used to punish any institution that refuses to cheer for oppression as heritage.
