Stony Todd Blanche glowers when slapped with 'smarmy' crypto corruption charge

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

Crypto, Pardons, and the Rot in Plain Sight

The Real Story

This hearing was not about one man’s awkward financial disclosures. It was about the executive branch using law enforcement as a tool of convenience, then wrapping the result in the language of administration and process. Sen. Dick Durbin used the Judiciary Committee to drag Todd Blanche into the light, but the substance of the exchange was bigger than Blanche’s silence: DOJ crypto enforcement was dismantled, investigations were shut down, and the White House appears to have benefited from the vacuum.

Who Had the Power

Blanche is the visible target, not the center of gravity. He reportedly issued the order that blew up DOJ’s crypto enforcement team after he was confirmed as deputy attorney general. That matters because enforcement power is real power. If you can stop investigations, you can decide whose misconduct becomes a case and whose misconduct becomes background noise.

Trump sits above that system. According to Durbin’s remarks, the president also profited from crypto under suspicious circumstances and later pardoned the Binance founder after a deal that routed $2 billion into the Trump family’s crypto business. That is not a marginal ethics problem. That is executive power being used to protect and reward a financial ecosystem tied to the president’s own interests.

Crypto as Cover

The source’s sharpest line is also its clearest indictment: “Every smarmy suspect deal in this administration has cryptocurrency behind the curtain.” That is not a technical critique. It is a description of concealment.

Crypto in this story is not presented as an innocent sector caught in regulatory confusion. It is the mechanism through which wealth moves, accountability stalls, and family enrichment gets dressed up as innovation. When an administration weakens crypto enforcement while simultaneously standing to gain from crypto deals, the issue is not misunderstanding. It is alignment.

Conflict of Interest Is the Floor, Not the Ceiling

Blanche’s own holdings, and his transfer of those assets to his children and grandchild, are bad enough on their own. They are the kind of maneuver that turns a conflict of interest into a family arrangement. But the deeper scandal is not simply that a senior official had crypto exposure. It is that the official with the leverage over enforcement was positioned inside an administration that stood to benefit from the enforcement going away.

That is the pattern. Personal enrichment is not an accident on top of policy. It is often the policy’s destination.

Oversight Is Doing the Bare Minimum

Durbin did what oversight is supposed to do: put the facts on the record and force the target to sit there while the record hardened. That is useful. It is also a sign of how much damage has already been normalized. A Senate hearing should not be the place where the machinery of influence-peddling gets its first serious public accounting, but that is where we are.

The weakness here is not the questioning. The weakness is a system that can be openly bent, then defended by silence and procedural fog.

What This Reveals

This is what institutional decay looks like before it becomes irreversible: enforcement is neutralized, financial self-dealing is routed through complex assets, and the pardon power sits ready to sanitize the worst actors after the money has moved. The corruption is not hidden well. It is protected well.

That is the real lesson. Authoritarian politics does not always arrive with uniforms and slogans. Sometimes it arrives as a regulatory team getting dismantled, an investigation going dark, and a president cashing in while the people around him are told to stare forward and keep quiet.
