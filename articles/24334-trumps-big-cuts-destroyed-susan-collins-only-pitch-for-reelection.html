Trump’s big cuts destroyed Susan Collins’ only pitch for reelection

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the Trump administration undercut Sen. Susan Collins’s central reelection pitch: that her seniority and role as the Senate’s lead Republican appropriator allow her to secure federal money for Maine. Office of Management and Budget Director Russell Vought told Congress that the administration would eliminate $1 billion lawmakers had approved, demonstrating that congressional appropriations can be blocked even with Collins in office.

According to the account, most of the cuts target Health and Human Services programs serving refugees and unaccompanied minors accused of entering the country illegally, with additional reductions affecting the Justice, Education, and Housing and Urban Development departments. Collins called the actions illegal and said she would work with colleagues to address them.

The reporting does not identify specific Maine projects among the eliminated funds. Its political claim is narrower: Collins campaigns as a protector of federal investment, while her own party’s administration is asserting the power to disregard spending decisions made by Congress.

Power belongs to the executive

Congress holds the constitutional spending power. But formal authority means little when lawmakers refuse to defend it.

Vought is the official reportedly deciding which appropriated programs receive money. Trump’s administration supplies the executive power behind that decision. Republican members of Congress supply the permissive environment: according to the reporting, they have neither mounted a meaningful public fight nor taken the administration to court.

That is the operative power structure. Vought acts, the White House backs him, and congressional Republicans absorb the institutional humiliation.

Collins helped build her own irrelevance

Collins’s objection exposes the weakness of her political brand. Her campaign argument is not merely that she supports Maine projects; it is that her position inside the Republican hierarchy makes those projects secure. An administration controlled by her party has now shown that legislative seniority offers no such guarantee when the executive branch treats appropriations as optional.

Calling the conduct illegal is not the same as stopping it. Collins leads the Republican side of the Senate Appropriations Committee. If she cannot convert that position into enforceable consequences, her influence becomes advertising copy rather than governing power.

The targets are politically useful

The reported concentration of cuts on programs serving refugees and unaccompanied minors matters. These are vulnerable populations with little electoral power and immense value as targets in Republican immigration politics.

The available reporting does not establish why each program was selected. But the foreseeable political effect is clear: executive defiance of Congress is packaged through cuts aimed largely at people the administration can portray as undeserving. Scapegoating helps obscure the institutional seizure underneath. The immediate victims draw the hostility while the White House accumulates discretion over public money.

The framing lets enablers hide

Saying Trump’s cuts “destroyed” Collins’s reelection pitch captures her electoral predicament but softens her agency. This is not merely something that happened to her. Collins belongs to the Senate majority that, by the article’s account, has declined to force a confrontation over the executive branch’s refusal to follow spending instructions.

Her anger therefore arrives after acquiescence has already made defiance cheap. The conflict is not between a powerless senator and an unstoppable presidency. It is between an executive testing the limits of its authority and legislators choosing whether their own institution is worth defending.

A legislature that will not legislate

The larger pattern is congressional self-erasure. Presidential power expands not only through dramatic orders, but through repeated violations that lawmakers condemn without attaching penalties, filing challenges, withholding cooperation, or otherwise raising the cost.

Collins’s campaign problem is the minor consequence. The systemic failure is that appropriations can become provisional whenever the executive assumes control and Congress refuses to enforce its decisions. A senator cannot credibly sell institutional influence after helping reduce the institution itself to a suggestion.
