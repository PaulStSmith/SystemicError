'There will be mistakes': Red state officials warn of new Trump plan

Based on an article by Kyle Pfannenstiel, Idaho Capital Sun

Paulo SantosSeptember 19, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Election Sabotage Disguised as Postal Procedure

The Power Is Federal

The Trump administration is attempting to impose new federal controls on absentee voting less than two months before the 2026 midterms. Through an executive order and a proposed Postal Service rule, it would subject ballot designs to federal review, mandate new envelope specifications and require states to upload information identifying voters who requested absentee ballots. A federal court has blocked the rule for now.

The institutional hierarchy is not complicated. County clerks administer Idaho’s elections. State officials supervise them. But the president, the Justice Department and the Postal Service are using federal authority to dictate election mechanics on a deadline local administrators cannot control.

The Chaos Has an Author

The resulting uncertainty is described as something election officials are “facing,” as though it arrived with the weather. It did not. The administration chose to propose major procedural changes after counties had begun finalizing hundreds of ballot variations and preparing ballots for military and overseas voters.

Ada County Clerk Trent Tripple, a Republican, stated the practical consequence plainly: last-minute changes guarantee mistakes because an election cannot simply be backed up and restarted. That is not bureaucratic resistance to security. It is an election administrator explaining that rushed intervention undermines the accuracy its sponsors claim to protect.

Intent cannot be established from the reporting alone. Responsibility can. Whatever justification the administration offers, it created the artificial timeline and imposed the risk.

“Security” as an Instrument of Control

The proposed tracking requirement illustrates the tactic. Tracking absentee ballots may be administratively useful; Ada County was already moving toward it. But a potentially sensible measure becomes destabilizing when Washington abruptly mandates it nationwide during an active election cycle.

The federal data demand is even more revealing. States would have to upload information about absentee-ballot requests so the federal government could verify voter registration—yet, according to the reporting, a Justice Department attorney could not tell a judge whether the system intended to receive that data was operational.

This is not evidence of a carefully engineered security program. It is evidence of federal officials issuing demands before establishing whether their own machinery can perform the function they are claiming.

The Voters Disappear From the Frame

Administrative coverage often makes officials’ stress the central problem: deadlines are tight, printing is difficult, counties may spend more money. Those consequences matter, but they are not the ultimate stakes.

About 182,000 Idahoans voted absentee in the 2024 general election. Seniors, military personnel, overseas voters and people who use mail voting for accessibility or convenience depend on a process whose reliability is built through preparation. Every hurried redesign, uncertain requirement and untested data transfer creates another opportunity for delay, rejection or error.

The disruption also does not follow the partisan caricature used to justify hostility toward mail voting. In Idaho’s May primary, Republicans accounted for nearly 32,000 of the roughly 46,000 absentee voters. The machinery being destabilized serves actual voters, not an abstract enemy electorate.

Republican Officials Are Warning Washington

Idaho’s resistance cannot honestly be dismissed as partisan obstruction. McGrane and Tripple are elected Republicans. Their objections concern operational reality, state authority, taxpayer expense and election accuracy.

The administration’s pressure also extends beyond this postal rule. The Justice Department has sued Idaho and dozens of other states for sensitive voter information and threatened prosecution over noncitizen voting, prompting an attorney representing McGrane to tell federal officials to “stop threatening your friends.”

That phrase exposes the political structure more clearly than the article’s softer language does. The administration is not merely offering election guidance. It is using federal leverage against the officials responsible for making elections function—even when those officials belong to its own party.

Manufacture the Disorder, Then Invoke It

The larger pattern is a familiar one: central authorities invoke security to seize more control, impose requirements that local institutions cannot safely absorb, and then leave those institutions to own the resulting failures.

If mistakes occur, the officials who warned against the timetable will be blamed for incompetence. If counties spend heavily on rules that never take effect, taxpayers absorb the loss. If voters encounter disrupted ballots, the administration can point to disorder as proof that elections required more federal intervention.

That cycle turns administrative competence into a political target. The damage does not require a formal order to discard votes. It can be produced through deadlines, unfunded mandates, unready systems and strategic uncertainty—then laundered through the bloodless language of postal regulation.
