'Failed to comply': Judge drops the hammer on Todd Blanche in brutal Epstein files ruling

Based on an article by David Edwards

Paulo SantosSeptember 20, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Justice Department’s Epstein Strategy Is Defiance by Attrition

The Context Is Simple

Attorney General Todd Blanche missed a court deadline, never requested an extension, resisted reviewing foreign-language records, and tried to withhold handwritten FBI notes under rationales U.S. District Judge Emmet Sullivan rejected. The dispute arises from Katie Phang’s lawsuit seeking enforcement of the Epstein Files Transparency Act, which requires the Justice Department to release most of its Epstein records.

That is enough summary. The political substance lies in who possesses the records, who controls their review, and who benefits when every obligation becomes another argument for delay.

Blanche Holds the Power

Phang can sue. Sullivan can issue orders. But Blanche runs the institution with custody of the evidence and the personnel needed to process it. That control gives the attorney general a practical advantage no formal deadline automatically erases: the power to make compliance slow, fragmented, and expensive to enforce.

Blanche’s conduct was not merely bureaucratic untidiness. Sullivan found that he failed to comply with a federal procedural rule, supplied no information supporting his claim of accidental oversight, and ignored half the factors courts consider when excusing a missed deadline. The Justice Department’s own lawyer had already said that responding earlier “would serve no purpose.” Phang’s description of the lapse as calculated is an allegation, but the department’s stated position makes the innocent-oversight account difficult to credit.

Administrative Inconvenience Is Not a Veto

The department treated foreign-language records as though translation were an extraordinary burden rather than an ordinary function of a federal law-enforcement bureaucracy. Blanche then attempted to convert Congress’s silence about that excuse into permission to disregard the statute.

Sullivan rejected the maneuver plainly: administrative inconvenience does not cancel a legal obligation, and congressional silence does not amend a law. Blanche’s theory would have created a useful executive loophole—an agency could evade disclosure whenever compliance required effort, then claim legislative acquiescence because Congress had not immediately objected.

That is not confusion about the rules. It is an attempt to make institutional resistance function as law.

“Duplicative” Is a Label, Not Disclosure

The same pattern appeared with handwritten FBI interview notes. Blanche argued that they were substantially similar to typed FD-302 reports; when Sullivan asked why the notes could not simply be redacted by hand, a department lawyer called them duplicative.

But the notes are not duplicates, Sullivan found. They are source material from which agents produced later reports. Differences between contemporaneous notes and formal summaries may matter, particularly in a case defined by longstanding suspicion about what powerful institutions recorded, omitted, or protected.

The department had already released nearly 100 other handwritten interview notes. Its resistance to these four sets therefore looks less like a coherent rule than selective obstruction. Sullivan ordered them submitted for private judicial review, denying Blanche the unilateral authority to declare them irrelevant.

The Framing Minimizes Agency

Calling these episodes “missing deadlines” risks laundering deliberate institutional choices into clerical mishaps. Deadlines do not miss themselves. Blanche failed to answer, failed to seek more time, and later requested a retroactive pause. His department declined to begin a required review and advanced legal theories the judge found unsupported or meritless.

The court has not yet held Blanche in contempt, and Sullivan called such a finding premature. That distinction matters. But so does Sullivan’s warning that contempt could damage the careers of department lawyers. The pressure created by political leadership does not erase the professional responsibility of the attorneys who carry its arguments into court.

The government’s public response adds another layer of misdirection. A spokesperson claimed the department had produced all responsive documents while the department was still contesting foreign-language materials and handwritten notes. That assertion asks the public to accept “all” as whatever the custodian has decided counts.

Secrecy Wins Through Delay

This case exposes a durable feature of executive power: officials do not need to defeat a disclosure law outright if they can exhaust its challengers through delay, narrow definitions, procedural disputes, and serial appeals. Each objection may appear technical. Together they can deprive the statute of practical force.

The weaker actor is then forced to prove, document by document and deadline by deadline, that the government must obey a law already written to bind it. The stronger actor retains the records while litigating the meaning of surrender.

Sullivan’s orders interrupt that strategy, but they also reveal the systemic failure beneath it. Transparency laws depend on the institution holding the secrets to process them honestly—and on judges willing to impose consequences when it does not. Without enforcement, statutory disclosure becomes optional for the officials it was designed to constrain.
