Retired general 'couldn’t be prouder' after Hegseth earned soldier’s rebuke

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 13, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that retired Brig. Gen. Ty Seidule praised an Old Guard soldier who ordered Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s group to remain quiet at the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier. Seidule told Democratic strategist James Carville that the soldier “did exactly what he was supposed to do” by enforcing the required silence and respect.

Their Veterans Day discussion also addressed Donald Trump’s proposed giant arch near Arlington National Cemetery. Seidule cited Washington Post reporting that the structure would obstruct established sight lines, including the view of John F. Kennedy’s grave, prompting the Kennedy family to sue.

Authority met discipline

Hegseth holds institutional power over the Defense Department. The soldier does not. Yet at the Tomb, the soldier had a defined duty: protect the ceremony’s silence and dignity, regardless of the visitor’s rank.

That distinction matters. The correction was not insubordination or personal confrontation. It was a service member applying a rule to a powerful civilian official. Seidule’s approval recognizes something increasingly scarce in political institutions: duty that does not dissolve when authority becomes inconvenient.

The rebuke was earned

The headline’s retired general is Seidule, and the conduct he celebrated was the soldier’s refusal to grant Hegseth an exemption from the standards imposed on everyone else.

Carville asked whether the soldier might receive a dressing-down. That possibility exposes the real power imbalance. Hegseth could face a moment of public embarrassment; the soldier could face consequences inside the hierarchy Hegseth leads. The meaningful act was not merely requesting quiet. It was enforcing institutional standards upward.

Seidule sharpened the indictment by naming the Tomb guard’s principles—silence, respect, dignity and perseverance—and saying they are qualities the defense secretary lacks. That is political criticism grounded in conduct, not ceremonial pageantry.

Arlington is not a backdrop

The proposed arch raises the same underlying question at a larger scale: whether powerful officials treat Arlington as a national cemetery with its own history or as scenery available for their preferred monument.

According to the reporting Seidule cited, the arch would interfere with carefully established views among Arlington’s memorial sites. Its size is therefore not an aesthetic footnote. A monument that dominates the cemetery’s visual landscape also asserts control over how the place is seen and remembered.

The misdirection would be to reduce opposition to taste or family sensitivity. The decision-making power rests with those advancing a structure capable of overriding the existing landscape. The Kennedy family’s reported lawsuit is a response to that exercise of power, not its cause.

Standards only matter upward

The soldier’s command and the arch dispute reveal the same political pattern. Institutions are praised for discipline, sacrifice and continuity until those values obstruct an official’s behavior or ambition. Then the people defending the institution risk being recast as the problem.

Arlington’s guards maintain exacting rituals through severe weather because the ceremony is not supposed to bend around individual importance. A government that celebrates such discipline while expecting exceptions for its leaders is not honoring military tradition. It is using that tradition as decoration.

Power wants the ceremony without the constraint

The larger conflict is between stewardship and possession. The Old Guard treats the Tomb as something entrusted to it. Political power too often treats public institutions and memorial spaces as assets to command.

One soldier’s correction briefly reversed that arrangement: the official with greater power was subject to the rule. That is why the incident resonates beyond etiquette. Institutions survive only when their standards bind the people at the top—and when those tasked with enforcing them are not punished for doing their jobs.
