Military leaders in 'denial' over Trump danger: ex-Air Force chief

The Brass Is Not Above the Coup

The Source of Power

Frank Kendall’s warning is useful because it names the real problem without dressing it up: Donald Trump is not testing the system from outside it. He is using the powers of the presidency, and senior military leaders are helping by behaving as if his behavior is just another policy disagreement. That is the central fraud here. The question is not whether the military feels uneasy. The question is whether it obeys, excuses, or resists unlawful or authoritarian use of force.

Normalcy as Collaboration

Kendall describes a retirement ceremony where the room performed polite denial, as if the bizarre had become routine. That is not institutional stability. It is elite surrender disguised as professionalism. When senior officers “play the same game” of pretending everything is fine, they are not preserving neutrality. They are laundering abnormal power through ceremony, procedure, and rank.

The Chain of Permission

The article points to Chairman of the Joint Chiefs Gen. Dan Caine and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth as part of the problem, but the deeper issue is the chain of obedience itself. Caine’s posture, as Kendall describes it, reduces military judgment to technical compliance: can the military do what civilian leadership asks, not should it? That distinction is how institutions excuse themselves while power gets weaponized. Once that line is accepted, the military stops being a constitutional backstop and becomes a delivery system.

Fear Is Doing the Work

Kendall says lower-level officers are privately appalled but afraid to challenge what they are seeing. That matters more than the polished public language from the top. Fear of demotion, removal from promotion lists, and forced retirement is how authoritarian habits take root inside supposedly lawful institutions. Pete Hegseth does not need to purge everyone to enforce discipline. He only needs enough examples to make everyone else comply.

The Story’s Real Pattern

The article cites Trump’s willingness to use troops in American cities, crush protests, seize election equipment, and possibly interfere in midterm elections. Those are not abstract hypotheticals. They are the recognizable contours of executive power turning inward on the public. Kendall is right to say no lawyerly workaround can wash that away. But the more important point is harsher: the system is already revealing which officials will hide behind “policy” while the president probes for instruments of coercion.

What This Reveals

This is not a story about confusion in the ranks. It is a story about elites training themselves to survive power by normalizing it. That instinct is the rot. Democratic institutions do not fail only when a strongman breaks them. They fail when too many people with authority decide their own careers matter more than the limits of office.
