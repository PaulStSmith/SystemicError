GOP Senator who assured Todd Blanche's confirmation makes stunning confession

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

Paulo SantosAugust 19, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A Senate Vote That Confessed Its Own Corruption

Power Was Clear

Bill Cassidy did not hand Todd Blanche a vote because he trusted him. He handed him a vote while admitting he did not. That is the story. The Republican senator from Louisiana helped make Blanche’s confirmation possible, then went on television to say he could not predict whether Blanche would uphold the Constitution. He knew what he was doing. He just wanted the record to show that he did it with a trembling voice.

The institutional power here sat where it usually sits: with the Senate majority that can convert private doubts into public cover. Cassidy was not a bystander. He was the mechanism.

The Decision Belonged to Republicans

This was not confusion. It was consent.

The source makes clear that Cassidy had concerns about Blanche’s record, including the condition of the Justice Department, the handling of the Epstein files, immunity protections for the Trump family, and repeated attacks on political rivals. He also says he was close to tears when he announced support, as if the emotional display could bleach the choice clean. It cannot. When a senator knows enough to worry about constitutional fidelity and votes yes anyway, the problem is not uncertainty. The problem is obedience.

Lisa Murkowski, at least, refused to go along. She voted no. That matters because it exposes how thin the excuse-making really is. One Republican could see the nomination for what it was. The rest chose the party line and tried to dress it up as seriousness.

Misdirection as Routine

Cassidy’s line about “black or white” decision-making is the kind of language politicians use when they want to smother accountability in nuance. But this was not a moral riddle. The article says he was already alarmed by Blanche’s role in a “shambles” of a Justice Department. That is not ambiguity. That is evidence.

The misdirection here is familiar. Republican senators perform hand-wringing after they have already provided the votes that matter. They present themselves as reluctant guardians of norms while delivering the personnel needed to hollow those norms out. The tears are for the camera. The vote is for power.

Blanche’s Nomination Was the Point

Blanche is not being elevated despite the Trump family’s influence and political retaliation. According to the source, those are part of the case against him. That is the point. The administration wants an attorney general who will not stand as a barrier to immunity, vendetta, or institutional degradation. The Senate Republicans who confirmed him did not fail to notice. They accepted the premise.

That is what makes the confirmation so ugly. The Justice Department is not treated as a neutral legal institution. It is treated as another instrument to be captured, degraded, and repurposed for the president’s protection and his enemies’ punishment. The Senate majority helped normalize that capture by turning its confirmation power into a ceremonial stamp.

The Shame Is Institutional

Judge J. Michael Luttig’s warning that they will wear this “badge of shame” for life is not just rhetoric. It names the record these senators are making. Cassidy did not merely vote for a nominee he distrusted. He helped ratify a pattern in which Republican officeholders preserve their own relevance by surrendering their constitutional role.

That is the larger political lesson. The damage does not require open dictatorship. It only requires elected officials who know better and comply anyway, then perform sorrow to keep their reputations intact. The Senate is not being tricked. It is participating.

The Pattern Beneath the Performance

This is how institutional decay is managed in real time: leaders admit enough truth to sound conscientious, then do the opposite. They preserve the appearance of skepticism while feeding the machinery they claim to fear. That is not moderation. It is complicity with better diction.

Cassidy’s vote shows a Republican Party that no longer treats constitutional duty as binding when it conflicts with loyalty to Trump’s project. The confirmation matters less as a personnel decision than as a demonstration of who still thinks the rules apply and who has decided they do not.
