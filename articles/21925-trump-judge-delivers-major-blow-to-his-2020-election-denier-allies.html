Arizona Refuses to Hand Trump Allies a Get-Out-of-Accountability Card

Power Still Belongs to the Prosecutors

The only consequential fact here is simple: Arizona’s state prosecutors still have the power to re-indict Trump allies, and Judge Sam J. Myers just declined to stop them from doing it. That is not a moral victory for anyone. It is the legal system refusing to preemptively immunize people who tried to overturn an election and are now asking the court to spare them another round of consequences.

The Source Softens the Offense

The reporting treats the story like a courtroom disappointment for Trump’s side, as if the central issue were the defendants’ stress, expense, and sense of unfairness. That is misdirection. The defendants are not ordinary litigants caught in bureaucratic static. They are political actors accused of participating in an effort to overturn the 2020 election results. Their complaint is not that the process is confusing. It is that the process has not protected them from accountability.

Sympathy Is Not Immunity

Myers is described as a judge who previously expressed sympathy for Trump’s election deniers and who had already dismissed charges against Trump allies. That matters. So does his ruling now: he still refused to block re-indictment. The line the court drew is narrow but important. Sympathy in a judge does not convert into a veto over prosecution. Even a sympathetic bench has limits when the question is whether active accountability should be halted altogether.

The Real Story Is Institutional Decay

The article tries to widen the frame toward “judicial fatigue” and distrust of the DOJ, but the deeper pattern is more damning. Trump and his allies have spent years turning institutions into weapons, then performing outrage when those institutions answer back. Once justice is bent into partisan service, every prosecution looks like a grudge and every defense looks like principle. That is the contamination at work here: the deliberate collapse of trust created by political prosecutions, loyalty tests, and repeated attacks on the separation of powers.

Accountability Is Not the Same as Revenge

Elie Honig’s point in the source is blunt: restoring DOJ political independence and credibility should be the first order of business, and the current leadership does not seem interested. That is the right diagnosis, even if the article doesn’t quite sit with its implications. A justice system abused for factional aims does not become illegitimate the moment it finally turns on the people who fed on it. The problem is not that accountability exists. The problem is that accountability has been made to operate inside a wrecked institutional environment.

The Pattern Is Authoritarian Evasion

The larger pattern is familiar: break the rules, exhaust the system, then call scrutiny persecution. Trump’s allies are not asking for fairness in any meaningful sense. They are trying to convert delay, stress, and legal cost into a shield against consequences for anti-democratic conduct. Arizona’s ruling matters because it refuses that bargain. It does not fix the damage. It does not restore institutional credibility. It simply declines to let those who tried to subvert an election escape a second round of scrutiny by pleading inconvenience.
