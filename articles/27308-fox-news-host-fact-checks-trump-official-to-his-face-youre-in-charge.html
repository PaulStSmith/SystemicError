Fox News host fact checks Trump official to his face: You’re 'in charge!'

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 17, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

An explanation that doubles as an indictmentFox’s criticism follows Republican defeat

Laura Ingraham’s question cut through Vice President JD Vance’s attempt to blame obsolete technology for the government’s infrastructure failures: Republicans had controlled the federal government for two years, she said, so why had they not fixed it? Vance’s answer effectively conceded the point. The administration had applied patches, he said, while a genuine rebuild still required Congress to provide the money.

That exchange, reported by AlterNet from a Mediaite account of Ingraham’s Monday interview, matters because it exposed the evasive structure of Vance’s argument. Aging equipment may explain why systems fail. It does not explain why officials with governing power have not replaced them.

An explanation that doubles as an indictment

Vance described a system operating with technology that is 50 years old. He praised Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy and said officials sometimes must assemble a “patchwork of solutions” to keep it running.

That is a description of deferred maintenance, not an alibi for it. When Vance asked rhetorically why the country still had such an old system and declared that it needed rebuilding, Ingraham interrupted with the relevant fact: “Well, you guys have been in charge the last two years. Why hasn’t it gotten fixed?”

Vance replied that the administration had been fixing and patching what it could. Modernization, he said, would require Congress to “reinvest” and supply the necessary resources.

His answer relocated responsibility from the officials holding executive power to an institution Republicans had also controlled during the period Ingraham identified. Congress does control appropriations, and a replacement project cannot materialize by administrative command alone. But that makes spending priorities, legislative choices and governing competence central to the story. It does not make the failure ownerless.

An airline pilot appearing with Ingraham broadened the blame across decades, saying the problem had been developing for 30 years and was “on all of us.” The first part provides useful context: infrastructure neglect typically outlives one administration. The second obscures agency. Passengers and workers did not write federal budgets, decide which upgrades to defer or settle for temporary repairs. “All of us” is what institutional failure becomes when the people empowered to act prefer a constituency too diffuse to answer back.

The pilot’s sharper point was that superficial repairs were no longer adequate: “The spackle on the drywall is not enough anymore.” Vance’s account suggested the administration knew exactly that while continuing to emphasize its patching work.

Fox’s criticism follows Republican defeat

The confrontation did not amount to a political conversion by Ingraham. AlterNet placed it within a broader shift by outlets that had reliably supported President Donald Trump but became more critical after Republicans suffered what the article describes as historic setbacks in the 2026 midterm elections.

Ingraham’s own criticism has remained rooted in a dispute over how Trump wielded power, not whether he should have held it. After the election, she attributed the Republican losses to Trump and other party leaders abandoning what she regarded as MAGA’s central commitments.

She singled out Trump’s invasion of Iran, arguing that he had neither escalated toward decisive victory nor withdrawn after failure. Her preferred analogy was George W. Bush’s escalation in Iraq, while her alternative was the logic of a businessman ending an unsuccessful venture: “cut your losses.” She said that was what voters wanted and therefore “the whole point of politics.”

That framing is revealing. Ingraham was not challenging the administration from outside its political project; she was policing it after voters imposed a cost. Her interview with Vance performed the same function. The vice president offered deteriorating technology, decades of neglect and congressional funding needs as explanations. Ingraham forced the responsibility back toward the governing coalition whose record voters could actually judge.

Her question was potent because it applied the standard that partisan media routinely suspends for favored officials: authority brings accountability. Vance could identify the age of the technology, praise his transportation secretary and request more resources. He could not turn two years in power into somebody else’s tenure.
