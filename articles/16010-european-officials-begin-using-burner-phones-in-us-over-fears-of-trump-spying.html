European officials begin using burner phones in US over fears of Trump spying

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 18, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The New Cold War: Digital Espionage Among Allies

Cell phone (AFP)

European officials traveling to the U.S. will reportedly use burner phones and other measures over fears that they will be spied on by President Donald Trump's administration.

Four sources familiar with the situation told the Financial Times that officials traveling to the IMF and World Bank spring meetings had been given new guidance to avoid espionage.

"They said the measures replicate those used on trips to Ukraine and China, where standard IT kit cannot be brought into the countries for fear of Russian or Chinese surveillance," the Times reported.

"They are worried about the US getting into the commission systems," one official noted.

Another official declared that the "transatlantic alliance is over."

Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick was expected to meet with EU trade commissioner Maroš Šefčovič next week to resolve a brewing trade war after Trump ordered tariffs on European countries.

"Officials said the guidance for all staff travelling to the US included a recommendation that they should turn off phones at the border and place them in special sleeves to protect them from spying if left unattended," the report noted.

Our Analysis:

The New Cold War: Digital Espionage Among Allies

In a revelation that would have seemed ludicrous a few decades ago, yet perfectly aligns with the dystopian trajectory of modern geopolitics, European officials are now adopting spy novel tactics to protect themselves from their ally across the Atlantic. The Financial Times has unearthed a narrative so steeped in irony it could only belong to the era of President Donald Trump: European officials, fearing espionage, will reportedly use burner phones and other James Bond-esque measures while visiting the U.S. This isn't a script for a Cold War thriller; it's the reality of the strained transatlantic relationship.

The Paranoia is Palpable

The parallels drawn between the U.S. and countries like Ukraine and China, where heightened surveillance by Russian and Chinese operatives is a well-documented concern, are stark and telling. The very notion that European officials must treat the U.S. with the same level of caution is both astonishing and deeply troubling. "They are worried about the US getting into the commission systems," one official pointedly noted, underscoring a level of distrust that would have been unimaginable in the pre-Trump era.

A Transatlantic Alliance in Tatters

One official's declaration that the "transatlantic alliance is over" is a dramatic, yet perhaps accurate, assessment of the current state of affairs. The fabric of trust that once formed the backbone of U.S.-European relations is fraying, and at its seams, a culture of suspicion and espionage appears to be emerging. This is not the foundation on which successful diplomatic or trade negotiations are built, as evidenced by the brewing trade war over Trump's tariffs on European countries.

Digital Iron Curtains

The guidance for European staff traveling to the U.S. is reminiscent of Cold War-era protocols: Turn off phones at the border and place them in special sleeves to protect them from spying. Such measures reflect a reality in which digital iron curtains are being erected, not between ideologically opposed foes, but between supposed allies. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick's expected meeting with EU trade commissioner Maroš Šefčovič to resolve the trade disputes seems almost quixotic against this backdrop of mutual suspicion.

The Bottom Line

The shift to espionage-like precautions by European officials is more than a quirky anecdote; it's a symptom of a larger, more disturbing trend in international relations. Under the Trump administration, the U.S. has increasingly been viewed not as a trusted ally but as a potential adversary on the world stage. This development is not just a diplomatic curiosity—it's a profound systemic failure that threatens the very foundation of international cooperation and stability.

In an era where digital surveillance is omnipresent, the move by European officials may be pragmatic, but it also highlights a significant erosion of trust. As these diplomatic fissures continue to widen, one must wonder: What is the cost of this new era of digital espionage among allies? The answer, it seems, is the steady dismantling of the post-World War II international order, piece by piece.
