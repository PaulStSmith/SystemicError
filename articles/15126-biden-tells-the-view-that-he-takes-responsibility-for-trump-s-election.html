At the top of the show, Biden trashed Trump as a "dictator" and estimated that the reason Trump hates him so much is because "I beat him" in 2020.

When asked about the 2024 election and some of the top issues during the election, Alyssa Farah Griffin asked if he takes responsibility for the loss of Vice President Kamala Harris, and he said he did.

"Now, you are understandably very proud of many achievements in your administration. But do you take any responsibility for not hearing the concerns of voters on the border security or cost of living sooner, and do you take any responsibility for Trump's re-election?" she asked.

"Yes, I do, because, look, I was in charge and he won. So, you know, I take responsibility, number one. But, number two, you know, you remember we put together a commission led by one of the most conservative members of the United States Senate from Oklahoma, running it. We came up with a significant means by which we could deal with the border. What did Trump do? He got on the telephone and he called Republicans — do not support it. It's legal. It'll work. It'll make Biden look good. Don't do it. And they backed off. They backed up."

"Secondly, look, what we didn't have a chance to do and I — I've been advised not to say it, but I'll say it anyway," Biden continued. "No one thought I could get past an over-trillion-dollar — over a trillion-dollar plan to rebuild the country. Well, we made a lot of commitments and we — you know, billion-dollar tunnels going through for Amtrak, bridges, so on. But we weren't quite as good as he was about advertising it. It doesn't say Biden brought you this. There's nothing saying the Democrats brought you this, and we knew some of this would occur for another six months to two years. It takes time to do all that."

Biden went on to say he thought Trump would simply take credit for the transportation projects rather than fight back against the funding for the projects.

Our Analysis:

The Art of Political Deflection: Biden's Interview on "The View"

In a recent episode of "The View," former President Joe Biden launched a salvo of criticisms against the current occupant of the Oval Office, President Donald Trump, branding him a "dictator" and attributing Trump's animosity towards him to the 2020 electoral defeat. Biden's remarks, while pointed, seem to be part of a larger strategy of deflection and responsibility evasion, a common tactic in the political playbook.

Biden's Attack: A Classic Diversion

Biden's characterization of Trump as a dictator is a potent rhetorical move, designed to invoke strong emotional responses. However, it's essential to peel back the layers of this attack to understand its strategic underpinnings. By focusing on Trump's disposition and alleged authoritarian tendencies, Biden conveniently sidesteps the substantive policy critiques and concerns that have been directed at his administration.

Taking Responsibility or Passing the Buck?

When confronted by Alyssa Farah Griffin about the loss of Vice President Kamala Harris and the administration's handling of border security and the cost of living, Biden's response was a masterclass in political obfuscation. He admitted to taking responsibility for Trump's re-election, a statement that, on its face, seems to be an admission of accountability but quickly morphs into a blame-shifting exercise.

Biden's mention of a commission, led by a conservative senator, to address border security issues is a diversionary tactic. It places the onus of political gridlock on Republicans, suggesting they acted purely out of partisan spite rather than substantive disagreement. This narrative conveniently overlooks the complexities of immigration policy and the legitimate critiques of Biden's approach.

Infrastructure Achievements: A Missed Marketing Opportunity?

Biden's lamentation about his administration's failure to adequately publicize its infrastructure achievements is telling. He implies that the quality and impact of his policies are not the issue but rather the marketing of these policies. This perspective reveals a fundamental misunderstanding of the electorate's concerns. Voters are less interested in who gets credit for infrastructure projects and more concerned with the tangible benefits and improvements to their daily lives.

Moreover, Biden's expectation that Trump would simply take credit for the transportation projects, without contesting the funding, underscores a naiveté or perhaps a misjudgment of political realities. It reflects a broader trend within Biden's administration: an underestimation of opposition strategies and an overreliance on bipartisan goodwill.

Conclusion: The Politics of Distraction

Biden's interview on "The View" serves as a microcosm of a broader strategy employed by his administration: deflect, blame, and claim misunderstood intentions. While it's standard fare in the political arena to attack one's predecessors or opponents, Biden's attempts to navigate the criticism directed at his tenure reveal a reluctance to engage with substantive policy critiques fully.

The real systemic error here is not just in the policies themselves but in the approach to politics—an approach that prioritizes optics over outcomes, deflection over directness, and partisanship over pragmatism. As the political landscape continues to evolve, one hopes for a shift towards more substantive debates and away from the politics of distraction.
