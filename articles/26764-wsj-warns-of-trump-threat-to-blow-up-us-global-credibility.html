WSJ warns of Trump threat to 'blow up US global credibility'

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that The Wall Street Journal editorial board warned President Donald Trump against pursuing a grand bargain with Vladimir Putin and Xi Jinping that would divide Ukraine and other regions into great-power spheres of influence. The board argued that such an arrangement would destroy U.S. credibility with its allies while rewarding governments hostile to American interests.

According to AlterNet, the Journal said Trump could not compel Ukraine to capitulate and would bear responsibility for foreseeable carnage if he withdrew U.S. intelligence and targeting assistance. It also warned that Xi could exploit any opening to weaken the American position in the Pacific and advance China’s ambitions toward Taiwan.

The editorial predicted a serious Republican split if Trump effectively conceded Europe to Russia and the Pacific to China. It acknowledged that Congress and the courts have limited power to stop a determined president in foreign affairs, though lawmakers could impose political costs.

The power belongs to the president

The decisive actor here is not “America,” an unruly alliance system or some abstract postwar order. It is the president, who commands enormous authority over diplomacy, military assistance and intelligence cooperation.

That power gives Trump agency. Ending support for Ukraine, marginalizing European allies or bargaining over Taiwan would be presidential choices with identifiable beneficiaries and foreseeable consequences. Describing Trump as governed by “tactical impulse” may characterize his behavior, but it can also obscure responsibility. Erratic decisions remain decisions.

Allies are not bargaining chips

The proposed “Yalta 2” framing is revealing because it reduces sovereign countries to territory that larger states may allocate among themselves. Ukraine and Taiwan become objects of negotiation rather than political communities whose residents bear the costs.

Putin reportedly floated the three-power meeting. But only Trump could supply the American participation and concessions that would give such an arrangement weight. Putin’s proposal does not diminish Trump’s responsibility for entertaining it.

The Journal’s warning is therefore less about diplomatic style than about hierarchy: whether the United States would use its power to support allies or help stronger states dictate the futures of weaker ones.

Confusion is the wrong frame

The weakest element of the Journal’s argument is its opening suggestion that it is difficult to know what Trump is doing. The editorial itself supplies a more concrete record: hostility toward Ukraine and Europe, interest in deals with Putin and Xi, and openness to dividing influence among powerful states.

Treating that pattern chiefly as inscrutability risks converting policy into personality. It invites readers to study Trump’s impulses when the consequential questions are institutional: What assistance will he withdraw? What concessions will he offer? Which commitments will he abandon, and who will be exposed afterward?

Uncertainty about motive does not erase clarity about leverage or possible effects.

The conservative warning has limits

The Journal’s case rests heavily on American credibility, deterrence and strategic advantage. Those concerns are real within the reporting, but they also reveal the boundaries of the objection: allied countries matter partly because they strengthen U.S. power.

That framing can still miss the people underneath the chessboard. The Journal invokes potential carnage in Ukraine and the freedom of 23 million people in Taiwan, yet its governing vocabulary remains alliances, military positioning and political legacy. Human consequences appear as proof that a strategy is unsound, not as the central stakes of allowing powerful governments to dispose of smaller societies.

The system concentrates the danger

The broader pattern is an executive branch with enough unilateral foreign-policy power to destabilize alliances before other institutions can respond effectively. Congress may raise the political price, as the Journal says, but punishment after the decision is weaker than control over the decision itself.

That is the structural failure exposed here. A president can treat security commitments as personal negotiating assets, weaker countries as tradable terrain and mass suffering as leverage. The scandal is not merely that Trump may use that power recklessly. It is that the American system has placed so much of it within one person’s reach.
