American Health Insurance: Paying a Fortune for the Privilege of Being Denied Care

The American healthcare system has accomplished something remarkable: turning the fear of sickness into a reliable revenue stream, while making actual medical treatment an expense to be negotiated, delayed, or denied.

A man recently posted his new health insurance premiums for 2027 on Threads.

For himself and his wife. No children.

$2,271.22 per month.

That's $27,254.64 per year.

And because this is supposedly a low-premium high-deductible health plan, the couple also faces a $7,600 deductible.

Add those numbers together and you get $34,854.64.

That's how much the couple could spend on premiums and deductible payments in a year before their insurance begins sharing the cost of many services subject to that deductible.

And, as anyone familiar with American health insurance knows, reaching the deductible doesn't necessarily mean the bills stop. There may still be copayments, coinsurance, and other expenses until the plan's applicable out-of-pocket maximum is reached.

The man concluded his post with a proposal:

“Just tax me at 10% for Medicare and call it a day.”

He also described America's privatized healthcare system as an abomination.

I find it difficult to disagree with the sentiment.

Because somewhere along the way, Americans accepted a business model in which protecting your health requires paying a corporation enormous amounts of money, only to discover that the corporation may dispute its obligation when you actually need medical care.

And we've somehow been conditioned to call this insurance.

The Numbers Are Not an Aberration

It would be tempting to dismiss that Threads post as an unfortunate outlier.

Except the broader statistics are hardly comforting.

According to KFF's 2026 Employer Health Benefits Survey, the average annual premium for employer-sponsored family health insurance reached $28,303.

Yes, twenty-eight thousand dollars.

Workers paid an average of $7,471 directly, while employers covered the remainder.

Some people might argue that this makes employer-sponsored insurance considerably cheaper for workers.

Except employer contributions are not money conjured out of thin air. They are part of the cost of employing someone. Economists generally recognize that employer-paid health benefits affect compensation, including wages.

In other words, whether the money appears as a deduction on your paycheck or as a benefit paid by your employer, it is still an economic cost associated with your labor.

The American healthcare system simply distributes that cost across different accounting columns, making the full price less visible.

The same KFF survey found that average family premiums increased 55% between 2016 and 2026, compared with cumulative inflation of 37.9%.

And this is before considering the actual cost of visiting a doctor.

Imagine buying automobile insurance that costs almost as much as a small car every year, then discovering that you must pay thousands more before the company contributes to repairing your vehicle.

People would understandably question the arrangement.

In healthcare, we've institutionalized it.

The Company Collecting Your Premium Also Decides Whether to Pay

Here is where American health insurance becomes particularly troubling.

Insurance companies collect premiums from customers to cover medical expenses according to their policies.

But paying claims costs money.

And for a profit-seeking insurer, reducing expenses can improve financial performance.

That creates an obvious conflict of interest.

The company responsible for determining whether a covered treatment qualifies for payment also has a financial incentive to control how much it pays.

To be clear, not every denied claim is illegitimate. Insurance necessarily involves contracts, coverage restrictions, fraud prevention, and administrative verification.

But the existence of legitimate reasons for denying claims does not make every denial legitimate.

Nor does it eliminate the conflict.

And the available evidence raises serious questions about how that conflict is managed.

KFF examined claims submitted to insurers selling plans through the federal HealthCare.gov Marketplace in 2024.

The findings?

Nineteen percent of in-network claims were denied.

Out-of-network claims fared even worse, with a denial rate of 37%.

These figures don't mean one in five patients was denied medically necessary treatment. Claims can be rejected for administrative reasons, and the data covers a particular segment of the insurance market rather than every American health plan.

But that distinction doesn't make the numbers reassuring.

Especially when we examine what happens next.

Denied. Appeal. Denied Again.

According to KFF's analysis, fewer than 1% of denied claims in the federally facilitated Marketplace were appealed internally in 2024.

And among those that were appealed, insurers upheld their initial decisions 66% of the time.

Think about the implications.

Millions of claims are denied.

Almost nobody appeals.

And most of the comparatively few internal appeals leave the original decision standing.

Now, some of those decisions may be entirely justified. The published statistics cannot establish that every denial was wrongful.

But consider the environment in which these disputes occur.

The insurance company has lawyers, claims-processing departments, administrators, medical reviewers, and extensive experience interpreting its own contracts.

The patient may be sick, frightened, financially vulnerable, or trying to keep a loved one alive.

And now that patient must navigate a bureaucratic appeals process to obtain payment for a medical service.

The imbalance is extraordinary.

A sick person should not need the administrative sophistication of a corporate attorney to establish whether a medical bill is covered.

Yet the system routinely places that burden on patients.

And here's the particularly ugly part.

A denial doesn't have to survive every possible appeal to save an insurer money. It only has to go unchallenged.

That is an incentive worth examining.

It is not proof that every insurer deliberately issues unjustified denials. But it demonstrates why meaningful oversight, transparent denial data, and accessible independent appeals are indispensable.

When customers are least capable of fighting, procedural complexity can become an extraordinarily effective barrier.

This Isn't Just About Paperwork

A 2026 Commonwealth Fund report provides an even more disturbing perspective.

In a nationally representative survey, 21% of working-age adults with private insurance reported that they or a family member had been denied coverage for doctor-recommended medical care during the preceding year.

Among respondents who experienced a prior-authorization denial, 41% said it delayed care.

And 28% said a health problem became worse because of the denial.

Among those experiencing claim denials, 43% reported medical debt they were still paying.

These are reported experiences, not independent medical determinations that every denied service was contractually covered.

But they are evidence of real consequences.

Delaying medical care is not equivalent to delaying the delivery of a television.

Sometimes the delay changes the outcome of treatment.

Sometimes a manageable condition becomes a medical emergency.

Sometimes patients abandon treatment because fighting their insurer seems financially or emotionally impossible.

And sometimes the costs become debts that follow a family for years.

A healthcare financing system must be judged by more than whether its balance sheets reconcile.

It must also be judged by what happens to the people it is supposed to protect.

But Premiums Aren't Pure Profit!

Someone will inevitably point out that the original poster made a technical mistake.

He suggested his insurer could pocket the entire $27,254 in annual premiums as profit if he and his wife never visited a doctor.

That's not how insurance works.

Premiums are pooled to pay other policyholders' medical expenses, administrative costs, reserves, and other obligations.

Furthermore, the Affordable Care Act generally requires regulated insurers in applicable markets to spend at least 80% or 85% of adjusted premium revenue on healthcare and quality improvement, depending on the market.

Insurers falling below those medical-loss-ratio thresholds must generally issue rebates.

So no, twenty-seven thousand dollars in premiums does not equal twenty-seven thousand dollars in corporate profit.

But correcting that accounting error doesn't rescue the system from criticism.

It merely clarifies where the money goes.

Because the remaining share of premiums can support administrative expenses and profits, and private insurers are not charities.

They exist within a commercial structure that rewards financial performance.

That is not inherently evidence of fraud. Many businesses operate profitably while providing valuable services.

The deeper issue is whether the financial incentives of private insurance are adequately aligned with patients' interests.

When the product being sold is protection against the potentially catastrophic cost of illness, the consequences of that misalignment can be severe.

The real question is not whether every dollar becomes profit.

It is why access to essential medical treatment should depend so heavily on an industry's ability to manage claims as business expenses.

Americans Already Pay a Healthcare Tax. They Just Don't Call It One.

The original poster proposed something beautifully simple.

Tax his income at 10% and provide Medicare-style universal healthcare.

Would a flat 10% tax actually finance comprehensive universal healthcare across the United States?

That requires serious actuarial and economic analysis. A workable system would need to account for existing public spending, employer contributions, income distribution, healthcare prices, and the services covered.

But his underlying comparison reveals something important.

Americans already finance healthcare through an elaborate collection of compulsory or practically unavoidable expenditures.

Premiums.

Employer contributions.

Payroll taxes.

Deductibles.

Copayments.

Coinsurance.

And medical bills that insurance doesn't cover.

Most of these costs don't appear on a tax return under a single convenient heading.

That makes them politically easier to obscure.

If a government charged a household $27,000 annually for healthcare coverage while requiring another $7,600 deductible, critics would undoubtedly scrutinize the value it provided.

When a private company charges those amounts, the expense is presented as the price of a market product.

But changing the name of the payment doesn't change its effect on household finances.

There is, of course, a real difference between taxation and private insurance premiums. Taxes fund public programs under collectively established rules, while premiums purchase contractual coverage with defined benefits and restrictions.

The financing mechanisms are not identical.

The financial burden on the household, however, is real in either case.

And any serious comparison of healthcare systems must account for the entire burden, not merely the amount collected through taxes.

The Rest of the Developed World Has Some Thoughts

One of the strangest features of American healthcare debates is the recurring suggestion that guaranteeing access to healthcare would be an unprecedented experiment.

It wouldn't.

Countries such as Canada, the United Kingdom, Germany, France, and Australia have long operated different forms of universal healthcare coverage.

Their models are not identical.

Some use national health services. Others combine public financing, regulated insurance, nonprofit insurers, and private providers.

Nor are these systems perfect.

They face waiting times, workforce shortages, budget constraints, political interference, and difficult decisions about resource allocation.

But they demonstrate something that American political debate too often treats as impossible:

A wealthy industrialized society can organize healthcare around universal coverage rather than making access depend primarily on private insurance arrangements.

And the international comparisons are revealing.

The Commonwealth Fund's 2026 international analysis found that the United States spends considerably more on healthcare than its high-income peers while experiencing worse outcomes on several important measures, including life expectancy and avoidable mortality.

This doesn't prove private insurance alone causes every poor American health outcome. Public health, inequality, lifestyle, medical prices, and other factors matter.

But it raises an unavoidable question.

If Americans spend more, why does that spending purchase such inconsistent access to care?

The Real Product Is Financial Protection

Defenders of private health insurance can reasonably argue that insurance pools risk, protects people from enormous medical bills, and finances treatment that would otherwise bankrupt households.

Those functions matter.

A person receiving a six-figure hospital treatment may benefit enormously from comprehensive insurance coverage.

But this is precisely why insurance must work reliably when it is needed.

A firefighter who refuses to extinguish fires would be failing at the essential purpose of the job.

An insurance system that makes necessary care excessively difficult to access deserves the same scrutiny.

And the solution cannot simply be to tell patients to read their policies more carefully.

Contracts should be understandable. Coverage decisions should be evidence-based. Legitimate claims should be paid promptly. Appeals should be accessible, independent, and meaningful.

Most importantly, the financial incentives governing healthcare coverage should not systematically conflict with the health of the people being insured.

Whether those goals are best achieved through a single-payer model, tightly regulated nonprofit insurance, or another universal system is a legitimate policy debate.

But it must begin with an honest examination of how the current arrangement operates.

Not with the assumption that any proposal involving taxes is automatically worse than an arrangement that already extracts enormous sums through premiums and cost-sharing.

The Failure Was Designed

The American health insurance system did not emerge from a single grand conspiracy.

It developed through decades of legislation, employer-benefit arrangements, private commercial interests, public programs, regulatory decisions, and political compromises.

But the incentives operating within it are not mysterious.

Premium revenue matters.

Claims expenditure matters.

Administrative costs matter.

Profitability matters.

And the people managing these businesses are expected to pay attention to all of them.

The problem arises when those priorities collide with the interests of patients who need treatment.

A system does not have to be fraudulent in every transaction to produce outcomes that feel exploitative to the people trapped inside it.

It does not have to deny every claim to make access unnecessarily difficult.

And it does not have to keep every premium dollar as profit for its financial incentives to deserve fundamental scrutiny.

The original poster may have overstated what Cigna earns from his individual policy.

But he identified something considerably more important than an accounting detail.

He is being asked to pay more than $27,000 a year for coverage that still leaves him facing thousands in additional expenses.

And if he needs expensive medical treatment, he may have to navigate another layer of decisions about whether that treatment qualifies for payment.

That is an extraordinary amount of financial uncertainty for a product supposedly designed to reduce financial uncertainty.

Americans deserve a healthcare financing system that reliably pays for necessary care, provides universal access, and treats patients as people rather than merely as entries in a claims database.

Because when the financial interests of the company providing coverage and the medical needs of the person purchasing it come into conflict, there is only one acceptable priority for a healthcare system.

The patient.
