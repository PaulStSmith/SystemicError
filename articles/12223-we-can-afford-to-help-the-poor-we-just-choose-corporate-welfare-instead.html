We Can Afford to Help the Poor. We Just Choose Corporate Welfare Instead.

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 31, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

In the land of billion-dollar bailouts and tax-dodging conglomerates, the average American taxpayer is footing a grotesque bill. If you earn $50,000 a year, you're likely paying more than $3,700 annually to subsidize corporate welfare, while a paltry $42 of your taxes support safety net programs like food stamps. This isn't just a math problem. It's a values problem. It's a revelation of where America's priorities lie: not with its people, but with its profiteers.

The breakdown, sourced from Citizens for Tax Justice and The Tax Foundation, is stark. Out of every $50,000 in income:

  • $36 goes to food stamps,

  • $6 to other safety net programs,

  • $870 for corporate subsidies,

  • $1,600 to offset corporate tax loopholes,

  • $1,231 to make up for losses from corporate offshore tax havens.

This isn’t fiscal prudence. It’s daylight robbery dressed up in a Brooks Brothers suit.

The Great Redistribution—Upward

The right wing loves to shriek about “redistribution of wealth,” imagining a dystopia where billionaires are forced to give up their third yacht. But redistribution is happening—just in the opposite direction. Taxpayers are funding the obscene profits of multinational corporations while being told there's no money for universal healthcare, tuition-free college, or even pothole repair.

Corporate subsidies are often justified under the guise of economic development. We're told they're necessary to keep businesses from moving overseas or to incentivize job creation. But history shows these promises are hollow. Many companies take the subsidies, slash jobs anyway, and relocate operations to lower-wage countries with impunity. The American taxpayer ends up paying for both the bribe and the betrayal.

Tax Loopholes: Legal Theft

The $1,600 you shell out to cover tax loopholes isn't just an accounting quirk. It's the cost of a tax code engineered by and for the corporate elite. Carried interest, accelerated depreciation, and offshoring profits through shell companies in Ireland or the Cayman Islands are just a few tricks in the book. And every dollar a corporation avoids in taxes is a dollar you and your neighbors must cough up to fund roads, schools, and the military-industrial complex.

This is not the invisible hand of the free market. This is a gloved fist rigging the game.

Offshore Tax Havens: The Final Insult

Then comes the $1,231 you pay to offset losses from corporate tax havens. Corporations like Apple, Amazon, and Google have mastered the art of parking profits in low-tax jurisdictions, effectively disappearing billions in potential tax revenue. These aren't mom-and-pop shops struggling to get by; they're trillion-dollar behemoths evading their social contract. And while they're sunbathing their profits in Bermuda, you're patching the financial holes they leave behind.

We are told that corporations are people. But find me the person who contributes so little and extracts so much without being called a freeloader.

The Safety Net Myth

Let’s turn to the other side of the equation. For all the venom spewed at the poor—the endless “welfare queen” tropes and Fox News outrage segments—the reality is that we spend a microscopic portion of federal revenue on safety net programs. Food stamps (SNAP) are one of the most tightly regulated, means-tested, and efficient programs in the federal arsenal. The myth of rampant abuse is exactly that: a myth, manufactured to distract from the real theft happening in boardrooms and political backrooms.

Americans are not being bled dry by single mothers buying groceries. They're being fleeced by billionaires who buy Congress.

The Lie of Scarcity

We are constantly told there's not enough. Not enough for universal healthcare. Not enough for free college. Not enough for a living wage. But there’s always enough for corporate handouts, defense contractors, and tax breaks for the ultra-rich.

This lie of scarcity is the cornerstone of austerity politics, and it only persists because it benefits those at the top. The truth is: we are the richest nation in history. If we can afford to fund endless wars and subsidize billionaires, we can afford to feed children and house the homeless. We simply choose not to.

The Political Class: Bought and Paid For

Why does this insanity persist? Because our political class is bought. Republicans and Democrats alike rake in campaign contributions from the same corporate interests they’re supposed to regulate. And when they leave office, they waltz into lobbying gigs and board seats as payment for services rendered.

The system is not broken. It is working exactly as intended—just not for you.

What Needs to Change

First, abolish corporate welfare. Full stop. End tax loopholes that allow corporations to pay zero or even negative taxes. Close offshore tax havens by implementing global minimum tax standards. Redirect those savings into programs that actually help people: education, healthcare, housing, climate infrastructure.

Second, stop demonizing the poor. Poverty is not a personal failure; it is a policy failure. When we treat poverty as a symptom of laziness rather than a consequence of systemic inequality, we end up blaming the victims while applauding the perpetrators.

Third, demand transparency. Make corporations disclose how much they receive in subsidies and tax breaks. Sunshine is the best disinfectant, and it’s long overdue.

Conclusion: Follow the Money

If you want to know a society's values, follow the money. In America, the trail leads not to the hungry, the sick, or the struggling single parent. It leads to Wall Street boardrooms and offshore accounts.

It's time to turn the tables. Not with slogans. Not with incremental reform. But with structural change that puts people over profits.

Because we can afford to help the poor. We just have to stop worshiping the rich.
