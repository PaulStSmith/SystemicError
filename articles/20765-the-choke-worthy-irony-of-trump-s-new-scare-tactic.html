The choke-worthy irony of Trump's new scare tactic

Trump’s Red Scare Is Cover for a Much Stranger Power Grab

The Smear Has a Sponsor

Trump’s July 3 anti-communist performance at Mt. Rushmore was not a warning. It was propaganda with a target list. The source story gives the central fact plainly: Trump used the language of a “communist menace” to frame Democratic gains as an existential threat, and Speaker Mike Johnson eagerly amplified it. That is not analysis. It is an organized effort to turn ordinary electoral competition into a panic about internal enemies.

The important question is not whether Trump understands communism. He does not need to. The point is that he understands fear. Red-baiting is useful because it replaces policy with accusation and converts political opponents into cultural contaminants.

Projection Is the Point

The story’s most damaging claim is also its most credible one: Trump’s accusations work like confessions. The source text ties that pattern to his false claim that the 2020 election was stolen and to his current efforts to rig the 2026 midterms. That is the real operating system here. He accuses others of what he is doing or wants to do.

So when Trump shrieks about “communists,” the issue is not ideological precision. It is political utility. He is trying to make voters mistake democratic socialism, or even mainstream social spending, for a hostile takeover. That is deliberate misdirection. It turns public life into a hall of mirrors where the man expanding executive control pretends to be defending liberty.

The State Is Already in the Boardroom

The source article’s strongest factual core is not the speech. It is the list of state entanglements Trump has pushed into major industries. According to the reporting, his administration has secured government stakes in Intel, Westinghouse, MP Materials, USA Rare Earth, Lithium Americas, Trilogy Metals, and a governance-heavy “golden share” arrangement at U.S. Steel. The article also says Trump wants stakes in Lockheed Martin and Boeing.

That is the actual story of power: the administration is not merely regulating industry; it is moving into ownership and control. The source frames this as “partial-nationalization,” and that description matters because it exposes the fraud in Trump’s rhetoric. He is not fighting state ownership. He is practicing it, selectively and opportunistically, under his own command.

If this looks like a state-directed economy, that is because it is one, except without democratic accountability. The public is told this is about national strength or reducing dependence on China. But the source makes clear who gets to steer the dealmaking: Trump, his administration, and the firms he chooses to pressure or reward.

China Is the Excuse, Not the Logic

Scott Bessent’s defense, as described in the source, is that grabbing equity in key industries reduces dependence on China. That argument is politically convenient and analytically thin. It gives a nationalist gloss to a much uglier reality: the executive branch is picking winners, accumulating leverage, and normalizing direct state involvement in private capital.

That is the misdirection here. Trump’s allies invoke “communism” when the administration is busy imitating the most coercive features of state capitalism: ownership stakes, control rights, and strategic pressure on corporate decision-making. The irony is not accidental. It is the cover story.

And because Trump’s coalition is built on spectacle rather than comprehension, the contradiction is safe. His supporters are asked to fear the word “communist” while the administration advances a model of government power that ordinary people did not vote to understand, let alone control.

The Real Authoritarian Pattern

The larger pattern is not just hypocrisy. It is consolidation. Trump inflates ideological panic to justify stronger executive reach, then uses that reach to entangle the state with favored industries and political interests. That is not democracy failing by accident. It is power working exactly as designed by people who treat public authority as a personal instrument.

The source article calls this authoritarian crony capitalism, and that is the right frame. It is a system where the government is not captured in secret; it is openly repurposed. The language of anti-communism is being used to sell a political project that centralizes control, rewards loyalty, and blurs the line between public authority and private profit.

The Point Is Control

The slogan war matters less than the machinery behind it. Trump’s “communist” attack is not a substantive critique of the left. It is a smokescreen for his own drive toward economic leverage and political domination. Johnson’s participation shows how willing Republicans are to launder that project through panic.

This is the pattern to remember: when authoritarian politics screams about internal enemies, it is usually preparing the ground for more concentrated power above everyone else. The enemy is rarely communism. It is accountability.
