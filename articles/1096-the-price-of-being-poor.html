The Price of Being Poor

The Poverty Surcharge

The sentence that started this was simple enough to fit on a social media post:

The bank says you are too poor for an $800 mortgage payment, so you have to pay $1,500 in rent instead.

It is one of those lines that sounds like a joke until you remember it is not a joke. It is a business model. The person who cannot qualify for the cheaper path is pushed into the more expensive one. The person without capital is charged extra for not having capital. The person who needs stability most is sold instability at a premium.

This is what poverty does in a market society. It does not merely deprive people of money. It makes every ordinary act of survival more expensive.

Housing is the cleanest example because the cruelty is visible. A mortgage payment can be lower than rent, but the mortgage builds equity while rent buys permission to remain indoors for another month. The renter may have proven, over years, that they can make the higher payment. That proof often does not matter. The bank wants a different kind of proof: down payment, credit profile, income shape, paperwork, debt ratios, institutional legibility.

So the person pays more because they do not have enough to be trusted with paying less.

That is not a paradox. That is class discipline with a calculator.

The same logic shows up in banking fees. In the United States, overdraft fees have long been one of the most grotesque examples of poverty being converted into revenue. You run out of money, and the bank charges you more money for having run out of money. A five-dollar mistake can become a thirty-five-dollar punishment. The fee is not there to solve the shortage. It is there to monetize the shortage.

Minimum-balance fees belong in the same moral sewer. If you cannot afford to keep enough money idle in your account, the bank charges you for not having enough money. It is a fee for being poor, dressed up as account maintenance.

The point is not that financial institutions never take risk. The point is that risk is not measured in a vacuum. Risk is socially interpreted. Wealth is treated as evidence of virtue. Poverty is treated as evidence of suspicion. The rich get lower rates, better terms, more forgiveness, more time, more options, more language to describe their failures as strategy. The poor get penalties, denials, late fees, moral lectures, and a permanent demand to prove they are not about to do something wrong.

When Risk Is Really Class Prejudice

One of the great myths of modern finance is that credit systems are neutral. They are not. They are mathematical systems built on political assumptions about who counts as trustworthy.

The poor are often described as risky borrowers, but this is a thin description of a much thicker reality. Poor people are not casual about reputation. For many, reputation is the only working capital they have.

A poor person cannot always call a lawyer, absorb a bad month, refinance a mistake, or move assets around until a failure looks respectable. Reputation is survival infrastructure. It is how you borrow from relatives, get an extra week from a landlord, ask a neighbor for help, convince a mechanic to wait until payday, or keep access to the informal networks that make life possible when formal systems fail.

For the rich, default can be a restructuring event. For the poor, default becomes character evidence.

That is the obscene asymmetry. When wealthy people fail to pay, the language gets sophisticated. It becomes liquidity, leverage, restructuring, strategic bankruptcy, portfolio loss, exposure management. When poor people fail to pay, the language gets moral. Irresponsible. Unreliable. Bad with money. A risk.

But many poor borrowers treat repayment with deadly seriousness because the consequences are not abstract. A damaged reputation does not just lower a number. It shrinks the world. It closes the next door before they even reach it.

This is where different credit systems reveal their moral assumptions. Some systems begin with suspicion and require people to build a quantified profile before they can be treated as credible. Others begin closer to a presumption of standing: you are trusted until there is evidence that you should not be.

Neither model is innocent. But the difference matters, because it decides whether poverty starts as an absence of money or as an accusation.

Brazil Before the Score

Brazil used to operate much more through that older negative-record logic: you were trusted until you proved otherwise. If your name was clean, you had standing. If you bounced a check, failed to pay a credit card, or had a debt formally registered, you lost that standing until you repaired it.

It was not gentle. It could be socially brutal. But the default was not always suspicion. The default was: you are a person in good standing unless there is evidence that you are not.

There were credit bureaus and debt registries, of course. There were checks, store credit, credit cards, court-registered debts, and the familiar terror of getting one’s name dirty. A bounced check could eventually put someone into the Cadastro de Emitentes de Cheques sem Fundos, the CCF, a national registry connected to checks returned without funds. The practical effect was not subtle: while listed, a person could be blocked from receiving new checkbooks, and the restriction could make other forms of credit harder.

That system punished failure after it happened. A clean name mattered because it meant there was no formal evidence against you. Trust was not sentimental; it was conditional. But it began from a presumption of standing.

Then Brazil changed.

Serasa, once a Brazilian banking-sector creature, became Serasa Experian after Experian acquired control in 2007. Brazil also built out Cadastro Positivo, a positive-credit registry designed to record not only default and delinquency, but payment behavior. The older system asked, in effect: has this person failed? The newer system asks: what does this person’s financial behavior show over time?

That sounds small. It is not small.

The difference between a negative registry and a positive registry is the difference between a criminal record and a resume. One says what went wrong. The other can say what has gone right.

Leaving One Brazil and Returning to Another

Here is the hundred-thousand-feet version of my own little story.

I left Brazil in 2012, when the older credit logic still felt dominant. I came back in 2022 to a different financial country.

When I returned, I did not have a modern Brazilian credit story. I did not have a meaningful score. In the United States, that kind of thin-file condition can be absurdly punishing. You can be responsible, solvent, careful, and still treated as a question mark because the system cannot see you in the format it prefers.

In Brazil, I was able to open a bank account for free, with ordinary modern digital onboarding. Phone. CPF. Selfie. Account.

Then everyday behavior started doing what older credit systems often refused to do: it counted.

Bills paid on time helped. Ordinary banking behavior helped. Regular financial life became visible. By mid-2023, my score was in the upper 700s. Not perfect, but already respectable. By June 2026, it was 950.

That is not proof that the Brazilian system is virtuous. No credit score deserves worship. But it is proof that the design of a scoring system matters enormously.

A score can be a wall, or it can be a record.

In the worst version, a credit score says: you cannot get credit because you have not already been granted credit. That is the American thin-file trap, the financial equivalent of demanding work experience for an entry-level job.

In the better version, a score can say: this person keeps ordinary obligations, pays bills, maintains accounts, and behaves reliably under real life conditions. That does not abolish class inequality, but it at least allows responsible poor and working-class people to become visible without first buying their way into visibility.

Brazil’s Newer Model Is Not the U.S. Model

This is where the comparison gets interesting.

Brazil did import a credit-score culture, and the Experian/Serasa connection is part of that history. But Brazil’s current system is not simply the American model with Portuguese labels.

Serasa’s public materials describe a score from 0 to 1,000 that estimates the probability of paying financial commitments on time. Its own breakdown gives the largest weight to payment behavior. It lists factors including payments, experience in the credit market, negative debts, searches for credit, personal data, and contracts. Serasa also says paying bills on time matters, including credit-card bills, loans, financing, and even utility-style obligations like electricity and phone bills.

Open Finance adds another layer. With user consent, connected accounts can share account movement, payment history, credit-card limits, financing, credit operations, and investments. That means the system can see more than traditional borrowing. It can see parts of everyday financial life.

This does not mean every grocery purchase magically raises a score. That would be too clean. A grocery purchase may matter indirectly if it is part of bank or card behavior that becomes visible through account data, or if it sits inside a paid-on-time credit-card cycle. But the larger point stands: modern Brazilian scoring can incorporate ordinary financial behavior in a way that the older U.S.-style credit trap often does not.

That matters for poor people.

Because the poor do not lack financial discipline. They lack margin. They lack cushions. They lack institutional patience. They lack the kind of inherited stability that makes risk models smile.

If a system only sees loans, mortgages, and formal credit lines, it will miss the working poor’s actual discipline. It will miss rent paid for years. It will miss utility bills kept current under pressure. It will miss groceries budgeted down to the cent. It will miss the survival accounting that middle-class people romanticize only after calling it meal planning.

But if a system can see ordinary payment behavior, the working poor are not invisible in the same way. They can build proof through life itself.

That is better.

Not good enough. But better.

Free Banking Changes the Floor

The other major Brazilian change is the collapse of basic banking friction.

Modern digital banking in Brazil can be shockingly simple compared with older bank culture or the American fee maze. Open an account. Use Pix. Move money instantly. If there is no money, the transaction fails. The bank does not need to turn your empty account into a revenue event.

That is a huge moral difference.

There is a world between “your transaction cannot proceed because the funds are not available” and “your transaction went through and now we are charging you a penalty because you were short.” One is refusal. The other is extraction.

The Brazilian model does not abolish poverty. It does not make rent cheap, wages fair, or credit benevolent. But free basic banking and failed transactions are materially better than fee traps. They reduce the number of places where poverty is automatically converted into additional debt.

This is where the United States looks especially barbaric. Not because Brazil is a paradise, but because the American system has normalized so many petty tollbooths on being broke that people forget they are choices.

Overdraft fees are a choice. Minimum-balance fees are a choice. Credit invisibility is a choice. Refusing to count rent and utilities while obsessing over credit utilization is a choice. Charging the poor more because they have less is a choice.

And choices can be changed.

The Tradeoff

Still, the old Brazilian system should not be romanticized too much.

The clean-name model had a humane presumption built into it, but it could also be opaque, local, reputational, and punitive. Once your name was dirty, the social and financial consequences could be severe. Repair required bureaucracy, payment, proof, and time. A person could be boxed out by a relatively small failure.

The new system can be more inclusive if it counts everyday responsibility. But it also expands surveillance. It creates a world where every bill, account, movement, and relationship can become part of an institutional profile. The danger is not only being unseen. The danger is being seen too completely by systems that do not owe you mercy.

That is the tradeoff.

The old model could be cruel after failure. The new model can be cruel before failure if it turns insufficient data into suspicion, or if proprietary algorithms decide that a person’s life pattern looks wrong.

The best credit system would keep the humane parts of both and discard the cruelty.

It would preserve free banking. It would reject overdraft exploitation. It would let transactions fail cleanly when funds are unavailable. It would count positive everyday behavior: utilities, rent, phone, regular payments, account stability, and responsible use. It would avoid making people buy credit products merely to prove they deserve credit. It would allow people to repair harm. It would limit data extraction. It would force lenders to explain decisions. It would treat a clean record as meaningful, not as an empty file.

Above all, it would stop pretending poverty is a character defect.

The Real Question

The question is not whether credit scores are good or bad in the abstract.

The question is what a society chooses to measure, what it chooses to ignore, and who benefits from the difference.

If a system ignores the fact that someone has paid $1,500 in rent every month but denies them an $800 mortgage because they lack the approved symbols of stability, the system is not measuring responsibility. It is protecting ownership from the people most exploited by exclusion.

If a bank charges a person for not having enough money, the bank is not teaching discipline. It is harvesting desperation.

If a credit model only rewards people who already have credit, it is not assessing trust. It is laundering privilege through math.

But if a system lets everyday responsibility become visible, if it allows a person to build reputation through ordinary life, if it lets the working poor be seen as reliable without first forcing them through expensive traps, then it is at least pointing in a better direction.

That is the lesson from the Brazil contrast.

Not that Brazil solved poverty. Not that credit scores are liberation. Not that data systems should be trusted without suspicion.

The lesson is sharper than that:

Poverty is expensive because systems are built to make it expensive. Credit is cruel when it treats need as evidence against the needy. And the measure of a financial system is not how politely it talks about risk, but whether it gives ordinary people a real way to be trusted before they are punished.
