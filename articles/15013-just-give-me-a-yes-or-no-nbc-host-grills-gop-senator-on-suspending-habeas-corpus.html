'Just give me a yes or no': NBC host grills GOP senator on suspending habeas corpus

Sen. John Barrasso (R-WY) declined to rule out voting to suspend habeas corpus if it helps President Donald Trump deport undocumented people.

In a Sunday interview with the senator, NBC host Kristen Welker pointed out that White House aide Stephen Miller had recently floated the idea of suspending the right to challenge detentions in court.

"The Constitution says that habeas corpus may not be suspended, quote, unless in cases of rebellion or invasion, would you vote to suspend habeas corpus since the power does ultimately lie with Congress?" Welker asked the Wyoming Republican.

"The president has said he will follow the law," Barrasso deflected. "The president says if he disagrees with the law, that he will appeal those things."

"And the president has now seen judges, district judges, radical district judges, using their courts to set national standards on making it harder for the president to deport individuals, criminals, and I stand with the president," he continued.

Welker pressed: "And yet, Senator, just to put a fine point on this, I want to know what you would do. Would you vote to suspend habeas corpus if this were brought before Congress? Ultimately, this power lies with Congress."

"The president said he is going to follow the law," Barrasso replied. "He was on with you last week. He said he has great respect for the Supreme Court. He said he expects the attorney general to do the right thing. And I expect that the president will."

"Can you just give me a yes or no?" Welker asked.

"I don't believe that this is going to come to Congress," Barrasso stated. "What I believe is [that] the president is going to follow the law. He has said it repeatedly."

Our Analysis:

A Dangerous Dance around Habeas Corpus

In an era where political discourse often teeters on the edge of the absurd, Sen. John Barrasso's (R-WY) recent interview with NBC's Kristen Welker has provided yet another glaring example of the lengths to which some lawmakers will go to avoid directly confronting the troubling implications of their allies' proposals. The topic at hand? The suspension of habeas corpus to facilitate the deportation of undocumented individuals—a concept so fraught with constitutional peril that even discussing it feels like a descent into a legal dystopia.

The Evasion Game

When Welker pointedly asked Barrasso if he would support suspending habeas corpus, a fundamental right designed to protect against unlawful detention, his responses were nothing short of a masterclass in evasion. Rather than addressing the gravity of the proposal floated by White House aide Stephen Miller, Barrasso deflected, choosing instead to hide behind President Donald Trump's vague assurances of adherence to the law.

"The president has said he will follow the law," Barrasso repeated, as if this mantra could somehow absolve him of the need to confront the ethical and legal quagmire his party is wading into. The senator's refusal to provide a direct yes or no to Welker's question is not just political sidestepping; it's a stark illustration of a willingness to entertain, or at least not publicly denounce, an idea that strikes at the heart of constitutional protections.

The Real Issue at Hand

Let's be unequivocally clear: the suggestion to suspend habeas corpus is not merely a policy proposal. It's a dangerous flirtation with authoritarianism. The Constitution explicitly states that habeas corpus should not be suspended "unless when in cases of rebellion or invasion the public safety may require it." To suggest its suspension to expedite the deportation of undocumented individuals is to argue that their presence constitutes an "invasion" so dire it justifies overriding one of the judiciary's primary checks on the power of the executive and legislative branches.

Barrasso's rhetoric about "radical district judges" using their courts to set national standards is a thinly veiled attack on the judiciary's role in checking the excesses of executive power. By framing the issue as one of judicial overreach, rather than an executive branch seeking unprecedented power to detain without oversight, Barrasso attempts to shift the blame and distract from the core issue.

The Bottom Line

What this interview reveals is not just one senator's reluctance to take a stand against a patently unconstitutional proposal, but a broader trend within certain political circles to test the waters of authoritarian measures under the guise of law and order. Barrasso's evasions and deflections are emblematic of a dangerous political calculus: the idea that adherence to the president's wishes, regardless of their constitutional implications, takes precedence over the defense of fundamental civil liberties.

In refusing to outright condemn the idea of suspending habeas corpus, Sen. Barrasso has laid bare the lengths to which some lawmakers will go to support their party's leader, even if it means trampling on the Constitution. This is not just political theater; it's a cautionary tale of the erosion of democratic norms and the rule of law.
