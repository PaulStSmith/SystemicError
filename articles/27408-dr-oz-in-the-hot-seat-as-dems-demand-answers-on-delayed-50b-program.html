Dr. Oz in the hot seat as Dems demand answers on delayed $50B program

Based on an article by Sarah Jane Tribble, KFF Health News

Paulo SantosNovember 22, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A deadline missed, an explanation withheldTemporary relief for damage Congress createdThe money trail remains obscuredHospitals are closing while Washington trades letters

The Trump administration delayed the second round of awards from a $50 billion rural health fund, then changed what states would receive without explaining why. Eighteen congressional Democrats are now demanding that Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services Administrator Mehmet Oz disclose how the money is calculated, where it is going and whether rural hospitals—or consultants and technology firms—are actually receiving it.

The nine-day delay is consequential because the program is not merely a grant initiative. Republicans created it as a temporary cushion after President Donald Trump’s budget law imposed an estimated $137 billion in Medicaid cuts affecting rural America over 10 years. States are being asked to plan around a five-year fund while absorbing much larger, longer-lasting losses engineered by the same government.

A deadline missed, an explanation withheld

CMS was supposed to announce the second-year awards by Oct. 31. One day before that deadline, the agency told states it had discovered “an additional administrative process requirement.” It did not announce the allocations until Nov. 9.

The resulting changes were generally small in percentage terms, but states are operating health systems in which small shifts can carry immediate consequences. Illinois received roughly $3 million less than its first-year award of $193.4 million, a reduction of about 1.5%. Alabama lost $4.1 million, while Colorado’s allocation fell by nearly $3 million.

Illinois Rep. Nikki Budzinski said CMS gave the state no reason for its reduction and no information about an appeals process. She joined Alabama Rep. Terri Sewell and Colorado Sen. Michael Bennet in leading the letter to Oz, which requests answers by Nov. 25 and a briefing on the delay and its effects.

“Those kind of delays prevent a state to fully prepare for what’s to come,” Budzinski said.

That is the immediate administrative failure: CMS controls the scoring, the schedule and the flow of funds, while states and rural providers bear the consequences of unexplained changes. The agency has published data maps, press releases and presentations about its scoring system. Yet Alan Morgan, CEO of the National Rural Health Association, said the method still needs to be explained clearly enough that every state can understand and reproduce success.

CMS officials did not respond to AlterNet’s questions before its deadline.

Temporary relief for damage Congress created

The Rural Health Transformation Program distributes $10 billion annually for five years. First-year awards ranged from $147 million to $281 million, with all 50 states receiving money through a federal scoring process. Allocations are reconsidered each year; most moved by less than 2% in the second round. North Dakota and Iowa posted the largest gains, while New Jersey, Florida and Vermont sustained the largest reductions.

Those figures can make the program look like a substantial federal commitment. The political arithmetic says otherwise. Republicans added the fund to their budget legislation as a last-minute inducement while advancing Medicaid cuts that hit rural hospitals and patients especially hard. The temporary program amounts to less than half the estimated decade-long Medicaid reduction for rural America—and it expires after five years.

Cara James, president and CEO of Grantmakers in Health, called the fund “retail therapy for Congress” at a Milken Institute event. Her description identifies the misdirection built into the policy: lawmakers can advertise a new rural health program while the same law removes far more support from Medicaid, food assistance and other grants on which rural communities depend.

A short-term, competitively scored fund does not replace a durable insurance program. Medicaid payments sustain routine operations and care for patients; transformation grants arrive through administrative contests, shifting formulas and annual federal review. The former supports the financial base of rural care. The latter leaves states competing for partial relief from a crisis federal policy is deepening.

The money trail remains obscured

The lawmakers’ concern extends beyond how CMS divides money among states. They want to know who ultimately receives it.

Their letter warns that too much could flow to large consulting and technology companies instead of hospitals, clinics and communities. Budzinski said the point is to ensure that the money reaches rural providers rather than consultants. Democrats asked CMS to create a searchable spending database and explain how it will evaluate state performance and set future awards.

Morgan said his association’s analysis indicates that states are directing federal money to local hospitals and clinics. But he also said the overall division between frontline providers and other recipients, including consultants, remains unclear. That distinction matters because the program’s branding does not guarantee its beneficiaries. A dollar classified as rural health spending may finance a hospital’s care, or it may purchase planning, software and professional services several steps removed from a patient.

The administration created an Office of Rural Health Transformation to oversee the initiative. What it has not provided is the basic public accounting needed to judge whether “transformation” means keeping facilities open or building an administrative industry around their precarity.

State governments were initially given just over seven weeks to prepare applications for the first awards. They had to compete under a complicated scoring system, then accept annual revisions whose logic remains insufficiently clear to the officials affected. That arrangement concentrates discretion at CMS while dispersing responsibility downward: federal officials set the rules, but states and providers must explain service losses when the numbers change.

Hospitals are closing while Washington trades letters

Five rural hospitals have closed or converted to lower-level facilities since the program was established, according to a list maintained by the Cecil G. Sheps Center for Health Services Research at the University of North Carolina. One Michigan facility eliminated inpatient beds and shifted to emergency-only care in an effort to survive.

The fund has therefore not stopped the contraction it was presented as addressing. Nor could a temporary grant pool plausibly neutralize every effect of much larger Medicaid reductions. The unresolved issue is whether the program is at least delivering its limited resources efficiently and transparently.

Congress has asked before. Democrats wrote to Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. about the rollout in August 2025, one month after the law passed. Budzinski later contacted Oz on Illinois’ behalf. Bennet, Republican Sen. Susan Collins of Maine and other senators asked in June that the money be aimed at rescuing rural hospitals and clinics.

“We haven’t gotten full responses to any of these inquiries,” Budzinski said.

That repeated silence is not a communications problem for states to solve. Oz runs the agency that controls the awards. CMS can disclose its calculations, provide an appeals process and show where the money lands. Until it does, the administration is asking rural communities to trust a shifting formula attached to a temporary program created to soften the political impact of far larger cuts.
