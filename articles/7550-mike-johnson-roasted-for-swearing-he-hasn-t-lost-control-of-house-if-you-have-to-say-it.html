Mike Johnson roasted for swearing he hasn't lost control of House: 'If you have to say it'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 18, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Unraveling of Speaker Mike Johnson’s Control: A Political Spectacle

U.S. Speaker of the House Mike Johnson (R-LA) attends a press conference, more than a month into the longest U.S. government shutdown in Washington, D.C., U.S., November 10, 2025. REUTERS/Evelyn Hockstein

House Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA) faced a massive blow to his leadership on Wednesday after four House Republicans crossed over to support Democrats’ discharge petition for a three-year extension of enhanced Affordable Care Act subsidies — and immediately faced questions from reporters over his leadership, to which he adamantly responded, “I have not lost control” of the House, despite this being the third discharge petition to overrule him this year.

This exchange, which came shortly before House Republicans sought to crack down and terminate a vote on the discharge petition early, led to an avalanche of mockery from commenters on social media.

“’I have not lost control of the House’ sounds like a guy who has definitely lost control of the House,” chided Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez (D-NY).

“Recent scientific studies confirm that the most reliable indicator of a Speaker losing control of the House: the moment he tells reporters, ‘I have not lost control of the House,’” jabbed Rep. Sam Liccardo (D-CA).

“Right up there with ‘I am not a crook,’” wrote Missouri State University president emeritus Mike Nietzel, referencing the notorious words of former President Richard Nixon.

“I was more convinced when Christine O’Donnell said she wasn’t a witch,” wrote Andrew Feinberg of The Independent, referring to an infamous moment for a Republican candidate in the 2010 U.S. Senate election in Delaware.

“[narrator] He has,” wrote former CNN analyst Chris Cillizza.

“If you have to say it...” wrote The Record cybersecurity reporter Martin Matishak.

“Narrator: He never had control to lose it,” wrote Furman University education professor Paul Thomas.

Our Analysis:

The Unraveling of Speaker Mike Johnson’s Control: A Political Spectacle

House Speaker Mike Johnson’s (R-LA) recent debacle over the enhanced Affordable Care Act subsidies is not just a mere political hiccup; it’s a glaring testament to the fractious and tumultuous nature of his leadership—or, perhaps more aptly, the lack thereof. In what can only be described as a dramatic irony, Johnson’s adamant insistence that he has not “lost control” of the House following the successful crossing over of four House Republicans to support a Democratic discharge petition, serves as a stark emblem of a leadership in crisis.

“I have not lost control”—a phrase now infamously associated with Johnson’s leadership, ironically echoes through the halls of Congress, not as a statement of fact, but as a whisper of doubt. This isn’t the first time Johnson’s leadership has been called into question; it marks the third discharge petition to overrule him this year alone. A pattern has emerged, one that undermines the very fabric of his authority and raises critical questions about the functionality and unity within the Republican Party under his gavel.

Let’s dissect the mockery that ensued, not just from political adversaries, but from a broad spectrum of public figures. Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez (D-NY) and Rep. Sam Liccardo (D-CA) utilized wit to highlight a universal truth in politics: denial often precedes downfall. Their commentary, likening Johnson’s predicament to infamous historical denials, not only amplifies the severity of Johnson’s loss of control but also underscores the broader implications of such a leadership void in a deeply polarized political climate.

The reference to Richard Nixon’s “I am not a crook” and Christine O’Donnell’s “I’m not a witch” by commentators only deepens the wound, illustrating how Johnson’s denial places him in a gallery of political figures whose denials have become synonymous with their controversies. These comparisons serve not just as political banter but as a critical lens through which we can examine the crumbling facade of Johnson’s leadership.

The avalanche of mockery on social media, highlighted by figures like Mike Nietzel and Chris Cillizza, isn’t merely a collection of snide remarks but a reflection of a broader public skepticism towards Johnson’s grip on power. The quips of “narrator: he has” and “narrator: he never had control to lose it” are not just humorous asides; they’re a scathing indictment of a leadership that appears to be in denial about its own efficacy and authority.

In essence, the fiasco surrounding the discharge petition and the subsequent fallout is a microcosm of a larger issue plaguing Johnson’s speakership. It’s a vivid illustration of a leader seemingly more invested in the optics of control than the actual wielding of influence and unity within his party. As the Republican Party grapples with internal divisions and external challenges, Johnson’s “I have not lost control” may very well be remembered not for its assertiveness, but as a poignant reminder of a leadership that was, perhaps, never as firm as it claimed to be.

In the grand theatre of politics, Johnson’s predicament is not just a personal failure but a cautionary tale of the fragility of political power and the perils of denial. The mockery and criticism, while entertaining to some, underscore a critical juncture for the Republican Party and its leadership. The path forward requires more than mere denials; it demands a reevaluation of leadership strategies, party unity, and, most importantly, a reconciliation with the realities of political dynamics in a divided government.
