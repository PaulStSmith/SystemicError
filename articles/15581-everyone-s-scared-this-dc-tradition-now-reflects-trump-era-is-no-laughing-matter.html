'Everyone's scared': This DC tradition now reflects Trump era is 'no laughing matter'

In 2025, the White House Correspondents' Association (WHCA) is celebrating its 114th anniversary. The organization, founded during Democrat Woodrow Wilson's presidency, was designed to be an alliance of journalists who covered the White House but were independent of it.

The United States has had some controversial presidents since then, from Richard Nixon to Donald Trump. But before Trump, all U.S. presidents attended the WHCA's dinner — an event that goes back to Republican Calvin Coolidge's presidency in 1924.

In an article published on April 26, The Guardian's David Smith describes some of the anxiety surrounding the WHCA's forthcoming 2025 dinner.

"It is no laughing matter," Smith reports. "The annual dinner for journalists who cover the White House is best known for American presidents trying to be funny and comedians trying to be political. But this year's edition will feature neither. Instead, the event in a Downtown Washington hotel on Saturday night will, critics say, resemble something closer to a wake for legacy media still trying to find an effective response to Donald Trump's divide-and-rule tactics and the rise of the MAGA media ecosystem."

Smith adds, "Joe Biden's effort to restore norms included the former president giving humorous speeches at the White House Correspondents' Association (WHCA) annual dinner. But just as in his first term, Trump will not be joining the group he has long branded 'the enemy of the people,' and most of his staff are expected to boycott."

The WHCA invited comedian Amber Ruffin to speak at their 2025 dinner but withdrew the invitation — a move that, Smith notes, is being criticized as an "exercise in capitulation and cowardice" and "a metaphor for the failure of the media to unite around a strategy to push back against Trump's all-out assault."

"Since returning to office," Smith observes, "(Trump) has seized control of the pool of journalists that follows the president, barred the Associated Press news agency from the Oval Office and handed access and prominence to far-right influencers.

READ MORE: 'Everybody is on edge': Trump cuts threaten to dismantle 'godsend' program

Author Sally Quinn, widow of the late Washington Post editor Ben Bradlee, isn't planning to attend this year's WHCA dinner —which, she laments, is taking place during a very dark time in U.S. history.

Smith quotes Quinn as saying, "Everyone's scared. You're scared you're going to get thrown in jail if you write something (Trump) doesn't like, and that's going to happen very soon. Then you have the owners of these news organizations who keep keeling over and bending the knee. So you've got all these people in the media who are quitting in protest. It's a horrible time to be covering Trump."

Quinn added, "If you're a journalist and you want to be on the story, this is the story to cover. But people are not having fun covering it. It's very intense and very upsetting."

Our Analysis:

The WHCA Dinner: A Symbol of Media's Muted Trump Critique?

The White House Correspondents' Association (WHCA), on its 114th anniversary, stands as a testament to the complex dance between the press and the presidency. Yet, in an era where former President Donald Trump's shadow looms large over American politics and media, the WHCA dinner—once a night of jest and journalistic camaraderie—has morphed into a somber reflection of the media's struggle against Trump's "divide-and-rule" tactics.

The Guardian's David Smith paints a grim picture of the current state of the WHCA dinner, an event that was historically marked by humor and political satire. This year, however, it's anticipated to be a stark departure from its jovial predecessors, with neither American presidents attempting humor nor comedians trying their hand at politics. Instead, what awaits is a night that critics argue will "resemble something closer to a wake for legacy media."

Trump's Persistent Shadow

Donald Trump, in his characteristic fashion, has once again chosen to abstain from attending the WHCA dinner, continuing to label the press as "the enemy of the people." His administration, and most notably himself, have been accused of an "all-out assault" on the media, an assault that the media, in return, has seemingly failed to counter effectively.

The decision to retract comedian Amber Ruffin's invitation to the dinner is criticized as "an exercise in capitulation and cowardice." This act alone serves as a poignant metaphor for the media's inability—or perhaps unwillingness—to band together in the face of Trump's relentless attacks.

The Rise of the MAGA Media Ecosystem

Smith's article highlights a worrying trend: the rise of the MAGA media ecosystem and its implications for journalistic integrity and independence. Trump's control over the pool of journalists, the barring of the Associated Press from the Oval Office, and the elevation of far-right influencers are all indicative of a media landscape that is increasingly becoming polarized and manipulated.

A Call to Arms or a Sign of Defeat?

Sally Quinn's lamentation over the current state of media coverage of Trump encapsulates the fear and trepidation that many journalists feel. The notion that writing something disfavorable about Trump could result in imprisonment is not only a stark reminder of the threats to freedom of press but also a call to arms for the media to resist capitulation.

Yet, as Smith's article suggests, the media's response to Trump's tactics has been anything but unified or strong. The withdrawal of Ruffin's invitation and the general anxiety surrounding the WHCA dinner are symptomatic of a larger issue: a media landscape that is struggling to find its footing in the face of authoritarian tendencies.

Conclusion: The Need for a Resolute Media

The WHCA dinner, in its 2025 iteration, is more than just a social event; it is a reflection of the state of American media in the face of political adversity. The media's role as the Fourth Estate—to hold power to account—is more crucial than ever. Yet, as Smith's article underlines, the path forward is fraught with challenges.

It's imperative that the media find a way to reclaim its position as a unified force capable of countering disinformation and authoritarianism. The future of American democracy may very well depend on it. The WHCA dinner should not be a wake for legacy media but a rallying cry for journalistic integrity and resilience.
