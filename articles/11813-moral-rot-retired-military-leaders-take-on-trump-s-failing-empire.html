'Moral rot': Retired military leaders take on Trump's 'failing empire'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 11, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Donald Trump is taking this country down a bad path, according to a couple of retired military leaders.

Retired Air Force officer William J. Astore on Sunday published an article in Salon called, "Our ailing, flailing, failing empire is lashing out," in which he argues, "To fight Trump, tyranny and war, America must have profiles in courage — not cowardice."

"As a retired U.S. Air Force officer, I firmly believe in civilian control of our military. This country should be a nation of laws — not of special interests, oligarchs or kings. Before committing our forces to battle, Congress should always declare war in the name of the people. Our military should indeed be a citizen-soldier force, not an isolated caste driven by a warrior ethos. And above all, the United States should be a republic ruled by law and shaped by sound moral values, not a greed-driven empire fueled by militarism," according to Astore. "Yet when I express such views, I feel like I’m clinging to a belief in the tooth fairy, the Easter Bunny and Santa Claus. It feels idealistic, naïve, even painful to think that way. Yes, I served this country in uniform for 20 years, and now, in the age of Donald Trump, it has, as far as I can tell, thoroughly lost its way. The unraveling began so long ago — most obviously with the disastrous Vietnam War of the 1960s and 1970s, though in truth this country’s imperial desires predated even the Spanish-American War of 1898, stretching back to the wanton suppression of Indigenous peoples as part of its founding and expansion."

He goes on to say that, "Signs of moral rot are everywhere" when it comes to the president.

"Our president, who would like to be known for his budget cuts, nonetheless giddily celebrates a record trillion-dollar war budget. Our secretary of defense gleefully promotes a warrior ethos. Congress almost unanimously supports or acquiesces in the destruction of Gaza. Images from the region resemble bombed-out Stalingrad in 1942 or Berlin in 1945. Meanwhile, for more than two decades now, America’s leaders have claimed to be waging a successful global 'war on terror' even as they fuel terror across the globe. What do they think all those U.S. weapons are for — spreading peace?" Astore asked.

And he's not the only one.

He adds, "For tyranny to be stopped, for a catastrophic war with China (and who knows what else) to be avoided, America must have profiles in courage, not cowardice. Yet even despair is being weaponized. As a retired colonel and friend of mine wrote to me recently: 'I don’t even know where to start anymore, Bill. I have no hope for anything ever improving,'" the veteran wrote. "And don’t think of that despair as incidental or accidental. It’s a distinct feature of the present system of government."

Our Analysis:

A Dissection of the "Ailing, Flailing, Failing Empire"

Retired Air Force officer William J. Astore's recent publication in Salon pulls no punches in its critique of the current trajectory of the United States under the leadership of Donald Trump. Titled "Our ailing, flailing, failing empire is lashing out," Astore's piece serves not just as a condemnation of Trump's administration but as a broader indictment of America's historical and ongoing militarism. Let's slice through the fluff and get to the marrow of Astore's argument, and examine where it stands in the context of reality.

The Ideals vs. The Reality

Astore's lament for the loss of American ideals is palpable. He speaks of a nation that should be ruled by laws, not by oligarchs or the whims of a pseudo-monarch. The picture he paints of a republic driven by sound moral values rather than greed and militarism is a stark contrast to the reality we're living in. However, isn't this contrast something that has been part of the American narrative for much longer than the Trump administration?

The mention of America's "imperial desires" dating back to its suppression of Indigenous peoples and the Spanish-American War of 1898 punctuates a history of expansionism and militarism that predates Trump by centuries. This context is crucial because it shows that while Trump may have exacerbated certain tendencies, he is far from the root cause of America's militaristic ethos.

The Symptoms of Moral Rot

Astore's critique of the current administration centers around what he perceives as signs of moral decay: a president celebrating a trillion-dollar war budget, a secretary of defense promoting a warrior ethos, and a Congress that supports the destruction of Gaza. These are not mere policy disagreements but fundamental moral failings that speak to a deeper sickness within the American polity.

However, one could argue that these symptoms are part of a much larger disease. The "successful global 'war on terror'" that Astore mentions has been anything but, breeding more terror and instability worldwide. This isn't a novel development of the Trump era; it's a continuation of policies that have been in place for decades.

The Need for Courage Over Cowardice

One of the more poignant elements of Astore's article is the call for "profiles in courage" to combat the current tide of tyranny and potential catastrophic war with China. The despair felt by veterans like Astore and his colleague, a retired colonel, is telling. It speaks to a broader disillusionment with the American project and a recognition that the path forward requires more than just a change in leadership; it requires a fundamental shift in values and priorities.

Yet, this call to arms (figuratively speaking) against despair and for courage seems somewhat quixotic in the current environment. How does one foster courage in a system that rewards cowardice? Astore's critique, while sharp and grounded in reality, falls short of providing a clear roadmap for how to achieve this monumental shift.

Weaponized Despair and the System of Governance

The notion that despair has been weaponized by the current system of governance is both fascinating and frightening. It suggests a deliberate strategy to keep the populace disengaged and disillusioned, thereby preventing any substantive change. If despair is a "distinct feature of the present system of government," as Astore suggests, then the task ahead is even more daunting.

In conclusion, while Astore's analysis paints a grim picture of the current state of America, it raises essential questions about the nature of American power and the possibility of reform. The article is a call to action, albeit one that lacks a clear direction. The challenge now is not just to resist the current administration's worst impulses but to envision and work towards a fundamentally different kind of America. One that lives up to the ideals that Astore, and countless others, have dedicated their lives to serving.
