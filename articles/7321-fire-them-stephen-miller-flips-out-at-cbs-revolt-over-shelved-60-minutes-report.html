'Fire them!' Stephen Miller flips out at CBS 'revolt' over shelved '60 Minutes' report

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 25, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:Anatomy of Censorship and the Iron Grip of Political Agendas

White House deputy chief of staff for policy Stephen Miller attends the annual White House Easter Egg Roll with his family, including his wife, Katie Miller, left, on the South Lawn of the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., April 21, 2025. REUTERS/Leah Millis

Senior White House adviser and speechwriter Stephen Miller blew a gasket on Fox News Tuesday, amid reporting of internal anger at CBS after newly-installed right-wing network chief Bari Weiss put a hold on a long-in-the-works “60 Minutes” investigation into the horrific conditions at the Salvadoran CECOT megaprison where President Donald Trump has shipped hundreds of migrants.

“Every one of those producers at ‘60 Minutes’ engaged in this revolt, fire them,” shouted Miller, an anti-immigrant fanatic known to crib Nazi Germany in his speeches. “Clean house, fire them. That’s what I say.”

Weiss, who was appointed as part of a Trump administration-approved merger deal between CBS’ parent company Paramount and Skydance, claimed the story needed further review because the Trump administration had not provided comment, even though they were afforded the opportunity to, and because the story didn’t give equal billing to claims by the Trump administration that the mass deportation flights were legal, even though the Supreme Court has already ruled unanimously they were not.

This decision triggered an outcry by “60 Minutes” staff, with CBS anchor Scott Pelley pointing out that Weiss had not even properly reviewed the segment before blocking it, and Sharyn Alfonsi, the reporter behind the story, calling out Weiss’ logic, saying, “Government silence is a statement, not a VETO. If the administration’s refusal to participate becomes a valid reason to spike a story, we have effectively handed them a ‘kill switch’‘ for any reporting they find inconvenient.”

Weiss has fired back, saying that “The only newsroom that I’m interested in running is one where we are able to have contentious disagreements about the thorniest of editorial matters, and do so with respect.”

Shortly after the reports that the “60 Minutes” segment had been blocked, it was actually leaked online after it accidentally was aired on a Canadian network. The segment features a number of interviews with migrants who were locked in torturous environments, including at least one person who evidently had not committed any crime or broken any U.S. law before being rounded up.

Our Analysis:

Anatomy of Censorship and the Iron Grip of Political Agendas

In an alarming display of authoritarian fervor, Stephen Miller, a senior White House adviser and speechwriter known for his hardline anti-immigration views, went ballistic on Fox News, demanding a purge of ‘60 Minutes’ producers over a shelved investigation. This investigation dared to shine a light on the inhumane conditions of the Salvadoran CECOT megaprison—a facility that has become a dumping ground for migrants as part of President Donald Trump’s controversial policies.

Miller’s explosive reaction is not just a temper tantrum aired on national television; it’s a manifestation of the Trump administration’s broader assault on press freedom and the integrity of journalism. His call to “fire” those involved in the revolt at ‘60 Minutes’ is eerily reminiscent of authoritarian regimes, not a democracy that prides itself on the First Amendment.

Bari Weiss, the newly-installed network chief at CBS, emerges as a central figure in this debacle. Her decision to put a hold on the investigation, under the guise of needing further review and balanced reporting, is a thinly veiled attempt at censorship. Weiss claims the need for equal billing to the Trump administration’s side, despite the Supreme Court’s unanimous ruling against the legality of the mass deportation flights. This is not just editorial caution; it’s a blatant disregard for judicial oversight and a capitulation to political pressure.

The outcry from the ‘60 Minutes’ staff, including notable figures like Scott Pelley and Sharyn Alfonsi, underscores a fundamental crisis within the journalistic profession: the battle between editorial independence and the encroachment of political agendas. Alfonsi’s point that “Government silence is a statement, not a VETO” is a powerful rebuke to Weiss’s rationale and highlights a dangerous precedent where governmental non-cooperation could nullify investigative journalism.

Weiss’s defense, advocating for a newsroom where contentious disagreements are welcome, rings hollow in the face of her actions. Blocking a critical investigation because it doesn’t align with the administrative narrative is not fostering debate; it’s stifling dissenting voices.

The inadvertent airing of the segment on a Canadian network, revealing the harrowing conditions faced by migrants, many of whom had not broken any U.S. laws, is a damning indictment of the administration’s policies. It also underscores the vital role of investigative journalism in exposing truths that power structures would rather keep hidden.

In a world where political figures like Miller can openly advocate for the purging of journalists who challenge the status quo, and network chiefs like Weiss can undermine journalistic integrity, the essence of a free press is under threat. This incident is not just about a blocked investigation; it’s a microcosm of a larger battle for the soul of journalism—a battle where the stakes are nothing less than the preservation of democracy and the right of the public to be informed.

As observers, critics, and participants in this democratic experiment, we must ask ourselves: Will we allow the powerful to dictate the narratives, silencing inconvenient truths? Or will we stand in support of those who dare to speak truth to power, ensuring that journalism remains a bulwark against authoritarianism? The answer to these questions will define the future of our democracy.
