The one word that can completely shatter the GOP

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosOctober 9, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

The word is “corrupt.” AlterNet argues that Democrats should make Republican corruption their central message in the 2026 midterms and the 2028 presidential election, citing an Axios-reported national poll in which a majority of registered voters surveyed reportedly chose “corrupt” to describe Donald Trump.

The article points to Péter Magyar’s anti-corruption campaign against Viktor Orbán’s system in Hungary and invokes movements associated with Alexei Navalny, Cory Aquino and other international figures. It contends that one frame can connect tax policy, deregulation, weakened enforcement, privatization, concentrated media ownership, industry influence and restrictions on voting.

AlterNet presents this as strategic advice, not proof that the strategy would succeed in the United States. It also levels numerous allegations—some attributed, others asserted without supporting evidence in the supplied text—about pardons, insider trading, foreign policy and personal enrichment. Those claims require more substantiation than the article provides.

Who holds the power

The strongest part of the argument is not the slogan. It is the identification of institutional control.

Presidents appoint regulators and judges. Congress writes tax laws, controls agency funding and determines whether enforcement bodies can function. Supreme Court justices define the constitutional boundaries of campaign finance and corporate political power. State governments set election rules. Agencies decide whether concentrated industries will face meaningful scrutiny.

Billionaires and corporations possess enormous leverage, but elected officials and appointees convert that leverage into public policy. Saying that “money controls politics” can become another passive construction that lets the officials taking the votes, making the appointments and weakening the rules disappear. Donors apply pressure; officeholders exercise state power.

Corruption needs evidence

“Corruption” is politically useful because it translates complicated arrangements into an intelligible charge: public authority is being used for private advantage. But the word becomes empty if it merely means every policy the speaker opposes.

A defensible case must name the decision, the beneficiary and the public cost. A regulator weakens a rule affecting a former employer. Legislators preserve an industry’s privileged position after taking its money. Congress strips enforcement capacity from an agency, making misconduct harder to detect. Those are structures readers can examine.

AlterNet weakens its own case when it rushes past that discipline. Its sweeping calculations, criminal implications and claims of corrupt collusion are not established by repetition or indignation. A serious anti-corruption politics cannot demand evidentiary standards from the powerful while relaxing those standards for its own accusations.

The misdirection trap

The article correctly recognizes that debates about affordability, privatization and regulatory failure often stop at visible pain. Voters are told that prices simply rose, agencies somehow failed or public services mysteriously deteriorated. That language conceals decisions.

The relevant question is not merely why an institution performs badly. It is who cut its capacity, who selected its leadership, who benefited from its weakness and who blocked repair. When government is deliberately deprived of staff or authority and the resulting dysfunction is offered as proof that government cannot work, failure becomes a sales pitch for transferring public functions to private interests.

But partisan branding can also become misdirection. Attaching “Republican” to every alleged abuse does not explain the mechanism, establish the allegation or prevent Democrats from tolerating donor influence within their own coalition. If the standard changes with the party of the officeholder, “anti-corruption” is only campaign merchandise.

The sharper indictment

The Republican slogan “drain the swamp” demonstrated the potency of presenting politics as a struggle against self-dealing elites. AlterNet’s central strategic point is that Democrats have often answered this visceral story with a catalog of programs and procedural promises.

The sharper response would connect material consequences to identifiable exercises of power: unaffordable medicine to rules protecting industry revenue; monopoly prices to abandoned competition policy; weak enforcement to budgets and appointments; voter exclusion to statutes enacted by named legislatures and governors.

That is more than a messaging adjustment. It rejects the fiction that harmful outcomes emerge from an ownerless “system.” Systems operate through offices, statutes, rulings, budgets and enforcement choices. Naming those choices makes accountability possible.

The systemic error

Corruption flourishes politically when it is treated only as envelopes of cash or spectacular criminal scandal. Modern influence is frequently exercised through conduct that may be legal: campaign spending, revolving-door appointments, privileged access, regulatory exemptions and legislation tailored to concentrated interests.

That is the larger pattern AlterNet is reaching for, even when its evidentiary discipline falters. Public power can be redirected toward private accumulation without every participant committing a prosecutable offense. The consequence is a government that remains formally public while becoming increasingly responsive to those wealthy enough to shape its choices.

“Corruption” can expose that arrangement, but only if it names power precisely. Otherwise it becomes another magic word—emotionally potent, institutionally vague and available to the very people profiting from the system it claims to condemn.
