Antisemitism comment jolts CNN's Harry Enten into angry eruption

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 8, 20255 min read

Solomon Jones , harry Enten (CVNN screenshot)

A comment by Philadelphia Inquirer columnist Solomon Jones as he battled with conservative Scott Jennings ended up angering CNN's normally unflappable Harry Enten on Saturday morning.

During a discussion on CNN's "Table for Five" over the Donald Trump administration's use of antisemitism as a pretext to continue his assault on higher education, Jones and Jennings went back and forth on the president's plans to punish California for ignoring his executive orders.

That led Jones to comment, "How do you, as the president, target an entire state because you disagree with two things that happened there. That makes no sense to me, and I don't think it should make sense to any of us."

"Because antisemitism should not be condoned," Jennings replied.

"Why is antisemitism the only prejudice that matters? Why does it feel like that?" Jones shot back.

That was when Enten entered the fray.

After some overtalk, he exclaimed, "I got to tell you, you know, I'm a Jewish-American, I've been so my entire life. I'm not exactly the most religious guy in the world but I will tell you, as a Jewish-American, I have been so sick of every single time that there's been hate against Jewish folks that all of a sudden, well, the hate against other minorities is just as bad, and we should elevate that as well."

"I'm not saying that we shouldn't talk about it, but sometimes it is okay just to talk about antisemitism," he added.

"It is okay to talk about it, but it's not okay to target an entire state and say that the only thing that we're targeting you for is antisemitism and what happened with a transgender athlete," Jones explained. "I think that there are other forms of prejudice that matter. I think that there are other forms of racism that matter."

"Certainly," Enten agreed.

"I think that there are other things that matter, and it feels like everything is kind of centered on –– in fact, the trump administration feels like they're weaponizing antisemitism in a way against those who disagree with it," the columnist finished.

"I'll just finish quickly," Enten replied. "I would just say, I just think it is nice that we're really actually focusing on antisemitism for a change in this country and talking about that. We do have a real issue and that there is real Jewish hate on the rise, and we all need to address it."

Our Analysis:

A Critical Dissection: The CNN Clash and the Weaponization of Antisemitism

In a recent eruption on CNN's "Table for Five," what could have been a productive discussion on antisemitism and prejudice was instead overshadowed by the all-too-common spectacle of political pundits talking past each other. Let's dissect the key moments and the profound implications of this televised tussle.

The Spark: Antisemitism as a Political Tool

At the heart of the matter was the Trump administration's alleged use of antisemitism as a pretext for its ongoing assault on higher education, specifically targeting California for not adhering to executive orders. Solomon Jones, a columnist for the Philadelphia Inquirer, challenged the rationale behind targeting an entire state over isolated incidents, highlighting the absurdity of such a broad stroke approach. Scott Jennings' response, that antisemitism should not be condoned, was a glaring oversimplification of Jones' critique.

The Escalation: Selective Prejudice Concerns

Jones' rebuttal, questioning why antisemitism seems to be the only form of prejudice that garners attention, strikes at the core of a larger issue. It's a poignant reminder of how society prioritizes certain types of discrimination over others, often based on political convenience rather than a genuine commitment to eradicating hate. Harry Enten's interjection, though emotionally charged and personal, inadvertently underscored this problem. By insisting on the exclusivity of discussing antisemitism, Enten, perhaps unknowingly, played into the very dynamic Jones criticized.

The Missed Opportunity: A Narrow Focus on Antisemitism

Enten's frustration is understandable. The rise of antisemitism is a grave concern that deserves focused attention. However, the insistence on discussing antisemitism in isolation, especially in a political context where it's being weaponized, is problematic. It creates a hierarchy of hate, where the suffering of one group is amplified above others, not for the sake of genuine concern, but for political expediency.

Jones' argument that the Trump administration is weaponizing antisemitism against its critics is a critical observation. It's a tactic that not only seeks to silence dissent but also muddies the waters of genuine discourse on prejudice. Enten's final remark, emphasizing the importance of focusing on antisemitism, falls into this trap. It suggests a singular focus in a world where multiple forms of prejudice often intersect and reinforce each other.

The Bigger Picture: Weaponization of Prejudice

This televised exchange is a microcosm of a larger, more insidious trend in political discourse: the weaponization of prejudice. By selectively amplifying certain forms of hate while ignoring or minimizing others, administrations and their critics can manipulate public sentiment for or against specific policies or groups. This not only dilutes the fight against all forms of hate but also divides communities that should be standing together against discrimination.

Conclusion: The Path Forward

The real tragedy of this CNN segment is not just the missed opportunity for a nuanced discussion on antisemitism and other forms of prejudice. It's a stark reminder of how easily important conversations can be co-opted by political agendas, leaving the root causes of hate unaddressed.

To move forward, it's imperative that we reject the weaponization of prejudice in all its forms. Only then can we hope to address the systemic issues that give rise to hate, rather than merely treating its symptoms based on political convenience. It's not just about talking the talk; it's about walking the walk—collectively and inclusively.
