Nobel economist turns Trump’s disaster excuse against him

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 17, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Donald Trump blamed Joe Biden for the nationwide air-travel breakdown that grounded departures for nearly five hours on Nov. 13. Economist Paul Krugman turned that excuse back on the administration now controlling the Federal Aviation Administration: Trump paused infrastructure spending that included billions for the agency, while Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy accelerated an air-traffic-control overhaul despite warnings that the effort lacked basic planning.

The precise cause of the outage remains under investigation. But the institutional failures surrounding it belong to the officials currently exercising power, not to the former president Trump invoked after thousands of flights were canceled.

A national breakdown, followed by a political diversion

The FAA temporarily halted departures nationwide in what CNBC described as the largest grounding of US flights since Sept. 11, 2001. FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford said the disruption affected 12,000 flights. FlightAware recorded more than 6,400 cancellations on Saturday, followed by roughly 1,500 more on Sunday as the system recovered. Krugman, stranded in Chicago, arrived home a day late.

Trump responded Sunday on Truth Social by declaring that the Biden administration “was an FAA disaster” and asking Congress for additional air-traffic-control funding. The post assigned blame backward while directing the remedy toward lawmakers, neatly avoiding the decisions made by Trump and his own appointees.

Krugman rejected that account in a Substack post, writing: “It wasn’t weather, it wasn’t a terrorist attack, it was simple incompetence.” His argument was not that investigators had already identified which official caused the equipment failure. It was that the administration had made a fragile system more vulnerable while treating speed as a substitute for competent management.

Trump froze money; Duffy accelerated the overhaul

Biden signed the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law in 2021, including $25 billion for the FAA. Shortly after returning to office, Trump signed an executive order pausing spending under that law. His demand for more money after the outage therefore omitted a pertinent fact: his administration had already interrupted congressionally approved infrastructure funding.

Duffy, meanwhile, has pushed an expedited transformation of the air-traffic-control system. According to Krugman, the transportation secretary proceeded without adequately consulting air-traffic workers or respecting warnings about the risks.

The Government Accountability Office supplied one such warning about two months before the outage. Reviewing the FAA modernization initiative launched in May 2025, the nonpartisan watchdog said the agency “could be prioritizing speed over costs and air traffic disruptions.” It found that the program lacked both a lifecycle cost estimate and a master schedule.

Those are not ornamental planning documents. A lifecycle estimate establishes what a project is likely to cost beyond its opening phase; a master schedule maps the dependencies and timing needed to replace critical infrastructure without disabling it. Racing ahead without either is an administrative choice, not an inheritance.

Krugman also noted that Duffy voted against FAA funding legislation in 2019 while serving as a Wisconsin congressman. After the outage, Duffy reportedly used his press conference to blame Democrats and praise Trump. That record makes the administration’s presentation especially evasive: officials who opposed or interrupted funding now depict themselves as rescuers from a failure assigned to people no longer running the government.

The failed equipment does not erase the policy failure

Federal officials traced the incident to a router installed by contractor L3Harris at a radio site in El Dorado, Arkansas, and another device in Amarillo, Texas. The router’s manufacturer said that, based on the information available to it, the equipment operated as intended. Investigators had not established the precise cause when AlterNet published its account.

Krugman said he had no particular reason to reject the official explanation. Nor does a hardware-level account resolve the larger question. Critical systems are supposed to withstand isolated equipment trouble through redundancy, maintenance, controlled transitions and experienced personnel. Finding the component associated with an outage does not explain why its failure—or the failure of the system around it—could paralyze departures across the country.

The FAA’s dependence on technology so old that it does not expect to eliminate floppy disks until 2028 illustrates the accumulated vulnerability. Krugman traces that deterioration to a Republican governing tradition, beginning with Ronald Reagan, that disparages public administration and then starves the services Americans still depend upon.

Trump added a more aggressive version through the Department of Government Efficiency. According to Krugman, DOGE eliminated or pushed out about 13 percent of the federal workforce within months, without consulting relevant experts. The supplied reporting does not establish that those personnel losses caused this particular outage. It does establish the governing posture surrounding it: dismantle capacity quickly, disregard institutional knowledge and blame the predecessor when a public system breaks.

The router investigation may eventually identify a narrower technical cause. It cannot make Biden responsible for Trump’s spending pause, Duffy’s accelerated modernization effort or the administration’s response after the grounding. The immediate malfunction remains under review; the attempt to redirect accountability is already in plain view.
