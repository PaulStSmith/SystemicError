Trump sics prosecutors on hush money trial judge's daughter: 'Outrageous abuse of power'

Trump Turns Federal Prosecutors Into a Revenge Squad

What the story is really about

The reporting is not about a stray campaign finance curiosity. It is about a president using federal prosecutors to pressure the family of the judge who oversaw his criminal hush money trial. A year after a jury found Trump guilty, the U.S. Attorney’s Office in Chicago spent months digging into Loren Merchan’s work for a Democratic fundraising firm and demanding records from Authentic Campaigns, including communications tied to Harris’s campaign.

Power, not mystery

The relevant power here sits with Trump and the Justice Department he commands. The source makes clear that U.S. attorneys, once insulated from day-to-day political vendettas, are being pulled into Trump’s effort to target adversaries. That is the point. This is not a neutral inquiry drifting through the system on its own. It is state power aimed downward at a judge’s family and outward at political opponents.

The target is the judge

The Merchan family has been a recurring target of Trump and his allies, who pushed unsubstantiated claims that the judge was biased and that his daughter profited from the trial. That framing matters because it functions as a smokescreen. Instead of confronting a criminal conviction, Trump’s orbit tries to recast accountability as a scandal about the people enforcing it. The move is familiar: attack the referee, then pretend the problem is the whistle.

The fishing expedition is the message

According to the reporting, prosecutors wanted records from internal communications, asked about a $468 Harris campaign payment, and sought emails between Loren Merchan and her father. Those details do not read like a serious public corruption case. They read like a search for leverage, embarrassment, and maybe a pretext. Mike Nellis called it an “outrageous abuse of power,” and that is the plainest description available from the source.

The function of intimidation

This is how authoritarian politics normalizes itself: not only by punishing enemies, but by making the punishment look procedural. A prosecutor’s letter, a records demand, an open-ended “inquiry” that may or may not go anywhere. The cruelty is bureaucratic on purpose. It tells judges, staffers, donors, and political consultants that proximity to Trump’s enemies can become a federal problem.

The pattern beneath the headline

The larger pattern is the conversion of law enforcement into personal counterattack. Trump does not just want loyalists in office; he wants institutions that can be bent into instruments of grievance. If Democrats and other defenders of democracy treat this as mere scandal management, they will miss the core fact: the state is being taught to serve retaliation. That is not drift. It is governing style.
