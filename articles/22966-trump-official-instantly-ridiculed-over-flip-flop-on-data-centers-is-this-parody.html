Trump official instantly ridiculed over flip-flop on data centers: ‘Is this parody?’

Lutnick’s Water Denial Is What Power Sounds Like When It Thinks You Won’t Check

The Lie Was Immediate

Howard Lutnick, the Commerce Secretary, went on CNBC and claimed data centers do not use water. The Department of Energy says the opposite: many do, and cooling systems can consume large amounts through evaporation. His old Fox News clip saying AI systems “suck water” surfaced almost instantly, turning the episode into a live demonstration of elite contempt for basic facts.

This was not confusion. It was a minister of the state talking down the obvious because power often assumes repetition can outrun reality.

Who Actually Decides

Lutnick is not a random pundit. He is a cabinet official speaking for an administration that wants the expansion of data centers to look clean, frictionless, and inevitable. That means the relevant power is institutional: the Commerce Department, the energy apparatus, and the political coalition treating AI infrastructure as untouchable growth.

The people who bear the water burden are not the ones setting the terms. Communities, utilities, and local governments absorb the consequences while federal officials sell the project as if cooling, power draw, and environmental strain are side issues. They are not side issues. They are the bill.

Misdirection As Method

Lutnick’s “propaganda by our adversaries” line is the tell. When facts become inconvenient, the instinct is not correction but militarized nonsense: blame outsiders, frame criticism as sabotage, and recast a material problem as ideological warfare.

That is how public debate gets polluted. The issue is not whether data centers use water. They do. The issue is that a cabinet secretary tried to deny a visible, documented reality rather than admit that the AI boom carries costs. Denial is not a lapse here. It is political strategy.

The Real Pattern

This is a familiar shape in U.S. governance: privatize the upside, externalize the damage, then insult anyone who names the damage. The people making money from infrastructure expansion get optimism and subsidies. Everyone else gets heat, strain, and a lecture about progress.

The ridicule Lutnick received is deserved, but the joke is broader than one bad clip. It is that a governing class can be this unserious about material consequences and still expect deference. When officials lie this casually about water, they are not just insulting the public. They are advertising how little they intend to be constrained by it.

The System Behind the Sentence

The sentence “data centers don’t use water” matters because it reveals the political function of modern technocracy: not to clarify the world, but to launder coercive decisions through polished incompetence. The point is not accuracy. The point is to keep expansion moving while pretending the costs are unreal, temporary, or somebody else’s problem.

That is the governing model. Facts become negotiable. Consequences become invisible. And the people with the most institutional power talk as if basic physics were a partisan rumor.
