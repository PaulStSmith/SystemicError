The comet has hit: Former party bigwig on the extinction of the old GOP

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 7, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Former New Jersey governor and EPA administrator Christine Todd Whitman told AlterNet that the Republican Party she left in 2022 is unlikely to return after Donald Trump leaves its leadership. Her answer to the headline’s extinction metaphor is blunt: the old GOP is gone because, in her assessment, the party has become a “cult” whose positions change with Trump’s commands rather than follow a stable platform.

Whitman contrasted that structure with the Republican tradition she identifies with—balanced budgets, alliances, science, refugee protection and negotiated problem-solving. She argued that public frustration with an unresponsive two-party system created an opening for Trump, while Republican officials surrendered their principles instead of constraining him.

Whitman is now building the Forward Party as an alternative. At the time of the interview, she said it had more than 80 officeholders and candidates for the Senate, House, governorships and other offices. AlterNet did not establish that this effort can displace either major party; it presented Whitman’s account of its organization and ambitions.

Power did not disappear

Calling the Republican Party a cult describes its obedience but can obscure who exercises power. Trump sets the line. Republican officeholders, party organizations and aligned media figures enforce it. Candidates learn that dissent can bring a primary opponent, lost support or political exile.

Those officials are not merely confused followers. They possess votes, offices, budgets and procedural authority. Every time they adopt a new position because Trump demands it, they make a choice about how to use institutional power. Personal domination succeeds because people with formal authority repeatedly convert one man’s preferences into party policy.

The convenient obituary

The “old GOP” deserves less sentimental treatment than AlterNet’s framing gives it. The article presents Whitman’s 1993 campaign as honorable, restrained and focused on taxes, spending and jobs. That may distinguish her rhetoric from Trump’s, but decorum is not a governing philosophy, and civility does not establish that policy consequences were fair.

The sharper point is not that Republicans were once good and then inexplicably became cruel. It is that a party accustomed to hierarchy, elite control and punitive enforcement proved vulnerable to a leader who discarded its euphemisms. Trump did not abolish the machinery of partisan discipline. He captured it and made loyalty to himself its organizing principle.

Frustration is not agency

Whitman says voters were understandably angry because Congress had stopped solving major problems, and that Trump successfully promised to “blow it up.” That explains an opening; it does not transfer responsibility for what followed onto an undifferentiated public.

Citizens cast ballots, but officeholders decide whether to resist abuses, enable them or profit politically from them. Party leaders control endorsements and resources. Legislators exercise public authority. Media institutions decide whom to amplify. Blaming “polarization,” loud voters or generalized frustration can turn a chain of identifiable decisions into political weather.

Whitman herself identifies the coercive mechanism: major parties can withdraw support and sponsor challengers against dissenters. That is organized power, not spontaneous extremism from below.

Centrism is not a substitute for leverage

The Forward Party’s promises—constitutional government, lawful voting access, pluralism and cooperation—describe minimum obligations of public office. Their necessity is an indictment of the existing system, but stating them does not answer how a third party overcomes the institutions that protect the two-party monopoly.

Whitman’s reported roster of candidates and officeholders demonstrates persistence, not yet governing power. The article accepts the “boldly centrist” label too easily and does not test what the party would do when compromise collides with rights, material interests or authoritarian conduct. A safe space for disagreement is useful only if it does not become shelter for officials avoiding consequential choices.

The system rewards surrender

This story is larger than the extinction of a Republican faction. It shows how democratic institutions can remain formally intact while their occupants abandon independent judgment. A party need not publish an authoritarian doctrine when obedience itself supplies the doctrine.

Trump holds the commanding position, but his dominance depends on officials and institutions that still possess agency. The old GOP was not struck by a comet. Its leaders repeatedly traded principle for access, office and protection from their own voters. Extinction is too passive a word for a process carried out through thousands of voluntary acts of surrender.
