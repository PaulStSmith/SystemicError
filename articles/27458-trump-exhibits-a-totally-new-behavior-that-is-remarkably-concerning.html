Trump 'exhibits a totally new behavior' that is 'remarkably concerning'

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 25, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Looking for an attorney general in plain sightThe administration’s power makes the question urgentA spectacle built to redirect scrutiny

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump appeared unable to recognize Attorney General Todd Blanche while speaking at the Justice Department on Monday, even after looking directly at him. Clinical and forensic psychologist John Paul Garrison called the episode a “totally new behavior” and “remarkably concerning,” distinguishing it from Trump’s familiar trouble recalling names. That assessment is not a diagnosis, but the underlying question is inescapably political: whether the president can reliably identify one of the most powerful officials in his own administration.

Trump had appeared with Blanche and FBI Director Kash Patel to attack California Gov. Gavin Newsom over the commutation of 548 death-row sentences. Yet the event meant to project federal command instead produced a scene in which Trump seemed uncertain about who was standing behind him.

Looking for an attorney general in plain sight

According to AlterNet’s account of Garrison’s video analysis, Trump entered to “Hail to the Chief,” shook Blanche’s hand and appeared to thank him—possibly while calling him “Bob.” Trump later thanked Blanche by name, turned around and looked toward him, then asked, “Where is he?”

“He’s around. He’s around here someplace,” Trump said.

“I’m right here,” Blanche replied, raising his hand.

Trump continued: “I can’t — I want to say hello to Todd. He’s special. He works all the time.”

Garrison’s interpretation was that Trump looked directly at Blanche but did not recognize him. “There’s nothing about that interaction that makes me think that he knew he was talking to Todd Blanche,” he said.

A video interpretation cannot establish dementia or any other neurological condition, and Garrison did not examine Trump in the account AlterNet published. He said such difficulty recognizing a familiar face can occur with dementia or other neurological dysfunction. The observable conduct and the clinical explanation must remain separate: the event supplies grounds for scrutiny, not a medical verdict.

The administration’s power makes the question urgent

Blanche is not a ceremonial aide whose face a president might reasonably lose in a crowd. He is the attorney general, the official at the top of the Justice Department, and he was appearing beside Trump at the department itself. If Trump truly failed to recognize him moments after an apparent greeting, the significance lies less in the awkwardness than in the authority both men wield.

The president directs the executive branch. The attorney general oversees federal law enforcement. Confusion at that level cannot be reduced to an eccentric performance or another clip in the permanent spectacle surrounding Trump. The relevant issue is functional capacity inside institutions empowered to prosecute people, enforce federal policy and deploy the state’s coercive machinery.

Garrison described Trump as “deceptively low-functioning,” arguing that years of unusual speech and behavior have lowered public sensitivity to new warning signs. That is Garrison’s judgment, but it identifies a real mechanism of political misdirection: repetition can make conduct seem ordinary without making it harmless.

A spectacle built to redirect scrutiny

Trump’s chosen subject was Newsom’s exercise of clemency in California. The intended arrangement was familiar: Trump would denounce a Democratic governor, invoke violent crime and place himself at the center of a punitive law-and-order display.

But Newsom does not control the federal executive branch. Trump does. Whatever political argument Trump wanted to make about California’s death-row commutations, it does not answer questions raised by his own performance while flanked by the officials carrying out federal power.

AlterNet also cited biographer Michael Wolff, who said the previous week on the Daily Beast show Trump’s Brain that Trump had difficulty recognizing a reference to Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. Wolff’s account is another attributed observation, not clinical proof. In combination with the Justice Department episode, however, it makes dismissal less responsible than examination.

The most consequential response would not come from online commentators or body-language analysts. It would come from the senior officials who work with Trump and can observe his functioning directly. Blanche and Patel were present. They hold institutional authority, proximity and access to information the public lacks. Treating an apparent failure to recognize the attorney general as merely another Trump oddity would protect the administration from scrutiny while leaving the public to absorb the risk.
