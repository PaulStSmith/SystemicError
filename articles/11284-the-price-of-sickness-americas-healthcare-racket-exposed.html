The Price of Sickness: America’s Healthcare Racket Exposed

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 24, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

America’s healthcare system is a grotesque spectacle—a Rube Goldberg machine of profit extraction that disguises itself as medicine. Year after year, politicians, insurers, and pharmaceutical giants tell the public that universal healthcare is a fantasy, “too expensive,” or a step toward tyranny. But the raw arithmetic tells a different story: the U.S. government already spends enough to cover every single American. The problem isn’t the size of the bill—it’s who gets paid.

The Numbers Don’t Lie, but the System Does

Let’s anchor ourselves in hard data. In 2016, the U.S. federal government spent roughly:

  • $588 billion on Medicare (healthcare for seniors and the disabled).

  • $363 billion on Medicaid (healthcare for the poor, with costs shared between states and the feds).

  • $60–63 billion on Veterans’ healthcare under the VA system.

That’s just over $1 trillion in federal healthcare spending in a single year. That trillion covered veterans, seniors, and the poorest Americans—but left everyone else at the mercy of the insurance cartels.

Now compare that to what a scaled-up, U.K.-style National Health Service would cost. NHS England’s 2016–17 budget was about £117 billion. Adjust for the U.S. population—roughly five times the size of England’s—and you’re looking at £577 billion, or around $750–800 billion USD at 2016 exchange rates. In other words: the U.S. federal government was already spending 200–250 billion dollars more than it would take to provide cradle-to-grave care to everyone.

The claim that universal healthcare is unaffordable is not just dishonest—it’s obscene.

Paying More, Getting Less

The scandal doesn’t stop at federal spending. In 2016, private households and employers shelled out another $1.8 trillion in premiums, co-pays, deductibles, and direct out-of-pocket payments. Every one of those dollars represents rationing by wallet: a cancer patient skipping treatment, a diabetic stretching insulin, a family bankrupted by an unexpected surgery. The cruel irony is that Americans pay more privately for the “privilege” of being underinsured than citizens in other developed nations pay in taxes for full coverage.

What this means is staggering: Americans already bankroll universal healthcare several times over. They just don’t receive it.

Where the Money Goes: Rent-Seeking and Waste

So why doesn’t $1 trillion in public money plus $1.8 trillion in private spending buy universal coverage?

Because the system isn’t designed to deliver care. It’s designed to deliver profits.

  1. Insurance middlemen: Private insurers skim off billions in administrative costs, marketing, and shareholder dividends. The U.S. spends around 8% of health dollars on administration, compared to the 2–3% typical of national health systems.

  2. Fragmentation: Thousands of insurers, each with their own billing codes, networks, and forms, create a bureaucratic labyrinth that costs providers and patients time and money.

  3. Pharmaceutical markups: Americans are charged two to ten times what other countries pay for the exact same drugs. Why? Because the government refuses to negotiate prices on behalf of its people.

  4. Hospital profiteering: “Non-profit” hospitals rake in billions while suing patients for unpaid bills and paying their executives seven-figure salaries.

All of this waste represents money that could have gone directly to patient care—but instead lines the pockets of executives, investors, and lobbyists.

The Manufactured Myth of Scarcity

The most perverse aspect of this racket is the myth it sustains: that universal healthcare is “too expensive.” That story keeps Americans divided and demoralized, convinced they must choose between coverage and financial solvency. It’s a lie, and the data proves it.

The reality is that the U.S. already spends more per capita in public healthcare dollars alone than countries like the U.K., Canada, or Japan spend to cover everyone. The difference is that those countries spend with purpose. America spends with abandon, and the beneficiaries are not patients but corporations.

The Human Cost

It’s easy to get lost in the billions and trillions, but behind every figure is a life distorted by a system that prizes profit over health.

  • A parent watching their child suffer because the “affordable” plan they could afford doesn’t cover the right specialist.

  • A veteran denied timely care despite billions supposedly earmarked for their wellbeing.

  • A senior splitting pills because Medicare doesn’t cover the full cost of prescriptions.

  • Families torn apart by medical bankruptcy—the leading cause of personal bankruptcy in the U.S., unheard of in countries with universal systems.

This isn’t just inefficient. It’s barbaric.

The Ideological Smoke Screen

How does such a broken system persist? The answer is political will—or rather, the deliberate lack of it. For decades, Americans have been fed propaganda equating healthcare reform with communism. Ronald Reagan himself warned in a 1961 recording that Medicare would lead to the destruction of American freedom. The fear-mongering worked. Medicare passed, but the broader dream of universal care was buried under Cold War paranoia.

Even the Affordable Care Act—hailed as the most significant reform in generations—was a half-measure that doubled down on the role of private insurers rather than phasing them out. It expanded coverage but cemented the middlemen.

Meanwhile, the health insurance and pharmaceutical lobbies spend hundreds of millions each year ensuring that serious reforms never see the light of day. They don’t fear government inefficiency—they fear government efficiency, because an efficient system would cut them out.

The Global Contrast

Every other developed democracy has faced the same choice: whether to treat healthcare as a public good or as a commodity. They chose the former. The results are indisputable:

  • Life expectancy is higher in countries with universal systems.

  • Infant mortality is lower.

  • Administrative costs are a fraction of those in the U.S.

  • And no one is forced to choose between bankruptcy and survival.

The U.S. stands alone as the world’s richest nation and the only advanced economy that treats healthcare as a luxury.

A Different Future Is Possible

If America redirected even a portion of what it already spends into a streamlined public system, the benefits would be immediate and profound:

  • Universal coverage without the premiums, deductibles, and copays that strangle household budgets.

  • Lower costs overall, as administrative waste and profiteering are stripped away.

  • Better health outcomes, as preventive care becomes accessible to everyone, not just the wealthy or lucky.

  • Economic security, as families are freed from the shadow of medical bankruptcy and businesses are relieved of the burden of providing insurance.

This isn’t utopian dreaming. It’s the lived reality of every other wealthy democracy.

The Real Question

So the debate isn’t whether America can afford universal healthcare. The debate is whether America can afford to keep throwing trillions at a system that fails its people. The answer is already visible in every medical GoFundMe campaign, every insulin rationing tragedy, every preventable death. The system as it stands is unsustainable, unjust, and inhumane.

The arithmetic is clear: the U.S. could cover everyone for less than it spends now. The persistence of the current system is not about cost—it’s about power. Power wielded by corporations that profit from misery, and by politicians too cowardly or complicit to challenge them.

Conclusion: The Price of Inaction

Healthcare in America isn’t broken. It’s functioning exactly as designed: a wealth transfer from the sick to the powerful. But design can be changed. The numbers show that universal healthcare isn’t just possible—it’s cheaper, smarter, and more humane. What stands in the way isn’t money. It’s political will.

Until that changes, Americans will keep paying more and getting less—proof that in the richest country on earth, the greatest sickness of all is greed.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
