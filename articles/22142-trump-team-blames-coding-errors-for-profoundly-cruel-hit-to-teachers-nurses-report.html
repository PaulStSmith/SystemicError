Trump team blames 'coding errors' for 'profoundly cruel' hit to teachers, nurses: report

Based on an article by Bennito L. Kelty

Paulo SantosAugust 20, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump Is Using Bureaucratic Fog to Steal Time From Public Servants

The Power Is Not in the Error

The article’s basic facts are not complicated: the Trump Education Department is rolling back student loan credit for thousands of borrowers in Public Service Loan Forgiveness, hitting teachers, nurses, and nonprofit workers who had been counting toward discharge. The department says “coding errors” are to blame, and it has not even said how many people are affected.

That is the surface event. The political event is simpler: the administration has the power, and it chose to use it against borrowers.

Who Actually Made the Decision

This is not a natural disaster and not some abstract systems failure. A federal department decided to yank credits from people who were told those credits counted. The department then wrapped that decision in the language of cleanup and correction, as if the state were merely tidying a spreadsheet.

That framing matters because it tries to relocate responsibility away from the current government. But the current government is the one doing the taking. If there were inherited data problems, the Trump administration still chose the punitive response. It chose to make borrowers absorb the cost of institutional incompetence.

“Coding Errors” as Political Cover

“Coding errors” is doing ideological work here. It turns a choice into a glitch. It turns administration into technical maintenance. It suggests there is no one to blame except the machine itself.

There is always someone to blame. In this case, it is the department that decided borrowers should pay for the state’s mess. That is not neutral correction. That is governance by attrition: delay, confuse, reduce the count, and let people lose months or years on the path to forgiveness. The cruelty is not incidental to the process. It is the process.

Borrowers Are Not the Error

The article quotes Julia Barnard calling this “profoundly cruel,” and she is right for the right reason: borrowers had nothing to do with the mistake and are being punished anyway. Randi Weingarten’s warning is even more concrete. Borrowers who were near the finish line are now being forced to pay more and postpone their lives.

That is the actual harm. Not a spreadsheet inconsistency. Not an administrative hiccup. The harm is that public servants planned around a promise the government is now backing away from. Teachers, nurses, and nonprofit workers do not get to outsource the consequences of federal incompetence. The department does.

What the Story Really Shows

The deeper pattern is familiar: the state offers a benefit, then treats access to that benefit as a privilege to be revoked whenever power wants room to maneuver. The people who do socially necessary work are asked to trust a system that keeps changing the rules after they have already done the work.

That is not merely bureaucratic failure. It is a political habit. Credit the vulnerable when it is cheap, claw it back when it is useful, and call it a correction when it is actually a transfer of risk downward.

The Larger Lesson

A department willing to punish public service workers over its own counting problems is telling you what it thinks government is for. Not to keep promises. Not to stabilize ordinary lives. To discipline those with the least leverage while shielding the people who made the mess.

The story is not about coding. It is about who gets to make errors and who gets made to pay for them.
