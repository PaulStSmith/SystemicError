A French reporter's perfect explanation of America just came true

Based on an article by D. Earl Stephens

Paulo SantosNovember 7, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet columnist D. Earl Stephens argues that the 2026 election fulfilled a French reporter’s 2008 description of Americans as capable of doing “the stupidest things ever” and then “the greatest things ever.” The reporter’s remark contrasted George W. Bush’s reelection with Barack Obama’s election; Stephens applies it to voters’ rejection of Donald Trump’s Republican Party.

Stephens says Democrats won the House, Senate, numerous governorships and state and local offices, while outperforming Republicans nationally by roughly 13 percentage points. He reports Democratic victories in Texas, Wisconsin, Michigan, Pennsylvania, South Carolina and other states, with some votes still being counted when he wrote.

This is celebratory commentary, not a documented election analysis. It asserts a sweeping Democratic realignment and lists results, but supplies no official returns, outside verification or explanation of how its national comparison was calculated.

The voters acted; institutions still hold power

The electorate made the immediate decision described here. Black voters—especially Black women, whom Stephens says backed Democrats by roughly 90% to 10%—formed a central part of the coalition. Democratic candidates then converted those votes into institutional control.

That distinction matters. Voters can remove officeholders, but elected officials acquire the power to write laws, confirm nominees, control budgets and administer states. Winning is not liberation by itself. It is the transfer of governing authority to people who must now decide whether to use it.

Representation is not a governing program

Stephens emphasizes the coalition’s racial and religious diversity: a Black governor-elect in Wisconsin, a Muslim senator-elect in Michigan, a Jewish governor in Pennsylvania, Latina Gina Hinojosa in Texas, Keisha Lance Bottoms in Georgia and Deb Haaland in New Mexico.

Those victories can dismantle entrenched barriers to office. But identity does not disclose what an official will do with power. A roster of historic firsts is evidence of broader access to institutions, not proof that those institutions will deliver material change. The article celebrates who entered the room while largely avoiding what they intend to do there.

The landslide framing outruns the evidence

Stephens treats Texas turning purple as proof that Republicans may never win another presidential election “in our lifetimes.” That conclusion does not follow from one election, however decisive. Coalitions move, turnout changes, candidates differ and state-level results do not mechanically determine later presidential contests.

The same problem infects the piece’s larger claim that Democrats won “the hearts of most of America.” Elections allocate offices; they do not reveal a permanent national soul. Turning a strong result into historical destiny flatters the victors and lowers scrutiny precisely when scrutiny becomes useful.

Celebration can misdirect accountability

The column places enormous moral weight on “America” rediscovering its better nature. That framing obscures the more concrete structure of responsibility. Republican officials exercised power, enabled Trump and advanced the conduct Stephens condemns. Democratic officials now possess power to reverse, constrain or preserve the resulting policies.

Blaming or praising the country as a single personality dissolves those decisions into national mood. The weaker actors in this account—ordinary voters, particularly the Black women credited as the coalition’s backbone—are asked to carry democracy through turnout. The stronger actors are the officeholders who will determine whether that effort produces anything beyond a different set of names on government stationery.

A mandate is a test, not an absolution

The larger pattern is familiar: an opposition party treats electoral victory as the end of a nightmare before it has confronted the machinery that made the nightmare possible. Relief is real, and the reported scale of the Democratic gains would materially shift power. Neither fact guarantees reform.

If Democrats control Congress and multiple state governments as Stephens reports, obstruction can no longer explain every failure. Institutional authority creates agency, and agency creates responsibility. The election’s political meaning will not be settled by the size of the wave or the diversity of its winners, but by whether those winners use their power—and who pays when they do not.
