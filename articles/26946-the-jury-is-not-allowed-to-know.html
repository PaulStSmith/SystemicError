The Jury Is Not Allowed to Know

By Paulo Santos

We ask twelve citizens to authorize the machinery of punishment. Then we tell them the punishment is none of their business.

In the last two articles, I kept circling the same problem from different directions.

One Jeopardy, One Shot asked how many attempts the government should receive after it puts a person in criminal jeopardy.

My answer was one.

One Jeopardy, Two Governments asked whether dividing government into separate sovereigns should multiply those attempts.

My answer was no.

One person.

One course of conduct.

One genuine prosecution.

One jeopardy.

One shot.

Make it count.

But while working through the second article, another question appeared.

Not about how many trials the government gets.

About what the people deciding the trial are allowed to know.

Suppose twelve citizens sit in a jury box.

They hear the accusation.

They hear the witnesses.

They examine the evidence.

They decide whether the government proved every element beyond a reasonable doubt.

Then they return the word that activates the next part of the machine:

Guilty.

What exactly have they authorized?

Five years?

Twenty?

Life?

A mandatory minimum?

Consecutive sentences?

Deportation?

Permanent loss of a professional license?

A lifetime sex-offender registration requirement?

Financial ruin?

Some combination of them?

In much of American criminal practice, the answer given to the jury is remarkably simple:

None of your business.

The jury is generally expected to decide guilt without considering punishment.

That rule is so familiar that it can sound almost self-evident.

It shouldn’t.

Because hidden inside it is a profound question about what a criminal jury actually is.

Is it merely a device for resolving disputed facts?

Or is it the group of citizens whose verdict authorizes the state to punish another human being?

If it is only the first, hiding punishment makes sense.

If it is also the second, the arrangement becomes much harder to defend.

“Punishment Should Not Enter Your Consideration”

The Supreme Court stated the conventional rule plainly in Shannon v. United States in 1994.

Terry Shannon raised an insanity defense and wanted the jury told what would happen if it found him not guilty by reason of insanity.

The trial judge refused.

Instead, the jury was instructed to apply the law regardless of the consequences and that punishment should not enter its consideration.

The Supreme Court approved the basic principle.

When the jury has no sentencing role, the Court said, it should reach its verdict without regard to the sentence that might be imposed.

The justification is the standard division of labor.

The jury determines facts and decides guilt.

The judge imposes punishment.

Therefore, the Court reasoned, sentencing information is irrelevant to the jury’s assigned task and may distract or confuse it.

That doctrine is not difficult to understand.

It is beautifully clean.

Possibly too clean.

Because criminal punishment is not an abstract event occurring in another universe after the jury goes home.

The guilty verdict is what makes the punishment possible.

Without that verdict, there is no sentence.

The jury may not choose the punishment.

But it opens the gate.

And the legal system often insists that the people opening the gate should not be told what is waiting on the other side.

Everybody Else Knows

Now place that rule inside an actual prosecution.

The prosecutor knows the potential sentence.

The defense attorney knows it.

The judge knows it.

The legislature created it.

The defendant certainly knows it.

Indeed, plea bargaining frequently depends upon the defendant understanding it with terrifying precision.

The prosecutor can say, in substance:

Plead guilty and face three years.

Go to trial and risk thirty.

The difference between those numbers is not some irrelevant distraction during plea negotiations.

It is the leverage.

The entire mechanism works because the defendant understands the consequences.

Then suppose the defendant refuses.

He invokes the constitutional right to trial.

A jury is sworn.

And suddenly the consequences that were important enough to pressure the defendant into surrendering his jury right become information the jury itself is not supposed to consider.

Think about that.

The government may construct the defendant’s decision around punishment.

But the citizens who decide whether the government proved its case may be deliberately insulated from that same punishment.

The defendant is expected to contemplate thirty years.

The jury may be instructed not to contemplate thirty years at all.

That is an extraordinary asymmetry.

The Sterile Verdict

The official theory attempts to sterilize the verdict.

The jury is not sentencing anyone.

It is merely answering a factual and legal question:

Did the government prove the elements of the charged offense beyond a reasonable doubt?

Conceptually, we can separate those things.

Humanly, we cannot separate them quite so easily.

Consider a hypothetical statute.

Possession of some prohibited item carries a mandatory twenty-year sentence.

The defendant unquestionably possessed it.

There is no factual dispute.

The jury hears the evidence and concludes:

Yes. The government proved possession.

Guilty.

Then the judge says:

Twenty years.

The legal system can divide responsibility among institutions.

The legislature wrote the punishment.

The prosecutor selected the charge.

The jury found the facts.

The judge imposed the sentence.

Every actor can accurately describe his own role as limited.

And yet one human being is still going to prison for twenty years.

The punishment did not descend from the sky.

It resulted from a chain of governmental decisions.

The jury’s verdict was one indispensable link in that chain.

Calling punishment “not the jury’s concern” does not make the causal relationship disappear.

It merely partitions responsibility.

The Assembly Line of Moral Responsibility

There is something unsettling about systems in which everyone performs one narrow function and therefore nobody feels responsible for the whole.

The legislature says:

We merely established the permissible punishment.

The prosecutor says:

We merely charged the applicable offense.

The jury says:

We merely determined guilt.

The judge says:

We merely applied the sentence required by law.

The corrections department says:

We merely execute the judgment.

Every sentence can be institutionally accurate.

Stack them together and a person disappears into a cage.

This does not mean that every participant is equally responsible.

It means that a system can distribute a coercive act among enough actors that each actor experiences only a fragment of it.

That fragmentation is especially peculiar at the jury stage because the jury is supposed to provide something bureaucratic institutions cannot.

Ordinary citizens.

Peers.

A layer between the accused and the machinery of the state.

Yet the jury is often asked to perform that function while being denied information about what activating the machinery will actually do.

The Blank Rifle

There is an old practice associated with firing squads: one rifle is secretly loaded with a blank cartridge.

The condemned person still dies.

The squad still kills him.

But each rifleman is given a small psychological refuge:

Perhaps my rifle contained the blank.

Criminal procedure has constructed something strangely similar without ammunition.

The juror says: I only decided guilt.

The judge says: I only imposed the sentence.

The prosecutor says: I only brought the charge.

The legislature says: I only wrote the law.

Nobody actually has a blank cartridge.

Every one of those decisions contributes to the result.

But responsibility has been divided finely enough that every participant can point somewhere else in the machinery and say:

That part wasn’t mine.

The comparison is not exact.

It is stranger than that.

The firing-squad member knows perfectly well what the consequence will be. He simply may not know whether his bullet caused it.

The juror knows perfectly well what decision he made.

What he may be deliberately prevented from knowing is what that decision will cause.

One system obscures causation.

The other obscures consequence.

Both create psychological distance from responsibility.

And that should bother us.

Because if twelve citizens are asked to authorize the government’s use of criminal punishment against another human being, they should not be handed the legal equivalent of a blank rifle and told that whatever happens afterward belongs to somebody else.

The Supreme Court Knows the Jury Is a Bulwark

Here the doctrine becomes almost internally poetic.

In the line of cases beginning with Apprendi v. New Jersey, the Supreme Court insisted that judges cannot simply find whatever facts are necessary to increase a defendant’s authorized maximum punishment.

With limited exceptions, facts that increase the legally authorized maximum punishment must be submitted to a jury and proved beyond a reasonable doubt.

Later, in Southern Union Co. v. United States, the Court applied that principle to criminal fines.

Its language matters.

The Court described the jury’s historic role as a “bulwark between the State and the accused.”

Exactly.

That is the institution we are talking about.

Not a spreadsheet formula.

Not a laboratory instrument.

A bulwark.

The jury exists partly because we do not trust the state to accuse, adjudicate and punish entirely by itself.

But now observe the strange result.

The Constitution may require the jury to determine a fact because that fact authorizes greater punishment.

Yet the jury may still be discouraged from knowing what that greater punishment actually is.

The jury can be constitutionally indispensable to establishing the factual basis for additional punishment while the punishment itself remains outside its assigned concern.

That deserves more scrutiny than the tidy phrase “division of labor” usually receives.

A Jury Can Authorize What It Would Never Endorse

Suppose a jury believes the defendant technically violated the statute.

Suppose it also believes the conduct was relatively minor.

The jurors imagine:

A fine.

Probation.

Perhaps a short jail sentence.

They return guilty.

Then they discover afterward that the conviction carried a mandatory sentence measured in decades.

Would they have returned the same verdict had they known?

Maybe.

Perhaps every juror would say:

The law was proved. The punishment is harsh, but that isn’t our job.

Fine.

But perhaps not.

Perhaps one juror would have stopped.

Perhaps three.

Perhaps all twelve.

The conventional response is precisely that this possibility is why they should not know.

Knowledge of punishment might contaminate their determination of guilt.

That is the strongest argument for the existing rule.

And it should not be dismissed.

If two defendants committed exactly the same offense under exactly the same evidence, legal guilt should not theoretically depend on whether one jury considers the punishment too severe.

A jury could acquit someone it knows violated the law because it dislikes the penalty.

A different jury could convict.

Uniform law starts becoming localized moral judgment.

That is a real danger.

But hiding punishment solves that problem by creating another one.

The jury can authorize consequences it would consider morally intolerable precisely because the system prevents it from knowing those consequences.

We should at least admit the trade.

The Word Everyone Is Thinking: Nullification

Eventually this discussion runs into jury nullification.

There is no point pretending otherwise.

A criminal jury possesses an extraordinary practical power.

If it acquits, that acquittal is ordinarily final.

The government does not get to appeal the jury’s not-guilty verdict and demand a replacement conviction.

That means a jury can acquit even when the evidence satisfies the statutory elements.

Federal courts, however, do not generally recognize a right of jurors to disregard the law.

In Sparf v. United States in 1895, the Supreme Court emphasized the division between judge and jury: the court declares the law; the jury applies that law to the facts.

Later federal cases have similarly rejected demands that juries be instructed that they may simply ignore the law.

So there is a distinction worth keeping precise.

A jury has the power to return an acquittal the government cannot undo.

That does not mean federal law gives jurors a formal right to nullify or requires judges to teach them how.

But sentencing information makes that distinction politically uncomfortable.

Because one obvious consequence of telling jurors the penalty is that some may decide:

I will not authorize that.

Not:

The prosecution failed to prove the elements.

But:

The punishment attached to this conviction is unjust, and I refuse to become the final factual instrument that activates it.

The modern system has spent a great deal of doctrinal effort ensuring that this is not how jurors understand their role.

That may produce greater uniformity.

It may also make the jury a considerably weaker check on governmental power.

Nullification Has an Ugly History Too

This is where romanticizing jury independence becomes dangerous.

Jury nullification is not inherently progressive.

It is not inherently merciful.

It is not inherently just.

A jury can refuse to convict because a law is grotesque.

A jury can also refuse to convict because the victim is Black and the defendant is white.

American history contains both kinds of jury defiance.

A mechanism that permits citizens to resist unjust laws can also permit citizens to protect local injustice.

That matters.

Any argument for informing juries about consequences has to confront it.

The answer cannot simply be:

Trust the jury’s conscience.

Consciences can be monstrous.

But that cuts in both directions.

The possibility that jurors may misuse information does not automatically justify keeping consequential information from them.

We routinely entrust juries with gruesome photographs, traumatized witnesses, contradictory experts, forensic uncertainty, questions of intent, questions of credibility and facts that may determine whether someone spends decades in prison.

The institution already rests upon the assumption that ordinary citizens can receive emotionally significant information and still deliberate.

The real question is why punishment receives such special treatment.

“It Might Distract Them”

Return to Shannon.

The Supreme Court worried that sentencing information could distract jurors from factfinding and create confusion.

Of course it could.

But consider what else criminal trials routinely expose jurors to.

A murder trial may include photographs of a corpse.

A child-abuse prosecution may include horrifying testimony.

A fraud trial may involve hundreds of exhibits and complicated financial structures.

A conspiracy case may require jurors to separate statements and evidence applicable to multiple defendants.

We do not respond:

This information may emotionally affect you, so we will conceal it.

We give instructions.

We explain the limited purpose for which evidence may be considered.

We trust jurors to compartmentalize when necessary.

Yet punishment is treated differently.

Not merely limited.

Often removed from the jury’s universe.

Why?

Because once punishment enters the room, the verdict becomes harder to pretend is morally sterile.

Mandatory Minimums Make the Fiction Harder

Judicial discretion provides one argument for withholding sentencing information.

A judge may have many lawful sentencing options.

The precise outcome may depend upon facts, guidelines, criminal history, statutory factors and information unavailable at trial.

Telling jurors one possible sentence could mislead them.

Fair enough.

But that argument weakens substantially when the consequence is fixed or nearly fixed.

Suppose conviction on Count X automatically requires a mandatory minimum sentence.

The jury is not being asked to speculate about what some judge might someday do.

There is a legal floor.

The guilty verdict has a knowable consequence.

Why should the jury necessarily be kept ignorant of it?

The traditional answer remains:

Because punishment is not their function.

But that is not an explanation.

It is a restatement of the institutional arrangement being questioned.

Some Consequences Are Not Even Called Punishment

Then the maze gets worse.

A criminal conviction can trigger consequences outside the formal sentence.

Immigration law supplies one of the starkest examples.

In Padilla v. Kentucky, the Supreme Court recognized that changes in immigration law had made removal practically inevitable for some noncitizens convicted of particular crimes.

The Court described deportation as an integral—and sometimes the most important—part of the penalty a noncitizen may face.

That recognition arose in the context of defense counsel’s duty to advise a defendant considering a guilty plea.

Again, notice who needs to understand the consequence.

The defendant does.

His lawyer does.

The plea decision may be constitutionally defective if counsel fails to provide required advice about sufficiently clear immigration consequences.

But the jury deciding guilt generally does not thereby acquire a right to hear:

If you convict this person, deportation will follow.

The consequence can be important enough that the defendant needs competent legal advice about it before deciding whether to plead guilty.

Yet it can remain outside the jury’s assigned field of vision.

The compartmentalization is almost perfect.

The Defendant Lives in the Whole System

Institutions experience a prosecution in pieces.

The defendant does not.

The defendant does not experience:

10 percent charging decision.

20 percent jury verdict.

30 percent sentencing.

15 percent immigration consequence.

10 percent occupational licensing.

15 percent post-release restrictions.

He experiences one life.

One conviction may mean prison, deportation, loss of employment, loss of housing, loss of professional status and separation from family.

Whether lawyers classify each consequence as “direct,” “collateral,” criminal, civil, regulatory or administrative does not change how the defendant experiences it.

This is the same mistake we encountered with dual sovereignty.

Government divides itself into categories and then treats those categories as though the human being standing underneath them had also been divided.

Federal government.

State government.

Criminal sentence.

Civil consequence.

Immigration consequence.

Licensing consequence.

Different institutions.

Different doctrines.

One person.

But What Exactly Should Jurors Be Told?

This is where a serious proposal has to become more disciplined than a slogan.

“Tell the jury everything” would be a terrible rule.

Sentencing law can be extraordinarily complicated.

Many consequences depend on criminal history, judicial findings, charging combinations, future administrative decisions and facts not properly before the guilt-phase jury.

Dumping an entire presentence report onto jurors before deliberation could turn a criminal trial into chaos.

So the meaningful question is not whether jurors should know every conceivable consequence.

It is what consequences are sufficiently certain, severe and directly triggered by conviction that hiding them distorts the significance of the verdict.

A workable rule could distinguish categories.

If conviction carries a fixed mandatory minimum:

Tell them.

If conviction carries a mandatory maximum but broad judicial discretion beneath it:

Tell them the legally authorized range, carefully and neutrally.

If particular counts must legally run consecutively:

Tell them.

If conviction automatically triggers a severe legal consequence such as a mandatory registration regime or clearly prescribed removal consequence:

There is at least a serious argument that the jury should know.

If the consequence depends upon uncertain future discretionary decisions:

Say so—or leave it out.

The point is not to transform the jury into the sentencing judge.

The point is to stop treating the actual meaning of its verdict as forbidden knowledge.

Then Give Them a Limiting Instruction

Courts already know how to do this.

The judge could tell the jury:

Your task is to determine whether the government has proved every element of the charged offense beyond a reasonable doubt. The law provides the following consequences upon conviction. You do not select the sentence, and you must not convict or acquit merely because you approve or disapprove of the punishment. This information is provided so that you understand the legal significance of the verdict you are being asked to return.

Would every juror obey?

Of course not.

Jurors sometimes misunderstand instructions now.

That is not an argument unique to sentencing information.

The legal system cannot simultaneously describe jurors as competent enough to decide guilt beyond a reasonable doubt in cases carrying decades of imprisonment and too fragile to be told that decades of imprisonment are what their decision may authorize.

The Capital-Punishment Exception Gives the Game Away

There is already an obvious setting where the separation between guilt and punishment breaks down:

capital cases.

When jurors have a sentencing role, punishment necessarily becomes part of their work.

And the Supreme Court has recognized situations in which sentencing consequences must be explained.

In Simmons v. South Carolina, for example, the Court held in the capital context that when the prosecution argues future dangerousness and the defendant is legally ineligible for parole, due process requires that the jury be informed of that parole ineligibility.

Why?

Because otherwise jurors may make a life-or-death decision while operating under a materially false understanding of what a life sentence means.

That principle is narrower than the argument I am making here.

But its logic is illuminating.

Sometimes consequences matter because misunderstanding them can distort the jury’s decision.

Once we concede that, the absolute cleanliness of “jurors decide guilt; consequences are irrelevant” begins to crack.

The remaining argument becomes one of degree.

We Already Trust Jurors With Punishment-Defining Facts

Apprendi creates another crack.

Suppose a statute authorizes ten years for the basic offense but twenty years if Fact X exists.

The Constitution may require the jury to find Fact X beyond a reasonable doubt before the higher punishment becomes available.

So the jury’s factual finding literally changes the amount of punishment government may impose.

Yet the jury need not necessarily be told:

Finding X is what makes another ten years legally possible.

That is a strange conception of democratic participation.

We insist that citizens make the finding because judicial factfinding alone would give government too much power.

Then we can conceal why the finding matters.

The jury acts as constitutional brake without being shown the hill.

Perhaps That Ignorance Is the Point

I do not mean there is a conspiracy.

There does not need to be one.

Institutions develop doctrines that make their machinery run more predictably.

Keeping punishment away from the guilt phase has obvious administrative virtues.

Trials stay narrower.

Juries receive fewer opportunities to disregard statutes.

Similar cases are more likely to produce similar verdicts.

Judges retain sentencing responsibility.

Legislatures retain control over statutory penalties.

Prosecutors retain charging authority.

Everything remains in its assigned box.

The machine runs smoothly.

But constitutional criminal procedure should not be judged only by how smoothly the machine runs.

Efficiency is especially dangerous as a governing value when one side of the process owns the police, the prosecutors, the prisons and the power to take years from a human life.

Sometimes friction is the safeguard.

The jury itself is friction.

The Jury Is Supposed to Stand Between

That phrase from Southern Union deserves to be taken seriously:

a bulwark between the State and the accused.

A bulwark that does not know what lies behind it is a peculiar bulwark.

Imagine asking twelve citizens:

Did he do it?

Yes.

Was the law satisfied?

Yes.

Then we will take him away.

For how long?

That isn’t your concern.

What happens to his immigration status?

Not your concern.

Does the conviction automatically activate another severe consequence?

Not your concern.

Would you still return the same verdict if you knew?

You should not ask that question.

At some point the insistence on institutional purity begins to sound less like protection of the jury’s role and more like protection of the system from the jury.

The Hardest Counterargument

There is one objection that cannot be waved away.

Law cannot function if every jury becomes a miniature legislature.

If twelve jurors may effectively veto any statute whenever they dislike its punishment, identical conduct may receive radically different treatment depending upon geography, race, class, sympathy, politics, prejudice or sheer luck.

That is not justice either.

A racist jury refusing to convict a white defendant for violence against a Black victim is not a heroic community conscience.

It is institutional failure.

So perhaps the solution is not to celebrate nullification.

Perhaps the solution is narrower.

Information without formal permission to disregard the law.

Tell jurors the concrete legal consequences that necessarily or predictably follow from their verdict.

Tell them their legal duty remains to decide whether the elements were proved.

Do not instruct them that they possess some free-floating authority to rewrite statutes.

Do not turn the trial into a referendum on sentencing policy.

But stop requiring ignorance.

If, after receiving accurate information and proper instructions, a jury nevertheless refuses to convict, the system then confronts the uncomfortable feature criminal juries have always possessed:

An acquittal is final.

That is not a defect accidentally left in the machine.

It is one of the restraints placed upon it.

Responsibility Requires Knowledge

There is a simple moral intuition beneath all of this.

You cannot meaningfully ask someone to take responsibility for a decision while intentionally withholding the foreseeable consequences of that decision.

We understand this almost everywhere else.

Doctors explain material risks before obtaining consent.

Contracts disclose obligations.

Judges taking guilty pleas ensure defendants understand important consequences and rights they surrender.

Criminal defense lawyers must explain enough for clients to make informed decisions.

Yet the jury’s decision can be treated differently.

Twelve citizens can authorize the factual predicate for punishment while the punishment itself remains deliberately obscured.

Perhaps that produces cleaner verdicts.

But cleaner is not necessarily more legitimate.

One Person, One Verdict, One Consequence

The previous two articles were about finality.

This one is about responsibility.

In One Jeopardy, One Shot, the central idea was that the state should bear the risk of failing to prove its accusation because the state possesses overwhelming power.

In One Jeopardy, Two Governments, the argument was that dividing governmental power among sovereigns should not multiply jeopardy against one human being.

The same perspective applies here.

The defendant does not experience the verdict as an abstract resolution of statutory elements.

He experiences what happens next.

The prison door.

The deportation order.

The registration requirement.

The ruined profession.

The lost years.

The jury need not control all those consequences.

But there is something deeply strange about claiming that the jury performs democracy’s great protective function in criminal justice while deliberately preventing it from seeing the human meaning of the decision it has been asked to make.

If twelve citizens are trusted to decide whether government has proved enough to take someone’s liberty, perhaps they should be trusted with one additional piece of information:

How much liberty the government intends to take.

The state should not be permitted to ask for a verdict in the abstract and reveal the price only after the jury has authorized the purchase.

If the consequences are definite enough to threaten the defendant with before trial, definite enough to structure the plea bargain, definite enough for the prosecutor to calculate, and definite enough for the law to impose—

they are definite enough for the jury to know.

A verdict should not be a blind signature.

If you are going to authorize the punishment, you should know what you are authorizing.
