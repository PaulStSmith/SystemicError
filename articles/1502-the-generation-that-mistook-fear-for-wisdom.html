The Generation That Mistook Fear for Wisdom

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 1, 202612 min read

There is a difference between blaming people and judging history.

That distinction matters when we talk about the Baby Boomers, because the conversation almost always collapses into one of two useless positions. Either Boomers are treated as cartoon villains who selfishly hoarded everything, or they are treated as innocent individuals who simply worked hard, bought homes, raised families, and now resent being told they are the problem.

Both versions miss the deeper truth.

Many individual Boomers are blameless in the ordinary moral sense. They did what they were told responsible adults were supposed to do. They worked. They bought houses if they could. They saved what they could. They voted for stability. They feared disorder. They tried to protect their families.

But generations can be guilty in ways individuals are not.

A generation can inherit institutions, reshape them, vote through them, defend them, and leave them worse than it found them. A generation can make individually rational choices that add up to collective damage. A generation can be frightened, manipulated, rewarded, and flattered into defending a system that eventually harms its own children.

That, I think, is the harder conversation underneath the recent debate about Baby Boomers, housing, work, and power.

The Article, the Backlash, and the Wound It Touched

Nick Lichtenberg’s original “pig in the python” column argued that the enormous size of the Baby Boom generation distorted nearly every American institution it passed through.

When Boomers entered the labor market, competition intensified. When they bought homes, prices rose. When they reached leadership, many stayed there. Now, as they age, they still hold a disproportionate share of large homes, senior jobs, political power, and institutional authority.

That argument provoked a furious response.

In his follow-up column, Lichtenberg described the backlash as less a comment section than an X-ray of how aging Americans feel about blame, insecurity, and a system that feels stuck. Some readers heard a structural argument. Others heard a death wish.

One Boomer wrote, “I apologize for not dying soon enough for you, so your generation can pick over my financial bones.” Another asked whether the argument implied putting Boomers on ice floes or turning them into Soylent Green. One correspondent escalated into violent language serious enough that the exchange went to the author’s legal team.

But many responses were more revealing than merely angry.

One reader listed a life of ordinary responsibility: buying a fixer-upper, working two jobs, going to night school, raising a family, caring for an aging parent, starting a business, employing young people. Then came the question that sits at the center of this whole debate: “What exactly did I do wrong?”

Another reader said they were 67, still paying a mortgage, unable to afford retirement, and facing medication costs of more than $2,000 a month after insurance. A woman living alone in a large house after her husband died explained that downsizing made no financial sense because smaller homes now cost more than the house she already owned.

The most important line in the follow-up may have come from the reader who wrote:

“Yes we are ALL STUCK.”

That is the thing the simple anti-Boomer narrative misses. Many Boomers do not experience themselves as hoarding anything. They experience themselves as trapped. Trapped in houses they cannot afford to leave. Trapped in jobs they cannot safely quit. Trapped in medical systems they fear. Trapped in technologies they did not grow up with. Trapped in a retirement system that no longer feels secure.

And yet their children and grandchildren are trapped behind them.

That is the tragedy. It is not simply that one generation took too much pleasure in winning. It is that a generation’s survival strategies became another generation’s blocked future.

The Missing Layer: Fear

But even that does not go deep enough.

To understand why so many Boomers became politically attached to the very forces that hollowed out the world their children inherited, we have to look at how that generation was formed.

The Boomers were not merely the children of postwar prosperity. They were also children of Cold War fear.

They were raised under the shadow of McCarthyism, the Red Scare, nuclear drills, anti-communist paranoia, and a national political culture that taught them to see radical change as existential danger. They grew up in a country where “communist” was not merely a political label but a moral accusation. They inherited a civic imagination in which enemies were everywhere: abroad, in universities, in unions, in civil rights movements, in cities, in art, in protest, in any demand that sounded too collective.

Fear became a political language.

And conservative politicians became fluent in it.

They positioned themselves as the solution to the terrors they helped amplify. Communism. Crime. Urban unrest. Drugs. Racial integration. Feminism. Secularism. Queer liberation. Terrorism. Immigration. Socialism. Wokeness. The vocabulary changed decade by decade, but the structure remained the same:

Something is coming to take your country from you.

Only we can stop it.

For many Boomers, conservatism was not first experienced as cruelty. It was experienced as safety.

Safe streets. Strong borders. Good schools. Strong defense. Family values. Low taxes. Personal responsibility. Respect for work. Respect for police. Respect for the flag. Respect for order.

Those phrases sound neutral until you examine what they were used to justify.

“Safe streets” became a way to launder racial fear into mass incarceration.

“Law and order” became a way to criminalize the social consequences of poverty, segregation, addiction, and neglect.

“No Child Left Behind” wrapped an attack on public education in the language of accountability.

“Welfare reform” converted economic resentment into punishment of the poor.

“Tax relief” framed upward redistribution as liberation for ordinary families.

“School choice” turned public education from a shared civic institution into a market battlefield.

“National security” made permanent militarization feel like patriotism.

The genius of modern conservative politics was not that it invented fear. The fear already existed. The genius was that it organized fear into identity.

The Party Choice They Never Revisited

There is another uncomfortable point here: many people make their political identity young and then never seriously revisit it.

This is not unique to Boomers. It is human. People choose a side, build a social world around it, absorb its slogans, marry into it, worship inside it, work alongside it, and slowly confuse their party’s interests with their own moral instincts.

For many Boomers, that political identity was formed during or shortly after a period of enormous national upheaval: civil rights, Vietnam, assassinations, riots, feminism, Watergate, stagflation, the energy crisis, crime panic, deindustrialization, and the conservative backlash that followed.

The Democratic and Republican parties were also changing. The great party realignment of the mid-twentieth century scrambled old loyalties. Conservative white Democrats in the South migrated toward the Republican Party. The GOP increasingly became the home of racial backlash, anti-tax politics, anti-union economics, evangelical moral politics, and Cold War militarism.

Many Boomers followed that current or were carried by it.

And once they were inside it, the system rewarded them just enough to keep them there.

That part is essential.

Conservative policy did appear, for a time, to work for a large slice of the white middle class. Not because the policy itself was building broad prosperity, but because the country was still living off the infrastructure, labor power, public investment, cheap housing, and wage structures built in earlier decades.

The spread between the bottom and the top was much smaller. A worker could still buy a house. Public universities were still affordable. Pensions still existed. Unions still mattered. Healthcare had not yet become the monster it is now. A middle-class person could vote for tax cuts, deregulation, anti-union policy, privatization, and punitive social policy while still personally benefiting from the older settlement those policies were quietly dismantling.

The roof did not cave in immediately.

That delay may be the whole story.

By the time the damage was obvious, the identity had hardened. The political habit had become common sense. The slogans had become reflexes. The enemy list had been updated again and again, but the emotional machinery remained unchanged.

So they kept voting as if the old bargain still existed.

They kept voting for people who promised to defend them from threats.

They kept voting against the public goods that had made their own stability possible.

And increasingly, they voted against the future stability of their children.

Hoodwinked, But Not Innocent

It is tempting to say Boomers were hoodwinked.

There is truth in that. They were sold a story. They were told that government was the enemy, except when it protected their property, subsidized their suburbs, funded their roads, guaranteed their mortgages, defended their retirement benefits, and policed the people they feared. They were told that unions were corrupt, public schools were failing, taxes were theft, welfare was dependency, regulation was tyranny, and markets were freedom.

They were told that the ladder beneath them was natural rather than constructed.

They were told that anyone below them had simply failed to climb.

But “hoodwinked” can become too generous if it removes agency entirely.

At some point, adults are responsible for noticing consequences.

They were responsible for noticing that wages stopped tracking productivity.

They were responsible for noticing that their children were drowning in student debt.

They were responsible for noticing that the starter homes disappeared.

They were responsible for noticing that public schools were being starved.

They were responsible for noticing that healthcare became a financial trap.

They were responsible for noticing that prisons filled with people whose lives had been written off as acceptable collateral damage.

They were responsible for noticing that the politicians promising to protect them were transferring wealth upward.

Some did notice. Many fought against it. No generation is politically uniform.

But enough did not. Enough refused. Enough explained it away. Enough converted every warning into another culture-war grievance. Enough mistook their own comfort for proof that the system was still basically fair.

That is where the line between victim and accomplice begins to blur.

Individual Blamelessness and Generational Guilt

This is why the reader’s question, “What exactly did I do wrong?” is so powerful.

The honest answer may be: individually, perhaps nothing.

You worked. You paid your bills. You cared for your family. You survived the world as it was presented to you. You made choices that were reasonable inside your own life.

But the generation did something wrong.

It mistook private responsibility for public virtue.

It treated homeownership as proof of moral worth while turning housing into an asset trap.

It accepted rising property values as personal success while ignoring the fact that those same rising values were locking out its own children.

It defended low taxes while demanding high benefits.

It praised education while defunding the systems that made education broadly useful.

It demanded respect for work while weakening labor.

It spoke constantly of freedom while building systems of debt, surveillance, punishment, and precarity.

It consumed the inheritance of public investment and then called public investment socialism.

No single Boomer did all of that. But as a political generation, enough of them endorsed, tolerated, ignored, or benefited from it that the distinction becomes difficult to escape.

The individual may be blameless.

The generation is not.

Succession as a Moral Test

This brings us back to succession.

The issue is not merely that Boomers own too many large houses or hold too many Senate seats or remain too long in executive roles. Those are symptoms of a deeper failure: the failure to treat leadership, wealth, and institutions as things that must circulate.

A healthy society teaches each generation to ask:

What did I inherit?

What did I preserve?

What did I improve?

What must I pass on?

Too much Boomer-era politics replaced that stewardship ethic with ownership.

My house.

My retirement.

My taxes.

My neighborhood.

My benefits.

My country.

Once politics becomes organized around defending “mine,” succession begins to feel like theft. Younger generations become threats instead of heirs. Change becomes desecration. Institutions become monuments to the people who captured them rather than bridges to those who come next.

That is why the housing issue is so emotionally charged. A Boomer staying in a large house may be making a perfectly understandable choice. But at scale, that choice becomes a locked door. A senior leader staying in power may believe they are protecting the institution. But at scale, that choice becomes a succession crisis. A retiree voting to protect their benefits may be acting rationally. But at scale, that choice can become generational extraction if the same voter refuses to fund the future.

The moral test of a generation is not whether its members suffered.

Every generation suffers.

The test is whether it converts its suffering into wisdom or into entitlement.

They Were Afraid. Then They Made Fear Policy.

The Boomers were shaped by fear, and that deserves compassion.

They grew up with mushroom clouds in the civic imagination. They watched institutions lie about war, race, corruption, and power. They saw cities burn on television. They lived through assassinations, inflation, oil shocks, and a country seemingly coming apart. They were told, again and again, that civilization was fragile and enemies were near.

But then many of them made fear the basis of governance.

They voted for cages and called it safety.

They voted for austerity and called it discipline.

They voted for privatization and called it freedom.

They voted for tax cuts and called it prosperity.

They voted for deregulation and called it common sense.

They voted for school destruction and called it accountability.

They voted for upward redistribution and called it growth.

They voted for politicians who told them that every demand for repair was a threat to what they had earned.

And now, late in life, many find themselves frightened again. Frightened of medical bills. Frightened of technology. Frightened of retirement. Frightened of being blamed. Frightened of being replaced. Frightened that the systems they trusted will not protect them either.

That is tragic.

It is also not an acquittal.

The Lesson

The point of saying this is not to hate Boomers. Hatred is too easy, and it teaches nothing.

The point is to understand how a generation can be both wounded and responsible.

The author’s follow-up article shows the wound clearly. Many Boomers are scared. Many are not rich. Many are not comfortable. Many feel accused after lifetimes of work, caregiving, sacrifice, and ordinary decency.

We should be able to say that plainly.

But we should also be able to say this:

A generation that inherited broad prosperity helped narrow it. A generation that benefited from public investment helped delegitimize public investment. A generation raised in fear too often allowed fear to become policy. A generation that experienced stability mistook that stability for personal virtue rather than a public achievement. A generation that should have trained successors too often defended incumbency.

The question is not whether individual Boomers deserve cruelty.

They do not.

The question is whether America can tell the truth about what happened without retreating into sentimentality.

Because the same mistake can happen again.

Millennials and Gen Z are not morally immune to history. Give any generation enough fear, enough property, enough political flattery, and enough incentive to defend what little security it has, and it can become exactly what it once criticized.

That may be the real warning.

The tragedy of the Boomers is not that they were uniquely evil. It is that they were human inside systems that rewarded fear, accumulation, and denial. They inherited a ladder, pulled at its bolts, and often convinced themselves the people below simply were not climbing hard enough.

Now the ladder creaks.

And the rest of us have to decide whether to rebuild it as an act of succession, or merely fight over the rungs that remain.
