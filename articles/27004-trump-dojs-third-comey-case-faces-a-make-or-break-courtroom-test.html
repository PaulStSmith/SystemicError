Trump DOJ’s third Comey case faces a make-or-break courtroom test

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 31, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that James Comey’s lawyers were set to ask U.S. District Judge Trevor McFadden in Washington, D.C., to dismiss the Justice Department’s third attempt to prosecute the former FBI director. The dispute centers on whether prosecutors could bypass a grand jury and file a “criminal information” to prevent the statute of limitations from expiring on an alleged 2016 media leak.

The Justice Department said the filing preserved its ability to seek an indictment. Comey, who maintains his innocence, argued that the maneuver was legally invalid. The allegation concerns anonymous disclosures about a secret surveillance order requiring Yahoo to scan emails for possible links to a foreign terrorist group.

Federal authorities had examined the episode twice before without filing charges. According to Comey’s court filing, John Durham recommended against prosecution in a classified December 2018 memorandum; a later investigation by the U.S. Attorney’s Office for the District of Columbia closed without charges in February 2020.

The power is at Justice

Comey is not the institution exercising coercive power here. The Justice Department is. It decides whether to revive a decade-old allegation, seek criminal process and force a defendant to spend time and money resisting the state.

That distinction matters because the personal feud between Comey and Donald Trump can make the story look like a clash between two powerful antagonists. It is not an equal contest. Comey can publish criticism and file motions. The administration controls the department bringing the case.

McFadden, a Trump appointee, holds the immediate judicial authority to decide whether this procedural gambit survives. His appointment is relevant context, but it does not establish how he will rule.

A deadline is not an explanation

The department’s stated reason for filing without a grand-jury indictment is that the 10-year limitations period was approaching. That explains the timing of the maneuver, not why prosecutors decided the underlying matter deserved revival after earlier inquiries ended without charges.

The institutional question is therefore larger than whether a “criminal information” can stop the clock. Prosecutors revisited conduct already examined by Durham and by the D.C. U.S. Attorney’s Office. If the government now possesses materially different evidence, AlterNet’s account does not identify it. If it does not, the procedural urgency was created by the government’s own decision to reopen an old file.

Treating that urgency as an impersonal scheduling problem would conceal agency. Officials chose to proceed.

The feud obscures the machinery

Comey investigated possible coordination between Trump’s 2016 campaign and Russia, later compared Trump to a mafia boss, and became a recurring target of Trump’s public accusations and demands for imprisonment. That history supplies an obvious political context, but personal hostility is not the most consequential feature of the story.

The consequential feature is the conversion of hostility into repeated prosecutorial action. One case alleging that Comey lied to Congress was thrown out because the prosecutor had been appointed unlawfully. Another pending indictment alleges that an Instagram image reading “86 47” constituted a threat; Comey has sought dismissal. The latest matter revives the alleged leak.

Calling this merely a bitter relationship would misdirect attention from the actor with charging authority. Only one side of the feud commands federal prosecutors.

Prosecution can be the punishment

Comey remains presumed innocent, and the reporting does not establish that Justice Department officials fabricated evidence or brought charges solely to satisfy Trump. It does establish a pattern of repeated attempts: an invalidated prosecution, another disputed indictment and now a procedural effort to preserve a third case involving conduct from 2016.

Even unsuccessful prosecutions impose consequences. They consume resources, attach criminal allegations to political enemies and signal that previously closed matters can be reopened when control of the department changes. A dismissal may prevent a conviction while leaving that disciplinary effect intact.

The timing reported by AlterNet—amid several politically charged department actions before the midterms—raises the stakes. It does not prove electoral intent, but it makes scrutiny of the department’s evidence, procedures and departure from earlier decisions indispensable.

The systemic error

The narrow courtroom question is whether this filing method was lawful. The broader political question is whether prosecutorial discretion has become a renewable instrument against a president’s critics.

Institutions are not depoliticized simply because officials describe their choices in procedural language. Statutes of limitation, appointment rules and grand juries are constraints on state power, not inconveniences to be engineered around whenever an administration wants another attempt.

When coverage centers the animosity between Trump and Comey, it risks shrinking an institutional abuse question into personality drama. The durable danger lies in the precedent: executive power repeatedly testing legal boundaries until one prosecution finally sticks.
