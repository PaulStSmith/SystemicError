SYSTEMIC ERROR / COMMENTARY

Psychotherapist issues chilling warning of 'absolute mess' Trump will create

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 4, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe diagnosis is not the evidencePower is the missing subjectPrediction replaces accountabilityThe systemic error

What happened

AlterNet reports that psychotherapist Elliott Connie warned that President Donald Trump would create an “absolute mess” if election losses left him politically cornered. Connie predicted that Trump would reject unfavorable results, pursue lawsuits and recounts, and lash out rather than cooperate with Democrats. These were Connie’s forecasts, not reported events.

Connie based that assessment partly on Trump’s election-closing remarks about maintaining the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool and possibly attaching his name to it. He said the fixation suggested concern with image and personal recognition rather than public needs.

Connie repeatedly acknowledged that he had not evaluated Trump and could not diagnose him with narcissistic personality disorder. Hillary Clinton separately predicted on The View that Trump would become angry and lash out if congressional power created leverage to hold him accountable. The article supplies no election result or subsequent evidence establishing whether either prediction came true.

The diagnosis is not the evidence

Connie’s caveat matters. Suspecting a disorder is not a clinical diagnosis, and an analysis built around that suspicion cannot convert televised behavior into medical fact. AlterNet foregrounds the psychiatric label anyway, giving a speculative diagnosis more authority than the reporting can support.

The stronger case requires no amateur clinical verdict. Trump’s conduct after the 2020 election—invoked by Connie as the basis for his forecast—is politically relevant because it concerns how a president used his position after losing. The institutional question is not what condition might explain that conduct. It is what Trump did, who enabled him, and whether the safeguards around presidential power held.

Power is the missing subject

Trump holds presidential power. Congress can investigate, legislate, control funding and, under defined constitutional procedures, impose consequences. Courts and election officials can reject unsupported challenges. Party leaders and lawmakers decide whether to reinforce false claims, resist them or remain silent.

Yet the article turns this structure into a personality story. Trump becomes a volatile patient; Clinton and Connie become interpreters of his emotional state; institutions recede into the background. That framing makes public outcomes appear to depend on one man’s temperament when they also depend on officials with concrete authority choosing whether to constrain or assist him.

Prediction replaces accountability

Connie predicted lawsuits, recounts and attempts to invalidate election results. Those are specific possibilities, but AlterNet provides no reported plan, new directive or completed action behind them. The distinction between warning and evidence is essential, especially when the warning concerns future attacks on election outcomes.

Calling the anticipated result an “absolute mess” also obscures agency. A mess sounds accidental. If officials knowingly promote unsupported fraud claims, misuse state power or help overturn lawful results, those are decisions. If courts, legislators or party leaders enable that effort, their participation cannot be reduced to Trump feeling cornered.

The systemic error

The article’s weakest move is to medicalize a political problem. Speculation about narcissism may explain a media persona, but it can also misdirect attention from the people and institutions capable of turning grievance into government action.

The larger pattern is institutional evasion through psychological spectacle. Coverage scrutinizes Trump’s inner life while treating surrounding power brokers as scenery. Democracy is not administered by diagnosis. The consequential facts are who commands public authority, who follows unlawful or unsupported demands, who refuses them, and who pays the price when institutional actors choose loyalty over duty.
