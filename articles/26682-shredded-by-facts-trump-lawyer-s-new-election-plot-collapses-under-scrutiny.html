Shredded by facts: Trump lawyer's new election plot collapses under scrutiny

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 18, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the Justice Department asked a court to require California to segregate ballots associated with roughly 20,000 people the federal government labeled “likely noncitizens.” California officials said the government had neither explained that classification nor supplied enough identifying information to verify it. The reporting does not establish that any listed person was ineligible or cast an illegal vote.

First Assistant U.S. Attorney Bill Essayli nevertheless declared that federal officials had “presented the evidence” and were trying to stop California from counting ballots “cast by noncitizens.” National security analyst Marcy Wheeler accused him of lying and argued that the process could disenfranchise naturalized citizens.

AlterNet points to Nevada as the warning. A federal list of as many as 15,903 suspected noncitizens reportedly shrank to 185 after manual review; Nevada officials said state records showed that all 185 were citizens and that the federal government had provided nothing substantive proving otherwise.

Suspicion becomes a verdict

“Likely noncitizens” is an unverified classification. “Ballots cast by noncitizens” is a conclusion. Essayli erased the difference in public, converting an allegation his own lawsuit reportedly qualified into an asserted fact.

That is not a technical quibble. The distinction determines whether the government is investigating possible ineligibility or asking a court to burden lawful voters on the strength of an opaque and apparently fallible screening system.

The power sits with Washington

California is not the dominant actor here. The Justice Department holds federal enforcement power, access to government data and the ability to drag a state into court. It chose to seek ballot segregation before establishing that the affected voters were noncitizens.

Essayli then portrayed California’s demand for usable evidence as hostility to election integrity. That framing reverses responsibility. The government making the accusation bears the burden of substantiating it; the accused voters should not have to recover rights lost to a defective federal list.

The misdirection is the point

The public argument centers on California supposedly refusing to enforce election law. The documented dispute is narrower and more damaging to the administration’s case: federal officials had not shown that the targeted people were ineligible.

Nevada’s reported experience makes skepticism rational, not evasive. Yet Essayli’s account treated scrutiny of federal evidence as proof of state indifference. That rhetorical move shields institutional power by redirecting suspicion toward officials resisting an unsupported demand—and toward voters with less ability to defend themselves.

Citizens bear the risk

Segregating ballots is not an abstract administrative precaution. When citizenship screening produces false positives, eligible voters face delayed scrutiny or possible exclusion because the government assigned them to a suspect category.

The source does not establish that the California list contains naturalized citizens, so Wheeler’s warning remains an inference rather than a confirmed description of those 20,000 people. But Nevada’s reported result demonstrates the foreseeable danger: a system presented as detecting noncitizens can instead identify citizens.

Manufactured certainty, institutional consequence

This is a familiar political mechanism: begin with unreliable suspicion, promote it publicly as established fraud, and describe demands for proof as obstruction. The evidentiary weakness then disappears behind the louder accusation that election officials are refusing to protect the vote.

The larger pattern is not merely careless messaging. It is government power operating with one standard for itself and another for the public. Federal officials claim latitude to be wrong about citizenship, while citizens are expected to absorb the consequences of that error. When accusation outruns evidence and courts are asked to enforce the gap, “election integrity” becomes a justification for making legitimate votes conditional on surviving the government’s mistakes.
