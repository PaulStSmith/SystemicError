'A drunk man waving a steak knife': Leaker unfurls new Trump prediction

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosNovember 4, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Miles Taylor, a former Department of Homeland Security chief of staff and first-term Trump administration critic, predicts President Donald Trump will react more aggressively to Republican midterm losses in 2026 than he did after Democrats gained 40 House seats in 2018. The article does not establish that a “blue wave” has occurred; it presents an Election Day forecast based on unspecified predictive metrics and Taylor’s expectations.

Taylor says Trump responded to the 2018 defeat by privately raging about prospective investigations, publicly threatening a “war-like posture,” forcing out Attorney General Jeff Sessions, and overseeing the revocation of CNN reporter Jim Acosta’s press pass. His “drunk man waving around his steak knife” remark described that 2018 press conference, not a new incident. Taylor now predicts Trump could contest close counts, pressure state officials, direct the Justice Department toward districts he calls stolen, and resist congressional investigations. Those are predictions, not reported events.

The power is presidential

The central political fact is not Trump’s temperament. It is his control of the executive branch. A president can remove senior officials, influence federal law-enforcement priorities, command public attention, and place subordinates throughout the government. Describing such a figure chiefly as “angry” or “unhinged” risks reducing institutional power to a personality disorder.

Taylor’s account of 2018 supplies the more useful evidence: Trump answered an electoral setback with an attorney general’s removal, threats toward an incoming congressional majority, and retaliation against a confrontational reporter’s access. Whatever one thinks of Taylor’s interpretation, those were exercises of presidential authority or actions taken by the White House—not merely embarrassing behavior at a press conference.

Loyalty changes the machinery

Taylor argues that two restraints from 2018 are now absent: advisers willing to refuse Trump and the incentive imposed by another presidential campaign. His first point matters most. Personnel determine whether extraordinary demands die in conversation or become government action.

If senior officials were selected primarily for loyalty, the danger Taylor describes would not come from one man becoming more furious. It would come from officials deciding that serving him requires using public institutions against electoral and congressional opponents. Taylor predicts pressure on state certification officials and Justice Department intervention in disputed districts. AlterNet provides no evidence that those steps have occurred, but the forecast identifies the relevant chain of agency: presidential demand, subordinate compliance, institutional consequence.

Prediction is not reporting

AlterNet weakens its own case by presenting a projected Democratic “blowout” with sweeping confidence while offering no named forecast, polling average, race analysis, or probability. “Most predictive metrics” is not a substitute for evidence. Neither is “blue wave” when votes and control of Congress have not been established in the supplied reporting.

That framing turns a serious account of possible executive abuse into political suspense. The article has firsthand testimony about Trump’s conduct after the 2018 election and a former official’s explicit forecast about 2026. It should distinguish those categories cleanly. Instead, it packages prediction as momentum and speculation as near-certainty.

The scapegoat script

Taylor’s predicted sequence is familiar in structure: reject an adverse result, portray ordinary counting or certification as theft, pressure weaker state actors, and enlist federal power in the accusation. The officials administering elections would then become targets for a conflict created from the top.

That is political misdirection with practical consequences. Attention shifts from the president’s refusal to accept institutional limits toward local officials forced to defend routine procedures. The supposed scandal becomes the count itself, while the actual power play—the attempt to make government validate an unsupported claim—recedes into the background.

A system built on voluntary restraint

The larger pattern is institutional dependence on officials choosing not to abuse the authority they possess. Taylor’s account suggests that advisers constrained Trump in 2018, at least intermittently. That is not a durable safeguard. It is an admission that formal power was being checked by personnel willing to risk saying no.

The decisive question is therefore not whether Trump will leave quietly, become angrier, or produce another grotesque spectacle. It is whether officeholders with concrete authority will obey improper demands, whether Congress can investigate without executive obstruction, and whether election officials can perform their legal duties without coercion. Political systems fail through decisions made by identifiable people. Temperament may generate the demand; compliant institutions deliver the consequence.
