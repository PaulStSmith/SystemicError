Trump policies single-handedly crushing US tourism

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 4, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet, citing Washington Post reporting, attributes a sharp decline in international tourism to policies under President Donald Trump. Federal figures reportedly showed year-over-year overseas visits falling 1.8 percent in June 2026, 7 percent in July, 11.8 percent in August and, provisionally, 4.3 percent in September—even as global tourism continued recovering from pandemic-era shutdowns.

The reporting identifies two pressures: higher jet-fuel costs following Trump’s attacks on Iran and increasingly intrusive border enforcement. The Post reported that Customs officers searched 55,000 international travelers’ devices in fiscal 2025, up 18 percent from the previous year, while visitors could be required to surrender passwords or risk denied entry and device seizure.

International visitors spent $176 billion in the United States in 2025, according to the World Travel and Tourism Council figure cited by the Post—a 4.6 percent annual decline. The evidence supports a substantial policy-driven drag on tourism, although the article does not establish that Trump’s policies alone caused every reported decrease.

Washington made the choices

Tourists did not collectively become irrational, and the industry did not simply encounter mysterious “headwinds.” The federal government chose the policies shaping the risk and cost of visiting.

The president holds the central institutional power described here: authority over foreign policy, immigration enforcement and executive-branch priorities. Customs officers conduct the searches, but the administration determines the enforcement regime that authorizes and rewards such scrutiny. Airlines, hotels and destination economies absorb consequences from decisions they did not control.

A border search is also an economic signal

A vacation depends on predictability. A traveler deciding where to spend thousands of dollars must consider not only airfare but whether an officer may inspect private messages, social-media accounts and work files at the border.

That prospect changes the product the United States is selling. The destination now comes bundled with possible digital exposure, confiscation or refusal of entry. Calling this merely “overzealous” enforcement understates the power relationship: government agents possess coercive authority, while foreign visitors can decline only by taking their money elsewhere.

The market cannot outrun the state

The administration’s economic posture, as presented here, is internally destructive. It celebrates commerce while imposing political costs that make commerce less attractive. The reported decline in visitor spending is not an abstraction: it means less revenue for airlines, hotels, restaurants, cultural institutions and workers whose livelihoods depend on international arrivals.

Businesses can advertise harder or cut prices, but they cannot reassure visitors that federal officers will not search their devices. Private industry is being asked to compensate for a deterrent created by public authority.

The framing still overreaches

AlterNet’s claim that Trump is “single-handedly” crushing tourism is sharper than the evidence supplied. The reporting presents falling visits, higher travel costs and expanding device searches, but it does not isolate the effect of each policy or exclude every other cause.

That exaggeration is unnecessary. The documented pattern is already damning: federal choices foreseeably made travel more expensive and entry more invasive while international spending declined. Precision assigns responsibility more effectively than a slogan that claims total causation without proving it.

The systemic error

This is what punitive government looks like when its costs escape the population initially targeted. An immigration crackdown advertised as control reaches tourists, employers and local economies because coercive systems do not remain neatly confined.

The larger pattern is an administration using institutional power to maximize suspicion at the border while treating the resulting economic damage as incidental. The weaker actors—the traveler subjected to a search and the business losing customers—are left to adjust. The officials who designed the conditions retain the power, and everyone downstream receives the bill.
