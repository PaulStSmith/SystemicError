Judge calls 'massive fire drill' because the Trump admin 'can't be trusted'

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 13, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that U.S. District Judge Ana Reyes ordered a 72-hour pause on work at a Washington, D.C., golf course after federal officials removed more than 500 trees, including historic cherry trees, despite the court’s repeated expectation that the government would provide meaningful notice. Government lawyers filed notice at 4:55 p.m. on the Friday before a holiday weekend; fewer than 24 hours later, the trees were gone.

National Park Service supervisor Kevin Greiss testified that discussions began on Oct. 6 at 5:39 p.m., while the court and plaintiffs were not notified until the afternoon of Oct. 9. Employees testified that Interior Department deputy chief of staff Greg Wischer directed the Park Service to proceed. With another 300 trees facing removal, Reyes imposed the pause and demanded Wischer’s testimony, saying she needed to determine whether government declarations about the plan, timing, and notice had misled her.

Reyes did not hold anyone in contempt, noting that she had never converted her expectations into a formal order. But she said the episode destroyed the trust on which her earlier refusal to issue a temporary restraining order or preliminary injunction had rested.

The power was federal

The trees did not remove themselves, and frontline Park Service employees did not possess the decisive authority described in the testimony. According to the account presented in court, Wischer told the agency to move forward. Interior officials controlled the operation, while Justice Department lawyers represented that apparatus before the court.

That is the relevant hierarchy. The institutional power belonged to the executive branch officials who could authorize the work, control its timing, and decide how much warning the court would receive. The damage was completed before meaningful judicial intervention became possible.

Notice engineered to fail

A filing at 4:55 p.m. before a holiday weekend may satisfy the thinnest conception of notification. It does not provide a realistic opportunity for plaintiffs or a court to respond before crews act the next morning.

The reporting does not establish who selected that precise filing time or prove why it was chosen. It does establish the result: the government preserved the appearance of notice while eliminating its practical value. That distinction matters because procedural compliance can become an instrument of evasion when powerful officials control both the clock and the machinery already in motion.

Accountability dissolved downward

The employees called before Reyes reportedly denied being the decision-makers. That may accurately describe their limited authority, but it also shows how bureaucratic systems disperse responsibility after executing a clear command. Each subordinate can describe a narrow task while the government’s collective action becomes strangely ownerless.

The Justice Department’s claim that Wischer would reveal nothing important compounds the problem. Testimony identified him as the official who directed the Park Service to proceed. Treating his testimony as irrelevant asks the court to investigate an order without questioning the reported source of that order. Reyes correctly recognized the institutional absurdity.

Trust is not judicial control

Reyes had declined stronger intervention because she believed the government would act in good faith. Once the trees were removed, that restraint could not restore them. Her eventual 72-hour notice requirement may protect the remaining 300 trees, but it also exposes the cost of relying on executive assurances when formal orders are available.

This is not a story about a judge suddenly becoming emotional or about generalized “confusion” inside government. It is about an administration obtaining room to act because a court withheld coercive oversight, then using that room to create an irreversible fact on the ground. The record described by AlterNet leaves unresolved whether officials lied, but the timing and chain of command plainly justified Reyes’s inquiry.

The systemic error

Institutions often extend credibility to government officials as though official status itself were evidence of good faith. That presumption is a form of power: it grants the executive time, discretion, and protection from immediate restraint. Weaker parties must prove danger in advance; the government receives trust until the damage demonstrates that trust was misplaced.

The larger pattern is government by accomplished fact. Act quickly, distribute responsibility across offices, litigate relevance afterward, and leave courts debating remedies for something already destroyed. When institutional trust replaces enforceable limits, accountability arrives late—and power learns that lateness is enough.
