Consumers reportedly placed fewer orders on Black Friday, yet another indication that consumer confidence is down. Despite the decrease, sales hit record highs, thanks in part to inflation and increased use of credit, including “Buy Now, Pay Later” (BNPL) programs. But the White House sees “good news” in the numbers.

“Although U.S. consumers spent more this Black Friday compared to last year,” Reuters reported, “price increases hampered online demand, according to Salesforce, with shoppers purchasing fewer items at checkout compared to last year.”

Newsweek, citing data from Salesforce published by Forbes, reported that “order volume fell by about 1 percent year over year, while average selling prices were up 7 percent—indicating that much of the growth was caused by inflation rather than any uptick in shopping enthusiasm.”

According to Politico, “White House economic adviser Kevin Hassett said Sunday that record Black Friday sales underscore the administration’s optimism on economic growth despite concerns around tariffs and inflation.”

Hassett cited Black Friday’s sales numbers “as a sign of economic resilience,” Politico added.

“The good news around Thanksgiving is the sign of something that’s stronger to come,” Hassett said on Sunday. “I think the reason is that incomes are up this year is, we had a great jobs report, and with strong income and the government shutdown over so that people have pent-up demand as well, I think that we’re looking at a great recovery from a weak few weeks because of the shutdown.”

Our Analysis:

The Mirage of Economic Success: A Closer Look at Black Friday’s “Record” Sales

In a recent flurry of reports and statements, the White House and various media outlets have painted this year’s Black Friday sales as a beacon of economic resilience and growth. However, a closer examination of the underlying data reveals a starkly different narrative—one that is far from the rosy picture of consumer confidence and economic strength being propagated.

Sales Up, Confidence Down: The Inflation Deception

First and foremost, the assertion that record high sales indicate a robust economic outlook is fundamentally flawed. According to Reuters, consumers indeed spent more this Black Friday compared to last year, but this was not due to an uptick in shopping enthusiasm. Instead, Salesforce data highlighted by Forbes via Newsweek shows that order volume actually fell by about 1 percent year over year, while average selling prices were up 7 percent. This discrepancy clearly demonstrates that inflation, rather than genuine consumer demand, is the real driver behind the so-called record sales.

The Dubious Cheer of the White House

Despite these concerning indicators, the White House, through the voice of economic adviser Kevin Hassett, chose to focus on the superficial success of Black Friday sales as a sign of economic resilience. Hassett’s optimism, as reported by Politico, hinges on a great jobs report and the end of a government shutdown—factors that he believes indicate a strong recovery on the horizon. However, this perspective conveniently ignores the critical issue that even though more money was spent, it was on fewer items, signaling that individuals are not actually buying more but are rather being forced to spend more due to rising prices.

The Alarming Rise of “Buy Now, Pay Later”

Adding another layer of concern is the significant reliance on Buy Now, Pay Later (BNPL) programs, such as those offered by Klarna and Affirm, which were predicted to account for over $760 million in Black Friday spending, as per Newsweek citing Adobe data. The 11 percent expected increase in BNPL use for November and December suggests a growing dependency on debt to sustain spending habits, a trend that is hardly indicative of a healthy economy. CNN’s endorsement of BNPL as an “important payment option” for the holiday season glosses over the potential long-term financial vulnerabilities these programs can create for consumers.

Conclusion: A Reality Check

The narrative of a thriving economy, as suggested by record Black Friday sales, crumbles under scrutiny. The decrease in order volume juxtaposed with the inflation-driven increase in spending paints a picture not of economic strength, but of consumers struggling under the weight of rising prices. Furthermore, the growing reliance on BNPL schemes is a ticking time bomb that could lead to increased household debt.

In this context, the optimism expressed by the White House and its economic advisers appears misplaced at best and misleading at worst. It is imperative that we look beyond the surface and understand the real dynamics at play, acknowledging the troubling signs of an economy that is far from the robust health being advertised.
