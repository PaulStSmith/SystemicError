'Proof' Trump 'secretly' wants Republicans to lose the midterms

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosSeptember 6, 20263 min read

Trump Is Burning the Party Down to Keep the Throne

The wrong question

The source article gets trapped in a familiar Washington parlor game: does Trump secretly want Republicans to lose? That question flatters him with mystery. The plainer reading is uglier and more useful. Trump is governing like a man whose only durable loyalty is to himself, even when that means detonating his own party’s electoral prospects.

The reporting gives enough to see the pattern without psychoanalyzing him. The war with Iran, the trade fight with Canada, the push for unpopular data centers, and the embrace of scandal-ridden candidates are all moving parts in one structure: power concentrated in one office, with the party reduced to a delivery system for his impulses.

Who actually holds power

Trump holds the real institutional leverage. The article says Capitol Hill has served as a rubber stamp, backed by a largely compliant Supreme Court, which has let him rule with near-unchecked power. That is the key fact. Republicans are not co-authors of policy so much as hostages to a man who can command the state while treating them as expendable campaign fodder.

That matters because it changes the moral accounting. This is not a situation where a party is merely suffering from bad messaging. Its leadership has surrendered agency to a president who can impose costs on farmers, commuters, borrowers, and candidates alike, then leave them to explain the damage.

The damage is not accidental

The source points to concrete consequences: rising petrol prices, higher grocery bills, a climbing national debt that feeds into interest rates, and more expensive credit card and mortgage payments. These are not abstract “headwinds.” They are political consequences created by deliberate decisions. The war is described as a reckless, failed war of choice. The trade war with Canada is also a war of choice. That language matters because it strips away the comfort of inevitability.

Trump is not being punished by events he cannot control. He is generating the events. The voters bearing the costs did not choose those costs. Republican incumbents now have to defend them anyway.

The scapegoating machine

The article also catches Trump insulting the very people he is supposed to represent. He dismisses communities that oppose data centers as backwards and poor. That is not just crude rhetoric. It is an exercise in domination: tell voters their resistance is stupidity, then demand that they thank you for the privilege of being overridden.

This is how authoritarian politics works in practice. It does not merely lie; it humiliates. It does not simply mislead; it conditions supporters to accept harm as proof of strength. The party’s job becomes absorbing the fallout while the leader keeps the spotlight.

Republicans are the instrument, not the victim

There is a temptation to treat the GOP as collateral damage in Trump’s personal drama. That would be too generous. Republican officials helped build the conditions for this by tolerating his rule, feeding his power, and accepting a Congress that functions as decoration. They are now learning the cost of serving as a vessel for someone who regards institutions as disposable and allies as temporary.

The source is right to note the possibility that Trump sees defeat not as repudiation but as proof of his indispensability. That is not strategic genius. It is the logic of a movement organized around grievance, personality, and submission rather than governing competence. If the party loses ground, he can still claim the brand belongs to him.

The larger pattern

The deeper lesson is not about one bad cycle or one erratic president. It is about what happens when a major party yields its institutional spine to a leader who treats state power as personal property. Once that bargain is made, policy becomes theater, voters become scenery, and electoral damage becomes acceptable so long as the ruler remains central.

That is the system’s error: not confusion, but surrender. Trump is not merely sabotaging Republicans. He is demonstrating that a party can be hollowed out from the top and still keep moving, while the public absorbs the cost.
