Judge Cannon comes back to haunt Trump as DOJ struggles to revive charges

The Justice Department Wants Its Political Prosecution Back

The Case Was Power, Not Paperwork

The Justice Department is asking an appeals court to revive indictments against New York Attorney General Letitia James and former FBI Director James Comey after a judge concluded that Lindsey Halligan—the sole prosecutor who signed the indictments and presented them to the grand jury—had been unlawfully appointed. DOJ attorney Henry Whitaker reduced that defect to an “error in the title.” The judges were plainly unconvinced.

That description is not merely weak. It conceals the central fact: prosecutorial authority comes from lawful appointment, not presidential preference. Halligan was not a properly empowered official with a clerical mistake on her business card. According to the defense argument accepted below, she exercised federal prosecutorial power without legal authority to do so.

Trump’s Justice Department Made the Choice

The institutional power here belongs to the president and the Justice Department. James and Comey may be prominent figures, but neither selected the prosecutor, conferred federal authority, presented these cases to the grand jury, or decided to appeal their dismissal.

Trump’s administration chose Halligan, his former personal attorney, to pursue two people he regarded as enemies. It then chose to defend her appointment. Now it wants the judiciary to preserve indictments produced through that disputed authority. The government is not an innocent victim of procedural complexity. It created the defect and is demanding insulation from its consequences.

“Respect the Grand Jury” Is a Distraction

Whitaker urged the court to “respect” the grand juries’ decisions. That appeal shifts attention from the officials who controlled the prosecutions to citizens who heard evidence presented by the government’s chosen advocate.

Grand juries do not appoint prosecutors or determine whether those prosecutors lawfully hold office. Invoking their decisions turns a dispute about executive compliance into a sentimental argument about institutional deference. But the legitimacy of an indictment cannot be separated from the legitimacy of the official who obtained it.

This is a familiar maneuver: powerful officials make a legally dubious choice, then hide behind a downstream institution once challenged. The grand jury becomes a shield for executive conduct it did not authorize and could not supervise.

Trump’s Precedent Has Become His Problem

The hypocrisy is unusually clean. Judge Aileen Cannon dismissed the classified-documents case against Trump after describing special counsel Jack Smith as a private citizen exercising the power of a United States attorney without lawful appointment. Now James and Comey are relying on the same basic principle against Halligan.

DOJ tried to distinguish the cases by arguing that Halligan could have been appointed under other statutory authorities. Chief Judge Albert Diaz supplied the obvious answer: she was not.

“Could have” is not legal authority. It is an admission that the administration had lawful mechanisms available and failed to use them. The government wants the court to treat an appointment requirement as optional when it obstructs Trump’s prosecutions, after that same kind of argument helped destroy a prosecution of Trump.

Retaliation Wrapped in Procedure

The source describes James and Comey as Trump’s perceived enemies. That political context cannot prove the charges themselves were retaliatory, but it makes the appointment controversy more consequential, not less. When an administration prosecutes presidential antagonists through a former personal lawyer whose authority a court found unlawful, procedural safeguards are the barrier between public justice and personalized state punishment.

Calling the problem “strong medicine” likewise misstates the stakes. Dismissal is severe because unauthorized prosecution is severe. A government cannot invoke the enormous coercive power of criminal law and then plead for leniency over who was permitted to wield it.

Law for Enemies, Exceptions for Power

This dispute reveals a broader governing pattern: rules are treated as weapons when they burden opponents and technicalities when they restrain the executive. Institutional language—grand-jury respect, harmless error, excessive remedy—then launders that double standard into something resembling neutral legal administration.

The real question is not whether judges should overlook a flawed title. It is whether presidential power can manufacture prosecutorial authority after the fact, especially when directed at designated enemies. The Justice Department’s position is that its failure to appoint a lawful prosecutor should not cost it the prosecutions. That is not respect for law. It is a demand that power be excused from the conditions that make its exercise legitimate.
