'All over the place': Fox News slammed as 'shaping narrative' in Pete Hegseth interview

U.S. Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth attends the annual White House Easter Egg Roll event, on the South Lawn of the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., April 21, 2025. REUTERS/Ken Cedeno

In the wake of his second scandal in weeks, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth appeared on Fox and Friends Tuesday for a softball interview that was immediately blasted by viewers.

Blaming leakers inside the Pentagon, Hegseth complained that he'd done nothing wrong after a Sunday report claimed he's shared military tactics in a Signal chat with his wife and brother. Just weeks earlier, he was accused of doing the same thing, but that time including an Atlantic journalist.

CNN Anchor Kaitlan Collins noted Hegseth claimed “staffers of his that were fired were leaking, and suggests an investigation could result in DOJ recommendations.”

"Disgruntled former employees are peddling things to try to save their a--,” Hegseth had said.

"This interview was a bit all over the place," said CNN reporter Natasha Bertrand. "On the one hand he said this investigation had led to these individuals being escorted out of the building; on the other hand he said that the investigation could ultimately exonerate them.

"But one thing is clear: he is using them as a scapegoat for the larger story that has come out over the last day or so about how he did use a second signal chat to communicate...about sensitive military operations."

White House correspondent for USA TODAY, Joey Garrison noted, that Hegseth did not deny he sent information to his wife and brother, but claimed it was not war plans.

Washington Post political reporter Azi Paybarah noted that Hegseth was asked about the “deep state” involvement in the scandal by Fox News' hosts before he is asked why he included his wife in a Signal chat.

Reader Jim Vigotty replied, "Fox is shaping the narrative. Hegseth the victim. Not Hegseth the drunk f--- up."

And Scripps News National Correspondent Elizabeth Landers noted Hegseth’s claims what he shared was “informal [and] unclassified"

Earlier this week the New York Times cited sources that the Defense Secretary used his personal phone to send information about U.S. military operations in a 13-person Signal group chat, including his wife and brother.

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Hegseth Scandal: A Symphony of Misdirection and Media Manipulation

In what appears to be a masterclass in deflection and blame-shifting, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth's recent interview on Fox and Friends has sparked outrage and incredulity in equal measure. Hegseth, finding himself embroiled in not one, but two scandals involving the mishandling of sensitive military information, opted for the time-honored tradition of political scapegoating. The interview, a glaring example of softball journalism, did little to address the gravity of the accusations against Hegseth, instead offering him a platform to blame "leakers" for his woes.

The Art of the Scapegoat

Hegseth's claims that disgruntled former employees are the architects of his downfall is as predictable as it is tired. According to Hegseth, these so-called leakers are not whistleblowers but saboteurs. However, the crux of the matter lies not in the act of leaking, but in the content of what was allegedly leaked: sensitive military operations discussed in a casual Signal chat involving Hegseth's wife and brother. The defense of such actions as informal and unclassified conversations is not only deeply troubling but indicative of a profound misunderstanding (or willful ignorance) of operational security.

Media Complicity: Shaping the Narrative

The role of Fox News in shaping the narrative around Hegseth's scandal is both glaring and alarming. By framing Hegseth as a victim of deep state machinations and internal sabotage, Fox News is not merely reporting news; it is actively participating in a disinformation campaign. The focus on the alleged betrayal by former staffers, rather than the potential compromise of military operations, is a deliberate choice that seeks to divert attention from the core issue at hand.

The Deep State Distraction

The invocation of the "deep state" by Fox News hosts prior to addressing the actual allegations against Hegseth is a classic diversion tactic. It's a term loaded with conspiracy and innuendo, designed to shift the focus from Hegseth's actions to an imaginary cabal intent on undermining him. The fact that Hegseth did not outright deny sending sensitive information, but instead quibbled over the nature of that information, is lost in the spectacle of deep state accusations.

Public Reaction: Seeing Through the Narrative

Viewer reactions, as cited in the article, reflect a clear-eyed understanding of the situation. Descriptions of Hegseth as a "victim" are met with skepticism and derision. The public's ability to see through the narrative being crafted around Hegseth is a reassuring sign of the collective critical thinking that is often missing in discourse shaped by partisan media outlets.

Conclusion: The Real Scandal

The Hegseth scandal is not merely about the mishandling of sensitive information; it is a case study in how political figures use media platforms to obfuscate and deflect. It raises serious questions about the integrity of our political and media institutions and their role in safeguarding or undermining national security. As this saga unfolds, one thing is clear: the real scandal is not just what was shared in those Signal chats, but the systemic failures that allowed it to happen and the subsequent attempts to manipulate the narrative.
