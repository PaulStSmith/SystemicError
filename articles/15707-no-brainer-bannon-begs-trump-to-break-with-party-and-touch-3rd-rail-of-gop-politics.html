'No-brainer': Bannon begs Trump to break with party — and touch 3rd rail of GOP politics

President Donald Trump's longtime far-right ally Steve Bannon wants him to touch the third rail of Republican Party politics — and raise taxes on the rich.

Specifically, some within Trump's circle are coming around to the idea that a tax increase on millionaires might be the only way to make his "big, beautiful bill" of tax cuts on everyone else, plus energy deregulation and border security, pencil.

According to The Washington Post, "While the prospect of a tax hike has gotten a largely chilly reception among Republicans on Capitol Hill, Vice President JD Vance and budget director Russell Vought have expressed openness to the idea in internal administration deliberations and are viewed as supportive, said the people, who spoke on the condition of anonymity to describe private talks. Stephen K. Bannon, who served as the president’s chief strategist during his first term, has been publicly urging Trump to endorse the plan in part as a way to defang Democratic attacks on the GOP as the party of the rich."

“This guts the AOC-Bernie ‘oligarchy tour,’” Bannon said, according to the Post. He was referring to recent dual rallies by Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez (D-NY) and Sen. Bernie Sanders (I-VT). “Politically, it’s game, set, match — it’s a no-brainer. This would destroy the Democrats.”

Bannon has notably broken with the GOP on this issue for a while, claiming that bills to reduce taxes on the wealthy are a "racket."

The tax hike could involve creating a new bracket for people making over $5 million, according to the report, which is a suggestion of Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent.

The idea is facing sharp pushback from some of the more traditionally Republican-aligned members of Trump's orbit, the report continued: "Newt Gingrich, Steve Moore and Larry Kudlow have come out strongly against it, arguing the plan undermines the president’s promise to cut taxes and will discourage economic growth, as has the influential Fox News host Sean Hannity."

Meanwhile, key Trump-aligned lawmakers in Congress, like Sen. Ted Cruz (R-TX) and House Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA) have made clear it would be a hard sell.

Part of the problem is that Republicans have gotten swift blowback, even from some members of their own party, such as Sen. Josh Hawley (R-MO), over the level of spending cuts originally envisioned for the bill, which would include harsh cuts to Medicaid.

Regardless, conservative analysts think even a Trump-backed tax hike on the rich would be doomed, with American Action Forum president Doug Holtz Eakin saying, “I don’t see it getting through the Senate Finance Committee — there’s not a chance," and that in the House, "They’ll deflect and say they want to consider all possibilities, but it won’t have the votes. … They’ll see this as a punitive tax on rich people for no reason. They don’t like the politics. They don’t like the economics. They’re not interested."

Our Analysis:

A Bold Move or Political Suicide? Trump and Bannon's Tax Hike Tango

In a surprising pivot that could either be seen as a masterstroke or political hara-kiri, President Donald Trump and his far-right consigliere, Steve Bannon, are flirting with the idea of raising taxes on the rich—a concept so alien to Republican orthodoxy it's akin to touching the third rail while doing a handstand. This isn't just a minor policy adjustment; it's akin to a seismic shift in the GOP's tectonic plates, with potential repercussions that could reverberate through the party for years to come.

The Unlikely Crusaders

Bannon, the erstwhile chief strategist and architect of Trump's "America First" ethos, has long positioned himself as a populist maverick within the GOP. His endorsement of a tax hike on millionaires, as reported by The Washington Post, is both a departure from traditional conservative dogma and a calculated political gambit. By aligning with this policy, Bannon and Trump seem to be aiming for a double-edged sword: undercut the Democrats' narrative of the GOP as the party of the wealthy elite while simultaneously funding their agenda of tax cuts for the broader populace, energy deregulation, and border security.

The Internal Divide

However, this proposal has opened a chasm within the Trump enclave, drawing ire from figures like Newt Gingrich, Steve Moore, Larry Kudlow, and Sean Hannity—luminaries who have historically been in lockstep with the broader Republican agenda of tax cuts for the economic upper echelon. Their opposition is not just a matter of ideological purity but a fundamental disagreement on the economics, viewing it as antithetical to growth and prosperity.

Congressional Cold Feet

The idea of raising taxes on the wealthy is also facing an uphill battle in Congress, with notable figures like Sen. Ted Cruz (R-TX) and House Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA) signaling their reticence. The skepticism isn't limited to the proposal itself but extends to the broader package of spending cuts, particularly those targeting Medicaid, which have already drawn backlash within the party. The sentiment among conservative analysts, epitomized by Doug Holtz Eakin of the American Action Forum, is one of doom and gloom, with predictions of the proposal's failure to even pass the Senate Finance Committee, let alone a full congressional vote.

A Racket or a Revolution?

Bannon’s characterization of past tax reduction efforts as a "racket" benefiting the wealthy is a stark indictment of his own party's policies. It's a bold, if not brazen, acknowledgment that the GOP's traditional economic stances may not be in the best interest of the broader electorate. Yet, the question remains: Is this proposed tax hike on the rich a genuine attempt to recalibrate the party's priorities towards a more populist, equitable stance, or is it a cynical ploy to defang Democratic criticism and repackage the same old policies in populist wrapping paper?

The Bottom Line

The proposal to raise taxes on the wealthy, as championed by Trump and Bannon, is nothing short of a political gamble. It challenges decades of Republican tax policy, risks alienating a significant portion of the party's base and donor class, and faces formidable opposition both within the party and in Congress. Whether this move is a strategic masterstroke that redefines the GOP's image or a miscalculation that exacerbates internal divisions will depend on its reception by the Republican rank-and-file and the electorate at large. In either case, it underscores a tumultuous ideological struggle within the party—a struggle that will define its identity and direction for years to come.
