'The case is over': Ex-judge floored as prosecutor's 'ignorance' kills Trump's revenge bid

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 20, 20254 min read

Former FBI Director James Comey testifies in front of the Senate Intelligence Committee during hearing in the Hart Senate Office building June 8, 2017, in Washington DC. (Photo credit: mark reinstein / Shutterstock)

Former Miami-Dade County Court Judge Jeff Swartz concluded that the Department of Justice’s case against former FBI Director James Comey was essentially “over” after interim U.S. Attorney Lindsey Halligan admitted that the grand jury never saw the government’s final indictment.

In a hearing on Wednesday, Halligan told U.S. District Judge Michael Nachmanoff that the full grand jury never saw the final two-count indictment against Comey.

“The foreperson and another grand juror was also present,” Halligan explained to the judge.

Speaking to CNN, Swartz noted that there had been two different indictments. The grand jury did not approve the first one. Instead, Halligan used the second indictment to charge Comey — even though the full grand jury had not seen it.

The former judge said Nachmanoff essentially wanted to know: “How did you get the jurors back in the jury room to look at this second indictment with only two counts in seven minutes?”

“And all that turned out was, all that happened was that Lindsey Halligan took it to the foreperson and one other person and had them sign it,” he noted. “Those jurors never voted on the second indictment. So therefore, there was no indictment. And if there was no indictment, Comey wasn’t charged. He wasn’t charged. The statute of limitations ran, and the case is over.”

“What we saw was the crossroads between arrogance and ignorance,” he continued. “And they both crossed. Halligan being the ignorance, arrogance coming out of Pam Bondi and Todd Blanche. And they cross it. It’s a very crucial point, and there’s no cure for it.”

Our Analysis:

Analysis of DOJ’s Flawed Indictment Process Against James Comey

In what can only be described as a staggering display of procedural ineptitude, the Department of Justice’s case against former FBI Director James Comey has crumbled into legal dust. The revelation that the grand jury never saw the government’s final indictment against Comey is not just a minor oversight; it’s a catastrophic failure of the justice system’s basic mechanisms. Let’s dissect this debacle, spearheaded by interim U.S. Attorney Lindsey Halligan, through the lens of former Miami-Dade County Court Judge Jeff Swartz’s commentary.

The Grand Jury’s Phantom Indictment

At the heart of this farce is the astonishing admission by Halligan that the full grand jury never reviewed the final two-count indictment against Comey. Instead, a clandestine presentation to the foreperson and one other grand juror somehow sufficed in Halligan’s eyes. This procedural shortcut not only flouts the fundamental principles of grand jury proceedings but also casts a shadow over the integrity of the entire indictment process.

Swartz’s insights are particularly damning. The revelation that there were two different indictments — the first rejected by the grand jury, the second mysteriously approved without a full grand jury vote — paints a picture of a justice system in disarray. The former judge’s question, relayed through CNN, underscores the absurdity of the situation: how could a legitimate indictment be procured in mere minutes without proper grand jury deliberation?

A Crossroads of Arrogance and Ignorance

Swartz’s characterization of the mishandling as “the crossroads between arrogance and ignorance” is both scathing and apt. Halligan’s actions, representative of ignorance, combined with the arrogance attributed to Pam Bondi and Todd Blanche, have not only jeopardized a high-profile case but also exposed a worrying vulnerability in the justice system’s operational integrity.

This situation transcends the mere dropping of charges against a former FBI director; it raises alarming questions about the procedural safeguards within our legal processes. If a case of this magnitude can evaporate due to procedural incompetence, what does that say about the countless other indictments processed under similar oversight?

The Inescapable Conclusion

There’s no dancing around it: if there was no legitimate indictment, Comey was never officially charged. With the statute of limitations now expired, the case against him disintegrates into nothingness. This outcome is not just a technicality; it’s a glaring testament to a failure that should concern anyone vested in the fairness and efficacy of our legal system.

Swartz’s parting words about there being “no cure” for this crossroads of arrogance and ignorance should send chills down the spine of anyone who believes in the principles of justice and accountability. The Comey case may be over, but the implications of its mishandling will reverberate for years to come, serving as a cautionary tale of what happens when legal protocols are not just ignored, but seemingly disregarded with impunity.

In conclusion, the Department of Justice’s failure to properly manage the indictment process against James Comey is not just an embarrassment; it’s a stark reminder of the fragility of our legal system’s procedural foundations. The case may be over, but the lessons it imparts are enduring and deeply troubling.
