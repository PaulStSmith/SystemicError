The GOP is dying right in front of our eyes

Based on an article by D. Earl Stephens

Paulo SantosNovember 13, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet argues that the Republican Party is facing possible political collapse after Democrats reportedly won overwhelming victories in the 2026 midterms. The article says Democrats were headed toward an 80-to-90-seat House majority and an eight-to-12-seat Senate majority, gained hundreds of state legislative seats, secured trifectas in 22 states, and won governorships in all 10 traditional presidential battlegrounds. Those results were still being counted when the piece was written.

The article attributes the Republican defeat largely to Donald Trump and contends that his election-night response—claiming Republicans would have won had he been on the ballot—showed no willingness to change course. It further reports that Trump installed Stephen Miller as White House chief of staff two days later, while House Republicans selected Jim Jordan as their leader.

From those developments, the author predicts another Republican rout in 2028 and even the party’s possible extinction. But the article also quotes Brookings cautioning that midterm results do not reliably determine the next presidential election.

Power survived the wave

The voters described here imposed a severe electoral penalty. They did not erase Republican power.

Trump still controlled the executive branch at the time of the report. Republicans still held 18 state trifectas. A conservative Supreme Court remained capable of shaping federal policy. State governments, courts, administrative agencies and party institutions do not vanish because one election produces a landslide.

Calling the GOP “dead” confuses defeat with disarmament. A party can lose nationally while retaining enough institutional machinery to obstruct legislation, restrict rights, entrench favorable rules and prepare its return. The article’s victory lap understates the durability of the very power structure it condemns.

Republicans made their choice

The central act of agency was not Trump’s refusal to become reflective. Expecting self-correction from a leader whose public response was to deny responsibility mistakes a repeated political strategy for a personality defect.

Republican officials had choices. According to AlterNet, House Republicans answered the defeat by elevating Jim Jordan, a figure identified with the party’s combative right wing. They continued operating under Trump’s dominance rather than using their institutional authority to break with him. Trump exerted power, but elected Republicans enabled that power because resistance threatened their standing inside the party.

The article sometimes lets them hide behind cowardice. Fear may explain submission; it does not cancel responsibility. These officials are not hostages to Trump. They are political actors who repeatedly decided that his coalition was more valuable than accountability.

Extinction is the wrong frame

The dinosaur metaphor turns politics into natural history, as though parties disappear through evolution rather than organization, money, law and strategic adaptation. Republican decline is not an automatic process. Neither is Democratic ascendancy.

The article’s own historical caveat punctures its forecast: major midterm losses have been followed by presidential victories for the defeated president’s party, while other waves have carried into the next cycle. Declaring that precedent irrelevant does not make it so. A reported Democratic popular-vote margin of 13 points in one midterm cannot, by itself, establish a filibuster-proof Senate or a Democratic presidency two years later.

The same overreach appears in the claim that Texas has effectively entered battleground status. The reported results may justify treating more states as competitive. They do not convert possibility into permanence.

The stronger indictment

The most consequential story is not that Republicans may cease to exist. It is that electoral punishment reportedly failed to produce visible moderation among the officials still controlling the party.

Trump denied responsibility. House Republicans chose Jordan. The party remained organized around grievance, confrontation and loyalty to its leader. Those were decisions made by people with institutional power, not symptoms of collective confusion.

AlterNet weakens that indictment when it substitutes insults and fantasies of annihilation for an exact account of responsibility. Stephen Miller’s elevation is politically significant because of the authority attached to the chief-of-staff position and the agenda he represents; calling him a Nazi without establishing that characterization in the supplied reporting adds heat while sacrificing precision.

Defeat is not disposal

A landslide can interrupt a governing project, transfer offices and expose the limits of a coalition. It cannot permanently settle the political conflict that produced that coalition.

The systemic lesson is harsher than the extinction story. Parties survive catastrophic losses when their donors, media networks, state organizations and officeholders retain incentives to rebuild. If Republican officials continue choosing Trump’s politics after defeat, they own the consequences. If Democrats mistake a wave for permanent realignment, they may surrender the advantage before those consequences arrive.

Political power does not die from embarrassment. It recedes when opponents defeat it repeatedly, dismantle the structures that protect it and govern well enough to make its return less likely.
