Americans are revolting against Trump's right-wing Christian makeover: report

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 20, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The revolt is less dramatic than the headline suggests, but the rejection is real: polling cited by Baptist News Global indicates that most Americans do not want government organized around Christian supremacy. Christian nationalism remains a minority ideology nationally even as it has become a majority position inside the Republican Party and a defining commitment among white evangelical Protestants.

That distinction matters. The movement does not need majority support to exercise power. It needs control of a major party, disciplined voters and access to officials willing to carry its leaders and ideas into institutions such as the Pentagon.

A minority ideology with a governing vehicle

Robert P. Jones, president of the Public Religion Research Institute, said surveys show broad support for pluralistic democracy rather than a political order that ranks Christians above everyone else—or white Christians above everyone else. Only about one-quarter of independents and one-fifth of Democrats qualify as adherents or sympathizers of authoritarian ideologies, including religiously based versions, he said during a webinar on religion and politics.

Republicans are the exception. According to the polling discussed in the report, 56 percent of Republicans believe government and society should be run by conservative Christians. White evangelical Protestants register even stronger support at 67 percent. Hispanic Protestants, at 54 percent, were the only other religious group in which a majority supported Christian nationalism.

“It has become the majority view of the current Republican Party,” Jones said, as well as the position of roughly two-thirds of white evangelical Protestants.

That is the operative political fact. Christian nationalists may be losing demographic ground and repelling much of the country, but the Republican Party gives their project institutional reach. Calling them merely “fringe” risks confusing public popularity with political capacity. A faction embedded in one of two major parties can shape government without ever persuading most Americans.

The distance between Texas evangelicals and other voters

Jones pointed to Texas exit polling as evidence that white evangelical voters are moving further from the rest of the electorate. He described them as “pulling away from all the rest of the voters in the state,” even as their share of the population declines.

White evangelicals now make up 13 percent of Texans, according to the report, down from 19 percent a decade ago. Their turnout remains higher than that of other Texans, continuing a long-established pattern, but mobilization cannot by itself reverse demographic contraction.

It can, however, magnify a shrinking bloc’s influence. High participation gives Republican officials an incentive to satisfy these voters even when their preferred system of government lacks broad public support. The result is not rule by popular religious consensus. It is minority power converted into policy through partisan organization and unequal turnout.

The word “revolt” therefore overstates what the evidence establishes. The reporting describes sustained public opposition, widening ideological separation and demographic decline—not an organized uprising against a newly documented Trump policy. It also supplies no specific act by Trump around which such a revolt has formed. The sharper story is that Christian nationalism remains nationally unpopular while retaining a powerful home inside Trump’s political coalition.

Extremism with access to the Pentagon

Sociologist Samuel Perry argued that Christian nationalists’ own public advocacy has exposed how extreme some of their commitments are. Baptist News Global highlighted Doug Wilson, an Idaho Calvinist pastor and self-described Christian nationalist who has advocated repealing the 19th Amendment, which guarantees women the right to vote.

Wilson has also led prayer services at the Pentagon for Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, according to the report.

That connection is more consequential than the spectacle of provocative social-media posts. A pastor who supports stripping women of voting rights is not politically important simply because his views are “bizarre.” His importance lies in his access to the country’s military leadership. The Pentagon did not become a venue for his ministry by demographic inevitability; officials with authority permitted it.

Treating Christian nationalism primarily as an eccentric subculture can obscure that chain of agency. Its advocates speak openly, Republican voters supply electoral leverage, and government officials decide whether to grant them legitimacy and proximity to power.

Church rule without church attendance

The coalition also contains a revealing contradiction. The report notes criticism that some outspoken Christian-nationalist figures post online during church hours and that many MAGA supporters who champion church-directed government do not regularly attend church.

That does not make their politics less potent. It suggests that “Christian” can function here less as a religious practice than as a political identity—a claim about who properly belongs, who should govern and whose rights may be subordinated. The proposed hierarchy is the point, whether every advocate occupies a pew on Sunday or not.

America is not uniformly embracing that hierarchy. The evidence presented by Baptist News Global points in the opposite direction: most Americans favor pluralism, white evangelicals represent a shrinking share of Texas, and Christian nationalism is concentrated within particular partisan and religious constituencies.

But demographic decline is not self-executing defeat. A shrinking faction can remain formidable when it votes consistently, controls a party’s incentives and receives invitations from officials who command the institutions of the state.
