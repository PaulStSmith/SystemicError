Trump humiliates top aide as insider warns of first-term chaos: report

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 14, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe president holds the powerLoyalty displaced procedureGossip obscures governanceChaos is a choice with consequencesThe system behind the spectacle

What happened

President Donald Trump chose conservative commentator Katie Zacharia as White House press secretary without consulting chief of staff Susie Wiles, according to AlterNet’s account of Daily Mail reporting. Four unnamed sources said the decision took roughly 72 hours and lacked the expected vetting process. Trump reportedly acted after seeing Zacharia on Fox News, consulted Boris Epshteyn and outgoing press secretary Karoline Leavitt, and personally offered Zacharia the job.

Wiles had reportedly preferred commentator Scott Jennings and expected Trump to wait until after the November midterm elections. She learned of Zacharia’s appointment from Leavitt rather than Trump. Administration sources interpreted the move as part of a struggle over staffing and loyalty, while some officials reportedly feared broader personnel changes. Zacharia, who previously spent six weeks at the Department of Homeland Security before leaving amid internal tensions, was said to want a working relationship with Wiles and not to be planning immediate reprisals or staff removals.

The president holds the power

The headline casts Wiles’s embarrassment as the event. It is not. The consequential fact is that Trump used presidential authority to bypass the official responsible for managing his White House and installed a principal spokesperson through a compressed, reportedly irregular process.

Wiles may wield considerable influence, but she serves at Trump’s discretion. He made the appointment. Epshteyn and Leavitt advised him. Leavitt approved Zacharia and then informed Wiles. Whatever internal factions wanted, the institutional hierarchy was never ambiguous.

Loyalty displaced procedure

One administration source framed the dispute as a choice between someone loyal to Wiles and someone loyal to Trump. That defense is more revealing than exculpatory. It treats personal allegiance as the legitimate standard for selecting an official who will communicate on behalf of the presidency.

The reported absence of normal vetting matters more than the insult to Wiles. A press secretary occupies a public office, not a private court appointment. When staffing turns on a president’s favorable television impression and competing claims of personal loyalty, competence and institutional accountability become secondary.

Gossip obscures governance

AlterNet leans heavily on the language of humiliation, “microaggressions,” and palace intrigue. That framing directs attention toward Wiles as a wounded power broker and away from the governing method the episode reportedly displays.

The unnamed claims that Wiles is “power-hungry” or wants people she can control cannot be independently assessed from this account. They may describe a real factional dispute, or they may be interested attacks from rival camps. Either way, personality gossip should not substitute for the documented chain of action: Trump bypassed his chief of staff, accelerated the selection, and made the offer himself.

Chaos is a choice with consequences

Officials reportedly described the appointment as evidence that Trump is returning to the disorder associated with his first term. But “chaos” can blur responsibility. This was not weather descending on the West Wing. It was the foreseeable result of a president choosing an improvised, personality-driven process over the structure his chief of staff was supposed to enforce.

The immediate consequence is uncertainty among staff who now fear a wider post-midterm reshuffle. The larger cost is institutional: aides spend their energy anticipating personal interventions, cultivating access, and defending factions rather than following durable lines of authority.

The system behind the spectacle

This story reveals a recurring pattern of personalized executive power. Formal managers can impose discipline only while the president permits it; television visibility can outweigh internal procedure; and anonymous aides narrate policy-adjacent decisions as feuds among courtiers.

Reducing that pattern to Trump “humiliating” Wiles makes the episode smaller than it is. Her status is not the public stake. The public stake is a White House in which administrative process reportedly yielded to impulse, loyalty tests, and factional maneuvering—and where insiders recognized the resulting instability only after the person with actual power chose to produce it.
