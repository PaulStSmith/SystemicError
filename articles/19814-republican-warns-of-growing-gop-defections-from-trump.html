Republican warns of growing GOP 'defections' from Trump

U.S. President Donald Trump attends a ceremony marking the 24th anniversary of the September 11, 2001, attacks on the United States at the Pentagon, in Washington D.C., U.S., September 11, 2025. REUTERS Evelyn Hockstein

A prominent House Republican who successfully advanced bipartisan legislation to release the Epstein files is predicting there will be more GOP “defections” once the primaries are over.

U.S. Rep. Thomas Massie (R-KY) told Politico that because Speaker Mike Johnson’s majority is so thin, “on any given day, I would just need one or two of my own co-conspirators to get something done” that goes against the Trump administration’s agenda.

He said that “what’s happening is that the retirement caucus is growing and primary days are coming up and passing. Once we get past March, April and May, which contain a large portion of their Republican primaries, I think you’re going to see more defections.”

Massie added that “quietly and privately, people are telling me they agree with me.”

In a surprising revelation, Massie said that House Republicans “are being told every week to stand down, bite their tongue, sit on their hands, do what they’re told, be part of the team and put their brain in neutral.”

Massie also offered several other pointed remarks.

He noted that after President Trump called him a “moron” at the National Prayer Breakfast earlier this month, pastors were “not impressed and I don’t think anybody was impressed by his performance at the prayer breakfast. It was completely political.”

The Kentucky Republican further directed strong criticism toward Attorney General Pam Bondi after being the only member of his party to, as Politico reported, “spar” with her at last week’s contentious congressional hearing.

“When the attorney general is reduced to a stack of pre-prepared insults to deliver, and when the DOJ is responding to my every tweet with additional unredactions, I don’t think I’m going to change what I’m doing just yet,” he said.

Massie described Bondi as looking “weak and frustrated” at the hearing “when she started talking about the Dow Jones, which has literally nothing to do with her job.”

“I thought that looked bad,” he said. He also pointed to her “stack of insults that were pre-prepared — in politics you might call it oppo research — and you could see her shuffling through them to try and find which one matched the person who was trying to ask her a question at the time. She found my card like right at the end, as you can see she was looking for it.”

Our Analysis:

The Art of Political Defection: A Closer Look at Rep. Massie’s Revelations

In a recent Politico interview, U.S. Rep. Thomas Massie (R-KY) has opened a veritable Pandora’s box on the internal dynamics of the House Republicans, revealing a narrative that’s as telling as it is troubling. Massie, known for his bipartisan efforts to release the Epstein files, forecasts a wave of GOP defections post-primary season. His insights provide a rare glimpse into the pressures and paradoxes that define today’s Republican Party, and they demand a rigorous dissection.

The Thin Red Line

Massie points to Speaker Mike Johnson’s razor-thin majority as a critical vulnerability, suggesting that “on any given day, I would just need one or two of my own co-conspirators to get something done” against the Trump administration’s agenda. The term “co-conspirators” is both ironic and illuminating, painting a picture of political maneuvering that’s more akin to a clandestine operation than the democratic process.

The Quiet Before the Political Storm

The claim that more GOP defections will occur after the primary season is particularly notable. Massie’s assertion is grounded in the growing “retirement caucus” and the passage of primary days, hinting at a strategic waiting game being played by certain Republicans. The implication here is clear: political survival, for now, takes precedence over public dissent.

A Chorus of Silent Agreements

Massie’s comment that “quietly and privately, people are telling me they agree with me” is a damning indictment of the current state of political courage within the party. It underscores a pervasive culture of fear and conformity, where dissent is stifled, and agreement is whispered in shadowed corridors rather than proclaimed in the light of day.

Stand Down, Bite Your Tongue

Perhaps the most striking revelation from Massie is the alleged directive for House Republicans to “stand down, bite their tongue, sit on their hands, do what they’re told, be part of the team and put their brain in neutral.” This command reeks of authoritarianism, suggesting a party more interested in obedience than in open debate or democratic principles.

The Prayer Breakfast Incident

Massie’s recounting of President Trump’s derogatory comment towards him at the National Prayer Breakfast — and the subsequent unimpressed response from pastors — is telling. It’s a microcosm of the larger issue at hand: the politicization of spaces and events that should transcend partisan bickering. Trump’s behavior, as described by Massie, is emblematic of a leadership style that prioritizes personal attacks over policy discussions.

Attorney General Pam Bondi Under Fire

Massie’s critique of Attorney General Pam Bondi’s performance at a congressional hearing is scathing. His depiction of Bondi as “weak and frustrated,” armed with a stack of pre-prepared insults, paints a bleak picture of the current state of political discourse. It’s a scenario where opposition research trumps substantive dialogue, and where the Dow Jones is bizarrely invoked in discussions completely unrelated to economic matters.

Conclusion: A Party at a Crossroads

Massie’s revelations, if accurate, depict a Republican Party at a crossroads. The pressures to conform, the strategic silence, and the prioritization of political over public interests reveal deep fractures within the party. As the primary season progresses and the promised defections potentially materialize, one must wonder: will the GOP heed the warning signs and course-correct, or will it continue down a path that prioritizes power over principle?

In the end, Massie’s narrative isn’t just about Republican defections; it’s a cautionary tale about the state of American politics — where the battle for the soul of a party is waged in whispered agreements and silent nods, far removed from the ideals of democratic governance and open debate.
