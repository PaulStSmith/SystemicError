SYSTEMIC ERROR / COMMENTARY

A miscalculated strategy is keeping Democrats out of power: Harvard expert

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosSeptember 4, 20263 min read

The Real Crisis Is Not Left vs. Center. It Is Rule by Patrons.

The article presents Democrats with a familiar dead end: revive Clinton-era moderation or lean into democratic socialism. Its strongest moments come when Theda Skocpol cuts through that staging and names the actual system underneath Trumpism: not “populism” in any serious sense, but a bargain between wealth, grievance, and a ruler who dispenses favors. That is the story worth taking seriously. The rest is party-internal costume drama.

The Wrong Question

The article asks whether Democrats should become more Clinton or more Mamdani, as if the central problem were ideological branding. That is a comforting lie. The actual problem is power: who can organize it, who can spend it, who can convert it into state action, and who gets blamed when institutions fail.

A party does not reclaim authority by choosing the right adjective. It does it by building a coalition that can govern, punish corruption, and deliver material results without surrendering to donors, consultants, or media-scripted fake dilemmas.

Trump Was Never a Populist

Skocpol is right to reject the populist label. Trump, as described here, is not the tribune of the people. He is a political opportunist whose appeals were calculated lies, now paired with “vanity projects,” family enrichment, and alignment with billionaires seeking contracts and regulatory breaks. That is not populism. It is patronage with a mass audience.

Calling that populism launders the arrangement. It turns deliberate deception into a vague mood, and it lets the people at the top of the machine disappear behind the noise of rallies, grievances, and cable-news theatrics.

The Article Smuggles In A Convenient Fog

The piece wants to treat electoral history as evidence that Democrats need better positioning. But the deeper lesson inside the interview is harsher: the party and its analysts have been too willing to confuse voter preference with political power. Polling is not governance. Winning a message war is not the same as controlling institutions, disciplining capital, or building durable public capacity.

Skocpol points to something closer to the truth when she says the country has drifted into “patrimonial corrupt capitalism,” where business leaders seek favors from the ruler. That is the real operating system. Everything else is narration.

Democrats’ Own Complacency Is Part Of The Problem

The Clinton achievements cited in the article, including tax increases on the wealthy, surpluses, and job growth, are presented as proof of effective Democratic rule. But they are also evidence of a party that too often mistakes macroeconomic management for democratic reconstruction. Balance sheets are not power. Labor organization is not a surplus. A booming labor market is not a durable answer to deindustrialization, class fragmentation, or the captured state.

The left is right to say centrism alone hollowed out social trust and left working people exposed. But the lesson is not that Democrats should simply lurch left in slogans. The lesson is that they need an actual governing project: public investment, worker power, anti-monopoly politics, and institutions that can survive contact with organized wealth.

What Managed Change Would Require

The interview briefly gestures toward the only serious question in the piece: what happens when AI and automation spread into the middle class, and what should replace jobs that cannot and should not come back? That is where politics lives now.

Not in nostalgia for factory jobs. Not in sanctimony about retraining as a universal cure. The real issue is whether Democrats will build a politics capable of managing economic change on behalf of ordinary people, or keep outsourcing that responsibility to market forces and elite reassurance. If they cannot answer that, no amount of ideological calibration will matter.

The Pattern

The broader pattern is simple: American politics keeps mistaking spectacle for structure. Trump’s coalition was built from wealth, resentment, and regulatory capture. Democratic discourse often responds with branding debates and consultant-safe language. That is how oligarchy survives in plain sight.

The democratic task is not to find a gentler slogan for the same broken state. It is to name who holds power, break the patronage logic, and build institutions that serve the public instead of renting themselves out to the highest bidder.
