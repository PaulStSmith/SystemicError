'Sense of dread': Ex-Trump official fears he could stumble i​n to a nuclear war

A former top Trump Department of Homeland Security official is warning that he fears the president could get the U.S. into a nuclear war for which it is not prepared — because he saw the president’s response in his first term, when fears ran high after North Korea launched a missile that could have reached the U.S.

“Few Americans realize how close the president took us to the brink of nuclear war in his first term before aides talked him down,” writes Miles Taylor, the DHS chief of staff during Trump’s first term. “What the public didn’t know at the time — and until years later — was that the president’s team was worried he might start a nuclear war.”

“Today, there’s no one prepared to stop him,” warns Taylor, who writes that Trump “has an eerie fascination with nukes.”

“My fear about this man has always been about his finger on the nuclear button. That’s usually just symbolism when we talk about the presidency. The ‘nuclear button’ is a stand-in for the concept of presidential power and the risks of instability,” says Taylor. “When we’re talking about Trump, it’s not a metaphor.”

During Trump’s first year in office, “the United States came closer to a nuclear conflict than most people realize,” Taylor says. He chastised the president for his “mishandling” of a confrontation with North Korea that “was so serious” that the team at DHS “was forced to do real-life, defensive planning for the possibility of a nuclear strike against the homeland — a situation DHS had never been in since its creation.”

Detailing the events that day, Taylor notes that “North Korea had launched an intercontinental ballistic missile,” its “most powerful weapon yet — the first North Korean missile capable of hitting anywhere in the world, including Washington, D.C.”

As the crisis grew, Trump called acting DHS Secretary Elaine Duke.

“But Trump wasn’t calling to ask about the missile — or even whether his defensive team at DHS was ready to protect the homeland against such a strike had it been the real thing,” Taylor writes. In an “angry” phone call, Trump “wanted to talk about deportations.”

“As Elaine recounted the call to me, her eyes began to well up. A nuclear-capable missile had just ripped through the skies over the Pacific, and the president of the United States was oblivious. All he cared about was getting foreigners off his land.”

DHS had to prepare for the “genuine possibility” that Trump “might stumble us into a nuclear confrontation with North Korea.”

Taylor detailed Trump’s “angry tweets,” in which he “threatened North Korea with ‘fire, fury and frankly power the likes of which this world has never seen before.’ National security officials woke up to these messages on their phones. Stunned. The president almost seemed to welcome the prospect of a global conflagration.”

As the months wore on, whenever DHS “got alerts that the North Koreans were preparing a missile launch, those of us working inside the administration worried it could be the real thing,” says Taylor, “or that the president might say something so stupid that he’d manifest it… or that he would be too distracted to care.”

Now, Trump has not changed, but what has is that “everything that kept him in check” is gone.

Taylor recounts how last year, Trump took to Truth Social to declare that, “Because of other countries testing programs, I have instructed the Department of War to start testing our Nuclear Weapons on an equal basis.”

“That process will begin immediately,” Trump wrote.

“As the president barrels forward with the Iran war, I’m getting the same sense of dread that I had then,” Taylor warns.

Summing up his concerns, he says that, “Regardless of what happens with the Iran war, I want you to remember this. I want you to remember what we’ve learned about how Donald Trump sees his gravest responsibilities as commander-in-chief, how he was gamified war, and how he has flirted with nuclear catastrophe.”

“It is, perhaps, the most urgent reason for Americans to demand the other branches of government do more to keep him in check. Our president is unstable, and there are no longer sensible people around him to send up a flare if he’s ready to do something deadly.”

Our Analysis:

Analysis of Claims Concerning Trump’s Nuclear War Risks

The article under discussion brings to light serious allegations and warnings from Miles Taylor, a former Department of Homeland Security (DHS) chief of staff during Trump’s first term. Taylor’s claims revolve around the fears of a nuclear confrontation instigated by former President Donald Trump, particularly in the context of North Korean missile tests and Trump’s alleged fascination with nuclear weapons. This analysis seeks to dissect the power dynamics, decision-making, and the framing of the article to assess the validity and direction of the criticism.

1. Institutional Power and Decision-Making

Who Holds Institutional Power: In the context of the United States, the President possesses significant authority in matters of national defense and foreign policy, including nuclear strategy. The President, as the Commander-in-Chief of the armed forces, has the ultimate say in military engagements and nuclear launch decisions. However, this power is not absolute and is designed to be checked by other branches of government, notably Congress, which holds the power to declare war, and the judiciary, which can adjudicate on matters of law and constitutionality.

Who Made the Decision: According to Taylor, the decisions and actions that brought the U.S. closer to a nuclear confrontation with North Korea were predominantly made by Trump. Taylor portrays Trump as someone with a dangerous level of interest in nuclear weapons and an inclination towards aggressive policies. The framing suggests that Trump’s decisions and rhetoric significantly increased the risk of nuclear conflict.

2. Framing and Responsibility

The article strongly implies that Trump’s handling of the situation with North Korea and his general demeanor towards nuclear weapons are reckless and pose a significant threat to global security. It underscores a lack of preparedness and a cavalier attitude towards the gravest responsibilities of the presidency. While the criticisms are pointed and alarming, it is essential to critically evaluate whether the article misdirects responsibility towards Trump, portraying him as a sole actor without checks and balances.

Misdirection of Responsibility: The U.S. political system is designed with checks and balances precisely to prevent the kind of unilateral action Taylor fears. The article mentions that during Trump’s first term, his aides were able to talk him down from potentially catastrophic decisions. This indicates that there were, in fact, checks on his power, at least informally, within his own administration. The claim that “today, there’s no one prepared to stop him” seems to overlook the structured resistance within the American government and military that can and has restrained presidential actions in the past. Furthermore, the statement might exaggerate the president’s unilateral ability to initiate nuclear war, ignoring the complex command and control procedures designed to prevent such an eventuality.

False Equivalence and Scapegoating: The article does not present a false equivalence but rather focuses solely on Trump’s actions and personality. It does not attempt to compare his behavior with that of other presidents or leaders in similar situations. However, it could be argued that it engages in a form of scapegoating by attributing broad, systemic risks to a single individual’s whims. While presidential power is vast, the systemic checks on that power, including legal, military, and bureaucratic constraints, are designed to prevent precisely the scenarios Taylor describes.

Conclusion

The warnings from Miles Taylor about the risks posed by Donald Trump’s approach to nuclear weapons and conflict are indeed grave and warrant public concern. However, the analysis suggests that the framing of the article may overemphasize Trump’s individual power and underplay the systemic checks that are in place to prevent unilateral military or nuclear actions by the President. While the personal disposition and decisions of the President are critically important, it is equally vital to recognize the broader system of governance and control that surrounds nuclear decision-making. The article, while shedding light on serious concerns, could benefit from a more nuanced discussion of the institutional and systemic safeguards against the misuse of nuclear power.
