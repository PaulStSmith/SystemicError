The truth about Trump was finally blared across this newspaper's front page

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosJune 24, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Problem Is Not the Insult. It’s the Impunity.

What Happened

A conservative Italian newspaper mocked Donald Trump over a fabricated story about Giorgia Meloni and a G7 photo, then the article pivots to a larger point: Trump’s record is not a matter of style or “controversy,” but of documented abuse, fraud, self-dealing, and war-making. The source’s real target is not one lie. It is the press culture that keeps laundering this man’s conduct into neutral vocabulary.

Power Names Itself

The central fact is simple: Trump is not a marginal provocateur. He is the most powerful political actor in the story, and the damage described comes from decisions made at or near the top of institutional power. He allegedly invented a humiliating anecdote about a foreign head of government. He is tied, in the source’s account, to civil findings of sexual abuse, taped admissions about grabbing women, charity diversion, fraud settlement, memecoin enrichment, and an unauthorized war against Iran.

That is not “polarization.” That is concentrated power producing consequences and then demanding euphemism in return.

Who Enabled What

The people with real agency are not the donors who believed the Eric Trump Foundation, not the students who bought into a fake university, not the small buyers who got burned by the memecoin, and not the voters forced to absorb the fallout. The decision-makers were Trump and the institutions around him that let the pattern continue: family business structures, insiders, compliant media, and a political system too weak or too cowardly to impose durable cost.

The source is right to name the pattern as enabling. Fraud does not survive on charisma alone. It survives because powerful people keep deciding that it is easier to accommodate the powerful man than to stop him.

The Press’s Favorite Lie

The article’s sharpest attack is on “sanewashing,” the press habit of translating obvious misconduct into managerial jargon. That framing is accurate. When a man has been found liable for sexual abuse, has a long public record of crude self-exposure, has been linked to financial extraction, and has allegedly launched an unconstitutional war, calling him “unconventional” is not caution. It is misdirection.

This is where the framing matters. Euphemism does not preserve neutrality. It shifts moral burden away from the actor with power and onto the audience, who are told to treat an arsonist as a complicated public servant. The result is not objectivity. It is reputational protection for power.

Why Europe Says It Plainly

The source contrasts American caution with the willingness of an Italian paper to say the obvious. That contrast is not about national temperament. It is about media structure and political memory. The piece points to a U.S. landscape dominated by giant corporations and wealthy owners, where legal pressure and access politics quietly narrow the language available to reporters.

That is the deeper story here. The issue is not that one foreign paper was rude. It is that American media so often act as if blunt truth is a breach of etiquette. They have confused access with responsibility and caution with professionalism.

The System Behind the Soft Words

The larger pattern is institutional cowardice in service of elite insulation. Trump’s behavior is the headline, but the system around him is what keeps turning theft, cruelty, fraud, and illegality into acceptable political weather. The press softens. Corporations settle. Political elites normalize. And the public gets taught that repeated abuse is just another version of governance.

That is the real scandal: not that one newspaper used a blunt word, but that so many major outlets still behave as if blunt words are the problem.
