Companies that told Trump to get lost outperformed competitors that caved: study

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosAugust 28, 20263 min read

Trump’s DEI Extortion Failed, and That Matters

The Source of Power

This story is not about “corporate courage” in some abstract moral sense. It is about power: a president trying to use the White House to dictate hiring and promotion policy inside private firms, and companies deciding whether to comply with the pressure. Trump’s anti-DEI order was never neutral management advice. It was a political weapon aimed at suppressing minority advancement under the cover of executive authority.

That is the real event here. Not a debate about “culture.” Not a question of brand positioning. A presidential administration tried to make anti-minority policy feel mandatory.

The Panic Was the Point

The article shows how coercion works when it is dressed up as governance. Some companies caved, some resisted, some reversed course after panicking. The purpose of that pressure was not merely to change policy at the margin. It was to train institutions to flinch first and ask questions later.

But the reported findings cut through the performance. UC Berkeley researcher Jacob Grumbach found that firms keeping DEI policies in place performed just as well as firms that complied with Trump’s executive order. The supposed business case for surrender was thin. The actual motive was fear.

Misdirection as Strategy

The weak framing here is the familiar one: treat deliberate political intimidation as if it were a complicated managerial dilemma. It was not. Trump targeted corporate policy to force ideological compliance, and the article makes clear that the economic penalties for resisting were overstated. The danger was never just market backlash. The danger was executive retaliation, or the belief that retaliation would arrive.

That distinction matters because it reveals where the fraud sits. Companies did not cave because evidence showed DEI was bad for business. They caved because authoritarian pressure is designed to make institutions confuse obedience with prudence.

What the Numbers Actually Say

The source is blunt on the central fact: large firms that kept DEI policies “performed just as well as firms that complied.” Consumer punishment, on balance, did not materialize. Regulatory retaliation against noncompliant firms also did not show up in a major way, at least in this study.

That means the surrender was mostly symbolic submission. Citigroup, Dollar General, Walmart, and others that weakened or eliminated policies did not buy themselves better results. Meanwhile, firms like Costco, Delta, Microsoft, JPMorganChase, and Apple either held course or later restored what they had dropped. Even the legal market responded: firms that stood up against Trump reportedly drew business away from those that made deals with him.

The lesson is not that corporations are brave. It is that Trump’s intimidation was less powerful than its targets assumed.

Cowardice Has a Cost

Robert Reich’s point about Target and Walmart underscores the political asymmetry. When companies rolled back DEI, they did not escape consequences; they invited them. Boycotts and stock pressure followed. That is not an argument for consumer purity theater. It is evidence that capitulation did not solve the problem. It only advertised weakness.

The relevant divide is not between “woke” companies and “anti-woke” companies. It is between institutions that can still act on principle without collapsing, and institutions that rush to appease authoritarian pressure the moment it appears. One side is defending pluralism. The other side is normalizing minority rollback as a management choice.

The Larger Pattern

This is a small case study in a larger political pattern: authoritarian politics often fails on the merits but succeeds through fear, compliance, and elite overreaction. The damage comes not only from the order itself, but from the institutions that treat illegal or abusive pressure as if it deserves accommodation.

The reporting shows something Democrats and democratic institutions should not forget. Trump’s power is real, but it is not magic. He depends on the reflex of surrender from people and firms that would rather pre-comply than be seen resisting. When those targets stand their ground, the spell breaks. That is the lesson. The regime of intimidation is built on the expectation that nobody will make it pay.
