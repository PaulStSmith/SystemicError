'There's a big however': Expert blows hole in DOJ's efforts to hide Epstein friends' names

After Donald Trump's administration asked a judge to keep secret the names of individuals who had monetary transactions with disgraced financier and convicted child abuser Jeffrey Epstein, a legal expert highlighted the administration's about-face on the documents.

Former federal prosecutor Kristy Greenberg, who previously commented on Trump's criminal legal matters, appeared on MSNBC over the weekend to discuss the administration's actions involving Epstein records.

With the host saying the report "may have opened a can of worms," Greenberg notes that the filing at first glance appears to be like any other DOJ filing.

"Well, I think when you look at this filing, this is something that you would generally expect from DOJ to say, 'Hey, these are uncharged third parties. They have privacy interests. They object to their names being made public, so we are going to object this being disclosed. We want this to remain secret.' That's kind of standard," Greenberg said. "However, and there's a big however. The problem with that motion is, not too long ago, we got a very different motion from this Department of Justice where they asked for grand jury materials to be unsealed, and there is a precedent there. They're talking about the fact that they are committed to transparency."

She added, "You can't have it both ways."

"You cannot continue to speak about being transparent and stating that the public interest is great in these materials, and therefore materials need to be unsealed and then say, well, actually, not so much with respect to x, y, and z. It doesn't make sense," she said. "It's inconsistent. Their positions are very difficult to understand, and I expect that the judge will have some problems in terms of really understanding where is the coherent position."

Our Analysis:

The Trump Administration's Transparency Charade: A Critical Examination

In a move that reeks of hypocrisy and a blatant disregard for the principles of transparency they once purported to champion, the Trump administration has made an about-face so sharp it could give you whiplash. The request from Donald Trump's administration, seeking to shroud the names of individuals linked through monetary transactions with the infamous Jeffrey Epstein, is a masterclass in selective secrecy. This comes after a history of vocally advocating for the unsealing of grand jury materials, riding high on the horse of transparency. How quickly the tides turn when the waters churn too close to home.

The Facade of Transparency

Former federal prosecutor Kristy Greenberg's comments on MSNBC shed a glaring light on this hypocrisy. Initially, one might be fooled into thinking this move by the Department of Justice (DOJ) is business as usual, protecting uncharged third parties and their privacy interests. But, as Greenberg points out, this stance is a stark departure from the DOJ's previous actions and verbal commitments to transparency.

The Inconsistency That Baffles

Let's break down the absurdity of the administration's position. On one hand, they've previously argued for the unsealing of grand jury materials, ostensibly because the public's right to know was paramount. Yet, now, when the documents in question could potentially involve individuals within or connected to their own sphere, suddenly the cloak of secrecy is deemed necessary. "You can't have it both ways," Greenberg rightly asserts. This isn't just a minor oopsie in policy consistency; it's a glaring testament to the administration's willingness to shift its principles to suit its convenience.

Selective Secrecy and the Public's Right to Know

The administration's selective approach to transparency is not just inconsistent—it's fundamentally dishonest. It sends a clear message: transparency is only a priority when it serves their interests. When it doesn't, privacy suddenly becomes paramount, and the public's right to know be damned. This hypocrisy underlines a contempt for the principles of open governance and accountability. It suggests a belief that they can manipulate the narrative and the rules to their advantage, without consequence.

The Court's Conundrum

As Greenberg anticipates, the judge is now left to untangle this web of inconsistency. The administration's position is not only difficult to understand; it's virtually indefensible when held up against their previous actions. This legal and moral quagmire the administration has created will test the judiciary's resolve in upholding the principles of transparency and accountability, against a backdrop of shifting political winds and convenient reinterpretations of those principles.

Conclusion: Transparently Opaque

The administration's request to keep Epstein-related documents under wraps is a telling episode. It reveals a willingness to abandon previously professed values for political convenience, engaging in a transparently opaque maneuver that prioritizes protection over the public's right to transparency. This episode is yet another entry in the ledger of the administration's legacy of inconsistency, hypocrisy, and selective secrecy. It's a stark reminder that, for some, transparency is not a principle but a tactic, deployed only when it serves their ends.
