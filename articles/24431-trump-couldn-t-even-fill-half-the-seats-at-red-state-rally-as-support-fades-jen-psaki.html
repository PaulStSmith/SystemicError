Trump 'couldn't even fill half the seats' at red state rally as support fades: Jen Psaki

Based on an article by David McAfee

Paulo SantosOctober 4, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that MS NOW host Jen Psaki argued Donald Trump’s support was fading because his Friday rally in Mobile, Alabama, appeared to leave more than half of a 10,000-seat venue empty. Psaki called the turnout a visual sign of political decline and contrasted it with Trump’s much larger rally in Mobile during his 2015 campaign. The article provides no official attendance figure or calendar date, so the “half the seats” claim rests on Psaki’s description and the footage aired by her program.

The rally had drawn criticism because Alabama is already overwhelmingly Republican while control of Congress is at stake elsewhere. Trump defended the trip the preceding Thursday as a gesture of thanks to states that support him “a thousand percent.” Former Republican representative Barbara Comstock amplified Psaki’s segment, while Democratic strategist Ameshia Cross criticized the decision to campaign in such safe territory.

The crowd is evidence, not a verdict

A poorly attended rally in a Republican stronghold is politically embarrassing. It can indicate weaker enthusiasm, poor organization, diminishing novelty or a bad strategic choice. It does not, by itself, establish that Trump’s broader support is collapsing.

Psaki’s comparison with 2015 is suggestive but incomplete. That rally occurred at the beginning of Trump’s first presidential campaign, when spectacle and novelty were central to his appeal. A smaller crowd years later may reflect decline, but RawStory supplies no polling, voter-registration data or election results connecting the empty seats to a measurable shift in Alabama.

Power is not measured in folding chairs

Trump holds institutional power as president. Republicans’ concern, as presented here, is not whether he can still command attention in Mobile. It is whether he is using his office and political dominance to help the party retain control of Congress.

That distinction matters. Rally attendance measures enthusiasm among people willing and able to appear at one venue. Congressional power is determined through elections, money, party machinery, candidate recruitment and presidential agenda-setting. Empty seats may bruise Trump’s image without weakening his control over those instruments.

The decision was Trump’s

Trump said he went to Alabama to thank a state loyal to him. That is an admission of priority, not an explanation of strategy. He chose symbolic affirmation in safe territory while critics argued that Republican resources and attention were needed in competitive races.

The reporting does not establish that the Mobile appearance harmed Republican candidates. It does show who exercised agency: Trump selected the destination and publicly defended it. Describing the trip merely as a puzzling campaign choice would obscure that hierarchy. A party organized around one leader’s preferences often treats his appetite for loyalty displays as strategy, then asks operatives to rationalize the result.

The framing outruns the proof

RawStory builds the story around Psaki’s interpretation that the crowd represents political decline. That framing turns a striking image into a conclusion the evidence cannot fully carry. The article offers criticism from a Democratic strategist and a former Republican lawmaker, but no response from rally organizers, verified attendance count or broader measure of support.

The sharper story is therefore not “support has faded” as an established fact. It is that Trump’s critics found a potent symbol of diminished enthusiasm, while his own explanation revealed a political operation still centered on rewarding loyalty and displaying personal allegiance.

A party built around affirmation

The larger pattern is the conversion of party politics into a continuous tribute to its dominant figure. Safe states receive presidential attention because they provide affirmation; competitive contests become secondary to maintaining the leader’s spectacle.

When the spectacle falters, commentators can mistake visible embarrassment for lost power. The more consequential question is whether Republican officials stop organizing their choices around Trump. RawStory presents no evidence that they have. Half-empty seats may expose fatigue. Institutional submission is measured by who still gets to choose the stage.
