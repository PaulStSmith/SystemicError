WATCH: Todd Blanche faces Senate grilling in high-stakes confirmation hearing

Todd Blanche Is Not a Neutral Lawyer. He Is the Point.

The Nominee and the Machine

Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche is heading into the Senate Judiciary Committee with the usual Washington fiction wrapped around him: a “tough” hearing, a contested nomination, and a few hours of ritualized concern. The reality is simpler. Donald Trump chose a former personal lawyer to run the Justice Department, and Blanche has already shown where his loyalties sit.

Who Holds the Power

The power here is not in the hearing room. It sits with Trump, who nominated Blanche, and with the DOJ leadership that Blanche has helped shape from the inside. The Senate can still stop him, but only if it acts like a branch of government instead of a live studio audience for executive misconduct.

The source makes that plain: Blanche is being scrutinized not for some abstract ideological difference, but because he has already used the department as a political instrument and presided over its damage.

The Epstein File Release Was Not a Mistake in a Vacuum

The DOJ’s “botched release” of Epstein-related files is being described as if it were administrative clumsiness. That framing is too soft for what the source describes: survivors were unlawfully named in the release. That is not a minor processing error. It is institutional carelessness with vulnerable people’s identities, produced by a department that either did not care or did not bother to prevent predictable harm.

The blame belongs where the authority was exercised. Blanche was leading the DOJ. The decision-making power was not with the people exposed by the release; it was with the officials who controlled the process and set the standards.

Loyalists, Not Professionals

Blanche’s background as Trump’s personal lawyer is not incidental context. It is the governing fact. Trump keeps appointing personal defenders to public offices and then acting surprised when those offices function like extensions of his defense team. That is not a staffing quirk. It is how capture works.

The concern raised by more than 1,200 former DOJ employees is therefore not merely about one nomination. It is about whether the department still has any boundary between law enforcement and political retaliation. Blanche has also faced scrutiny for spearheading what critics call politically motivated prosecutions of Trump’s perceived enemies. That is the pattern: use government power downward, against targets selected by loyalty politics, then demand that everyone pretend the process is normal.

Attrition as Policy

The reported departure of around 16,000 DOJ employees under Blanche’s leadership, including FBI agents and more than a quarter of the department’s attorneys, is not a side note. It is the institutional consequence of politicized control. People do not leave that fast because morale had a rough quarter. They leave when the agency becomes degraded, unstable, or openly subordinated to the ambitions of the people at the top.

That is the deeper damage. Corruption is not only what gets prosecuted or leaked. It is the hollowing out of the capacity to do public work without fear, favoritism, or retaliation.

The Real Question

The Senate should not be asking whether Blanche can be trusted to be “fair.” That assumes fairness is a personality trait. The real question is whether a Justice Department led by Trump’s personal lawyer can function as anything other than a political weapon, a cleanup crew for executive damage, and a shield for power.

This story is not about a single bad release or a rough nomination fight. It is about a ruling style that treats public institutions as disposable and then acts offended when the institutions start breaking. The misdirection is the point: blame the process, blur the decision, soften the harm. But the authority was centralized, the choices were deliberate, and the consequences landed on people with the least power to absorb them.
