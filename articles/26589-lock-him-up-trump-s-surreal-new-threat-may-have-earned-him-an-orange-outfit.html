Lock him up: Trump's surreal new threat may have earned him an orange outfit

Based on an article by Sabrina Haake

Paulo SantosOctober 12, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power belongs to the presidentCongress enabled the vacuumThe cleanup operationThe treason claim outruns the evidenceExpendability as government policy

What happened

AlterNet reports that Donald Trump said at a Republican rally in Grand Island, Nebraska, that Iran could “take out” Los Angeles or San Diego as “a very small price to pay,” while predicting that the war would end soon. The article presents the remark as treating two American cities as acceptable losses in a conflict Trump announced on February 28, 2026, without prior congressional authorization or public debate.

The headline’s “orange outfit” refers to the author’s argument that Trump should be imprisoned for treason. But the article identifies no charge, investigation, or legal proceeding arising from the remark. Trump’s precise intended meaning remains disputed: White House communications director Steven Cheung said Trump meant that American cities would have faced threats if Iran’s nuclear program had not been stopped, while California Governor Gavin Newsom and Republican congressional candidate Jim Desmond condemned the words as dangerous and unacceptable.

The power belongs to the president

The central fact is not merely that Trump said something grotesque. It is that the president who initiated the reported military campaign publicly described catastrophic retaliation against American cities as a tolerable cost.

Trump holds command authority. Californians do not. Neither Newsom’s denunciation nor Desmond’s objection can undo military decisions already made by the executive branch. Treating the episode principally as another controversy over Trump’s rhetoric risks shrinking a question of war-making power into a dispute about tone.

Congress enabled the vacuum

According to AlterNet, Trump began major combat operations without consulting Congress, allies, or the public and had still offered no consistent objective seven months later. If that account is accurate, the institutional failure extends beyond one man’s volatility.

Congress possesses powers over war authorization, appropriations, oversight, and impeachment. The article provides no evidence that lawmakers used those tools to impose an effective constraint. A legislature that permits an open-ended conflict and responds mainly through partisan commentary is not an innocent spectator. It is surrendering authority while preserving deniability.

The cleanup operation

Cheung’s explanation does not match the language AlterNet quotes. Saying cities might have faced danger is materially different from saying an adversary can “take out” those cities for a “small price.” The White House response therefore functions less as clarification than as an attempt to replace the president’s words with a safer statement he did not make.

That pattern matters. Presidential communications staff can turn incoherence into ambiguity, ambiguity into alleged media distortion, and alleged distortion into another loyalty test. The public is pushed to debate what Trump supposedly intended instead of what the commander in chief actually said while Americans and military personnel faced the consequences of his policy.

The treason claim outruns the evidence

AlterNet is right to center the obscenity of declaring American cities expendable. Its leap to treason and life imprisonment is far less grounded. The supplied reporting establishes an alarming public remark; it does not establish that Trump adhered to an enemy or intentionally gave it aid and comfort.

That overreach weakens the sharper indictment available from the facts. A president need not satisfy the Constitution’s narrow definition of treason to demonstrate reckless judgment, contempt for civilian life, or an inability to state coherent war aims. Turning every abuse into a maximal criminal accusation can obscure the mechanisms that actually sustain it: executive power, congressional passivity, partisan protection, and official spin.

Expendability as government policy

Los Angeles and San Diego were not rhetorical abstractions. AlterNet notes that the San Diego area contains major Navy and Marine Corps installations and supports more than 115,000 active-duty personnel. Dismissing attacks there as a small price converts residents and service members into bargaining chips in a war they did not authorize.

The larger pattern is rule through unilateral escalation followed by institutional evasion. The president acts, Congress declines to restrain him, aides rewrite his language, and critics reach for sensational legal claims because ordinary accountability has failed. The systemic error is not confusion about what Trump meant. It is a political order willing to leave immense war-making power in his hands while arguing over how politely to describe the consequences.
