Texas cops search license plate cameras across US to find woman who had abortion

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 30, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Thin Line Between Surveillance and "Safety"

Officers from the Johnson County Sheriff’s Office in Texas. (Johnson County Sheriff’s Office)

An officer from the Johnson County Sheriff's Office in Texas reportedly used a nationwide database to search for a woman who gave herself an abortion.

According to 404 Media, the officer conducted the search earlier this month using a database of license plate readers created by a company called Flock. The search included states where abortion was legal, like Washington and Illinois, the report said.

"We weren't trying to block her from leaving the state or whatever to get an abortion," Sheriff Adam King of the Johnson County Sheriff's Office told 404 Media. "It was about her safety."

King said the woman's family contacted authorities out of concern that she would "bleed to death."

"We were trying to find her to get her to a hospital," he insisted.

Privacy advocates, however, were concerned that the technology could be misused in a state like Texas, where nearly all abortions are illegal.

"The idea that the police are actively tracking the location of women they believe have had self administered abortions under the guise of 'safety' does not make me feel any better about this kind of surveillance," Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF) cybersecurity director Eva Galperin explained.

Elizabeth Ling of If/When/How, a reproductive rights group, told 404 Media that 26% of abortion prosecutions occurred after a family member or acquaintance reported a woman to law enforcement.

"Self-managed abortion is extremely safe," Ling said. "What we have found in our work and our research is that the greatest risk posed to people self-managing their abortion is state violence and criminalization."

"All of this shows why it is essential for people to have access to accurate information about their options and legal risk. Because no one should face criminalization for their abortion," she added.

Our Analysis:

The Thin Line Between Surveillance and "Safety"

In an era where privacy seems to be a fading concept, the actions of an officer from the Johnson County Sheriff's Office in Texas serve as a stark reminder of the invasive capabilities of modern surveillance under the guise of protection. Utilizing a nationwide database from a company known as Flock, this officer embarked on a digital manhunt for a woman who had given herself an abortion, with the search stretching across states where abortion remains legal, including Washington and Illinois. This incident, as reported by 404 Media, raises profound questions about privacy, autonomy, and the role of law enforcement in personal health matters.

Sheriff Adam King's defense that the operation was for the woman's "safety," as her family feared she might "bleed to death," might appear noble at first glance. However, this narrative quickly unravels under scrutiny. The claim that this invasive search was a benevolent act of rescue does little to mask the underlying issues of surveillance and the potential for abuse of power.

Privacy advocates are rightly alarmed. The use of such technology in Texas, a state notorious for its stringent abortion laws, sets a dangerous precedent. The Electronic Frontier Foundation's cybersecurity director, Eva Galperin, succinctly captures the essence of the problem: the police tracking women under the pretext of safety does not alleviate concerns about surveillance; it amplifies them.

Moreover, Elizabeth Ling from If/When/How, a reproductive rights group, provides a chilling statistic: 26% of abortion prosecutions were initiated by reports from family members or acquaintances. This fact alone shatters any illusion of privacy or community support, highlighting instead a landscape of fear and potential for state violence against women exercising autonomy over their bodies.

Ling's assertion that "self-managed abortion is extremely safe" and that the "greatest risk posed to people self-managing their abortion is state violence and criminalization" is a critical point that cannot be overstated. It underscores the absurdity of utilizing law enforcement resources and surveillance technology under the guise of health and safety when, in reality, the greatest threat comes from the state itself.

The Bottom Line:

The actions of the Johnson County Sheriff's Office, while perhaps well-intentioned in the most generous interpretation, starkly illustrate the dangers of a surveillance state where personal health decisions are subjected to scrutiny and potential criminalization. This incident should serve as a wake-up call for the urgent need to safeguard privacy and reproductive rights against the encroachment of state surveillance.

In this age of technological omnipresence, the distinction between safeguarding and surveilling becomes blurrier by the day. We must ask ourselves: at what point does the scale tip too far? When do the measures taken in the name of safety become threats themselves? This case from Texas provides a grim answer, highlighting a systemic error in our approach to privacy, autonomy, and the role of law enforcement in our lives.
