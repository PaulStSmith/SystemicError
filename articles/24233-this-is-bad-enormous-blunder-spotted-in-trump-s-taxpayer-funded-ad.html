'This is bad': Enormous blunder spotted in Trump's 'taxpayer-funded' ad

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that a U.S. government-funded advertisement featuring President Donald Trump declared, “This is the final battle,” promoted his record, and ended with the disclaimer “Paid for by the US Government.” On Monday, an MS NOW “Morning Joe” panel argued that the ad used public money for political self-promotion while inadvertently highlighting promises they said Trump had not fulfilled.

Republican Sen. John Kennedy told CBS the ad was “probably illegal” and said public money should not be spent to promote an officeholder. Joe Scarborough characterized the spending as “stealing taxpayer dollars,” while he and Jonathan Lemire argued that the ad’s references to foreign wars and prices reminded viewers of Trump’s failures rather than his claimed achievements.

The report does not identify which agency commissioned the ad, who approved it, how much it cost, where it ran, or what law it might violate. It also provides criticism from television commentators, not a legal finding or evidence establishing that Trump personally authorized the expenditure.

The power sits behind the disclaimer

The crucial words are not Trump’s apocalyptic slogan. They are “Paid for by the US Government.” An administration controls agencies, communications machinery, and access to appropriated funds. Taxpayers finance that machinery; they do not authorize its conversion into personal campaign theater.

But RawStory leaves the chain of command blank. Without naming the agency, appropriation, approving official, or production process, the article cannot tell readers who exercised that power. Trump is the beneficiary and centerpiece. The actual decision-maker remains unidentified.

That omission matters because government propaganda is not produced by weather. Someone requested it, someone approved it, and someone determined that public resources could carry a message centered on the president’s political persona. Accountability begins with those names.

A legal question reduced to television reaction

Kennedy’s criticism is politically significant because it came from a Republican senator, but “probably illegal” is not a legal analysis. The report cites no statute, inspector-general review, ethics determination, or court ruling.

Scarborough’s claim that taxpayer money was being stolen is similarly presented as commentary, not an established finding. The defensible conclusion is narrower and still serious: the advertisement carried a government-funding disclaimer and appeared to promote Trump personally. Whether that crossed a legal boundary is unresolved in the reporting.

By building the story around a panel’s verdict, RawStory substitutes outrage for documentation. The sharper investigation would follow the money and authority: which office paid, under what program, after whose approval, and subject to what restrictions.

The blunder framing lets power escape

Calling the advertisement a “massive misstep” treats the central problem as poor messaging. Scarborough and Lemire argued that references to wars and prices reminded viewers of promises they believe Trump failed to keep. That may explain why the ad was politically ineffective. It does not explain why the government was paying to burnish the president’s image.

A blunder is accidental self-injury. Publicly financed leader promotion is an institutional choice, even when it backfires. Focusing on whether the ad helps Republicans redirects attention from the officials who authorized it to the consultants who apparently failed to make it persuasive.

The weaker actor here is the taxpayer, who has no operational control over the communications apparatus bearing the government’s name. The powerful actors are those inside the executive branch who can commission, approve, and distribute such material. The article identifies the first group’s money but not the second group’s responsibility.

Propaganda does not become harmless when it is clumsy

“This is the final battle” casts ordinary political struggle as existential conflict and places the president at its center. In a government-funded message, that framing collapses the distinction between the state, the administration, and one politician’s political identity.

The larger pattern is the personalization of public institutions: government resources become scenery for the leader, official communication becomes self-promotion, and accountability becomes a debate over optics. Critics then ask whether the message will hurt the ruling party instead of first establishing who used state power, by what authority, and at what public cost.

RawStory identifies a legitimate warning sign but stops at the spectacle. The systemic error is not that propaganda can be badly written. It is that institutional power can be converted into personal promotion while coverage fixates on whether the conversion was electorally competent.
