Susie Wiles skewered by her own Vanity Fair profiler as 'inmate in chief runs the asylum'

The Press Ban Was Trump’s Decision, Not Susie Wiles’s Failure to Babysit Him

The Order Came From the President

CNN reported that President Donald Trump barred CNN, MS NOW, and Politico from the White House after aide Natalie Harp showed him coverage of legal and political setbacks, including a Politico graph that reportedly angered him. The three organizations sued the administration in federal court, while five major television networks suspended coverage of presidential events that required a shared camera pool.

That is the essential power structure: Trump held the authority and imposed the ban. Harp reportedly supplied the offending coverage. Chief of Staff Susie Wiles either failed to stop the decision or was not present to try. None of that transfers responsibility away from the president who acted.

The Babysitter Theory of Government

Presidential historian Chris Whipple told CNN that Wiles had “totally abdicated” her duty to check Trump. His criticism has a factual foundation: Wiles presented herself during 11 on-the-record interviews for a Vanity Fair profile published last December as someone willing to deliver hard truths.

But Whipple’s framing also diminishes the central offense. A chief of staff can advise, obstruct, delay, or contain. The president still controls the White House and chose retaliation against news organizations whose coverage displeased him.

Calling Wiles incompetent may be justified as an assessment of her performance. Treating her competence as the decisive issue turns presidential abuse of institutional power into a staffing failure.

Wiles Has Power, but Not Ultimate Power

Wiles is not a powerless bystander. A White House chief of staff controls access, information flow, scheduling, and internal process. If she was present and declined to intervene, that would expose the limits of her advertised truth-telling. If she was away, as Whipple suggested might have been the case, the reporting does not establish what she knew or could have prevented.

That uncertainty matters. The evidence supports scrutiny of Wiles’s role, not a definitive claim about why she failed to restrain the decision. Her larger political function, however, is clear: she helps operate an administration in which access to the presidency and access to the White House press apparatus can become instruments of personal retaliation.

Punishment Disguised as Press Management

The reported sequence contains no policy dispute about credentials, capacity, security, or journalistic procedure. The ban followed unfavorable coverage that was placed before Trump and reportedly angered him.

On the evidence presented, this was retaliation, not ordinary press management. The institutional message is straightforward: coverage that provokes the president can cost an outlet access. That creates pressure extending beyond the three named organizations, because punishment works politically through the example it sets.

The response from the five television networks shows that the White House does not possess all the leverage. By suspending coverage of events dependent on a shared camera pool, the networks made retaliation costly to the administration as well. The lawsuits moved the dispute into federal court, where the administration would have to defend its conduct rather than merely announce it.

The Attention Economy Is Not the Main Injury

Whipple predicted the move would backfire because Trump would miss the television attention. That may prove true, but it reduces the stakes to presidential vanity and media access.

The deeper consequence is institutional. White House access is a public-facing function of government, not a favor owed only to organizations producing agreeable coverage. When access is conditioned on avoiding the president’s anger, the administration converts state authority into a mechanism for disciplining scrutiny.

Whether that strategy succeeds is separate from whether it is abusive. A retaliatory act does not become trivial because its author may later regret losing airtime.

Personal Rule Produces Institutional Cowardice

This episode reveals a system organized around managing one officeholder’s reactions rather than enforcing durable boundaries on presidential power. Harp reportedly delivered the coverage. Wiles allegedly failed to impose restraint. Trump made the decision. The press organizations then had to supply the resistance that the administration’s internal structure did not.

The fixation on whether Wiles could have controlled Trump is itself a warning about personal rule. It imagines responsible government as the product of a sufficiently skilled subordinate quietly preventing the president from acting on his impulses. That is not accountability. It is containment—and, according to the events CNN reported, containment failed.

The systemic error is not simply that Trump resents hostile coverage or that Wiles may have broken her promise to tell him hard truths. It is that public institutions were made dependent on private restraint, leaving access to government vulnerable to the president’s displeasure.
