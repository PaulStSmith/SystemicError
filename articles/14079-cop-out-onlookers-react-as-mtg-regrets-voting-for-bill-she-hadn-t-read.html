'Cop out': Onlookers react as MTG regrets voting for bill she hadn't read

U.S. Congresswoman Marjorie Taylor Greene speaking with attendees at the 2023 Young Women’s Leadership Summit hosted by Turning Point USA at the Gaylord Texan Resort & Convention Center in Grapevine, Texas. (Photo credit: Gage Skidmore)

Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene (R-GA) caused a stir on Capitol Hill on Tuesday when she publicly admitted, in a lengthy screed posted to X, that she had not read Trump's One Big Beautiful Bill Act before voting for it — and she is "adamantly opposed" to the provision barring states from regulating artificial intelligence technology for 10 years.

"Full transparency, I did not know about this section on pages 278-279," said Greene, adding that she would have voted down the bill if she were aware of it. "We have no idea what AI will be capable of in the next 10 years and giving it free rein and tying states' hands is potentially dangerous. This needs to be stripped out in the Senate."

While a number of commenters on social media agreed with her fundamental reasoning, Greene also caught a lot of heat for not having read the bill she voted for in the first place.

"'I would have voted NO if I had known this was in there.' Hey MTG, your job is to know what you're voting YES on," wrote the Public Citizen account. "It's unacceptable for an elected official to vote for policies that will harm millions of Americans & cop out by saying they didn't read all of the text."

"Here's MTG announcing that she never read Trump's 'Big Beautiful Bill' before voting yes, and she actually hates it now," wrote Joshua Reed Eakle of Project Liberal. "We should bring back shame."

"Recall, Pelosi was pilloried for a poor choice of words around the ACA," wrote the influencer "Peter Twinklage." "She said in 2010: 'We have to pass the bill so that you can find out what’s in it.' but ‘Obamacare’ had been debated for months & was given 8 hearings. Now MTG defends herself by saying she doesn’t read bills."

"MTG and Mike Flood, two peas in a pod for voting for something that they didn’t read because leadership told them to. And now they both openly regret their votes," wrote attorney Blake Allen, referencing the fact that just a few days ago, Rep. Mike Flood (R-NE) admitted in a town hall that he wasn't aware the bill contained a provision stripping federal courts of the power to punish Trump administration officials for defying judicial orders.

"So just today on the House's AI moratorium: Bipartisan group of 260 state lawmakers from all 50 states opposes; MTG threatens to tank the whole bill if it remains; Sen. Ed Markey publicly confirms he'll challenge it as not complying with Senate rules," wrote Politico reporter Anthony Adragna.

"To somewhat defend MTG here (lol), I’m a realist and know that members are not going to read 1000 page bills. But you or your staff should at least read the section-by-section summary," wrote University of Maryland adjunct law professor Ben Yelin.

"I’m certainly no MTG fan, but I actually respect her and staff for posting this," wrote entrepreneur Deva Hazarika. "Easy to dunk on for obvious reasons, but a rep publicly admitting they vote on legislation without reading or knowing what’s included in it is a rare moment of candor from habitual liar."

Our Analysis:

The Illustrious Case of Reading Before Voting: A Novel Concept for Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene

In a revelation that shocks absolutely no one but disappoints on a near-cosmic level, Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene (R-GA) has once again catapulted herself into the spotlight for reasons that would make any high school civics teacher bury their head in despair. In a lengthy screed on X, Greene admitted to not having read Trump's "One Big Beautiful Bill Act" before casting her vote in favor. The twist? She's now "adamantly opposed" to a significant provision she somehow missed in her thorough (non)review. Welcome to another episode of Legislative Responsibilities: Lost in Translation.

The Unseen Pitfall: AI Regulation

The crux of Greene's late-stage opposition centers on a provision that would bar states from regulating artificial intelligence technology for a spanking ten years. "We have no idea what AI will be capable of in the next 10 years," Greene protests, a sentiment that rings true with a touch of irony considering her prior obliviousness to the bill's content. The dangers of giving AI "free rein" are indeed worrisome, but what's potentially more dangerous is the habit of legislators voting blind on policies that shape our future. If only there were a way to avoid such pitfalls—perhaps by reading the bill?

The Public Reaction: A Collective Facepalm

Social media, in its infinite capacity for subtlety and nuance, didn't hold back. The Public Citizen account's response encapsulates the collective frustration: "'I would have voted NO if I had known this was in there.' Hey MTG, your job is to know what you're voting YES on." A sentiment so basic yet evidently elusive for some. Meanwhile, Joshua Reed Eakle of Project Liberal and the influencer "Peter Twinklage" draw parallels to past legislative controversies, highlighting a pattern of regrettable legislative practices that transcend party lines. Yet, Greene's candid admission of ignorance sets a new low bar for accountability.

Misery Loves Company: Rep. Mike Flood Joins the Club

Greene isn't sailing this ship of regret alone. Rep. Mike Flood (R-NE) recently admitted a similar oversight, voting for a bill without being aware of its implications. This bipartisan negligence points to a systemic issue within our legislative process—the normalization of uninformed decision-making. Attorney Blake Allen's comparison of Greene and Flood as "two peas in a pod" underscores a broader trend of leadership-driven voting that eschews individual responsibility and due diligence.

A Rare Moment of Candor or a Confession of Incompetence?

In a twist that could be seen as either refreshingly honest or deeply troubling, entrepreneur Deva Hazarika and adjunct law professor Ben Yelin offer a smidgen of defense for Greene's transparency. Yes, admitting to a mistake is a rare trait in the political arena, but let's not confuse accountability after the fact with the fundamental duty of understanding the legislation one votes on. The bar for legislative competence shouldn't be so low that mere admission of not doing one's job becomes praiseworthy.

In Conclusion: A Call for Legislative Literacy

The saga of Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene and the unread bill serves as a stark reminder of the importance of legislative literacy. Voting on laws that will shape the fabric of society without a rudimentary understanding of their contents is not just irresponsible; it's a dereliction of duty. As amusing as it may be to witness these admissions of incompetence, the underlying issue is no laughing matter. It's high time for our elected officials to embrace a radical approach: actually reading the bills they vote on. Revolutionary, isn't it?
