Loose Data Sinks Ships: How Fitness Trackers Exposed Secret Military Bases

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 26, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

When Nothing Becomes SomethingThe Metadata Is the MessageThe New Loose LipsA Secret That Wasn’t Supposed to Be SharedEighty Years Later, the Warning Still Applies

In World War II, careless conversations could reveal military secrets. Eight decades later, fitness trackers were drawing maps of sensitive military installations.

During World War II, the American government popularized a simple warning:

Help fuel the work. Independent analysis doesn’t fund itself:
☕ Buy us a coffee

“Loose lips sink ships.”

The message was straightforward. Military personnel, defense workers, and civilians were reminded that even seemingly harmless conversations could reveal sensitive information to the enemy.

You didn’t need to disclose an entire military operation. A casual comment about a ship’s departure, a troop movement, or a delivery schedule could provide the missing piece of an intelligence puzzle.

Fast-forward to 2018.

An Australian student named Nathan Ruser discovered that Strava, a popular fitness-tracking application, had inadvertently published an intelligence gold mine.

Its global heatmap, designed to show where people ran and cycled, was also revealing the locations and approximate layouts of sensitive military installations in places such as Syria and Afghanistan.

Nobody had to hack the Pentagon.

Nobody had to infiltrate a classified military network.

Nobody had to bribe an intelligence officer.

Soldiers simply went jogging.

And their fitness trackers did the talking.

When Nothing Becomes Something

The brilliance of the discovery lay in something counterintuitive.

Strava’s global heatmap aggregated recorded exercise activities, illuminating frequently traveled routes. In cities like Chicago or New York, the result was a dense web of glowing streets.

But in remote desert regions, the picture was quite different.

Most of the surrounding landscape was dark. And then, suddenly, there were bright little loops and lines.

People were running somewhere.

Repeatedly.

In the middle of nowhere.

And those patterns weren’t random.

They traced paths around compounds, along perimeters, and through areas where civilian recreational activity was otherwise scarce.

The result was a set of geographical signatures pointing to military installations, including facilities whose locations were not publicly acknowledged.

To be clear, Strava wasn’t exposing classified battle plans or revealing ongoing military operations.

It was doing something much simpler.

It was inadvertently publishing maps of places that were supposed to remain obscure.

And that distinction matters.

The danger wasn’t necessarily that an enemy could discover what soldiers were doing inside those installations. It was that the existence, location, and sometimes physical layout of those installations could be inferred from publicly accessible data.

All thanks to people trying to improve their running times.

The Metadata Is the Message

There’s an old misconception about privacy and security: that information is harmless as long as it doesn’t explicitly reveal anything confidential.

It’s the same reasoning behind claims that collecting metadata isn’t particularly intrusive because nobody is reading the actual messages.

But metadata is information.

Where someone goes, when they go there, how often they return, and which routes they repeatedly follow can reveal an astonishing amount about their activities.

There’s a wonderful scene in the 2019 film Midway that illustrates this principle beautifully. Admiral Chester Nimitz asks intelligence officer Joseph Rochefort whether he has direct evidence that the Japanese are preparing to attack Midway. Rochefort admits he doesn’t, then offers an analogy: Imagine someone is planning a wedding. You haven’t seen an invitation, but you know the caterer has been booked, the florist is buying up roses, and the best band in town is already reserved. Nobody has explicitly told you there’s going to be a wedding, but the evidence is becoming rather difficult to ignore.

That’s intelligence analysis in a nutshell. You don’t necessarily need the secret itself. You need enough seemingly unrelated observations to reconstruct it. A florist buying flowers isn’t suspicious. A caterer accepting a booking isn’t suspicious. A band reserving a date isn’t suspicious. But put those observations together, and a picture emerges. Not absolute proof, as Rochefort himself acknowledges, but a potentially compelling inference.

The Strava incident demonstrated precisely the same principle, eighty years later.

The heatmap didn’t need to identify individual soldiers or disclose their communications. The geographical patterns themselves revealed sensitive locations.

The soldiers never sent anyone an invitation to visit their bases.

But they had, unwittingly, booked the caterer, ordered the flowers, and hired the band.

And here’s the uncomfortable truth:

Information doesn’t become harmless simply because nobody intended it to be informative.

The New Loose Lips

In the 1940s, military authorities worried about servicemen chatting in bars, civilians gossiping in restaurants, and defense workers casually discussing their jobs.

Today, the same concerns extend to smartphones, smartwatches, fitness applications, connected vehicles, social media, and countless other devices continuously generating information about our activities.

The problem isn’t necessarily malicious behavior.

It’s the accumulation of seemingly innocent disclosures.

A person uploads a jogging route. Another posts a photograph. Someone else’s phone records a location. An application collects timestamps. A public database supplies geographical context.

Individually, each piece may appear worthless.

Together, they can reveal an extraordinary amount.

And with modern analytical tools, including artificial intelligence, connecting those dots can be dramatically easier.

You don’t need an advanced military intelligence system to notice dozens of runners repeatedly circling the same isolated patch of desert.

You just need a map.

And someone curious enough to look at it.

A Secret That Wasn’t Supposed to Be Shared

There’s a particularly delicious irony in the Strava incident.

Military installations can have layers of physical security: fences, checkpoints, armed guards, restricted airspace, and elaborate access controls.

Yet none of those measures prevent a fitness tracker from recording a jogging route.

And once that route becomes part of a publicly accessible dataset, the perimeter fence is irrelevant to the disclosure.

Of course, not every installation exposed by the heatmap was necessarily secret. Some were already publicly known. Others were in locations whose military significance had not been officially acknowledged.

But the broader vulnerability remained the same.

You can spend a fortune protecting a location while allowing the people inside it to broadcast its outline.

Not intentionally.

Not maliciously.

Just by going for a run.

Eighty Years Later, the Warning Still Applies

The Strava incident is amusing on the surface.

There’s something almost absurd about sophisticated military secrecy being undermined by people trying to improve their five-kilometer running times.

But underneath the absurdity lies a serious lesson.

Security isn’t merely about protecting classified documents, encrypting communications, or restricting access to sensitive systems.

It’s also about understanding what ordinary information can reveal when combined with other ordinary information.

The original wartime slogan assumed the danger came from people saying too much.

Today, our devices can do the talking for us, often without our appreciating what they’re revealing.

In 1943, a careless sailor might inadvertently tell someone where his ship was headed.

In 2018, a soldier’s smartwatch could help show the world where his base was located.

Different technology.

Same fundamental problem.

Which suggests a modest update to that eighty-year-old warning:

“Loose data sinks ships.”

And unlike loose lips, loose data can keep talking long after everyone has gone home.
