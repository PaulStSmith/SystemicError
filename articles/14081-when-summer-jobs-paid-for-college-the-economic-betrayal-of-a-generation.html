When Summer Jobs Paid for College: The Economic Betrayal of a Generation

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 3, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

In 1978, a college student could work a minimum wage job over the summer and earn enough to pay for an entire year of in-state tuition at a four-year public university. That statement reads like fiction today. But it was reality—an era when higher education was an attainable milestone, not a financial trap. This stark contrast between then and now reveals more than just an economic shift; it exposes a systemic betrayal.

The 1978 Reality Check

Let’s crunch the numbers.

  • Federal minimum wage (1978): $2.65/hour

  • Summer work (12 weeks, 40 hours/week): 480 hours

  • Total summer earnings: $1,272

  • Average annual in-state tuition at a public university: ~$688

A student could cover tuition with room to spare. That excess could go to books, rent, or even be saved. Education was affordable because it was treated as a public good—a worthwhile investment in the country’s future.

Fast Forward to 2025

  • Federal minimum wage (2025): $7.25/hour (unchanged since 2009)

  • Summer earnings at that wage: $3,480

  • Average in-state tuition: ~$10,500/year (excluding fees and living expenses)

Today’s student can work the same hours and earn nearly three times as much nominally. But the cost of tuition has ballooned over 15 times, far outpacing both inflation and wage growth. That student would need to work almost three full summers just to cover tuition alone, not including room, board, textbooks, or transportation.

How Did We Get Here?

The answer is as simple as it is enraging: deliberate policy choices.

1. Stagnant Wages

While productivity has skyrocketed since the late 1970s, wages have barely budged. The minimum wage, when adjusted for inflation, is worth significantly less than it was in 1978. A wage that once supported a student’s aspirations now barely covers rent.

2. State Disinvestment

In the post-war decades, states funded a significant portion of public university budgets. That support began to erode in the 1980s and accelerated in the 2000s. Institutions, left to fend for themselves, passed the burden to students in the form of ever-rising tuition.

3. The Student Loan Industrial Complex

Enter predatory lending. As costs rose, federal and private loans became the norm. The student loan industry ballooned into a $1.7 trillion behemoth, transforming education from a pathway to prosperity into a lifelong financial ball-and-chain. Default rates soared. Mental health plummeted. Dreams were deferred.

The Illusion of Personal Responsibility

Politicians and pundits love to wag their fingers at young people: “Don’t major in art history. Learn to budget. Stop eating avocado toast.” It’s gaslighting at scale. No amount of scrimping can close a $7,000 tuition gap on a $7.25 wage.

Worse, these critics often benefited from the very system they now deny exists. Many of today’s lawmakers went to college in the 1970s and 1980s—the golden era of affordable education. Their rise was paved with policies they now gleefully dismantle.

Education as a Right, Not a Luxury

Education should be a ladder, not a lottery. If a summer job once covered college, that wasn’t a fluke—it was proof that the system can work when the political will exists.

We should be asking:

  • Why isn’t college free or low-cost for all who qualify academically?

  • Why are we tolerating a system where 18-year-olds are signing up for decades of debt before they can legally rent a car?

  • Why does the richest country in the world treat higher education like a privilege reserved for the already privileged?

The Real Cost

This isn’t just about tuition. It’s about the erosion of faith in institutions. It’s about the loss of mobility. It’s about a generation working multiple jobs, sacrificing mental health, delaying families, skipping homeownership—all because their predecessors decided profits mattered more than people.

And the most grotesque irony? The very people who dismantled the ladder now stand atop it, preaching bootstrap sermons to the youth clinging to the last rungs.

Where We Go From Here

Fixing this isn’t impossible. It requires courage, clarity, and collective rage.

  • Raise the minimum wage to match productivity and living costs.

  • Reinvest in public education at the state and federal levels.

  • Cancel student debt and implement tuition-free public college.

  • End predatory lending and regulate the education financing industry.

These aren’t radical ideas. They’re a return to what worked.

Conclusion: A Generation Robbed

The meme hits hard because it’s true. In 1978, a summer job was enough. Today, it's barely a Band-Aid on a bullet wound. This isn’t the result of natural forces. It’s the consequence of a system engineered to extract wealth from the young and funnel it upward.

We owe it to this generation—and the next—to rebuild what was lost. To stop treating education as a commodity and start treating it as a right. The price tag on justice may be high, but the cost of doing nothing is far higher.

And no, you can’t pay for that with a summer job.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
