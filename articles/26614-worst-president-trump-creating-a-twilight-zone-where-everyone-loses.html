'Worst president': Trump creating a Twilight Zone where 'everyone loses'

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 14, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower made the choicesA market with no winnerBelligerence has a priceThe framing lets policy hideLosses travel downward

What happened

AlterNet reports that conservative commentator Jonathan V. Last argued Donald Trump may be the “worst president” because his administration’s economic and foreign-policy choices are producing losses for both homeowners and prospective buyers. Last cited a 4 percent drop in pending home sales from August to September—the lowest level since 2014 outside the pandemic—and said average 30-year mortgage rates had risen from 6.6 percent in late August to roughly 7.4 percent.

Last attributed the squeeze to Federal Reserve rate hikes, higher energy costs tied to war with Iran, and Trump’s trade wars. Economists Richard Wolff and Robert J. Shapiro separately told AlterNet that military conflict, tariff pressure, damaged oil infrastructure, and uncertainty about the administration’s next moves could raise costs, provoke resistance abroad, and suppress investment. These are their assessments; the article does not independently establish that each economic change was caused by Trump’s policies.

Power made the choices

Homebuyers did not set foreign policy. Homeowners did not launch trade wars. The Trump administration holds the institutional power described in this story: it can impose tariffs, pursue military escalation, threaten trading partners, and create uncertainty across markets.

The Federal Reserve controls interest-rate policy, so assigning every mortgage-rate increase directly to Trump would be sloppy. But Last’s larger argument concerns the environment in which those decisions occur. Policies that raise energy costs or intensify inflationary pressure can leave the Fed confronting conditions the administration helped create. Responsibility must be divided according to actual authority, not dumped into the foggy category of “the economy.”

A market with no winner

The housing figures illustrate a political failure disguised as a market puzzle. Sellers lose demand and watch the value of their investment come under pressure. Buyers face financing costs high enough to push them out. Lower sales do not automatically restore affordability when borrowing has become markedly more expensive.

That is the “Twilight Zone” Last describes: not a violation of supply and demand, but several policy-driven costs landing at once. The laws of economics remain intact. What fails is the comforting political story that every economic loss must produce an equivalent domestic winner.

Belligerence has a price

Wolff’s account places tariffs and military pressure inside the same governing instinct: coercion followed by surprise when the target resists. He said Canada’s response showed that threatened countries can seek alternatives to capitulation. His description of the June 2025 fighting with Iran and renewed conflict beginning February 28 likewise challenges the premise that superior force guarantees submission.

Shapiro identified the longer tail: risk premiums, tanker-insurance costs, degraded oil infrastructure, constrained supply, and uncertainty that deters investment. Even if those effects cannot be cleanly isolated from every other economic force, they are foreseeable channels through which military escalation reaches household budgets.

The framing lets policy hide

AlterNet organizes the piece around the provocative “worst president” verdict. That label attracts attention, but it is analytically weaker than the evidence beneath it. Presidential rankings are spectacle. Decisions, authorities, and material consequences are politics.

The more useful question is not whether Trump wins a contest for historical disgrace. It is why coverage so often converts government action into economic weather. Rates “rise,” markets are “shocked,” and uncertainty “appears,” while the officials choosing tariffs, confrontation, and war recede from view. Passive framing launders agency.

Losses travel downward

The pattern is familiar: concentrated executive power makes high-risk decisions, while the costs disperse through mortgages, fuel, insurance, investment, and trade. Those consequences arrive as separate household problems, making a political chain of cause and responsibility harder to see.

That fragmentation protects power. A family priced out of a home experiences a personal defeat; a homeowner facing a frozen market sees a financial setback. But when public choices systematically raise costs for both, the result is not an inexplicable malfunction. It is policy transmitting risk downward while the people who exercised the power argue over branding.
