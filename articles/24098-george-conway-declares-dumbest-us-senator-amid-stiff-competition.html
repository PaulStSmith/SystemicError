George Conway declares 'dumbest US Senator' amid stiff 'competition'

Tommy Tuberville’s Furry Panic Is Politics by Distraction

A Senator Invents a Crisis

On September 22, Sen. Tommy Tuberville used the Senate floor to suggest that pilots making animal noises over aviation radio—a longstanding joke, according to ABC News—proved that people with a “deranged fetish” might be flying commercial aircraft. He linked the behavior to what he called “a bizarre subset of transgenderism,” then amplified the allegation on X without presenting evidence that pilots were wearing animal costumes or endangering passengers.

The Power Is Tuberville’s

George Conway’s mockery may be satisfying, but calling Tuberville stupid obscures the more consequential fact: Tuberville is a United States senator with access to the chamber floor and the authority that office supplies.

The pilots and “furries” he targeted do not control Senate proceedings. Transgender people did not choose the subject of his speech. Tuberville did. He converted an aviation meme into an official spectacle, attached it to a marginalized group, and invoked national security to give the insinuation institutional weight.

That is not merely a failure to understand a joke. It is an exercise of power through accusation.

Ridicule Conceals the Method

The story’s “dumbest senator” framing reduces the episode to a contest in personal foolishness. That framing is weak. It makes Tuberville the punchline when the more important question is why an elected official can broadcast unsupported claims from the Senate floor and turn them into campaign material.

Tuberville doubled down after the speech, asserting that an “ALARMING” number of “DERANGED people” work in aviation. The reporting provides no evidence for that claim. His escalation therefore matters more than whether he initially misunderstood the radio chatter: once the aviation-joke explanation was available, he continued portraying an undefined population as a safety menace.

Calling this stupidity offers him an excuse. The observable political method is scapegoating—select a culturally stigmatized group, associate it with danger, and force everyone else to debate the fantasy.

Alabama’s Problems Have Names

Tuberville is running for governor of Alabama. According to the reporting, a 2026 United Way ALICE report found that 71 percent of Alabama residents struggled with basic costs and ranked the state 47th in the country by the report’s hardship measure. The article also cites rising health-care and fuel costs and declining child-care affordability.

Those conditions implicate budgets, regulation, public services, employers and elected officials. They require choices from people who hold power. A furry panic requires none. It offers a candidate an enemy who cannot set tax policy, fund hospitals or determine child-care costs—and therefore cannot threaten the institutions responsible for material hardship.

The contrast does not establish Tuberville’s private motive. It does establish his public choice: given a Senate platform, he focused attention on an unsupported culture-war alarm rather than the documented affordability pressures facing Alabama residents.

Propaganda Does Not Need Coherence

The political value of a panic is not necessarily that everyone believes it. Its value can lie in making the accusation itself unavoidable. Tuberville fused pilots, furries and transgender people into one insinuation, then cast criticism as a left-wing campaign against him. Evidence became secondary; opposition could be recoded as proof that hostile forces were mobilizing.

That structure is familiar propaganda: manufacture contamination, attach it to an already targeted minority, and present scrutiny as persecution. The weaker the factual foundation, the more aggressively identity and outrage must carry the argument.

The Joke Protects the System

Tuberville deserves ridicule. But ridicule alone leaves the machinery intact. The institutional failure is that a senator can use public office to elevate an evidence-free stigma while coverage packages the event as an eccentric personality story.

The systemic error is not that government occasionally contains fools. It is that concentrated political power can turn nonsense into an agenda, redirect accountability toward weaker people, and make material suffering compete with an invented enemy for public attention. Calling the performance stupid is accurate as far as it goes. It does not go far enough.
