'We are finished!' Enraged Trump aide hurls personal attacks at BBC journalist

Senior Trump aide Sebastian Gorka threw a fit when asked by a BBC reporter about the president accepting a massively expensive gift from the Qatari royal family, according to a report in HuffPost.

Gorka, who serves as President Donald Trump’s senior director for counterterrorism, appeared on Radio 4′s World At One program.

Host Sarah Montague asked, “The Qataris are giving a jet worth $400 million. The U.S. Constitution has provisions that no elected official should accept any present from the leader of a foreign state without congressional approval. Is there a problem with this, because it doesn’t look like it’s draining the swamp?”

Rather than answer the question, a clearly irritated Gorka attacked the journalist.

“Do you ever have pangs of conscience that you are so utterly and completely biased that all you can do is give in to your Trump derangement syndrome?" Gorka said. “Have you ever once said anything positive about President Trump or not knelt at the altar of left-wing ideology?”

Montague tried again, asking, "Is there a response to the question?”

Gorka shot back, "Is there a response to my question?”

Montague quickly corrected him, telling Gorka that "she was the one asking the questions," according to HuffPost.

“You know what, I like it when a head of state saves the taxpayer $400 million," Gorka finally replied. “I like that, OK, especially when Boeing has failed to deliver the new Air Force One for years on end, is behind schedule, and afterwards the plane is donated to the presidential library."

He added, "Weird how BBC doesn’t know that. That’s because you’re not journalists.”

Earlier in the interview, Gorka threatened to walk out when confronted about aid to starving children in Gaza.

"I will terminate this interview, Okay?" Gorka claimed. "If you persist in talking about fake news about starvation in Gaza, we are done — we are finished."

Our Analysis:

The Art of Deflection: Gorka's Masterclass

In what can only be described as a spectacular meltdown, Sebastian Gorka, President Donald Trump’s senior director for counterterrorism, delivered a performance on BBC's Radio 4 that Shakespeare himself couldn't have penned better for a tragedy. Gorka, confronted with a question that demanded nothing but straightforward honesty, chose instead to embark on a journey through the lands of evasion and personal attacks.

The Core Issue: A jet, worth a whopping $400 million, is reportedly being gifted to President Trump by the Qatari royal family. The U.S. Constitution, apparently unbeknownst to those in Trump's orbit, stipulates a very clear framework regarding the acceptance of gifts from foreign states – it's a no-go without Congressional approval. The question posed by BBC's Sarah Montague was not just relevant but critical in understanding the administration's stance on adherence to constitutional values.

Gorka's Gambit: Rather than addressing the constitutional dilemma head-on, Gorka deflected. His retort, accusing Montague of bias and suffering from "Trump derangement syndrome," was nothing short of a schoolyard taunt. This approach, while perhaps entertaining to a certain audience, does nothing to address the ethical quagmire at hand. It's a classic move in the political playbook: if you can't defend your position, attack the questioner.

The Irony: Gorka's defense, when it finally emerged, was that the jet’s acquisition was somehow a cost-saving measure for the taxpayer. The logic here is so convoluted it would require a map to navigate. He suggests that because Boeing has delayed the delivery of a new Air Force One, accepting a $400 million gift is justifiable – a gift that, by the way, contravenes constitutional mandates. His jab at the BBC for not being "real journalists" is the cherry on top of this illogical sundae.

Gaza and the Walkout Threat: Earlier in the interview, Gorka threatened to terminate the conversation when pressed on the issue of aid to starving children in Gaza, labeling it "fake news." This maneuver is telling; it showcases a refusal to engage with challenging topics, particularly those that paint the administration in a less than favorable light.

Conclusion

Gorka's interview is emblematic of a broader strategy employed by members of the Trump administration: when faced with difficult questions, deflect, attack, and, if all else fails, threaten to leave the conversation. It's a disservice to the public and to the principles of democratic accountability.

The real question we should be asking isn't whether Gorka can handle tough questions from journalists – it's whether anyone in the current administration is willing to uphold the Constitution and answer to the American people. Given the evasive and combative responses on display here, the outlook isn't promising.
