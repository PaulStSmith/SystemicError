The day Trump woke America's sleeping giant

Based on an article by John Stoehr

Paulo SantosNovember 4, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet argues that Donald Trump and Republicans may have triggered a powerful electoral backlash among Hispanic and Black voters in the South. The article attributes the reported surge in turnout to high prices and low wages, but especially to anger over aggressive immigration enforcement and Supreme Court decisions weakening the Voting Rights Act.

The piece was published on Election Day, before results were known. Its claims about Democratic gains in Texas and elsewhere—including a possible James Talarico victory and competitive statewide races—were predictions based on polling and early-vote figures, not established outcomes.

Political scientist Joshua Zingher told AlterNet that changing Southern demographics and voting rules could reorganize the parties’ electoral coalitions. He also cautioned that the long-term consequences remain uncertain and that Hispanic and Asian voters are likely to become more politically diverse over time.

Power did not disappear

The “sleeping giant” metaphor risks confusing electoral potential with institutional power. Black and Hispanic voters can punish officeholders, but they do not control ICE, federal enforcement policy, congressional procedure, or the Supreme Court. Republican officials exercised that power; vulnerable communities absorbed its consequences.

Nor did the Supreme Court merely introduce uncertainty. According to the article’s account, its Voting Rights Act rulings disrupted an electoral structure that had protected Black representation while leaving Republicans dominant statewide. The uncertainty concerns what follows—not who possessed the authority to rewrite the rules.

Backlash is not reform

The article is strongest when it refuses to reduce the election to inflation. Material hardship matters, but so do immigration raids, racial targeting, voting access, and the judiciary’s power over representation. Voters can react to several injuries at once.

Yet backlash alone repairs nothing. Even a large Democratic victory would not restore voting protections, constrain enforcement agencies, change the Court, admit new states, or produce accountability. Those outcomes require officeholders to make decisions after the ballots are counted.

Celebrating turnout without naming that responsibility turns people facing state power into inspirational scenery. They provide the votes; party leaders retain the discretion to decide whether those votes produce structural change.

The Democratic alibi

AlterNet identifies Joe Biden’s failure—or inability—to pursue major institutional reforms as part of the cycle. The distinction matters. “Couldn’t” describes a constraint; “didn’t” describes a choice. The article does not supply enough evidence to settle every disputed reform, but it correctly locates agency inside governing institutions rather than among voters who lacked the authority to act.

If Democrats gain power and again treat court reform, voting rights, statehood, or oversight as optional, they cannot plausibly blame public apathy. The constituency described here is already participating under rules shaped against it. Institutional caution by officials would be a decision, not a weather event.

The media watches the palace

The article also indicts political journalism for preferring elite intrigue to slower changes in participation, migration, and representation. That criticism lands. Palace stories arrive with recognizable protagonists and quotable feuds; structural disenfranchisement demands attention to rules, institutions, and people largely absent from elite newsrooms.

But demographic storytelling carries its own trap. Hispanic and Black voters are not fixed partisan assets, and Zingher explicitly warns against projecting a short-term backlash indefinitely. Treating racial groups as a Democratic reserve army replaces analysis with entitlement—and gives party leaders another excuse to avoid earning durable support.

The system behind the surge

The larger pattern is not that Trump accidentally awakened a demographic force. It is that officials used institutional power, courts altered the terms of representation, affected voters reportedly mobilized in response, and Democrats were handed an opportunity whose value depends on what they do with it.

Elections can expose a system’s pressure points. They do not automatically change the system. If energized voters deliver power and Democratic officials decline to use it, the failure will not belong to a sleeping electorate. It will belong to the people who were awake, in office, and unwilling to act.
