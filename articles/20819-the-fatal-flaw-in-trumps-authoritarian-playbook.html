The fatal flaw in Trump’s authoritarian playbook

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosJuly 15, 20263 min read

The Authoritarianism Is Not Subtle. The Court Is Just Helpful.

The Source Material

Don Moynihan’s core point is simple: Trump’s project is not a dramatic overnight coup, but a systematic tilt of the state. The administration, he argues, has weakened the civil service, politicized law, and used federal power to pressure universities, media, and businesses. The remaining barrier is elections, where Trump still does not fully control the mechanism.

Power Does Not Hide Here

This story is not really about “institutions” in the abstract. It is about who has power to stop abuse and who chooses not to use it. Trump and Stephen Miller came back with a clearer plan, sharpened by the failures of the first term and operationalized through Project 2025: capture the bureaucracy, stock the legal system with loyalists, and turn executive authority into a tool for retaliation. That is not drift. It is design.

The article is right to frame this as a competitive authoritarian moment, but the crucial detail is agency. The harm is not happening because the system is confused. It is happening because the administration is acting with intent.

The Court’s Real Role

Moynihan points to the Supreme Court as the actor most responsible after Trump himself. That is the right place to look. Lower courts have checked Trump. The Supreme Court, by contrast, has “bought into Trump’s legal theories.” That is not neutrality. It is institutional enabling dressed up as jurisprudence.

The Court is not a bystander in this story. It is part of the machinery that converts executive aggression into something that can survive legal challenge. When the highest court blesses these theories or declines to stop them, it does not merely interpret the law. It reallocates power upward and outward to the very office trying to concentrate it.

Civil Society Under Pressure

The administration’s target set is telling. Universities, media, businesses, civil servants: all the institutions that can resist capture, shape public judgment, or slow state coercion. Trump’s method is to make those institutions flinch before he has to formally break them. Intimidation works because too many elites treat intimidation as political weather rather than deliberate statecraft.

That framing is the misdirection worth rejecting. This is not a generic “polarization” story. It is a domination strategy that depends on cowed intermediaries who keep mistaking pressure for policy dispute.

Elections Still Matter, But Don’t Romanticize That

The article is correct that states still control much of the voting process and that Trump does not yet have a button that can rewrite elections at will. That is a real constraint. It also makes the next phase obvious: when formal control is incomplete, authoritarians lean harder on intimidation, administrative obstruction, and symbolic occupation of the process.

The midterms matter because they are one of the few remaining leverage points. But it is a mistake to turn that into comfort. The fact that Trump has not fully captured elections is not proof that the system is healthy. It is proof that he has not finished the job.

The Pattern Is Capture, Not Chaos

The larger pattern is familiar: weaken neutral administration, legalize aggression, and make resistance look procedural. The goal is not to abolish democracy in one theatrical move. It is to hollow out the parts of democracy that make opposition feasible, then present the result as ordinary governance.

That is why blame matters here. Trump is the driver. Miller and the Project 2025 apparatus are the planners. The Supreme Court is the enabler. And the political class that keeps describing this as mere hardball is doing the regime’s work for it.
