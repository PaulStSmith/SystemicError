Alan Dershowitz has candid words for Trump — and a blunt take on free speech

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 18, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Speech rights that stop at the borderThe narrowest possible account of 2020Government censorship, selectively defined“Debate” as a license for political control

Alan Dershowitz’s blunt position on free speech is that immigration enforcement does not meaningfully suppress it so long as the person detained or expelled can still speak from somewhere else. In an interview with AlterNet, the former Harvard law professor defended the Trump administration’s treatment of noncitizens, denied that Donald Trump tried to retain power after losing the 2020 election, and endorsed government intervention against public institutions he considers ideological. He objected sharply, however, when universities and cultural institutions denied him an audience.

The result is less a coherent defense of free expression than a hierarchy of whose speech counts. Dershowitz treats state custody and exclusion as compatible with liberty, while describing a bookstore’s refusal to stock his book as hostility to the First Amendment. Actual government power recedes from view; private disapproval becomes the emergency.

Speech rights that stop at the border

Asked about Mahmoud Khalil, a green-card holder whom Immigration and Customs Enforcement detained for three months following his criticism of Israel, Dershowitz said he had “no respect for the free speech rights of non-American people.” He argued that the First Amendment applies “in America to Americans” and that a noncitizen removed from the country could continue speaking through Zoom, podcasts or video conferences.

Dershowitz disputed the premise that Khalil had been detained for his speech, saying immigration authorities acted because of Khalil’s status. He also called Khalil a “vicious antisemite,” defining the label broadly enough to include anyone who focuses on Israel and its right to exist without giving comparable attention to the Kurds, Uyghurs or other groups.

That definition does political work. It shifts scrutiny from the government’s use of immigration authority against a critic to the critic’s alleged moral inconsistency. Selective activism may invite criticism, but Dershowitz offered it as a basis for classifying Khalil’s position as antisemitic. The government’s coercive capacity — detention, immigration proceedings and physical control — then becomes administrative background.

His functional test for free speech compounds the evasion. Under it, confinement or removal need not burden expression if technology leaves some channel available. But speech is not preserved merely because a person can theoretically communicate after the state has detained him or forced him elsewhere. Location, legal security and access to an audience are part of political participation, not incidental luxuries.

Dershowitz applied a strikingly different standard to himself. Harvard, New York’s 92nd Street Y and Temple Emanu-El, he said, had effectively banned him from speaking. Because people at those venues could not hear him, he described his free-speech rights as “severely restricted” — even while giving an interview capable of reaching a public audience.

The asymmetry is plain. A noncitizen held by federal agents supposedly retains meaningful freedom because Zoom exists. Dershowitz, free and nationally prominent, portrays rejected speaking invitations as a grave restriction.

The narrowest possible account of 2020

Dershowitz also rejected the suggestion that his defense of Trump during the first impeachment helped embolden the president. In 2020, he argued that an otherwise lawful presidential act did not become impeachable merely because it was partly motivated by reelection. In the AlterNet interview, he stood by that position, saying such a rule could expose virtually any president to impeachment and comparing Trump’s exercises of authority with those of Abraham Lincoln, Thomas Jefferson and Franklin Roosevelt.

When confronted with his 2019 prediction that Trump would not refuse to leave office after an electoral defeat, Dershowitz declared that events had proved him right. Trump ultimately vacated the White House, he said, despite challenging and continuing to criticize the result.

That answer reduces acceptance of an election to one final physical act: leaving the building. It excludes the sustained challenge preceding the transfer of power and makes departure the only relevant test. Dershowitz likewise rejected the interviewer’s description of January 6 as a coup attempt. Trump, he argued, called for lawful protest and did not personally urge illegal conduct or attempt to prevent succession through physical force; those who broke the law acted on their own.

This formulation again narrows responsibility until almost nothing fits inside it. Only an explicit instruction to commit a crime or a personal application of force would apparently count. Political power exercised through rhetoric, pressure or mobilization drops out of the frame. Dershowitz did not merely dispute a label; he adopted a standard that isolates the most powerful actor from the conduct of the people acting in his cause.

Government censorship, selectively defined

Dershowitz blamed the decline of debate largely on Democrats, citing Harvard’s refusal to host a debate with professor Michael Sandel and what he described as efforts to drive Sen. John Fetterman from the party. He also urged listeners not to patronize independent bookstores, alleging that many refuse to carry his book, Why I Quit the Dems: and So Should You!, while stocking only liberal or progressive titles.

He called those businesses “enemies of free speech” and “enemies of the First Amendment.” Yet independent bookstores are not the state. Their purchasing decisions may be narrow, commercially foolish or politically conformist, but they do not wield immigration detention, grant-making authority or control over public institutions. Treating an unwanted book order as constitutionally comparable to government coercion does not clarify free speech. It disguises the power difference at the center of it.

When AlterNet raised Trump administration intervention at the Smithsonian, Dershowitz defended it. He said publicly supported institutions should not promote an ideological account of the United States and agreed with the administration telling the Smithsonian what it could not do. Because the Smithsonian itself is part of the state, he argued, one part of government may restrain another.

That position assigns the administration authority to decide which historical interpretation is objective and which is propaganda. Dershowitz acknowledged that canceling millions of dollars in university research grants was wrong and said he opposed restrictions on “pure science.” But he immediately reopened the door to political defunding when scientists, in his view, use their positions to indoctrinate students. He also approved of defunding National Public Radio and public television on those grounds.

The decisive category is therefore not state interference but Dershowitz’s judgment about the targeted institution. Government restrictions become legitimate correction when he considers the speech ideological; private institutions declining to feature him become censors.

“Debate” as a license for political control

The same instability appeared in the discussion of climate science. Dershowitz said firing people for teaching particular subjects could be wrong, but added that he would want to examine what they taught. He suggested that a professor who presented climate change as beyond dispute or excluded dissenters should be fired. He described the extent of humanity’s effect on the climate as an issue for classroom debate and invoked a creationist applicant as an example of why institutions should admit people with fundamentally contrary views.

There is a defensible principle buried here: education should teach students to examine arguments rather than enforce personal allegiance. Dershowitz described his own goal as helping students think independently, even when they reached conclusions he opposed.

But intellectual openness is not the same as requiring institutions to treat every factual proposition as equally sound. Nor does it explain who should decide when instruction becomes indoctrination. In Dershowitz’s account, that judgment repeatedly migrates upward to officials empowered to remove funding, control exhibits or fire educators.

His advertised commitment to challenge everything thus operates alongside a willingness to let the government punish institutions for conclusions he regards as political. The freedom is expansive for the challenger and conditional for the challenged. It protects Dershowitz’s demand for a platform, but not necessarily a noncitizen’s ability to remain in the country while speaking; it condemns a bookstore’s inventory choices, but tolerates executive control over public history.

Dershowitz told AlterNet that debate is dead. His own argument shows a more specific problem: “debate” can become a flattering name for power deciding which speakers deserve protection, which expertise must be reopened on command and which institutions may be disciplined until they adopt the government’s preferred definition of neutrality.
