'Saw' director: Hollywood is shy about Trump content — and that's scary

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 29, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that director Kevin Greutert believes studios and financiers avoid explicit Trump-era political stories because they fear alienating “half of the American body politic.” He said that concern killed The Endorsement, a proposed horror film about a small-town journalist threatened by a politically motivated serial killer after persuading her newspaper to endorse Hillary Clinton in 2016.

Greutert also said a planned version of Saw XI involving two mayoral candidates and an election-themed trap was abandoned when “a different approach was selected,” though he did not say political pressure caused that decision. He contrasted those experiences with Saw VI, whose attack on health-insurance power reached theaters in 2009 partly, he suggested, because producers had not anticipated how politically charged healthcare reform would become.

In the interview, Greutert condemned the killing attributed to Luigi Mangione and rejected political violence. He also argued that horror can expose abusive power through stories of captivity, exploitation and helplessness, even when filmmakers avoid naming contemporary politicians directly.

The veto belongs to capital

The decisive actors here are not moviegoers imagined as two equally sized, permanently offended camps. They are studio executives, producers and financiers who control which scripts receive money, distribution and an audience.

According to Greutert, those gatekeepers rejected The Endorsement because explicit political content might repel part of the market. That is not public censorship, and the interview does not establish government coercion behind the decision. It is private capital exercising cultural power through risk avoidance.

The distinction matters. “Hollywood is shy” turns an institutional choice into a personality trait, as though an industry became bashful. Executives made—or declined to make—specific investments. Their caution protects revenue and access; its consequence is a narrower political imagination on screen.

Neutrality for sale

The demand not to “offend half” sounds like fairness only if every political subject is treated as an interchangeable consumer preference. A story about intimidation following a newspaper endorsement is not merely partisan branding. It concerns whether threats can punish political speech.

Yet the financing logic Greutert describes converts that conflict into market segmentation. The aggressor, the target and the institution capable of protecting expression disappear into a spreadsheet labeled “controversy.” This is how commercial neutrality launders power: the powerful retain their capacity to intimidate, while artists are told that naming the conflict would itself be divisive.

Ambiguity can produce excellent art. Greutert points to Invasion of the Body Snatchers and The Twilight Zone as works that used genre to approach politics indirectly. But creative indirection and compulsory evasiveness are not the same thing. One expands meaning; the other limits what can be financed.

Where the interview overreaches

AlterNet’s strongest evidence concerns Greutert’s own projects and what he says financiers told his team. Its broader claims are considerably less secure. The interviewer asserts that Trump allies are purchasing media companies and that the government has tried to stifle critical comedians, but the supplied article does not substantiate those claims.

Greutert also speculates that Trump, given the opportunity, would treat political opponents as Augusto Pinochet’s regime did. That is an opinion and a hypothetical, not a reported act or established plan. Presenting it alongside concrete accounts of rejected film projects risks substituting analogy for evidence.

The article’s political framing is therefore uneven. It identifies a real gatekeeping mechanism, then dilutes that finding with claims it does not document. Scathing conclusions require stronger sourcing, not louder comparisons.

Horror knows who controls the room

Greutert’s most useful observation is simpler: horror is often organized around unequal power. Someone controls the basement, the trap, the institution or the terms of escape. Someone else bears the pain. That structure can illuminate politics because politics also determines who may impose costs and who is forced to absorb them.

But the language of universal helplessness can blur responsibility. Greutert says audiences are implicated in systems from which they benefit, yet shared participation does not mean equal agency. A consumer, a filmmaker and a studio executive do not possess the same ability to decide what gets produced. Nor does a patient denied coverage wield the power of the executive administering the policy.

The sharper political horror story is not that everyone is trapped. It is that identifiable institutions build the trap, authorized decision-makers maintain it, and weaker people are then blamed for failing to escape.

The system prefers a monster without a name

This episode reveals how cultural institutions domesticate political art. They may tolerate injustice as metaphor, corruption as atmosphere and authoritarianism as an unnamed monster. What alarms them is specificity: a recognizable election, an identifiable movement, a conflict whose beneficiaries cannot pretend the story concerns somebody else.

No censor needs to issue an order when concentrated financing already rewards ambiguity and penalizes confrontation. The result is not silence, exactly. It is an industry permitted to depict terror while remaining coy about who holds the keys.
