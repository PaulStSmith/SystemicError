'I really can't point to much': Republicans admit they got little accomplished in Congress

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 25, 20255 min read

Republican lawmakers admitted 2025 was a legislative wasteland, with Congress setting a modern record for lowest output in a president’s first year, but some attributed their inaction to a simple explanation: President Donald Trump did much of their work for them through executive orders.

With fewer than 40 bills signed into law, the House and Senate managed historically low productivity, reported the Washington Post. The House cast just 362 votes — barely half the number from 2017, Trump’s first year, when Republicans also held the majority. Meanwhile, nearly 60 percent of Senate votes focused on confirming Trump’s nominees rather than passing legislation.

Rep. David Joyce (R-OH), a 13-year veteran lawmaker, captured the sentiment succinctly: “I guess we got the big, beautiful bill done. Other than that, I really can’t point to much that we got accomplished.”

The problem, according to some Republicans, was Trump’s reliance on executive orders rather than legislative action. Sen. Josh Hawley (R-MO) gave Congress an “incomplete” grade, noting that Trump signed more executive orders in 11 months than during his entire first term.

“He has signed every executive order he could possibly think of on this,” Hawley said. “There just comes a point at which it’s like, Congress sooner or later has to legislate.”

Trump issued more than 70 percent of the combined 12-year total of executive orders from the Barack Obama and Joe Biden presidencies, according to former House parliamentarian Thomas Wickham. This shift has fundamentally altered the balance of power between branches — all numbers indicating healthy congressional productivity are declining, while presidential power expands.

Rep. Jim Jordan (R-OH) defended Trump’s approach, invoking the president’s March address: “He said people said you needed a new law to secure the border. Turns out all you needed was a new president.”

However, this approach created significant challenges. House Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA) repeatedly shuttered the House due to gridlock, including a seven-week closure that contributed to a 43-day government shutdown. Republicans never developed a coherent health care strategy, leaving the party divided heading into 2026 as expiring tax credits loomed.

Sen. Susan Collins (R-ME), serving since 1997, noted unprecedented divisiveness, while Sen. Rand Paul (R-KY) warned of ballooning debt after Republicans raised the ceiling by $5 trillion in a single vote.

The practical consequence: Congress ceded authority to the executive branch, reducing its own relevance and legislative capacity while establishing a troubling precedent for future administrations.

Our Analysis:

Analysis: The Legislative Drought of 2025

In a year that could have been marked by significant legislative achievements, Republican lawmakers have instead presided over a legislative wasteland, setting a record for inaction in a president’s first year that could only be described as historic — and not in a good way. The Washington Post’s report on this dubious achievement reveals a Congress crippled by its own complacency, with fewer than 40 bills signed into law and a House that seems more interested in setting records for inactivity than in governing.

The Executive Order Overreach

The root of this barren legislative landscape, according to some within the GOP, is none other than President Donald Trump. Trump’s voracious appetite for executive orders is cited as a primary reason for Congress’s dormancy. Trump, it seems, has confused the presidency with a monarchy, issuing more executive orders in 11 months than he did in his entire first term and eclipsing the 12-year total of Obama and Biden combined. This is not governance; it’s a power grab.

Sen. Josh Hawley (R-MO) perhaps put it most succinctly, albeit generously, giving Congress an “incomplete” grade. But let’s be clear: this is a failing grade. The legislative branch has effectively outsourced its responsibilities to the executive, abandoning its duty to legislate.

The Consequences of Inaction

Rep. Jim Jordan (R-OH) and others may defend Trump’s unilateral approach to policy-making, but this defense is as short-sighted as it is dangerous. The reliance on executive orders has not only led to legislative atrophy but has fundamentally altered the balance of power in Washington, eroding the checks and balances that are critical to our democracy.

House Speaker Mike Johnson’s repeated closures of the House, including a seven-week hiatus contributing to a 43-day government shutdown, is emblematic of a broader issue: a Congress that cannot or will not function. Meanwhile, the looming crisis of expiring tax credits and a divided party with no coherent health care strategy underscore the practical consequences of this inaction.

Sen. Susan Collins (R-ME) and Sen. Rand Paul (R-KY) voice concerns over divisiveness and ballooning debt, respectively, pointing to the broader ramifications of a Congress that has ceded too much ground to the executive. By raising the debt ceiling by $5 trillion in a single vote, Republicans have not only contributed to the fiscal irresponsibility but have also set a dangerous precedent for future administrations.

A Troubling Precedent

The practical consequence of this legislative drought extends beyond the immediate policy implications; it has fundamentally weakened the legislative branch, reducing its role and relevance in the federal government. This shift towards a more executive-centric approach to governance establishes a troubling precedent for the future, where presidents, emboldened by the example set by Trump and acquiesced to by a compliant Congress, may seek to further consolidate power.

In essence, the Republican lawmakers of 2025 have not only presided over a legislative wasteland; they have actively participated in the erosion of democratic norms and the dismantling of the separation of powers that is so vital to our republic. This year of inaction should not be seen merely as a failure of one party or administration but as a warning sign of the fragility of our democracy when one branch abdicates its responsibility to another.

In conclusion, the record set in 2025 is not one to be proud of. It is a stark reminder of the need for a Congress that is willing and able to fulfill its constitutional duties. The legislative drought of 2025 should be a wake-up call, a call to action for lawmakers and citizens alike to demand better, to insist on a government that works, legislates, and balances power as our founders intended.
