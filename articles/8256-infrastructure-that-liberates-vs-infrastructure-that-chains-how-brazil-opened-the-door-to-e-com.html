Infrastructure That Liberates vs. Infrastructure That Chains: How Brazil Opened the Door to E-Commerce While the U.S. Slams It Shut

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 29, 20253 min read

There’s a quiet irony playing out across the global economy: Brazil, a country routinely dismissed by American pundits as bureaucratic or backward, built one of the most accessible logistical infrastructures on the planet—while the United States, patron saint of “free markets,” erected a private gauntlet that strangles its own small businesses.

And the saddest part? The U.S. already has the tools to fix it. It simply chooses not to.

Brazil’s CEP: A Public Good That Actually Works

Brazil’s postal code system—the CEP—isn’t just a list of numbers. It’s a public, government-maintained database with near-surgical precision. A single CEP can identify a specific building, a stretch of street, even one side of the block. This isn’t an accident. It’s policy.

By offering a free, accurate, universal system of geolocation, Brazil quietly did something Washington hasn’t attempted in decades: it democratized access to economic participation.

A micro-bakery operating out of someone’s garage and a billion-real marketplace like Mercado Livre both run on the same logistical backbone. New businesses don’t need to license proprietary address datasets, pay for third-party verification, or integrate with private routing APIs before they can even make their first sale.

Brazil lowered the barrier to entry. Full stop.

Meanwhile in the U.S.: Gatekeepers Everywhere

The United States had every opportunity to build a similar public good. Instead, it systemically gutted the one agency capable of doing it—the USPS—and handed logistics to private gatekeepers.

The U.S. invented the ZIP Code in 1963. That’s fine. But in 1983, it introduced ZIP+4, a format every bit as precise as the Brazilian CEP. It can pinpoint a block, a building, sometimes a single floor. In practice? Most Americans don’t even know their ZIP+4.

Why? Because the U.S. government never mandated, publicized, or integrated it meaningfully. Instead of developing a public geolocation infrastructure, Washington:

  • starved the USPS financially, politically, and operationally;

  • forced it to pre-fund retiree health benefits 75 years into the future (a poison pill designed to break it);

  • let FedEx, UPS, and later Amazon carve out private empires on top of the government’s retreat.

By the time the “digital economy” arrived, the U.S. didn’t have democratized logistics. It had privatized chokepoints.

Small businesses today must navigate:

  • USPS’s politically engineered inefficiency,

  • UPS and FedEx’s cartel-like pricing,

  • Amazon’s logistical supremacy,

  • address-verification APIs locked behind paywalls,

  • and a labyrinth of private delivery zones.

So much for the “land of opportunity.”

The Big Lie: ‘Necessity Forced Innovation’

Corporate apologists love to say that the U.S. produced Amazon, UPS, and FedEx because the government “failed,” forcing the private sector to innovate.

It’s a seductive myth—because it conveniently turns public neglect into corporate sainthood. But the numbers expose the fantasy:

  • UPS was founded in 1907, long before ZIP Codes even existed.

  • FedEx was founded in 1971, driven by changes in aviation and deregulation—not postal codes.

These companies didn’t rise from government failure. They rose because the U.S. made a political choice: hollow out the public sector so private giants could fill the void.

That isn’t innovation. It’s abandonment.

Two Models, Two Futures

Brazil’s model:

A public, universal, free logistical infrastructure.

  • Low barriers of entry.

  • Equal footing for micro-businesses and major retailers.

  • A foundation for broad-based prosperity.

The U.S. model:

Privatized infrastructure masquerading as efficiency.

  • High startup costs.

  • Dependence on monopolistic platforms.

  • Constant tolls to participate.

One creates opportunity.
The other sells it back to you with interest.

The Trump Administrations Made It Worse

Both the first and second Trump administrations openly tried to cripple the USPS—kneecapping mail-in voting was only the most public example. Destroying public infrastructure has always been part of the Republican orthodoxy: the less the government can do, the more corporations can charge.

Why invest in ZIP+4 as public infrastructure when private corporations can monetize the vacuum?

The result is predictable: a nation where the barrier to participating in e-commerce is not creativity or effort, but whether you can afford Amazon’s fees, UPS’s prices, or FedEx’s surcharges.

The Bottom Line

Brazil built an on-ramp.
The United States built a toll road.

Brazil invested in public goods.
The U.S. manufactured private chokepoints.

Brazil democratized access to the digital economy.
The U.S. stuffed it behind paywalls.

Never underestimate the power of infrastructure. Some countries build it to empower their people. Others dismantle it to empower their corporations.
