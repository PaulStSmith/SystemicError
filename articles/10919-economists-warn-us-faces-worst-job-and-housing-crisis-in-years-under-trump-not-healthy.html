Economists warn US faces worst job and housing crisis in years under Trump: 'Not healthy'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 4, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

U.S. President Donald Trump reacts while sitting next to the President of Poland Karol Nawrocki during a meeting in the Oval Office at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., September 3, 2025. REUTERS/Brian Snyder

President Donald Trump fired the director of the Bureau of Labor Statistics last month after he didn't like the jobs report for July. Looking at economic data today isn't much better, and in some respects, it has gotten worse.

Economists said they're seeing some of the worst job numbers in at least a decade.

Heather Long, chief economist at Navy Federal Credit Union, revealed that the United States officially has more unemployed people than job openings — the first time that's happened since April 2021. Meanwhile, the unemployment number is climbing.

"This is yet another crack in the labor market that illustrates how much harder it is to get a new job right now than what we've seen in a long time," she wrote on X.

She also pointed out that the "hiring rate" in the country has fallen to just 3.3%, which is the lowest it has been since 2013.

"It's not healthy to have so little hiring. It's not dynamic. People can't grow in their careers. This is a frozen job market," she warned.

She also added the note, "Yes, the June 2024 hiring rate was 3.2%, but you can see below how low 3.3% really is compared to the past decade."

When it comes to housing, things are even worse, and Long thinks that Trump should declare a "national housing emergency."

"Honestly, it could be a good idea. Housing affordability in the US is basically at the worst point in about 40 years. It's been stuck there for ~3 years now," said Long.

She showed two charts that illustrate the problem: "1) Housing unaffordability is worst in 40 years, 2) Home prices and rent prices have shot up dramatically compared to median incomes."

"While housing is mostly a local government issue (zoning, etc.), there are some things the national government can do to help," Long added. 'And that list goes well beyond the Fed lowering interest rates. We need more home building. There are many ways to help incentivize (or subsidize) that."

It's the same thing that real estate developers have been saying for the past two months, CNBC reported. Quality low-income housing is too expensive to develop and still generate the profits they seek. He's hoping that government rescue might be on the way.

Also writing on Wednesday, Nobel Prize-winning economist Paul Krugman sounded the alarm.

The problem with Trump declaring a national housing emergency is that Trump will then work to "solve" the housing emergency.

"Unfortunately, everything Trump is doing that affects housing availability will make the emergency worse," said Krugman.

"Donald Trump loves emergencies," Krugman explained. "He has been in office less than 8 months, but has already formally declared 9 national emergencies, as well as a 'crime emergency' in Washington, D.C."

He noted that the prospect of the Trump administration saying there is a housing emergency gives him a "sinking feeling."

"The popular perception that housing has become unaffordable is grounded in reality," Krugman said, citing the data.

The fear is that Trump will follow the Heritage Foundation's "Project 2025" manifesto, "Mandate for Leadership." Rather than helping to subsidize affordable housing with tax breaks or subsidies to developers to drive down costs, the far-right plan is simply to deregulate pollution controls. Housing is all about "not in my back yard."

"Congress should prioritize any and all legislative support for the single-family home," the plan demands.

Our Analysis:

The Troubling Intersection of Politics and Economic Policy: A Critical Analysis

In the latest episode of what can only be described as political theatrics masquerading as governance, President Donald Trump's decision to fire the director of the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) over an unfavorable jobs report is not just an overreach of executive power; it's a dangerous precedent that undermines the integrity of vital economic institutions. This move, coupled with the dismal economic indicators now coming to light, paints a picture of an administration more concerned with optics than with addressing the root causes of the nation's economic woes.

Economic Indicators: A Grim Reality

Economists are sounding the alarm, revealing some of the worst job numbers in at least a decade. Heather Long of Navy Federal Credit Union points out a particularly troubling statistic: the United States now has more unemployed people than job openings for the first time since April 2021. This stark reality is underscored by a hiring rate that has plummeted to a mere 3.3%, the lowest since 2013. Long's analysis is both clear and dire: the job market is "frozen," stifling career growth and dynamism in the workforce.

The housing market fares no better, with affordability at its worst in 40 years. Long's call for a "national housing emergency" is not just warranted; it's a necessary recognition of a crisis that requires immediate and decisive action. Yet, the suggestion that Trump's declaration of such an emergency could lead to meaningful or beneficial reforms is met with skepticism, and rightfully so.

Trump's Track Record: A Cause for Concern

The article rightly highlights Nobel Prize-winning economist Paul Krugman's concerns regarding Trump's handling of national emergencies. Trump's penchant for declaring emergencies, nine in less than eight months, coupled with a "crime emergency" in Washington, D.C., reveals a pattern of using crisis management not as a tool for genuine reform but as a mechanism for consolidating power and advancing politically expedient agendas.

Krugman's apprehension that Trump's approach to a housing emergency would align with the Heritage Foundation's "Project 2025" manifesto is particularly alarming. This plan, which advocates for deregulating pollution controls under the guise of addressing housing affordability, is a clear example of policy misdirection. Rather than tackling the housing crisis through constructive measures like subsidies or tax breaks for affordable housing development, this approach seeks to exploit the crisis for ideological gains, potentially exacerbating the very issues it claims to address.

Conclusion: The Need for Genuine Solutions

The article lays bare the consequences of allowing political motivations to dictate economic policy. The Trump administration's handling of economic data, its response to worsening job and housing markets, and its reliance on declaring national emergencies as a fix-all solution are indicative of a broader disregard for the complexities of economic management.

The call for more home building, as echoed by real estate developers and economists alike, alongside demands for substantive legislative support for affordable housing, underscores the need for policy interventions grounded in reality, not political expediency. As the nation grapples with these economic challenges, the need for a shift away from the current administration's approach is not just clear; it's critical. The path forward requires a return to data-driven, empirically sound economic policies that prioritize the welfare of the American people over political posturing. The stakes are too high for anything less.
