Teachers fume after Trump's education chief fumbles question about student 'guinea pigs'

Paulo SantosAugust 24, 20263 min read

A Wrestling Promoter Is Selling the Classroom to the Machine

The People With Power

Linda McMahon is not a random cable-news guest. She is Donald Trump’s education chief, speaking from the top of the federal education apparatus and using that platform to normalize AI in classrooms. That matters because institutional power is not held by the teachers reacting on X. It is held by the official who can help turn weak evidence into policy momentum.

Her interview was simple enough: she described students spending the first two hours of the school day on computers and praised the setup as if a screen could substitute for instruction. When pressed, she admitted there are not many metrics yet. That is the key fact. The government’s education leadership is advancing a tool before it can show it works.

The Sales Pitch

McMahon tried to frame AI as a “one-on-one tutor,” which is the standard tech-industry bait: abolish the messy human relationship, call the replacement personalized, and insist the downgrade is innovation. It is a familiar move because it flatters administrators, vendors, and politicians who want a cheap miracle without paying for actual educational investment.

The problem is not that technology exists in classrooms. The problem is the political project hiding behind the technology. If students are already spending hours in front of computers, the next step is obvious: less teacher, more software, less judgment, more automation, less public institution, more private platform.

The Real Harm

Teachers immediately understood what McMahon was describing because they know who gets sacrificed when ed-tech becomes ideology. The classroom loses a professional adult and gains a product. Students become data points. Teachers become compliance managers. The public school becomes a delivery system for someone else’s software contract.

The outrage from educators is not pearl-clutching. It is a defense of a basic fact that the people in power keep trying to erode: learning is not just content transfer. It is supervision, interpretation, correction, trust, and human judgment. AI cannot do that, and the secretary admitted she does not have the evidence to prove otherwise. Yet the rollout continues because the point is not evidence. The point is rearranging labor and authority.

Misdirection by Design

The article’s framing is weak because it treats the issue as if McMahon merely “fumbled” a question about classroom technology. That sanitizes the offense. This was not an awkward answer. It was an official selling an unproven system while admitting the measurement gap out loud.

The backlash from teachers is being pointed at the spectacle of one bad interview, but the larger target is the governing class that wants to replace public capacity with branded automation. McMahon’s background as a wrestling promoter is not a quirky detail. It is a clue. She knows performance. She is doing performance now, only the venue is public education and the stakes are children’s instruction.

The Pattern

This is the broader authoritarian pattern in miniature: degrade public institutions, call the degradation modernization, and present skepticism as backwardness. First comes the claim that the old system is inefficient. Then comes the vendor solution. Then comes the quiet transfer of power away from educators and toward machines, managers, and contractors.

That is why the teachers were right to be furious. The issue is not whether AI can occasionally assist learning. The issue is who gets to decide what education is for. Under Trump, with McMahon at the helm, the answer is increasingly clear: not teachers, not students, and certainly not the public.
