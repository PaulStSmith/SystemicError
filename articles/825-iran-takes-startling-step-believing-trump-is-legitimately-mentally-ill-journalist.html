Iran takes startling step believing Trump is 'legitimately mentally ill': journalist

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosJune 15, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Diplomacy by Cognitive Workaround

The Source Material

The reporting says Iranian negotiators, amid peace talks with Washington, added “senior psychologists” to help shape messages for Donald Trump after Iranian sources concluded he may be mentally impaired. Jeremy Scahill said the Iranians reportedly began screening communications through psychologists and saw “some progress.”

The Real Center of Power

This is not a story about manners, bedside concern, or diplomatic creativity. It is a story about who actually holds power in a negotiation and who has to adapt to that power. Trump is the institutional center here. He is the president of the United States, the person to whom the messages are ultimately being tailored, and the one whose condition allegedly reshapes the entire diplomatic process.

That is the crucial fact the source makes plain even while flirting with tabloid fascination: when one man’s apparent instability becomes a variable in statecraft, the system is already degraded. Foreign negotiators are not merely negotiating policy; they are negotiating the president’s perceived mental state.

Who Enabled the Outcome

If the reported account is accurate, the Iranian side made a deliberate tactical decision. They did not treat Trump as a normal interlocutor. They built a psychological filter into their diplomatic pipeline because they believed his cognition required it.

That is not a joke. It is an admission that diplomacy has become personalized to the point of absurdity. Statecraft should run through institutions, process, and continuity. Instead, the story describes a regime where messaging must be calibrated around one man’s volatility, age, and apparent impairment.

The enabling structure is American. A presidency that concentrates immense authority in one individual invites exactly this kind of external adaptation. When power is this centralized, everyone else starts gaming the person instead of the office.

The Frame Does the Work

The article’s framing is weak because it invites readers to treat this as a bizarre anecdote about Trump’s health. That is the most superficial possible reading. The relevant question is not whether Trump looked tired, bruised, or swollen. The relevant question is why a nuclear adversary believes it must route communications through psychologists to make contact with the American presidency.

The health gossip is decorative. The institutional failure is the substance. Reducing the story to speculation about Trump’s body or mental fitness risks obscuring the harder truth: governance has become so personalized that foreign actors may see psychological manipulation as part of normal diplomacy.

What the Story Reveals

The larger pattern is political decay through personalization. When a president becomes the main chokepoint for policy, the state stops acting like a state and starts acting like a mood-driven court. Advisors become handlers. Diplomats become translators. Foreign governments become amateur clinical analysts.

That is how institutional cowardice and concentration of power feed each other. Everyone around the office adjusts to the man, and the office itself shrinks. The result is not just secrecy or dysfunction. It is a system that invites manipulation because it no longer trusts its own structure.

The Systemic Error

This story is not mainly about Iran, and it is not mainly about Trump’s health. It is about a political order so personalized that other governments may now think they need psychologists to communicate with the president. That is not diplomacy in any healthy sense. It is administrative collapse dressed up as strategy.

When power is built around one unstable figure, the damage does not stay contained inside that figure. It spreads outward through every institution that has to compensate for him.
