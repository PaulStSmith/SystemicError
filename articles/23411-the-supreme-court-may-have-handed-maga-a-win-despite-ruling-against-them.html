The Supreme Court may have handed MAGA a win —despite ruling against them

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Six Words of Judicial Evasion

The Map and the Machinery

Missouri Republicans are trying to replace an electoral map in a way that could eliminate a Democratic congressional seat. Justice Brett Kavanaugh rejected one attempt with a six-word order offering no rationale; minutes later, federal district judge Stephen Clark ordered the state to use the Republican-friendly map in a nearly identical case brought by different plaintiffs. The conflict now sits before the Eighth Circuit while election officials remain unsure which districts they are supposed to administer.

Power Has a Name

The powerful actors here are not the election administrators trapped between contradictory commands. They are the Republican officials seeking a more favorable map and the federal judges deciding whether that map will govern the election.

Kavanaugh made a decision, even if he declined to explain it. Clark then made another, fully aware that Kavanaugh had just denied substantially the same result. Missouri officials sympathetic to Donald Trump may now obtain through Onder v. Missouri what a Trump supporter failed to secure through Hoskins v. von Glahn.

That is not institutional confusion arising spontaneously. It is the foreseeable consequence of identifiable officials exercising power.

Silence Is Not Restraint

The source treats Kavanaugh’s terse denial as potentially hostile to the Republican claim. Perhaps it was. But an unexplained order does not establish a rule that lower courts, state officials, or voters can reliably follow.

One possible interpretation is that Kavanaugh rejected Hoskins because the applicant had “unclean hands”—because he helped create the problem he wanted the Court to solve. If so, that reasoning may leave other Republican plaintiffs free to pursue the same partisan objective. Because Kavanaugh supplied no explanation, his order blocked one litigant without necessarily blocking the maneuver.

Judicial opacity is often mistaken for judicial modesty. Here it operates as discretionary power without public accountability: the justice determines the immediate outcome while withholding the principle governing what comes next.

Chaos Has Beneficiaries

Election officials will bear the administrative burden. Voters may confront changing maps, altered ballots, conflicting eligibility rules, or uncertainty about whether votes will count. But describing this merely as “chaos” risks laundering agency out of the story.

Republican litigants are pursuing a map that benefits Republicans. Clark authorized that map despite the Supreme Court’s near-simultaneous denial in the parallel case. Kavanaugh withheld the reasoning that might have resolved—or at least narrowed—the dispute. The disorder follows those choices.

It would be an inference to claim that every actor intended every resulting disruption. No such inference is necessary. Political institutions routinely reward officials who create uncertainty and then exploit it. A party seeking structural advantage can benefit from compressed deadlines, exhausted administrators, confused voters, and litigation that lasts until ordinary remedies become impractical.

The Technicality Is the Politics

The distinction between Hoskins and Onder is procedural: different plaintiffs, virtually identical legal issues. That difference may determine whether Republicans can impose their preferred map. The same pattern appears in the reported dispute over mail voting, where a technical ruling left election officials uncertain about longstanding procedures and voters unsure whether mailed ballots would be counted.

Calling these outcomes technical understates their political content. Procedure decides who may sue, when officials must act, which ballots survive, and which electorate a map produces. When courts repeatedly resolve urgent election disputes through narrow orders and unexplained distinctions, procedural ambiguity becomes a form of governance.

A System Built to Avoid Owning the Result

The larger pattern is institutional power exercised through fragmented responsibility. Republican officials can say they merely pursued available legal remedies. A district judge can say he ruled only on the parties before him. A Supreme Court justice can issue six words and decline to explain them. Election administrators are then left to implement the consequences, and voters absorb the risk.

That arrangement does more than obscure blame. It allows partisan transformation to proceed without any single institution clearly owning it. The map changes, voting becomes harder, uncertainty spreads—and every powerful actor points to someone else’s docket.

The systemic error is not that the machinery malfunctioned. It is that the machinery permits officials to produce consequential disorder while presenting themselves as passive custodians of process.
