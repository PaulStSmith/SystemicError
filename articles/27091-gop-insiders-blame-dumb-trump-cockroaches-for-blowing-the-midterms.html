GOP insiders blame 'dumb' Trump 'cockroaches' for blowing the midterms

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 5, 20263 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that Republican insiders blamed President Donald Trump, his White House political team, and congressional campaign leadership for a sweeping midterm defeat that included losses in South Carolina, Texas, and Kansas. Operatives singled out Susie Wiles, James Blair, Chris LaCivita, and Richard Hudson, arguing that poor candidate selection, an aggressive redistricting strategy, and a campaign detached from voters’ economic concerns produced the losses.

The insiders also blamed Trump directly. Republican staffers told the Intelligencer that voters were focused on high prices, which they linked to the war in Iran, while Trump emphasized renovation projects, allegations about vandals, and other subjects far removed from household finances. One operative reported shifts of 30 to 40 points among some voters who had previously supported Trump, though the article does not provide the underlying polling data or define the affected constituencies.

A Republican pollster predicted a “reckoning in Trumpworld” and compared Trump’s advisers to “cockroaches,” suggesting they would survive despite the defeat. That insult supplies the headline’s teaser, but it also exposes the insiders’ preferred explanation: the people around Trump failed him.

Power was concentrated at the top

The people named in the recriminations were not spectators. According to the reporting, White House officials and Republican campaign organizations helped choose candidates, shape the message, and initiate a redistricting fight. They exercised institutional power over money, strategy, personnel, and the party’s national agenda.

But Trump held more power than any adviser now being offered up for blame. He hired the team, dominated the campaign, and delivered the closing pitch. The account therefore describes more than staff incompetence. It describes a party organized around one president’s priorities and then surprised when voters judged those priorities.

The scapegoat hunt has limits

Calling advisers the “dumbest people” is emotionally satisfying for operatives seeking distance from a collapse. It is not a serious theory of responsibility. Wiles, Blair, LaCivita, Hudson, and campaign officials may have made consequential errors, but the insiders’ own testimony says Trump repeatedly focused public attention away from affordability and toward his preferred grievances and projects.

That contradiction matters. Republican officials want to portray the defeat as a management failure because management can be replaced without reconsidering the political arrangement that empowered it. The “cockroaches” framing turns accountability into palace intrigue: Which courtiers survive? Who gets pushed out? Who inherits the same machinery?

“Messaging” conceals the governing failure

Several operatives reduced the result to bad messaging, poor candidates, or “unforced errors.” Yet their account identifies a substantive problem: voters were struggling with prices while the administration and its campaign concentrated on other priorities. If the war in Iran contributed to those prices, as the quoted staffers claimed, then this was not merely a failure to explain policy. It was a governing choice with electoral consequences.

“Messaging failure” is Washington’s preferred solvent for dissolving responsibility. It recasts unpopular decisions as communication defects and treats voters as an audience that received the wrong advertisement. The possibility that voters understood the administration’s priorities and rejected them receives far less attention.

Insiders discovered the public too late

The reporting depicts party elites recognizing household economics only after suffering losses. Before the votes were counted, they had enough power to select candidates, wage a redistricting battle, and build a campaign around Trump. Afterward, they described themselves as horrified observers of decisions made by the same political structure they served.

That is institutional cowardice in its familiar form. Responsibility flows downward when power succeeds and sideways when it fails. Advisers blame other advisers; operatives blame the convention; strategists blame the closing speech. The people who controlled the party apparatus become, in retrospect, helpless witnesses.

A reckoning designed to preserve the system

The larger pattern is not simply that a powerful political operation can become insulated and lose. It is that insiders often respond to defeat by personalizing failure while protecting the structure that produced it.

A genuine reckoning would examine who controlled policy, candidate selection, campaign resources, and the party’s agenda—and why so few Republicans challenged those choices before voters imposed consequences. The reckoning described here is narrower: remove some personnel, refine the sales pitch, and leave concentrated power intact. That is not reconstruction. It is maintenance after a crash.
