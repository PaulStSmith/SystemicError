'It will never exist again': Steve Schmidt spots path to 'break MAGA for all time'

Based on an article by Robert Davis

Paulo SantosOctober 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that former Republican operative and anti-Trump activist Steve Schmidt argued on his podcast that voters could “break MAGA” as a nationally viable coalition by defeating Trump-aligned candidates in the November midterm elections. He predicted a sweeping repudiation but offered no polling, electoral analysis, or other evidence in the article to establish that outcome.

Schmidt pointed to reported fractures over President Donald Trump’s decision to wage war against Iran and his dismissal of rising living costs as a “Democratic hoax.” RawStory cites criticism from former Representative Marjorie Taylor Greene as evidence of internal strain. Schmidt nevertheless warned that even an electorally defeated MAGA faction could remain large enough to destabilize future elections, particularly by treating defeat as proof of fraud.

Prediction is not a pathway

The headline promises a path to permanent political defeat. What the article delivers is Schmidt saying people should vote Trump’s supporters out of office. That is an electoral objective, not a strategy—and certainly not evidence that MAGA “will never exist again” as a national coalition.

No decisive states, races, constituencies, turnout problems, or institutional mechanisms appear in the reporting. Schmidt’s certainty is therefore rhetoric. A movement organized around grievance and personal loyalty does not disappear merely because an opponent declares its expiration date.

Power still sits inside government

Trump holds presidential power. His administration can direct foreign policy, shape federal enforcement, command public attention, and force elected allies to choose between loyalty and dissent. Critics inside MAGA may expose fractures, but disagreement from prominent supporters is not the same as removing that institutional control.

Voters have agency at the ballot box, while parties, candidates, donors, election officials, courts, and legislatures determine how electoral power is organized and constrained. Reducing all of that to bravery makes for a clean podcast ending, but it conceals the institutions that enabled Trump’s coalition and would survive a midterm defeat.

The article sells confidence it does not substantiate

RawStory adopts Schmidt’s forecast as its central premise without seriously testing it. The phrase “spotted a path” upgrades an exhortation into a discovery. References to “cracks” in MAGA likewise suggest a measurable collapse, although the article supplies only selected disputes and public frustration over living costs.

That framing misdirects attention from the stronger actors. MAGA’s durability is not solely the product of devoted voters. It also depends on officeholders, party organizations, media figures, donors, and institutions willing to convert loyalty to Trump into governing power. Treating the coalition as a mass mood lets those enablers fade conveniently into the background.

Losing is not disappearing

Schmidt’s more defensible point cuts against his grand prediction: a faction can lose an election and retain enough power to obstruct government, intimidate officials, dominate a party, or reject legitimate outcomes. Electoral defeat and political extinction are different claims.

His description of a “Trump or else” message also identifies the authoritarian structure at issue: loyalty is elevated above pluralism, opposition is cast as illegitimate, and defeat is preemptively reframed as theft. Those reported claims deserve scrutiny. Declaring the coalition permanently broken does not answer them.

The system outlives the slogan

The larger pattern is liberal politics mistaking one election for institutional repair. Personalistic movements endure when political parties keep nominating their candidates, media systems reward their provocations, and officials face greater costs for dissent than obedience.

A midterm repudiation could restrict Trump’s governing power. It could not, by itself, erase the machinery that made MAGA electorally potent. RawStory’s framing turns Schmidt’s hope into a supposed roadmap; the actual story is harsher. Elections can remove officeholders, but dismantling an authoritarian political structure requires confronting every institution that continues to finance, amplify, normalize, and enforce it.
