'Capitulation': Editor quits after bosses tell staff to tiptoe around Trump

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 23, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Told by his “chief content officer” to dial back coverage of Donald Trump lest the publication incur his wrath, the editor of a smaller outlet detailing the inner workings of the government quit his job and took to Politico to make his case.

In a column published Wednesday afternoon, Alan Greenblatt, formerly of NPR and Congressional Quarterly, explained that he was willing to go out into an uncertain media landscape rather than, as an editor, have his hands tied while reporting the news.

Writing that he was employed as the editor of Governing, which is dedicated to state and local issues, Greenblatt, without naming names, wrote, “Earlier this year, the chief content officer for our parent company, e.Republic, stated in a meeting that we should not run articles that could draw the attention of the Trump White House and have them try to shut us down.”

According to the journalist, there has been a rising tide of “edicts” on a variety of topics over the past year, so he claims he told his bosses, “if we weren’t going to reflect reality — if we weren’t going to do journalism — I’d have to quit.”

When it came to mentions of Trump, he noted that Governing, with its focus on below-the-radar issues, is likely not on the radar of the Trump White House.

“It’s a wonky publication, and it’s not huge, but it has a sterling reputation for covering a crucial niche,” he wrote of his former employer before adding, “The notion that the litigious Trump would hit us with a lawsuit was not impossible, but it was unlikely. We certainly weren’t reliant on federal contracts to stay afloat. But after Trump’s second term began, the corporate anxiety about rocking the boat with our coverage became a constant.”

With that in mind, he noted, “That’s one of the saddest parts of Trump’s anti-media drive. After the government has gone after the big guys — Trump has engaged in court fights this year with CBS, ABC, The New York Times, The Wall Street Journal and the Associated Press, not to mention defunding NPR, my former employer — the little guys too often decide they lack the resources to stand up” and warned, “Capitulation becomes the easier course.”

Citing examples of colleagues who are increasingly being targeted by fearful publishers, he warned, “As official government data becomes more politicized, it’s increasingly important that independent sources maintain their integrity. Journalists in particular have a professional obligation to pursue and provide the truth to the best of their abilities. That’s the job. I hope and trust that most of my colleagues still have the courage to do it right.”

Our Analysis:

The Chilling Reality of Media Self-Censorship

In an era where the freedom of the press is more critical than ever, the resignation of Alan Greenblatt from Governing magazine sheds a stark light on a distressing trend: the self-censorship of media under the looming threat of political retribution. Greenblatt’s departure is not just a personal career move; it’s a canary in the coal mine for journalistic integrity and the relentless pressure exerted by political entities on media outlets.

A Fear-Driven Decision-Making Process

At the heart of Greenblatt’s resignation is a dire warning: when a publication’s editorial decisions are driven by fear of political backlash rather than the pursuit of truth, journalism itself is in jeopardy. According to Greenblatt, the “chief content officer” of e.Republic, Governing’s parent company, explicitly advised against publishing content that might draw the ire of the Trump White House. This is not merely caution; it’s capitulation—a clear sign that the specter of potential lawsuits or political vendettas can sway a publication’s content strategy.

The True Cost of Self-Censorship

What’s particularly alarming here is the implication that even smaller, niche publications like Governing, which prides itself on its “wonky” focus on state and local issues, are not immune to the chilling effects of political pressure. This self-censorship has far-reaching consequences, eroding public trust in media and undermining the very foundation of democratic discourse. When publications alter their coverage to avoid conflict with political powerhouses, they betray their fundamental duty to the public: to report the truth, unabashedly and without compromise.

The Domino Effect on Smaller Outlets

The plight of Governing is emblematic of a larger issue facing the media landscape. As Greenblatt notes, after high-profile media entities have been targeted by the government, smaller publications, lacking the resources to fight back, often choose the path of least resistance. This domino effect creates an environment where journalistic courage is replaced by caution, and in-depth, critical reporting is sacrificed at the altar of political expediency.

A Call to Arms for Journalistic Integrity

Greenblatt’s parting shot is a rallying cry for journalists to resist the urge to capitulate to external pressures and to remain steadfast in their commitment to truth-telling. The media’s role as a watchdog is predicated on its ability to operate independently of political influence. When that independence is compromised, so too is the public’s ability to stay informed about the machinations of their government and hold their leaders accountable.

Conclusion: The Price of Silence

In the final analysis, Greenblatt’s resignation is a sobering reminder of the precarious position in which many journalists find themselves today. The choice between editorial freedom and self-preservation is a false dichotomy that no journalist should have to navigate. The real cost of media self-censorship is not just the loss of individual voices like Greenblatt’s but the gradual erosion of our collective ability to confront power with truth. It’s a price we cannot afford to pay.
