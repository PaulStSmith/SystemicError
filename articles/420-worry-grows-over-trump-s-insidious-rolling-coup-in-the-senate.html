Worry grows over Trump's insidious 'rolling coup' in the Senate

The Coup as Forecast, Not Accident

A Scenario Built Around Power

Jonathan Alter’s piece is a fictionalized projection: Trump, facing possible Democratic gains in 2026, tries to overturn the result, weaponize emergency powers, and drag the country into a constitutional crisis. The value of the exercise is not the plot twist. It is the clarity it gives to the real structure underneath: one man with control of the executive branch, backed by Stephen Miller and a machinery of institutional intimidation.

Who Actually Holds the Levers

The source is blunt about the central fact that matters: this is not a mood, a misunderstanding, or a chaotic drift. The institutional power sits with the president, the federal security apparatus, and the emergency authorities the presidency can claim. If Trump can invoke police powers, designate enemies, and interfere with election administration, then the problem is not “polarization.” It is concentrated executive power being used as a political weapon.

The Decision Is the Crime

Alter’s scenario does something useful by refusing the usual evasions. It does not pretend the crisis would emerge from confusion or legal ambiguity. It names deliberate choices: Trump telegraphing contempt for elections, Trump warning Republicans about impeachment, Trump saying he wished he had seized voting machines in 2020, Trump floating the idea that elections should not even happen. That is not drift. That is advance notice.

Misdirection as a Governing Style

The most familiar fraud in stories like this is the habit of treating the weaker actors as the main characters. Citizens are told to rescue democracy after the fact, as if the burden belongs first to the public rather than the officials who break the system and the institutions that enable them. Even the language of “plausible scenario” can soften the underlying reality: the president is imagined as if he might become dangerous, when the article’s own evidence shows he already is.

Emergency Powers Are the Point

The real warning here is not just election subversion. It is the use of emergency doctrine as a domestic political tool. NPSM-7 and PEADs appear in the piece as instruments that could be repurposed from national catastrophe into internal repression. That is the authoritarian trick: convert extraordinary powers into normal campaign leverage, then call the resistance a crisis. Once the executive gets to define dissent as threat, the election is no longer the final authority. It becomes a target.

The Pattern Beneath the Story

This is a study in how modern authoritarianism works in the United States: premeditation wrapped in procedural language, law turned into a weapon, and institutional cowardice dressed up as constitutional concern. The danger is not that someone like Trump might stumble into overreach. The danger is that the system already contains too many openings for him to exploit. The crisis is built into the design, and the people with the power to stop it are the same ones most likely to normalize it until it is too late.
