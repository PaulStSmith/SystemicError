Trump’s notorious 'infidelity' problems are crashing our cred: expert

Trump Treats Trade Like a Con

A Deal-Maker Who Breaks Deals

The source article gets the central fact right: Donald Trump is not operating as a negotiator in good faith. He is the holder of executive power threatening to blow up an existing trade arrangement with Canada, including goods already protected under USMCA, by dangling 50 percent tariffs. That is not bargaining. It is coercion dressed up as strategy.

Justin Wolfers’ marriage analogy lands because it exposes the core issue Trump’s defenders keep trying to blur: this is not about “toughness.” It is about whether a partner who has already broken the contract can be trusted to keep the next one. Trump has already answered that question.

Power Sits in Washington

Canada is not the actor with leverage here. Trump is. He controls the machinery that can impose tariffs, rattle supply chains, and force companies to treat uncertainty as a permanent business condition. The damage is not abstract. The article points to auto parts manufacturers and General Motors, where the lack of trust makes long-term investment rationally impossible.

That is the real power structure. Trump creates instability, and everybody else has to price that instability into their decisions. The weaker party is told this is diplomacy. It is actually extortion with a flag on it.

Consent Is Not a Side Issue

The article is also right to center Trump’s contempt for consent, even if it treats the point as a colorful aside. The president “wanted Canada to be the 51st state, whether they wanted it or not.” That is not just insulting rhetoric. It reveals the authoritarian impulse underneath the trade theatrics: agreement is secondary to domination.

This is where the framing matters. Trump’s people talk as if the problem is tone, unpredictability, or poor personal chemistry. No. The problem is a political actor who treats consent as optional and obligation as disposable. That is not a broken relationship. It is a power grab in slow motion.

The Shallow Deal Is the Point

Wolfers says the best outcome now is a “shallow, transactional, and not really binding” deal. That is not a bug in Trump’s approach. It is the design. A durable agreement limits arbitrary power; a flimsy one preserves it. If no one can trust the United States to honor commitments, then every negotiation gets pushed down to the level of temporary compliance and private hedging.

That means Trump does not expand American influence by threatening partners. He shrinks it. He turns the U.S. into a country whose word cannot anchor investment, planning, or alliance. The tariff threat is not strength. It is the public monetization of unreliability.

What the Story Really Shows

The larger pattern is simple: authoritarian politics corrodes institutions by making promises unusable. Trade agreements, diplomatic commitments, and business expectations all depend on a baseline belief that the state will honor what it signs. Trump keeps converting that baseline into a joke, and then his allies ask everyone to admire the punchline.

So yes, this is about Canada. More precisely, it is about how one man in control of the presidency can turn a formal commitment into a threat and then call the threat leadership. The article’s metaphor is useful because it exposes the machinery beneath the spectacle: a ruler who cannot be trusted, a system forced to absorb the consequences, and a political culture still pretending this is merely bad manners instead of institutional damage.
