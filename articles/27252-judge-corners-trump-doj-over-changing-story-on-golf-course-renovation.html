Judge corners Trump DOJ over changing story on golf course renovation

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 14, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

An emergency that appeared after the directiveThe president’s order, finally acknowledgedA long-known problem becomes suddenly urgent

The Justice Department’s defense of closing East Potomac Park unraveled in court Friday when its own lawyer acknowledged that the government acted after President Donald Trump directed it to take over the golf course—not because an Oct. 9 National Park Service assessment had declared the entire park an imminent danger. The admission mattered because Justice Department attorneys had tried to justify the shutdown with a far more alarming story: a deteriorating seawall posed such a grave hazard that children could die.

Judge Ana Reyes exposed the contradiction by forcing the government to account for its documents, its chronology and the roughly 30,000 people allowed onto the island for the Marine Corps Marathon shortly before officials characterized the site as an emergency.

An emergency that appeared after the directive

According to Lawfare’s Molly Roberts, who reported the hearing on Bluesky, Justice Department lawyers Stanley Woodward and Adam R.F. Gustafson argued that conditions around the island required its complete closure. Gustafson distinguished the marathon from ordinary park use by saying that “a well-organized race in broad daylight doesn’t present the same risk as children walking in the dark along the deteriorating seawall.”

The courtroom laughed. East Potomac Park closes at night: a gate blocks access to its parking lot at dusk. More importantly, the government still had to explain why risks near portions of the seawall required shutting down the whole island, particularly when earlier statements indicated that hazardous areas would be fenced off.

Reyes repeatedly asked Gustafson to identify where acting National Park Service Director Jessica Bowron’s Oct. 9 declaration said conditions endangered life and limb or warranted closing the entire park. Gustafson read technical descriptions of deteriorating mortar and a sidewalk that floods with the tides twice daily. Those details described maintenance problems. They did not supply the warning the Justice Department had attributed to the declaration.

After trying and failing to locate such language, Gustafson conceded: “I’m sorry, my mistake.” He then said the declaration had been filed “well before the decision to close the park.”

That retreat created another problem.

The president’s order, finally acknowledged

Reyes pointed to Trump’s posts on Oct. 18 and 19 saying he had issued a directive. By Oct. 30, the government said it was carrying that directive out.

“You’re carrying out the president’s directive, right?” she asked.

“And that’s the way government works,” the Justice Department replied.

The statement was less a legal explanation than an admission about the chain of command. Trump made the decision; executive agencies implemented it; Justice Department lawyers supplied the courtroom defense. Reyes noted that she had made essentially that point for hours during an earlier hearing, only to have the government insist she was wrong.

“But I guess I wasn’t,” she said.

The exchange stripped away the administration’s misdirection. The operative source of power was not a newly discovered engineering condition independently forcing park officials’ hands. It was a presidential directive connected to Trump’s plans for the golf course. Safety concerns may be real—the seawall is deteriorating, and the government says it is spending $300 million to repair it—but the existence of a repair problem does not establish that an immediate, island-wide closure was necessary.

A long-known problem becomes suddenly urgent

A second National Park Service declaration, filed Oct. 28, described the seawall as dangerous and falling apart. Reyes asked what had changed since Oct. 9, when the earlier declaration did not characterize the entire park as an urgent threat.

The deterioration was not a surprise. Reyes said the Park Service had known about the issue since 2011 and that assessments had been conducted beginning in 2019. Yet officials permitted the Marine Corps Marathon to bring more than 30,000 people along Ohio Drive near the end of October without previously announcing that the seawall posed an emergency requiring the island’s closure.

“Suddenly, on Oct. 28, days after 30k+ people ran around Ohio Drive, it’s an emergent situation,” Reyes observed.

The Justice Department invoked limited resources and agency discretion. That explanation did not persuade the judge, who contrasted it with the government’s $300 million seawall repair project. Nor did it resolve the shifting chronology: an Oct. 9 filing without the claimed emergency, Trump’s Oct. 18 and 19 statements about his directive, and an Oct. 28 declaration presenting the condition as newly urgent.

The seawall can be hazardous and the government’s account can still be evasive. Reyes was not required to deny the physical deterioration to examine whether officials elevated it into a rationale for a closure ordered for another reason.

Her closing quip captured the credibility problem the Justice Department created for itself: “I’m just trying to figure out which iteration of me not paying attention to the president I’m supposed to go with today.”

The administration’s lawyers ultimately confirmed the fact they had resisted: the president’s directive drove government action. Their safety argument did not erase that decision. It merely obscured who made it.
