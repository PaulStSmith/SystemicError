The Mar-a-lago pitch that pushed Trump too far

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosOctober 10, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower was the productThe scandal Trump noticedAppointment without accountabilityA government of private channels

What happened

AlterNet reports that Donald Trump grew frustrated with Kimberly Guilfoyle after she solicited investments for her businesses from Mar-a-Lago members, including through a 2022 dinner that guests reportedly discovered midway was a pitch for her American Dream alcohol brand. According to The Wall Street Journal reporting cited by AlterNet, Trump complained that Guilfoyle was supposed to raise money for him, not herself. The story does not establish that this episode produced any formal punishment.

The more consequential allegation concerns Trump donor Eric Deters. After the 2024 election, Deters reportedly offered Guilfoyle as much as $1 million if she secured access to incoming IRS Commissioner Billy Long regarding an IRS examination of Deters’s tax deductions. Messages reviewed by the Journal reportedly show Guilfoyle contacting Long about Deters and emphasizing his closeness to her and Donald Trump Jr.

Days before her July 9, 2025 confirmation hearing to become ambassador to Greece, Guilfoyle allegedly asked Deters to pay a $100,000 American Express bill in a manner she said would not “show up anywhere.” Deters did not pay it. The Senate confirmed Guilfoyle on September 18, 2025, and senior congressional Democrats have since called for an investigation.

Power was the product

Guilfoyle’s apparent asset was neither diplomatic expertise nor tax-law authority. It was proximity: to Trump’s family, his club, his donors and his appointees. The reported offer to help Deters did not depend on changing tax policy through legislation or contesting an IRS decision through ordinary channels. It depended on getting special attention from the agency’s incoming chief.

Deters could offer money. Guilfoyle could advertise access. Long held the governmental authority. Trump controlled the political network connecting them. That is the relevant power structure—not a jumble of colorful personalities, but a chain linking private wealth to public office.

The scandal Trump noticed

Trump’s reported objection to Guilfoyle’s Mar-a-Lago solicitations was not that political access had become commercialized. AlterNet says he objected because she was raising money for herself at his club when she was supposed to be raising it for him.

That distinction is damning. The alleged violation was competitive, not ethical: Guilfoyle appeared to be monetizing a network whose central beneficiary expected to be Trump. The account portrays patronage as an accepted operating system, with conflict arising when someone lower in the hierarchy tries to capture too much of the revenue.

Appointment without accountability

Trump nominated Guilfoyle as ambassador to Greece on December 10, 2024, after her engagement to Donald Trump Jr. ended. The Senate later confirmed her despite the office’s obvious public purpose: representing the United States, not rehabilitating a political insider’s finances or extending a family influence business overseas.

The reported request for a payment that would leave no paper trail is especially serious, but it remains an allegation based on messages described by the Journal. What is established in AlterNet’s account is that Deters did not make the payment and that Guilfoyle nevertheless received Senate confirmation. Institutional gatekeepers did not prevent the appointment.

A government of private channels

The article’s framing occasionally turns systemic conduct into Trump-world spectacle: Mar-a-Lago intrigue, family relationships and extravagant business ventures. That risks misdirecting attention toward Guilfoyle’s personal opportunism while understating the institutions that made it useful.

The larger pattern is government administered through privileged relationships. Donors seek access unavailable to ordinary taxpayers. Political intimates sell their ability to attract an official’s attention. Public appointments reward membership in the network. Congressional scrutiny arrives after the Senate has already transferred formal power to the beneficiary.

This is not mere dysfunction or social tackiness. It is the conversion of public authority into a private market. The central political fact is not that one insider allegedly asked for money. It is that proximity to the president’s circle could plausibly be presented as something worth buying.
