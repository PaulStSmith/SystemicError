Invisible coup: Shadow-docket decision just handed Trump Congress' power

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

The Ballroom Was Never the Point

Power, Not Procedure

Leah Litman’s critique gets to the core of the matter: the fight over Trump’s White House ballroom is not really about a construction project. It is about who gets to spend public money, who gets to authorize public work, and whether the Supreme Court will keep laundering executive overreach as legal confusion.

The source article treats the ballroom dispute as a dispute over judicial blocking. That framing is too small. The real offense is the president allegedly moving ahead with a taxpayer-funded construction project without Congress’s approval, then trying to retrofit legitimacy after the fact when the donations he promised did not materialize.

Congress Got Written Out

The Constitution is not subtle here. Congress holds the power of the purse. That means the president does not get to spend on federal property, including the White House, because he feels like it or because he announced a private fundraising story that later collapsed.

What Litman is describing is not a technical slip. It is an institutional theft of authority. Trump’s executive branch initiated the work; Congress never authorized it. The Court’s use of the phrase “the federal government” to describe what the president started is not neutral language. It is an act of erasure. It collapses Congress into the presidency and pretends the legislature is just decorative furniture.

Standing as a Convenient Fog

The Court then hides behind standing, as if the problem were that the wrong people complained too early. That move is familiar because it works: procedural haze is one of the easiest ways to shield raw power.

The National Trust for Historic Preservation raised the alarm over demolition, construction, federal-law violations, and the lack of congressional approval. The Court answered by narrowing who counts as injured enough to object. That is not a serious defense of legality. It is a method for making illegal conduct harder to challenge while the damage advances.

Executive Power With Judicial Cover

Litman’s bigger point is harsher and more accurate: this is the unitary-executive theory in practice, now swollen beyond even its own logic. The theory already claims that all executive power resides in the president. The Court has repeatedly indulged that claim. But the pattern does not stop there. It keeps growing until the president is treated as if he can also absorb legislative power when it suits him.

That is the real constitutional disease here. If the president can spend money Congress did not authorize, or refuse to spend money Congress did authorize, then Congress is no longer governing. It is merely being consulted after the fact, if at all.

Irreparable Harm, For Whom?

The “irreparable harm” argument is the kind of legal theater that only makes sense if you already accept the premise that executive wishes outrank law. Stopping potentially illegal conduct is not harm to the president. It is the ordinary function of courts when government crosses a line.

The Court’s willingness to treat restraint on executive illegality as injury to the executive tells you what is being protected. Not the Constitution. Not congressional power. The president.

The Real Pattern

The ballroom case is not an isolated vanity project. It is part of a larger judicial program: weaken enforceable limits, narrow who may challenge abuse, and steadily convert the presidency into an office with legislative reach and minimal accountability. That is why the source article’s procedural focus is misleading. The issue is not whether the courts temporarily slowed construction. The issue is that the high court keeps helping a president turn public power into personal prerogative.

When the Court calls that “the federal government,” it is not making a mistake. It is participating in the rewrite.
