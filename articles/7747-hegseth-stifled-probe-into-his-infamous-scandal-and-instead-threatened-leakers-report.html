Hegseth stifled probe into his infamous scandal — and instead threatened leakers: report

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 12, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Hegseth Fiasco: A Study in Ego and Endangerment

Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth reacts during a cabinet meeting with U.S. President Donald Trump at the White House in Washington, D.C. on Dec. 2, 2025. REUTERS/Brian Snyder

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth reportedly stifled a routine investigation of his disclosure of sensitive military plans in a group chat on the non-secure Signal app with a reporter present, and he instead threatened staffers he believed had leaked damaging information.

Typically a classification review would be conducted to assess damage from an unauthorized disclosure of sensitive defense information, but Hegseth didn’t order an assessment, according to two former senior officials who worked within the Pentagon at the time and a current U.S. official with knowledge of the situation who spoke to CNN.

“After the Signal messages came to light, Hegseth, instead turned his attention inward and focused on formally investigating suspected leakers on his own staff, even threatening to polygraph defense officials he believed were disclosing potentially embarrassing details about him, multiple current and former officials said,” CNN reported. “Those threats had a ‘chilling effect’ among DoD officials who became increasingly wary of doing anything that could be perceived by Hegseth as an attempt to undercut him, according to those officials.”

These new revelations came days after an internal watchdog report showed Hegseth potentially endangered U.S. troops by sharing highly sensitive attack plans against Houthi rebels in Yemen, and the sources told CNN they would have expected a damage assessment to also be conducted because the inspector general’s probe had a limited scope.

“One hundreds percent a breach like that would warrant a top-to bottom damage assessment by both DoD and the IC,” said a former senior defense official.

The Pentagon has no plans at this point to investigate the matter further, even though the inspector general concluded Hegseth violated Department of Defense regulations and risked troops’ safety by compromising sensitive military plans, and the defense secretary himself insists those findings represent a “total exoneration” of himself.

“A damage assessment is forward-looking and focused on risk rather than personal culpability,” Brianna Rosen, a former White House official specializing in national security and tech policy. “Even without the official’s cooperation, analysts can evaluate what material was shared, who had access to it, and any potential operational or counterintelligence implications. In that sense, a damage assessment would not have been constrained by the same limitations that hampered the IG investigation.”

Our Analysis:

The Hegseth Fiasco: A Study in Ego and Endangerment

In a tale that reads more like a political thriller than a report on the operational conduct within the Pentagon, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth has reportedly turned the Department of Defense (DoD) into the backdrop for a saga of secrecy, retaliation, and dubious claims of exoneration. According to a CNN report, Hegseth’s handling—or mishandling—of a routine investigation into his own reckless behavior has laid bare the underbelly of power dynamics within the Pentagon.

First, let’s address the elephant in the room: Hegseth’s decision to share sensitive military plans in a group chat on the non-secure Signal app, with a reporter no less, is not just a breach of protocol; it’s a glaring testament to a catastrophic lapse in judgment. The irony of utilizing a platform known for its end-to-end encryption to commit what is essentially an act of gross negligence would be laughable if the stakes weren’t so devastatingly high.

Adding insult to injury, rather than initiating a classification review to assess the damage caused by his unauthorized disclosure—a standard procedure in such scenarios—Hegseth chose to embark on a witch hunt within his own department. His focus, as reported, was not on rectifying the potential harm he had inflicted on national security or the safety of U.S. troops, but on rooting out those he suspected of exposing his folly. The mere thought of subjecting defense officials to polygraph tests over leaks, while the elephantine issue of compromised military plans looms large, is absurdity at its peak.

The chilling effect of Hegseth’s threats among DoD officials cannot be overstated. In an environment where the paramount concern should be national security, creating a culture of fear and suspicion only serves to undermine the very foundation of effective defense operations. It’s a classic case of misdirected priorities, with devastating potential repercussions.

What’s more astonishing is the Pentagon’s apparent decision to not investigate the matter further, despite the inspector general’s conclusion that Hegseth’s actions violated DoD regulations and endangered U.S. troops. Hegseth’s interpretation of these findings as a “total exoneration” is not just a gross misreading of the situation; it’s a dangerous precedent for accountability—or the lack thereof—at the highest levels of defense administration.

Brianna Rosen’s insight into the nature of a damage assessment highlights a critical point: such assessments are designed to mitigate risk and safeguard against future breaches, not to allocate blame. Hegseth’s refusal to order an assessment, coupled with his retaliatory focus on leaks, suggests a profound misunderstanding of the purpose and importance of these procedures.

In sum, the Hegseth debacle is not merely a story of leaked information and the failure to follow protocol. It is a disconcerting narrative of how ego and the fear of personal embarrassment can seemingly override an official’s duty to national security and troop safety. The Pentagon, and indeed any institution tasked with the defense of a nation, should operate on principles of integrity, transparency, and accountability. When those principles are compromised, it is not just the reputation of an individual at stake, but the security of the nation itself.
