Red state notches dubious record as governor pushes for families to 'see justice'

Florida Turned Execution Into Campaign Theater

The Decision Was the Point

Florida did not stumble into this record. Ron DeSantis pushed it. The governor made the state’s death chamber the busiest in the country and timed that escalation to his final term, while insisting that too many families had waited too long to “see justice.” On Tuesday, Florida executed two men in one day, including 80-year-old Dominick Anthony Occhicone and former police officer James Aren Duckett.

That is not administrative drift. It is a deliberate use of state power to produce a political message: toughness, retribution, and control. DeSantis holds the institutional authority. He chose how it would be used.

“Justice” as Political Cover

The language is familiar because it is designed to blur responsibility. “See justice” sounds solemn and procedural. It is neither. It is a moral varnish for the state’s decision to kill, presented as if the machinery of execution were a neutral response to grief.

That framing shifts attention away from the only actor who matters here: the governor who accelerates executions and then wraps the result in the language of closure. The article’s wording helps expose the mechanism. The state is not simply carrying out law. It is staging punishment as legitimacy.

The Record Florida Broke

The Guardian report says Florida became the first state in a decade to execute more than one person on a single day, and that since 2025 began the state has carried out nearly half of all executions nationwide. That matters because it shows what the decline in the death penalty has been obscuring: when the norm weakens, an aggressive executive can revive it by sheer will.

This is not a national consensus. It is a state-level cruelty project. Florida is dragging a declining practice back into visibility and treating the resulting bloodshed as proof of seriousness.

Who the Story Lets Off Easy

The weak framing here is the suggestion that this is about families waiting and justice finally arriving. That language misdirects blame toward an abstract “process” and away from the people making the decisions. There is no confusion in the chain of command. The governor wants the executions. The state carries them out. The publicity is part of the point.

The fact that one of the men executed was 80 years old should also puncture any fantasy that this is about public safety. This is not protection. It is state-sanctioned killing of people already locked inside the system for decades.

The Pattern Behind the Chamber

Florida is not alone in making cruelty feel procedural, but it is unusually blunt about it. The death penalty survives politically not because it works, but because some leaders still find value in performing domination. It is a tool for turning punishment into a loyalty signal for a right-wing audience that mistakes vengeance for order.

That is the larger pattern: when democratic politics is weakened, executives reach for spectacles of force. The state stops pretending to be restrained and starts advertising its capacity for irreversible harm. The executions in Florida are not a side story. They are what authoritarian politics looks like when it puts on a suit and calls itself justice.
