Latest Trump hire failed a 'make or break' test almost immediately: report

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 14, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet, citing MS NOW, reports that newly appointed White House press secretary Katie Zacharia struggled during her first briefing on Tuesday. In a 14-minute appearance, she reportedly relied heavily on a binder and answered only three questions, all from conservative-leaning outlets. Anonymous White House officials described the performance as poor and questioned whether she could keep the job.

White House Communications Director Steven Cheung publicly called the briefing “amazing and effective” and dismissed contrary accounts as uninformed or fabricated. One America News Network host David Pollack likewise labeled the criticism “fake news.” The report does not establish that Zacharia will be fired; it establishes that unnamed officials and a former Trump aide believed her position could quickly become precarious.

The power is above the podium

Zacharia occupied the lectern, but she did not hold the decisive power. The president chose the administration’s spokesperson and could remove her. Senior White House officials controlled the internal verdict, while Cheung controlled the authorized public version.

That distinction matters because the article’s personnel-drama framing risks shrinking an institutional story into a performance review. The consequential question is not whether Zacharia impressed colleagues. It is what the White House expects its press secretary to do: distribute information, dominate reporters, or perform loyalty under pressure.

Failure, as defined by Trump’s operation

The criticism reported by MS NOW was revealingly narrow. Zacharia allegedly looked unprepared, appeared weak and failed to project command. The former aide did not warn that she had misled the public or withheld material information. The stated danger was that she did not look “tough and strong.”

That is a political standard, not a public-service standard. The administration insiders quoted in the report treated press relations as a test of dominance. Even the favorable comparison with predecessor Karoline Leavitt emphasized combative engagement, suggesting that access across the press corps mattered partly as another arena in which to display control.

Friendly questions are still a choice

Taking only three questions, all from conservative-leaning outlets, was not merely an aesthetic problem. If accurately described, it restricted scrutiny while preserving the appearance of a briefing. Zacharia had agency over that conduct, although the reporting does not establish whether she selected those outlets independently or followed instructions.

The article concentrates on her nervous presentation because that is easy to dramatize. The more important institutional fact is that the White House press operation reportedly limited questioning to ideologically friendly media. A shaky delivery may embarrass an administration. Managed access protects it.

The denial machine

Cheung’s response did more than defend a colleague. He declared the performance successful, then suggested that critics were either irrelevant employees or inventions. Pollack supplied the movement-media version by invoking “fake news” and “Trump Derangement Syndrome.”

The reporting supports calling this message discipline, not proving a coordinated deception. But its function is plain: it offers supporters a reason to reject an unfavorable account without examining the conduct described. The dispute becomes a referendum on the critic’s legitimacy rather than a factual question about whom Zacharia called on and how she handled the room.

Disposable messenger, durable system

If Zacharia is replaced, the White House will have changed a performer without changing the power structure. The president remains the principal. Senior officials still define competence as effective advocacy. Friendly media still help convert criticism into evidence of persecution. Anonymous insiders can attack an appointee while avoiding responsibility for the operation that hired and prepared her.

That is the larger pattern: personalized government makes subordinates disposable while insulating the institution’s choices from scrutiny. The press secretary absorbs the ridicule; the administration retains the machinery of selective access, loyalty enforcement and reflexive denial.
