Author Michael Wolff, who has written several books about the inner workings of the White House under Trump, claimed the president has brought his executive powers to bear on the Ivy League school because he was not accepted there as a student decades ago. Trump angrily denied his account.

"Michael Wolff, a Third Rate Reporter, who is laughed at even by the scoundrels of the Fake News, recently stated that the only reason I’m 'beating up' on Harvard, is because I applied there, and didn’t get in," Trump posted Monday evening on Truth Social. "That story is totally FALSE, I never applied to Harvard. I graduated from the Wharton School of Finance at the University of Pennsylvania."

"He is upset because his book about me was a total 'BOMB,'" Trump added. "Nobody wanted it, because his 'reporting' and reputation is so bad!"

Trump attacked Wolff back in February, when his latest book, "All or Nothing: How Trump Recaptured America," was released, calling it a “total FAKE JOB, just like the other JUNK he wrote," and said he had repeatedly rebuffed his invitations for an interview.

Wolff said last week that there's a running joke going around the White House that the president has targeted the Ivy League school because his son Barron didn't get in, which Melania Trump publicly denied, but the author said that it was Trump himself who was shut out as an applicant.

“It’s important not to lend too much calculation and planning to anything he does,” Wolff told The Daily Beast Podcast. “But the other thing is that, by the way, he didn’t get into Harvard. So one of the Trump things is always holding a grudge against the Ivy Leagues.”

Our Analysis:

The Trump-Harvard Feud: A Deep Dive into the Ego Clash

In what reads like a script out of a political satire, we are yet again subjected to the theatrics of Donald Trump, a figure whose petulance seemingly knows no bounds. The latest chapter in this ongoing drama involves Trump's alleged vendetta against Harvard University, a narrative brought to light by author Michael Wolff. Wolff, a frequent thorn in Trump's side, claims that the former president's animosity towards the Ivy League institution stems from a personal grudge: a rejected application from his youth.

The Allegation

Wolff's assertion that Trump's executive wrath is a result of his failure to secure admission to Harvard decades ago is not just amusing—it's indicative of a deeper, more troubling penchant for pettiness among the political elite. Trump's response, predictably, was swift and severe, relying on his favorite platform, Truth Social, to denounce Wolff as a "Third Rate Reporter" and his story as "totally FALSE."

Trump's Counterclaim

Trump's defense—that he never applied to Harvard because he was a proud graduate of the Wharton School of Finance at the University of Pennsylvania—is an interesting diversion. It's as if the mere notion of rejection from Harvard is an affront to his self-crafted image of success and infallibility. However, what truly stands out is his attack on Wolff's credibility and the commercial success of his books, a classic Trump move to discredit his critics by targeting their professional standing and public image.

The Underlying Issue

At the heart of this saga is not whether Trump applied to Harvard or not; it's about the destructive nature of wielding executive power to settle personal scores. The idea that the leader of the free world would use his position to retaliate against an educational institution—regardless of the reason—is disturbingly authoritarian. It underscores a fundamental disregard for the separation of powers and the principles of democracy.

Wolff's Perspective

Wolff, for his part, suggests that attributing too much strategic planning to Trump's actions might be overthinking it. Instead, he points to a simpler, more visceral motivation: grudge-holding. This insight is particularly revelatory, as it touches on the recurrent theme of Trump's tenure in the White House: the personalization of professional and national issues.

The Bigger Picture

Beyond the immediate comedy of errors this story presents, there lies a grave implication for American politics. When personal vendettas can potentially shape policy decisions and executive actions, the entire governance structure is at risk. It's a stark reminder of the necessity for checks and balances, and for leaders who can rise above personal grievances for the greater good.

Conclusion

This latest episode in the Trump saga is more than a footnote in the annals of political oddities; it's a cautionary tale about the perils of personality-driven politics. As entertaining as it might be to witness these squabbles play out in public, the consequences of such pettiness can be far-reaching and damaging to the fabric of democracy. It's high time for a return to principles over personalities, facts over fiction, and statesmanship over spite.
