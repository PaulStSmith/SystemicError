Trump plays 'game-show' tricks with donors as insiders raise doubts: report

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Paulo SantosAugust 15, 20263 min read

Trump Is Not Choosing a Successor. He Is Running a Court.

Trump’s latest chatter about making JD Vance his heir apparent is being sold as succession intrigue, but the reporting points to something uglier: a sitting president using donor access, public ambiguity, and factional jealousy to keep everyone orbiting him. The Washington Post says Trump has told donors to “elect JD,” while advisers and allies still doubt anything is settled, and Trump keeps reopening the contest with Marco Rubio. That is the story. The rest is noise.

The Real Power Is Still Trump

Trump holds the institutional leverage here. He is the president, the party’s dominant figure, and the only person whose blessing appears to matter. He can float a successor, withhold endorsement, and keep top officials and donors guessing. That uncertainty is not incidental. It is the mechanism.

The article treats this like a personality-driven circus, but the deeper reality is that Trump is managing a loyalty structure, not a normal political transition. He is not transferring power. He is demonstrating that power still flows through him.

This Is Not Succession. It Is an Audition.

Vance and Rubio are being used as props in a contest Trump controls. Vance has spent his vice presidency building relationships with the MAGA base and clearly wants to look like the inheritor. Rubio has establishment donor support, but he is legally boxed out while serving as secretary of state and national security adviser, which makes him useful as a placeholder, not a serious immediate rival.

Trump benefits from that asymmetry. Vance can be dangled as the favored heir. Rubio can be kept as a respectable foil. Donors can pick sides. Online provocateurs can harass whoever becomes inconvenient. The point is not clarity. The point is discipline through confusion.

The Article Soft-Pedals the Mechanism

The framing in the source leans into “game-show-style drama,” which is accurate as far as it goes, but still too soft. This is not harmless theater. It is deliberate political management through spectacle.

Trump has reportedly told different audiences different things, probed advisers about the matchup, and even floated a third term, “mostly in jest.” That kind of joking is part of the method. Authoritarian politics often arrives wearing a smirk. The joke makes the boundary blurrier. The blur makes the norm weaker. Then the next outrageous idea feels like just another riff.

That is how power gets normalized: not by announcing a break with democracy, but by turning the break into content.

The Party Has Become a Personal Property

What stands out most is how little independent institutional force exists around Trump. The source describes advisers, donors, activists, and online influencers all reacting to his latest signals. That is a court, not a party in any serious democratic sense.

Even the supposed rivals are constrained by Trump’s preferences. Vance is waiting to see what Trump does after the midterms. Rubio is pulling back from visibility to lower the temperature. Everyone is adjusting to the preferences of one man. That is not evidence of a healthy coalition. It is evidence of political dependency.

The Democratic side of the system, for all its own failures and cowardice, is still the necessary institutional barrier to this kind of personalized rule. The Republican side has been more willing to remake itself around one figure’s whims, resentments, and need for constant attention.

The Pattern Beneath the Spectacle

The larger pattern is simple: Trump keeps power by making everyone else compete for proximity to it. That is why the succession talk matters. It is not about who comes next. It is about how a movement trains people to accept that legitimacy comes from one man’s signal.

That is a corrosive model for any democracy. It degrades public life into backstage intrigue, rewards obedience over principle, and turns the future of a major party into a contest over who can best perform deference to an aging strongman. The reporting captures the pageant. The deeper story is the architecture of submission underneath it.
