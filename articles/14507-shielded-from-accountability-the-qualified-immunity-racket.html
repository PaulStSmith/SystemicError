Shielded from Accountability: The Qualified Immunity Racket

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 23, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

In a tale of two governments, the contrast couldn’t be more jarring—or more revealing. In Ohio, a bold grassroots movement is surging with momentum, capturing headlines and stirring public debate. A proposed constitutional amendment to abolish qualified immunity is not only heading for the ballot but has withstood legal challenges all the way to the U.S. Supreme Court. Their refusal to block the initiative is not just a procedural shrug; it’s a tectonic shift, a subtle signal that maybe, just maybe, this country is ready to have a serious reckoning with a judicial doctrine that has for too long operated as a license to harm with impunity. It’s a rare, electrifying moment when democracy dares to confront its own grotesque distortions head-on.

Now contrast that with the ongoing farce in Washington. In the marble mausoleum of Congress, where accountability goes to die, a cadre of legislators has introduced the “Qualified Immunity Act of 2025”—a bill so brazen in its intent, it might as well have been ghostwritten by the worst precincts of authoritarianism. It seeks not only to uphold qualified immunity but to canonize it in federal law. A perverse achievement, considering this legal doctrine has never been democratically enacted to begin with. It was a judicial fiction, now being inked into potential legislative reality. This is not an effort to address police abuse. It is an effort to immunize it. To sterilize it of consequence. To embolden the worst by protecting them from the very concept of justice.

So what is qualified immunity? It’s the Frankenstein’s monster of American jurisprudence—stitched together by the Supreme Court in 1967’s Pierson v. Ray, where the Court carved out a protection for officers acting in “good faith.” Originally pitched as a modest defense against frivolous lawsuits, it was never debated in Congress, never subjected to public scrutiny, and never ratified by any democratic means. Yet over the decades, through rulings like Harlow v. Fitzgerald (1982) and Anderson v. Creighton (1987), it has metastasized into a legal force field, one that shields government officials—not just police, but any public servant—from being held liable, even for the most flagrant violations of constitutional rights.

This isn’t theoretical. The legal standard at the heart of qualified immunity is the so-called “clearly established law” requirement. Translation? You can only sue a public official if there exists a previous court decision that ruled the exact same behavior under almost identical circumstances was unconstitutional. If no such precedent exists—if your abuse is novel in its depravity—then congratulations, you’ve stumbled into a legal black hole where your rights don’t apply. Imagine a police officer stealing $225,000 in cash during a raid and facing no liability because, as one court held, there was no previous ruling that specifically prohibited officers from doing exactly that. That’s not a system of justice. That’s a system of sanctioned theft.

Let’s say you’re thrown to the ground, your face bashed into the pavement by an officer, and you suffer permanent injury. You sue. But the court asks: has any other court ruled that an officer bashing someone’s face into pavement, in this specific posture, with this amount of force, in these conditions, is unconstitutional? If the answer is no—if your exact nightmare hasn’t already been litigated—then sorry, no justice for you. This doctrine doesn’t just distort the law; it calcifies injustice into precedent.

That’s why what Ohio is doing matters. This isn’t just another state-level skirmish over public policy. It’s a revolutionary gambit to gut a federally sanctioned atrocity. The proposed amendment would obliterate qualified immunity at the state level, creating a legal mechanism by which state and local officials could be held accountable for civil rights violations. It doesn’t rely on federal courts. It doesn’t wait for precedent. It gives ordinary citizens the power to seek damages against officials who trample their rights. It’s the embodiment of federalism at its best—local governance pushing back against a failed national consensus.

And that is what makes the federal legislation so heinous. The “Qualified Immunity Act of 2025” is not a response to abuse; it’s a prophylactic against accountability. It’s a bureaucratic sandbag against the rising flood of public outrage. It would override state reforms like Ohio’s. It would ensure that the rotting edifice of unaccountable power remains untouched and untouchable. It is cowardice masquerading as lawmaking.

We must be clear: Qualified immunity doesn’t protect good policing. It protects bad policing from the consequences of its own malice. It doesn’t encourage professional behavior. It encourages recklessness and contempt. It doesn’t preserve institutional trust. It erodes it from the inside out. It undermines constitutional rights by creating a parallel universe where those rights depend not on their substance, but on whether someone else got abused the same way first.

This is why public servants—especially those who carry a badge and a gun—must be held to a higher standard, not a lower one. Doctors are held to strict standards because they hold lives in their hands. Pilots are held to rigorous protocols because a single mistake could cost hundreds of lives. Why, then, do we allow police officers—granted the extraordinary authority to detain, wound, or kill—to operate with less accountability than a surgeon or a commercial airline pilot? The burden of power must come with proportionate responsibility. Anything less is a betrayal of the public trust.

It is not a check against frivolous lawsuits. It is a stranglehold on legitimate claims. It is not a necessary protection for public servants. It is an invitation to abuse. It tells victims: you do not matter. Your suffering does not matter. What matters is protecting the system from scrutiny. From consequence. From change.

If public servants want the respect of the communities they serve, they must be accountable to those communities. Not insulated by a legal doctrine that makes them unassailable. Accountability is not a threat to democracy—it is the oxygen that keeps it breathing. Until we abolish qualified immunity, our justice system remains not just flawed, but fundamentally dishonest. And no republic built on dishonesty can endure.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
