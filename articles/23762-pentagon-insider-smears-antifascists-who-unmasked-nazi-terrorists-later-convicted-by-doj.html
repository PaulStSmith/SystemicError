Pentagon insider smears antifascists who unmasked Nazi terrorists later convicted by DOJ

Based on an article by Jordan Green, Investigative Reporter

Paulo SantosSeptember 20, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The State Is Protecting Fascists From the People Who Expose Them

Sanction the Watchdogs

The Trump State Department designated Italian technology collective Autistici/Inventati a terrorist entity, prompting the shutdown of its NoBlogs hosting service. Days later, Pentagon special government employee Jennica Pounds indexed thousands of sites formerly hosted there and marketed the database as an exposure of a covert “antifa” network.

That sequence matters. The institution with sanctioning power disabled infrastructure used by antifascist researchers. A person employed by the Pentagon then organized their work into a searchable target map and told the federal government that some researchers had been identified. This is not merely another online feud over “doxxing.” It is state power operating in the same direction as a campaign against people who investigate violent fascists.

The Evidence Breaks the Frame

Pounds describes NoBlogs as a global platform recklessly publishing personal information. The reporting provides evidence of genuine privacy concerns, including her claim that some posts identified relatives and a minor. Raw Story could not independently verify her totals, and those concerns do not explain the indiscriminate political framing of the entire network.

The sites swept into Pounds’ database helped identify people later convicted of terrorism-related offenses, attempted destruction of energy infrastructure, conspiracy to rob banks, and threatening a prosecutor with lynching. Their research exposed Terrorgram leader Dallas Humber before she received a 30-year federal sentence. It identified Skyler Philippi months before his arrest while preparing an explosive attack on an electrical substation. It also surfaced posts by David Bloyed that police used to identify him before his conviction for threatening Nashville District Attorney Glenn Funk.

Those facts do not prove that every antifascist publication practice was justified. They do establish that the material cannot honestly be reduced to an “organized hit list.” Some of it functioned as an early-warning system that law enforcement either used or later corroborated.

“Doxxing” as Political Camouflage

The article’s central misdirection is embedded in the category itself. Publishing a child’s home address and documenting a neo-Nazi organizer’s public threats are both placed under the same label, stripping away purpose, context, and consequence. Once everything becomes “doxxing,” the person exposing a prospective terrorist can be rhetorically exchanged with the terrorist compiling a kill list.

That false equivalence protects the stronger side. Neo-Nazi and accelerationist networks described in the reporting promoted mass violence, threatened officials, targeted Jewish and LGBTQ people, and planned attacks on public infrastructure. Antifascist researchers documented those networks, sometimes using identifying information. Treating these activities as morally interchangeable converts scrutiny of organized fascism into persecution of fascists—and converts the researchers into the primary menace.

Pounds’ own selectivity makes the politics clearer. She calls conservative anti-antifa writer Andy Ngo a friend, says she redacted information about the white nationalist Patriot Front, and dismisses documented successes against violent extremists as insufficient to justify the broader platform. Her standard is therefore not simply opposition to public identification. It depends heavily on who is being identified and by whom.

Government Power Has a Direction

The actors with decisive institutional power are not anonymous bloggers. They are the State Department officials who imposed the terrorist designation, the government capable of making a hosting service untenable, and Pounds, whose Pentagon role gives additional weight to a project she describes as private and unpaid.

Her insistence that she acted “on my own time” does not dissolve that relationship. Nor does claiming she needed no government authorization answer the more serious disclosure: she sent identities directly to the U.S. government. The unanswered questions are concrete. Which agencies received them? Under what authority? For what investigative purpose? What safeguards, if any, protected researchers whose work had already helped expose people later prosecuted for severe crimes?

The FBI’s refusal to say whether it relied on antifascist research leaves one portion of that relationship unresolved. But the source supplies at least one clear example of federal investigators citing research from a NoBlogs-hosted antifascist group in a criminal case. The state may benefit from this work when it produces evidence, then tolerate or participate in destroying the infrastructure that made the work possible.

Repression Through Reclassification

This is how political repression can proceed without an explicit order to silence particular journalists or researchers. First, classify the infrastructure as connected to terrorism. Then collapse researchers, activists, publishers, and alleged abusers into one contaminated category. Finally, present the resulting shutdown and exposure campaign as neutral protection against privacy violations.

The technique does not require proving that every targeted site committed misconduct. It relies on collective suspicion. Thousands of pages become evidence of a hidden network; antifascist investigation becomes “militant far-left” activity; identifying violent organizers becomes indistinguishable from facilitating violence. The weakness of the article’s conventional “doxxing” frame is that it risks accepting this classification before examining who gains power from it.

Here, the beneficiaries include fascist organizers whose names, affiliations, vehicles, propaganda channels, and threats become harder to track. The immediate consequence is not hypothetical: hosting disappeared, researchers were exposed to government scrutiny, and archives had to be rescued by volunteers.

The State Chooses What Counts as Dangerous

The larger pattern is institutional inversion. Fascist networks openly circulate kill lists, praise mass killers, threaten lynching, and prepare attacks. Researchers expose them. The government eventually prosecutes some of the violent actors—but it also uses terrorism powers against infrastructure serving the researchers and receives their identities from a Pentagon employee.

That is not confusion about extremism. It is a political allocation of protection and vulnerability. The people documenting organized fascism are made more visible, less secure, and easier to investigate. The movements they document gain friction against scrutiny.

A government reveals its priorities not only through whom it prosecutes, but through whose capacity it destroys. In this case, state power did not merely pursue violent extremists. It helped dismantle part of the ecosystem that had been finding them first.
