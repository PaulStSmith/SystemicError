Trump scheme to militarize arch just backfired big time: report

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

An Arsenal With a Gift Shop Is Not a Military Strategy

The Arch and the Alibi

Reporting cited by The Atlantic describes a proposed “Triumphal Arch” at Memorial Circle that would rise roughly 25 stories and include an observation deck, café, and gift shop. The Trump administration has also characterized the structure as a military facility capable of housing snipers, drones, and ammunition. Meanwhile, the vice chair of the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation ended the Section 106 review before consultations were complete, leaving the public three and a half business days to comment before the reported deadline of September 29.

Power Ended the Review

The public did not truncate the historic-preservation process. An official of the Advisory Council did. The National Park Service, identified as the project’s lead agency, has also argued against a veterans’ lawsuit that congressional approval is unnecessary because a 1925 law authorized two columns near that end of Memorial Bridge.

That is the institutional machinery behind the monument: executive agencies advancing an expansive interpretation of old authority while a federal review designed to solicit public input is cut short. The administration’s haste matters because speed can create its own political advantage. Once contracts are issued and construction begins, stopping a project becomes costlier even if courts, Congress, or a later administration objects.

“Military” Does Not Make Scrutiny Disappear

Calling the arch a military facility may be intended to strengthen the administration’s authority to build it. On the evidence presented, however, the label creates questions that ceremonial architecture does not answer.

Patrick Suermann, a Texas A&M construction-science professor and retired Air Force officer, told The Atlantic that ammunition storage ordinarily requires protected “quantity-distance” zones. If those requirements apply here, an arsenal could be incompatible with the public access promised by the observation deck, café, and gift shop. It could also affect Memorial Bridge, a major route between Washington and Virginia.

The reporting does not establish which military safety rules would ultimately govern the project. It does establish the contradiction officials would have to resolve: a structure cannot be sold as an accessible tourist destination while its claimed military functions potentially require keeping tourists away.

Arlington Becomes the Backdrop

Veterans opposing the project argue that its scale and observation deck would intrude upon Arlington National Cemetery, including by allowing visitors to overlook funerals. They also object that the monument would redirect a historic sightline now centered on Lincoln, Memorial Bridge, and Arlington toward a structure associated with Trump.

That objection identifies the political substance more clearly than the administration’s shifting labels do. Memorial Circle is not empty space awaiting a presidential signature. Its setting already carries a national meaning rooted in the Civil War, military burial, and reunion. A monumental arch there would not merely join that landscape. At the reported scale, it would compete with it.

The power at issue is therefore not simply the power to construct. It is the power to reorder public memory—to place a president’s preferred monument between existing symbols and then present the alteration as military necessity.

Safety Is Not Decorative

The proposed military functions also sit near a restricted flight corridor used by aircraft approaching Reagan National Airport. The article invokes the deaths of 67 people in a collision between an Army helicopter and a commercial flight during the Trump administration as evidence of the stakes surrounding military operations in that airspace. That tragedy does not by itself prove that drones at the arch would cause another collision. It does make casual assurances inadequate.

If drones and ammunition are genuine operational requirements, officials owe the project the full rigor applied to military infrastructure. If they are not genuine requirements, then the military designation looks less like a security plan than a legal theory attached to a monument.

Either conclusion defeats the fantasy that officials can obtain the authority associated with national defense while avoiding the safety obligations that accompany it.

The Architecture of Executive Impunity

The arch exposes a broader governing pattern: declare a presidential preference urgent, locate a convenient statutory rationale, compress public scrutiny, and treat technical constraints as obstacles to personal authority. The reported ballroom dispute, in which The Washington Post said Trump proclaimed, “I am the code,” distills that posture, though a declaration of supremacy does not repeal construction law.

Institutions reveal their weakness less dramatically. They shorten consultations. They accept strained classifications. They force opponents—including veterans contesting a monument beside Arlington—to spend time and money defending constraints that agencies were created to enforce.

The central conflict is not between an ambitious builder and fussy regulators. It is between personalized executive power and the legal processes meant to prevent public land, military authority, and national memory from becoming instruments of presidential self-commemoration. An administration may call an arch a defense facility. It cannot make the contradictions disappear merely by changing the label.
