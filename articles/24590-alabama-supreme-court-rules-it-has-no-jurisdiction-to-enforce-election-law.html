Alabama Supreme Court rules it has no jurisdiction to enforce election law

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 10, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe court chose the gatekeeperPower without independent reviewProcedure displaced the real questionAccountability outsourced

What happened

The Alabama Supreme Court did not decide whether Sen. Tommy Tuberville satisfied Alabama’s residency requirement. AlterNet reports that the court upheld the dismissal of a challenge to his eligibility, ruling that courts lacked jurisdiction to reconsider the Republican Party’s determination that he qualified as a resident.

The justices said a primary-election contest under state law was the exclusive way to challenge a nominee’s qualifications before the general election. They also found no statutory right to appeal a party committee’s decision to a circuit court or the state Supreme Court. Tuberville had argued that courts had no role in reviewing his eligibility.

Tuberville’s campaign released seven years of tax and property records in June to support his residency claim, but AlterNet says those documents generated additional questions about his voting history. The court left those factual disputes unresolved.

The court chose the gatekeeper

The headline’s suggestion that Alabama’s highest court declared itself unable to enforce election law is broader than the reported ruling supports. The court interpreted a particular statutory process and held that this challenge belonged inside the party’s primary-contest machinery, without judicial appeal.

That distinction does not make the result harmless. It identifies the political party—not an independent court—as the decisive gatekeeper for its nominee’s constitutional qualifications. Once the Alabama Republican Party approved Tuberville, the challengers had no judicial route under the process described by the court.

Power without independent review

The institutional power here is divided but plainly located. The Legislature wrote the election statutes. The Alabama Supreme Court defined their jurisdictional boundaries. Within those boundaries, the state Republican Party exercised the practical power to determine whether its own nominee was eligible.

That arrangement asks a party to police a candidate it has political reasons to protect. The reporting does not establish that Republican officials acted corruptly or ignored contrary evidence. It does establish that their decision survived without an independent judicial ruling on the underlying residency question.

Procedure displaced the real question

Tuberville’s residency documents and voting history may attract public attention, but they were not the basis of the decision. The controlling issue was who had authority to hear the challenge. Treating the case principally as a mystery about where Tuberville lived risks obscuring the more consequential fact: no court reached an answer.

Attorney Barry Ragsdale warned that the ruling could permit ineligible candidates to proceed whenever parties fail to screen them properly. That is his interpretation, not a finding by the court. But the structural concern follows directly from the reported framework: a qualification rule is only as effective as the institution empowered to enforce it.

Accountability outsourced

This case reveals a familiar political design flaw. Formal eligibility requirements create the appearance of constraint, while procedural rules can leave enforcement to partisan organizations with immediate stakes in the outcome.

The result is not legal confusion. It is an allocation of authority. Alabama’s system, as described in the ruling, gave the party the decisive initial judgment and provided no statutory judicial appeal from that judgment. When law delegates enforcement to interested actors and courts decline review, the rule remains on paper while accountability becomes optional.
