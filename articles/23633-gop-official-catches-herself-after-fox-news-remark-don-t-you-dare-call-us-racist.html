GOP official catches herself after Fox News remark: 'Don't you dare call us racist!'

The “Cryptic” Pen and the Manufactured Enemy

Power Is Not Being Persecuted

New York City Council Member Vickie Paladino used a Fox News interview to tell Hasan Piker, a New Jersey-born American, to “go back to where you came from,” then demanded immunity from the obvious judgment that followed. The immediate dispute concerned Mayor Zohran Mamdani’s planned attendance at the September 11 commemoration. Paladino turned it into something larger and uglier: a campaign to cast Muslim participation in city government as an intrusion upon the city itself.

The power structure matters. Mamdani is the mayor. Paladino is an elected council member with access to national television. Fox News supplied the platform and introduced Piker as “anti-American and antisemitic” before asking Paladino to respond. This was not a private misunderstanding among equal participants. Public officials and a major broadcaster were using their institutional reach to decide who counts as legitimately American.

Racism Followed by a Denial of Racism

Paladino’s sequence was revealingly efficient. First came the nativist command. Then came the preemptive defense: “Don’t you dare call us racist.”

That denial does not neutralize what preceded it. “Go back to where you came from” assigns foreignness to an American because the speaker considers him politically, culturally, or ethnically suspect. Paladino then folded Piker, Mamdani and “radical Islam” into the same rhetorical frame. Her stated insistence that the issue had nothing to do with Muslims or religion collided with the argument she was making.

Piker’s 2019 statement that America “deserved” September 11 was reprehensible, and Mamdani has denounced it. That fact can support criticism of Piker. It does not establish collective culpability for a Muslim mayor, a Muslim city lawyer or Muslim participation in a memorial ceremony. Paladino’s maneuver depends on erasing those distinctions.

The Pen Becomes Evidence

The most grotesque element is the effort to transform an ordinary administrative gesture into an Islamist signal. After signing two September 11-related executive orders, Mamdani asked Police Commissioner Jessica Tisch to pass the ceremonial pen to chief counsel Ramzi Kassem. The mayor’s office said Kassem received it because his team drafted the orders.

Paladino called the handoff “very cryptic” and described it as “a message to each and every one of us.” Her apparent evidence was Kassem’s former legal representation of an al Qaeda operative, supplemented by the client’s family connection to one of the September 11 hijackers.

This is guilt by association stripped of even its usual disguises. A lawyer’s representation of a client does not make the lawyer an adherent of the client’s crimes. A pen passed to counsel is not evidence of conspiracy. The insinuation works only if a Muslim official’s professional history is treated as inherently suspicious and routine conduct is interpreted through a presumption of disloyalty.

Fox News Supplied the Frame

Fox News did not merely document Paladino’s rhetoric. Faulkner established the segment’s terms by branding Piker before presenting his allegation of “manufactured racial hysteria.” Paladino then demonstrated the allegation in real time.

The resulting coverage shifts scrutiny away from the people exercising power and toward the targets expected to prove their innocence. Piker must answer for an old statement. Mamdani must justify attending his own city’s commemoration. Kassem must survive insinuations drawn from fulfilling a lawyer’s professional duty. Meanwhile, Paladino’s explicit nativism is repackaged as emotional candor from someone supposedly defending “truth.”

That is propaganda’s practical function: not necessarily inventing every fact, but arranging facts so that prejudice appears evidentiary and the prejudice itself disappears from view.

Exclusion Disguised as Remembrance

The petition demanding that Mamdani “not show his face” at the memorial site is not simply an argument over ceremonial decorum. Mamdani is New York City’s mayor and its first Muslim mayor. The campaign seeks to make his religion and political associations disqualifying at a civic event he attends in an official capacity.

September 11 remembrance is being used here as a gatekeeping instrument. The attack becomes permanent political property, controlled by those who claim authority to decide which New Yorkers may mourn publicly and which must enter under suspicion. The victims’ families invoked as justification are not politically uniform; the source itself notes that many voted for Mamdani. Their grief is being conscripted into an exclusionary campaign they do not collectively endorse.

The System Rewards the Accusation

This episode reveals a durable political pattern: powerful actors manufacture suspicion around a minority official, demand that the target disprove insinuations, and portray any accurate description of the tactic as an intolerable accusation of racism.

The point is not to establish that Mamdani, Kassem or Piker committed some relevant wrongdoing. The point is to make Muslim identity itself carry an endless burden of explanation. A mayor’s attendance becomes provocation. A lawyer’s work becomes complicity. A ceremonial pen becomes a coded threat.

Paladino’s rhetoric is not confusion. It is scapegoating conducted from public office and amplified by national media. Its political utility lies precisely in its lack of proof: suspicion can be renewed indefinitely, while the people spreading it continue to pose as the injured party.
