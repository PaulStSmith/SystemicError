'They're just stealing your money': Fury as Trump drops another 'absolutely demented' ad

Based on an article by Robert Davis

Paulo SantosSeptember 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that President Donald Trump’s administration released a black-and-white advertisement on September 27, 2026, using footage and rhetoric from his 2024 campaign. Trump speaks of a “final battle” to purge enemies from government and confront the media; the closing screen identifies the ad as paid for by the U.S. government.

The administration released it roughly five weeks before the midterm elections and only days after another disputed government-funded Trump advertisement, according to RawStory. Journalists and an immigration lawyer condemned the new ad as campaign propaganda financed by taxpayers, with some alleging the expenditure was illegal. The report does not establish the ad’s cost, where it aired, which agency authorized it, what appropriation funded it, or whether any authority has found the spending unlawful.

The power is inside the executive branch

The decisive actor is not the collection of critics quoted from X. It is the administration controlling the government personnel, money, and communications machinery used to produce or distribute the advertisement.

Trump supplies the message and political persona. His administration supplies the institutional conversion: campaign imagery becomes government speech, and government resources become a vehicle for promoting the president’s political narrative. Taxpayers have no comparable power over that transaction at the moment it occurs.

Propaganda with an official receipt

The most consequential detail is not that the advertisement looks “demented” or resembles a horror film. Those descriptions turn an exercise of state power into an argument about taste.

The closing government-funding notice raises the real question: why is public money carrying rhetoric associated with Trump’s electoral campaign shortly before a federal election? Calling the press “fake news” and promising to purge vaguely defined enemies may be familiar Trump material, but government sponsorship changes its political function. It places the authority and resources of the state behind a leader’s factional message.

Outrage is not accountability

RawStory documents anger but leaves the machinery largely concealed. Readers are given posts from journalists and a lawyer, yet not the name of the agency that approved the ad, the official who signed off, the funding source, the distribution plan, or the legal rationale.

That is a serious reporting weakness. One critic alleges illegality, but the article supplies no statute, ruling, or official review supporting that conclusion. The allegation matters; it is not a finding. Without following the authorization chain, coverage risks substituting social-media fury for institutional scrutiny.

The enemy list does political work

The advertisement’s language divides the country between Trump’s supporters and a collection of internal enemies: the “deep state,” “warmongers,” and the press. The categories are broad enough to absorb civil servants, policy opponents, journalists, or anyone else the administration chooses to place inside them.

The reported language therefore does more than advertise policy. It frames government as a weapon to be reclaimed from illegitimate occupants and used in a final confrontation. RawStory does not establish what concrete actions would follow, but the political structure is clear: loyalty is presented as public virtue, opposition as contamination, and institutional restraint as enemy control.

When government becomes campaign property

The larger pattern is the erosion of the boundary between governing and campaigning. An incumbent administration possesses public funds, official platforms, staff, and the symbolic authority of the presidency. Using those assets to circulate campaign-era messaging gives the officeholder an advantage that opponents and ordinary citizens cannot reproduce.

The scandal is not merely that taxpayers may have financed an ugly advertisement. It is that public institutions can be treated as the incumbent’s political property while coverage stops at reactions. The unanswered questions—who authorized it, under what authority, for how much, and through which channels—are where responsibility lives.
