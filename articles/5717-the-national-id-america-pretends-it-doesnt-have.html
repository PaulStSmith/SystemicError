The National ID America Pretends It Doesn’t Have

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 5, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The United States loves a particular story about itself.

It is a story of rugged individualism, suspicion of centralized authority, and a deep cultural allergy to anything that smells like “papers, please.” Every election cycle, the same talking point resurfaces: America doesn’t need a national ID. National identity cards are framed as foreign, authoritarian, un‑American.

And yet.

In practice, the United States already operates one of the most pervasive identity systems in the developed world. It simply refuses to admit it.

This article was sparked by a video on YouTube that lays out the history and mechanics of the Social Security number. Not as folklore, not as conspiracy, but as a slow, bureaucratic drift — one decision piggy‑backing on another until a supposedly narrow administrative identifier quietly became the spine of American identity.

The result is a system that is more invasive than a formal national ID, less secure than a library card, and almost completely unaccountable.

The Accident That Became Infrastructure

The Social Security number was never meant to identify people. It was meant to track contributions.

Created during the Great Depression, Social Security was conceived as a compulsory retirement insurance scheme: you pay in while working, you draw out when old. To manage this, the government needed a way to associate wages with future benefits. A unique number per worker was sufficient.

At first, you only received a Social Security number when you entered the workforce.

Then came a problem.

Once multiple agencies began interacting with the same population — taxation, employment, benefits — names and birthdates proved hopelessly inadequate. People share names. They move. They marry. They change surnames. In a pre‑computer era, reliably tracking millions of individuals was a logistical nightmare.

And sitting right there was a ready‑made identifier.

The tax authorities were the first to seize it. Despite explicit warnings from Social Security administrators that the number was not designed for identification, the IRS adopted it anyway. From there, the logic spread.

Banks adopted it.
Employers adopted it.
Landlords adopted it.
Schools adopted it.

Not because Congress debated and approved a national identity system — but because it was convenient.

That distinction matters.

How a Tax Rebate Turned Into Universal Assignment

The final step toward universality came through incentives, not mandates.

For decades, parents could claim children as dependents on their taxes largely on the honor system. Predictably, this produced “phantom children” — dependents who existed only on paper.

The fix was simple: require a Social Security number for each claimed child.

Overnight, the SSN stopped being something you acquired when you started working and became something assigned at birth. A number originally scoped to retirement accounting became a cradle‑to‑grave identifier.

And once everyone had it, institutions stopped asking questions.

This is a recurring American pattern: refuse to design a system deliberately, then accept a worse one because it already exists.

A National ID Without the Safeguards

Here’s the part defenders of the status quo rarely confront.

A formal national ID system would have required:

  • explicit legislation defining its scope

  • technical standards for security

  • legal limits on who may request or store the identifier

  • democratic accountability

The Social Security number has none of that.

It has no built‑in error detection. No checksum. No cryptographic validation. For much of its history, its structure encoded place and time of issuance, making large portions of the number guessable. Institutions routinely authenticate people using the “last four digits,” a practice that would be laughable if it weren’t so dangerous.

The physical card itself is an insult to modern security thinking: a piece of paper or cardboard with no photo, no biometrics, no tamper resistance.

Yet this flimsy artifact unlocks bank accounts, employment, housing, credit, and government services.

The United States didn’t avoid a national ID. It built one accidentally, then refused to secure it.

Surveillance by Inertia

Opponents of national IDs often argue that centralized identifiers enable surveillance.

Ironically, the American workaround produces more surveillance, not less.

Because the SSN lacks formal governance, it is copied endlessly across databases, traded between institutions, leaked in breaches, and hoarded without clear retention rules. There is no single authority responsible for limiting its spread because no one ever officially acknowledged what it became.

When identity theft occurs — and it does, constantly — the burden falls almost entirely on individuals. You didn’t consent to the system. You can’t opt out of it. You can’t meaningfully replace your number. But you are expected to absorb the consequences when it fails.

That is not liberty. That is liability without agency.

“Universal,” Except When It Isn’t

Even the supposed universality of the system collapses under scrutiny.

Certain religious groups are exempt.
Some categories of workers were grandfathered out.
Special carve‑outs exist for professions that had enough political leverage at the program’s creation.

These exceptions reveal something important: universality was never a principle. It was a convenience.

The system applies most rigidly to those with the least power to resist it.

The Real Failure

The failure here is not technical — though the technical flaws are severe.

The failure is political.

By refusing to openly debate and design an identity system, the United States allowed one to emerge through bureaucratic drift and institutional opportunism. The result is a system that is harder to reform precisely because it was never formally acknowledged.

Americans were told they were rejecting a national ID.
What they actually rejected was transparency.

And in that vacuum, a worse system took root.

One that tracks nearly everyone.
One that protects almost no one.
One that pretends it isn’t what it clearly is.

The Social Security number is not a national ID in name.

It is one in every way that matters.

And the longer the country insists otherwise, the longer it will remain trapped with the worst of both worlds.
