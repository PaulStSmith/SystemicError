Texas GOP loser wants Democrat who beat him stripped of power

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 11, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Texas Lt. Gov. Dan Patrick, after losing reelection to Democrat Vikki Goodwin by six points, urged Republican state senators to transfer the lieutenant governor’s powers to a Republican majority leader before Goodwin takes office.

The office controls major levers of Texas government, including committee assignments, budget oversight and influence over the legislative agenda. Patrick exercised those powers for 11 years. Republicans retained a state Senate majority despite Democrats winning every statewide office and taking the state House, giving GOP senators the votes needed to diminish the office in January.

Patrick told conservative radio host Mark Davis that the change would “protect the conservative majority.” Goodwin warned that Republicans would act “at their peril” and said she had contacted Republican senators in an effort to cooperate.

The power is in the rules

Goodwin won the office, but Republican senators still control the chamber rules that determine what the office can do. That is the decisive institutional fact. Patrick can propose the maneuver; the Senate Republican majority can enact it.

This is not an abstract dispute over legislative procedure. Committee appointments, chairmanships, budgeting and agenda control determine which constituencies receive hearings, which bills advance and which priorities quietly die. Moving those powers would redirect authority from the statewide winner to officials whose party retained control of one chamber.

Patrick’s convenient conversion

Patrick’s argument exposes the principle at work: power belongs in the lieutenant governor’s office when Republicans hold it and in the Senate majority when they do not. He acknowledged that he had contemplated changing the rules had he won, but his present proposal has a specific and immediate beneficiary—a Republican majority confronting a Democratic lieutenant governor.

His record makes the proposed demotion especially stark. Patrick did not spend 11 years treating the office as ceremonial. He accumulated and exercised its authority, including while advancing school vouchers and presiding over Attorney General Ken Paxton’s impeachment trial, which ended in acquittal. Only after voters selected someone else did the office supposedly become too powerful to leave intact.

Voters are being edited out

Patrick described stripping Goodwin’s authority as Republicans “standing up for their voters.” That formulation erases the voters who elected Goodwin and converts a statewide defeat into permission for the losing party to redesign the prize.

Republican senators unquestionably represent their own constituents. But Goodwin’s statewide mandate cannot honestly be reframed as an intrusion upon Republican voters. The proposed rule change would not correct an electoral imbalance. It would use one surviving pocket of Republican power to blunt the consequence of a broader Democratic victory.

Cooperation cannot substitute for authority

Goodwin’s outreach to Republican senators may help manage the transition, but her stated preference for a “deliberative, collaborative body” does not answer the power grab. Cooperation is meaningful only when the participants retain the authority voters assigned them.

If Republicans transfer the office’s powers, Goodwin will be invited to collaborate from a position they deliberately weakened. That is not institutional comity. It is an elected official being offered courtesy in place of control.

The office follows the party

The larger pattern is rule-by-contingency: institutions remain legitimate only while they produce the preferred partisan result. When control changes hands, authority is relocated, procedures are rewritten and the electorate is told that the maneuver protects somebody else’s vote.

The issue is not whether Texas gives its lieutenant governor too much power. That could be debated through a durable reform applied prospectively and irrespective of party. The reported proposal is narrower and more revealing: preserve the machinery when it serves Republicans, dismantle it when a Democrat wins, and call the reversal representation.
