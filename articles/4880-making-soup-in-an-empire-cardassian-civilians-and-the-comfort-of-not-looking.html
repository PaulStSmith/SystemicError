Making Soup in an Empire: Cardassian Civilians and the Comfort of Not Looking

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 25, 20267 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There is a particular kind of dread that only science fiction can produce.

It doesn’t come from monsters. It doesn’t come from space battles. It comes from recognition.

A social media post making the rounds recently posed a deceptively simple thought experiment: what must it be like to be a Cardassian civilian? Not a Gul. Not an Obsidian Order operative. Not an architect of occupation. Just a person. A restaurant owner. Someone who makes soup, balances accounts, pays taxes, goes home.

And then — mid-sentence — the writer realized what they were actually describing.

That moment of realization is the point. It’s the entire moral engine of Star Trek: Deep Space Nine distilled into a single uncomfortable thought.

The Empire Is Not Just Its Villains

The Cardassian Union, as depicted in Deep Space Nine, is a militarized, surveillance-obsessed state. It ran labor camps on Bajor. It institutionalized torture. It elevated state security into a cultural virtue. It justified expansion and occupation as civilizational necessity.

From the outside, Cardassia is easy to categorize: the empire. The aggressor. The villain.

But Deep Space Nine never let that simplification stand unchallenged. It introduced characters like Garak — complicated, charming, morally ambiguous — and civilians who believed in order, in stability, in national pride. The show understood something fundamental about authoritarian systems: they are not sustained solely by monsters. They are sustained by ordinary life.

Empires do not function on villain monologues. They function on paperwork, logistics, and cultural stories. They function because most people wake up, go to work, and do not see themselves as participating in cruelty.

That is the unsettling brilliance of the Cardassians as a narrative device. They are not chaotic evil. They are bureaucratic evil.

And bureaucracy thrives on normalization.

“I Just Run a Restaurant”

The post’s central image is mundane: a Cardassian civilian who “just runs a restaurant,” who makes soup while the rest of the galaxy debates the brutality of their government.

That image is devastating because it strips away the fantasy armor. It forces us to ask what it means to live inside a system whose violence is obvious to outsiders but compartmentalized at home.

It is easy to imagine a tyrant ordering atrocities. It is harder — and more disturbing — to imagine a society where atrocity coexists with dinner reservations.

Authoritarian regimes do not demand that every citizen participate directly in harm. They demand something subtler: disengagement. Compartmentalization. The ability to “abstractly know” what is happening while continuing daily routines.

“I’m not involved.”

“That’s politics.”

“I can’t do anything about it.”

“I just run a restaurant.”

History is littered with variations of that sentence.

The Architecture of Compartmentalization

What Deep Space Nine captured with unusual sophistication is how cultural narratives make compartmentalization possible.

Cardassians believed in order. In discipline. In strength. They saw themselves as guardians of civilization against chaos. They had grievances. They had myths of decline and humiliation. They told themselves stories about necessity.

These stories are not unique to fiction.

Every authoritarian movement constructs a narrative that reframes cruelty as defense. Surveillance becomes protection. Suppression becomes stability. Occupation becomes security. Torture becomes information gathering. Civil liberties become luxuries.

Once the narrative hardens, ordinary people can inhabit it without feeling monstrous. They can look at the machinery of harm and see instead a regrettable but necessary apparatus. They can believe that the worst stories are exaggerated by enemies. They can convince themselves that the “bad parts” are isolated.

And then they go back to making soup.

The genius of science fiction is that it lets us examine this architecture at a safe remove. We are not being accused. We are not being named. We are observing aliens.

Until we are not.

The Mid-Sentence Realization

The writer of that post describes stopping mid-sentence and sitting down for ten minutes, staring at the floor.

That is the moral vertigo moment.

Because the thought experiment collapses distance. It moves from speculative world-building to ethical proximity. It forces a reckoning: what does it mean to be an ordinary person inside a system that others experience as oppressive?

The horror is not that there are villains. The horror is how easily ordinary life coexists with systemic harm.

In Deep Space Nine, the Federation often serves as contrast — aspirational, rights-oriented, pluralistic. But even the Federation is not immune to moral compromise. Section 31 exists. “In the Pale Moonlight” exists. The show refuses the comfort of moral purity.

No society is automatically inoculated against ethical erosion.

The question is never simply “Are we the empire?”

The more uncomfortable question is “What does daily life look like inside systems that others experience as empire?”

Ordinary People and Structural Harm

One of the most persistent myths about authoritarianism is that it requires universal enthusiasm. It does not.

It requires compliance.

It requires indifference.

It requires people who can tell themselves that they are not personally responsible.

Structural harm thrives on diffusion of responsibility. When no single person feels fully accountable, the system hums along. Each individual contribution seems small. Each act of disengagement seems harmless. The totality is catastrophic.

Hannah Arendt’s concept of the “banality of evil” was not an accusation that evil is boring. It was an observation that large-scale harm is often administered by people who see themselves as ordinary functionaries.

That insight maps perfectly onto the Cardassian civilian. The restaurateur is not signing deportation orders. They are not overseeing labor camps. But they are part of a society that funds, tolerates, and normalizes those systems.

The discomfort comes from recognizing that this pattern is not alien. It is human.

The Stories We Tell Ourselves

Authoritarian systems endure because they provide psychological escape hatches.

“They’re criminals.”

“It’s necessary for security.”

“It’s exaggerated.”

“It’s not my department.”

These narratives allow people to retain a self-concept of decency while benefiting from or passively enabling harm.

Deep Space Nine never caricatured this. It depicted Cardassian pride, cultural richness, family bonds. It made them human.

That is what makes the analogy sting. If they were cartoon villains, there would be no lesson. The point is precisely that they are recognizably complex.

Science fiction functions as a moral rehearsal space. It allows us to test our instincts against fictional empires. We condemn Cardassian occupation. We recoil at torture chambers. We applaud resistance.

But the thought experiment pushes further: what if you were not the rebel? What if you were not the dissident? What if you were simply busy?

What if the empire was background noise?

Comfort as a Political Force

Comfort is underrated as a political variable.

People with routines, mortgages, careers, and social circles are not eager to destabilize their lives. Even when confronted with disturbing information, they often prioritize continuity over confrontation.

Authoritarian movements exploit this. They do not need universal fervor; they need enough comfort to dull urgency.

The Cardassian restaurateur is comfortable enough. Their daily life functions. The labor camps are distant — geographically, emotionally, narratively.

Distance is the anesthetic.

Modern information ecosystems complicate this dynamic. Atrocities are no longer hidden in remote sectors of the galaxy. They are documented, archived, circulated. And yet, exposure does not automatically translate into action.

The capacity to scroll past suffering is a contemporary form of compartmentalization.

We know. Abstractly.

And then we make dinner.

The Value of the Gut Punch

Why does this particular thought experiment resonate so strongly?

Because it bypasses defensive reflexes.

It does not accuse. It invites reflection.

It uses aliens to ask human questions. It does what Star Trek has always done at its best: it reframes contemporary ethical dilemmas through speculative distance, then slowly removes the distance.

The result is not a lecture. It is a pause. A moment of sitting down and staring at the floor.

That pause is valuable.

It interrupts the assumption that morality is only tested in dramatic moments. It reminds us that most ethical decisions are incremental. They are embedded in daily life. They involve attention, voice, refusal, participation.

Or silence.

No Easy Exits

The purpose of this reflection is not self-flagellation. It is clarity.

It is easy to imagine ourselves as heroes in narratives about empire. It is harder to imagine ourselves as bystanders.

But ethical maturity requires that we consider both possibilities.

The Cardassian civilian is not a caricature of villainy. They are a mirror. They embody the unsettling truth that structural harm can coexist with ordinary decency — that people can love their families, cook good food, and participate in unjust systems simultaneously.

That duality is not uniquely Cardassian.

It is human.

Science Fiction as Moral Instrument

Star Trek endures not because of phasers and warp drives, but because it insists on moral examination.

From Cold War allegories to occupation narratives, from surveillance states to wartime compromises, it has consistently used speculative storytelling to interrogate power, responsibility, and complicity.

The Cardassians are not merely antagonists. They are case studies in how societies rationalize themselves.

And the civilian making soup is the most chilling figure of all.

Because they are not extraordinary.

They are plausible.

The discomfort we feel when the analogy clicks is not accidental. It is the point. It is the show’s quiet demand that we examine not only overt tyranny but also the conditions that allow it to persist.

The empire is not sustained solely by its architects. It is sustained by its background characters.

By people who wake up, go to work, and tell themselves they are not involved.

That is the moral vertigo embedded in a single thought experiment about Cardassians.

And once you feel it, it is very hard to go back to thinking that empires are only other people’s stories.
