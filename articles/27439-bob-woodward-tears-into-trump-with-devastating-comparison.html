Bob Woodward tears into Trump with devastating comparison

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 24, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Corruption measured in billionsA war sold as presidential strengthAdvisers who “feed the beast”The costs outside Washington

Bob Woodward’s comparison is blunt: Donald Trump is “worse, much worse than Nixon.” His case rests not on temperament or aesthetics, but on two exercises of presidential power—Trump’s reported personal enrichment while in office and his decision to wage war against Iran. Woodward argues that the corruption is embedded, the aggression was avoidable, and Trump’s advisers have encouraged rather than restrained him.

Corruption measured in billions

Speaking with Axios co-founder Mike Allen on The Axios Show, Woodward said Trump had made billions of dollars while serving as president. He contrasted that scale of enrichment with an earlier political culture in which even accepting a free flight on someone else’s jet could provoke scandal.

Woodward called Trump’s corruption “deep and embedded.” That distinction matters. His indictment is not that Trump occasionally crossed an ethical line; it is that the presidency and the president’s financial interests have become entangled on a scale Woodward considers historically exceptional.

The Nixon comparison carries particular authority because Woodward, alongside Washington Post reporter Carl Bernstein, exposed the Watergate scandal that helped drive Nixon from office in 1974. But biography is not proof, and Woodward does not ask readers to rely on biography alone. He identifies the conduct behind his verdict: immense reported gains by a sitting president and a war launched against a country that had not attacked the United States.

A war sold as presidential strength

Woodward said the defining event of Trump’s presidency may be the Iran war. He described the attack as unprovoked aggression and “a mistake,” while acknowledging that Iran is “an unsavory country.”

That qualification dismantles a familiar pretext for war: treating another government’s brutality as sufficient authorization for an American attack. As Woodward observed, applying that standard consistently would leave the United States at war almost perpetually. The relevant question is not whether Iran’s government deserves approval. It is whether its character justified Washington bringing US military force against it.

Trump has offered a radically different account. In a June appearance on The Axios Show, he presented the conflict as evidence of his power, saying there were “no limits” to it. He also claimed that his dealmaking had prevented a “worldwide depression.”

The contrast is revealing. Woodward evaluates the war through the decision to attack and the consequences that followed. Trump describes it as a demonstration of personal strength. In that framing, military violence becomes presidential branding, and the destruction borne by people beyond Washington’s reach is pushed outside the picture.

Advisers who “feed the beast”

Woodward also distinguished the machinery surrounding the two presidents. Nixon, he said, had Cabinet officials who attempted to stop wars. Trump’s administration, by comparison, is “feeding the beast” by encouraging aggression.

That does not absolve Trump, who holds the authority and owns the decision. It identifies the broader power structure that makes escalation easier: senior officials who could impose friction instead reinforcing the president’s impulses. An administration without meaningful internal restraint does not merely reflect its leader. It expands his capacity to inflict damage.

Woodward reached the same broad judgment in his 2024 book War, concluding there as well that Trump was worse than Nixon. The present comparison sharpens the basis for it. Nixon’s criminal abuse of office remains the benchmark, but Woodward argues that Trump has surpassed him through the combination of financial self-interest, military aggression and an official circle unwilling to check either.

The costs outside Washington

The war’s effects are still unfolding in Iran. President Masoud Pezeshkian said US and Israeli attacks on the country’s gas infrastructure eliminated 240 million cubic meters of daily production capacity. According to Pezeshkian, only about 100 million cubic meters had been restored as Iran prepared for a worsening winter shortage.

Those figures describe more than damaged facilities. They point to an energy deficit confronting civilians during winter, long after the attacks that produced it. Trump’s language of limitless power omits that consequence, as boasts about force usually do.

Woodward’s comparison is therefore devastating for a specific reason. Nixon’s downfall established that presidential power could be abused for political survival. Woodward argues that Trump has joined personal enrichment to war-making while surrounded by officials who encourage escalation. The immediate result is not an abstract debate over historical ranking. It is an Iranian gas system still operating far below the capacity destroyed by American and Israeli attacks—and a president presenting that wreckage as proof of his strength.
