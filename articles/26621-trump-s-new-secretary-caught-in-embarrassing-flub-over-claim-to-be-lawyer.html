Trump's new secretary caught in embarrassing flub over claim to be 'lawyer'

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 14, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Katie Zacharia, President Trump’s new press secretary, repeatedly called herself a lawyer despite searches finding no record that she was licensed to practice law in any state or Washington, D.C. Pepperdine University confirmed that she earned a J.D. in 2011, but a law degree does not by itself make someone a licensed attorney.

Popular Information searched nationwide bar records, while AlterNet separately checked California records under Zacharia’s current and maiden names. Neither found a listing. Zacharia also identified herself as Fix California’s general counsel from January 2024 to March 2025 and continued using that title in social-media biographies into 2026. The reporting does not establish what work she performed, whether she was otherwise authorized to practice, or whether she violated California law.

The unanswered question

The evidence supports a discrepancy, not a conviction. Zacharia publicly said, “I’m a lawyer,” Trump described her as “an attorney,” and her résumé claimed a senior legal title. The available licensing records, as reported, do not substantiate those descriptions.

That demands an explanation from Zacharia and from the institutions that promoted the claim. Whether her conduct met California’s legal definition of advertising or practicing law without authorization remains unresolved. AlterNet correctly notes that the investigation presented no evidence of a criminal violation.

Power signed off on the credential

This is not merely an individual résumé problem. Fix California, founded by Trump ally Ric Grenell, reportedly gave—or at least permitted Zacharia to advertise—the title of general counsel. Trump then repeated the attorney designation when appointing her press secretary. Those are decisions by powerful political actors and organizations, not errors committed by spectators.

The central institutional question is therefore broader than whether Zacharia passed a bar examination: Who verified her qualifications, who approved the title, and who allowed it to circulate? The reporting supplies no answers. That absence does not prove complicity, but it exposes an accountability failure around an appointment whose entire function is communicating official claims to the public.

The “flub” framing is too small

Calling this an embarrassing flub reduces a sustained, documented representation to a momentary verbal mistake. AlterNet’s account describes repeated statements, an employment title, a LinkedIn entry, social-media biographies, and Trump’s own announcement. Whatever ultimately explains the discrepancy, it was not a single slip.

The headline also risks turning the story into personality-driven humiliation when the meaningful issue is institutional validation. Zacharia holds less power than the president who appointed her, but she still has agency over how she describes her credentials. Trump and Fix California, meanwhile, had the power to confer legitimacy on those descriptions and the responsibility to check them.

Credentialing as political theater

A J.D. can supply the prestige of legal expertise without conferring authority to practice law. That distinction matters especially in political communications, where credentials are used to manufacture trust quickly and corrections rarely travel as far as the original claim.

The larger pattern is a political ecosystem in which affiliation can substitute for verification. Titles circulate among allied organizations, media appearances and appointments until repetition itself begins to look like proof. When scrutiny finally arrives, the episode is treated as a gaffe rather than the foreseeable result of institutions that reward assertion and neglect validation.

The system behind the résumé

The unresolved legal question belongs to regulators and evidence the reporting does not provide. The political conclusion is narrower but already visible: a claim to professional authority passed through a conservative organization, public interviews and a presidential appointment without the reported record establishing the credential.

That is how institutional decay often presents itself—not as one spectacular deception, but as a chain of powerful actors declining to perform elementary checks. The individual claim matters. The machinery that authenticated it matters more.
