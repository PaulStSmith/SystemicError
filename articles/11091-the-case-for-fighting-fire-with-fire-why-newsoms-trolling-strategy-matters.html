The Case for Fighting Fire With Fire: Why Newsom’s Trolling Strategy Matters

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 29, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The New York Times piece dissected by The Root highlights a fissure at the heart of Democratic strategy heading into 2028. California Governor Gavin Newsom, often caricatured as the slick “coastal elite” archetype, is carving out space by doing something almost unthinkable in Democratic circles: mocking Trump in Trump’s own language. To some insiders, this looks dangerous. To others, it looks like the first sign the party might finally understand what it takes to beat a demagogue.

Bruni’s Error: The Politics of Politeness

Frank Bruni, the NYT columnist quoted in the piece, frets that Newsom’s trolling “bucks the political truism that elections are about contrasts” and risks “blurring the line between Democratic decorum and the moral obscenity of Trump.” It’s a line of argument that sounds erudite in a seminar room but collapses under the weight of electoral reality.

For nearly a decade, Democrats have tried the “contrast” strategy by elevating civility and governance against Trump’s chaos. How well has that worked? In 2016, Hillary Clinton projected competence and seriousness while Trump turned the election into a reality show. The result was disaster. In 2020, Joe Biden managed to scrape out a win, largely because Trump had personally mismanaged a once-in-a-century pandemic. Even then, the margins were razor-thin. In 2024, Democrats reverted to form, offering lectures about norms while Trump roared across the landscape with blood-and-soil demagoguery. They lost badly.

Bruni’s longing for contrasts is code for retreating to the familiar posture of being the “adult in the room.” But politics isn’t scored like a high school debate. Elections are not polite conversations; they are primal contests over legitimacy and power. When one side is willing to go scorched-earth, responding with a shrug and a sigh of “we’re better than that” is a losing strategy.

Newsom’s Gamble: Speaking in the Language of Power

Newsom’s August 12th post on X is a case study in why his strategy has traction:

“FINAL WARNING DONALD TRUMP — MAYBE THE MOST IMPORTANT WARNING IN HISTORY! STOP CHEATING OR CALIFORNIA WILL REDRAW THE MAPS. AND GUESS WHO WILL ANNOUNCE IT THIS WEEK? GAVIN NEWSOM (MANY SAY THE MOST LOVED & HANDSOME GOVERNOR) AND A VERY POWERFUL TEAM. DON’T MAKE US DO IT!!! THANK YOU FOR YOUR ATTENTION TO THIS MATTER.”

This reads like Trump’s own caps-lock carnival barking, except flipped into parody. It’s exaggerated to the point of absurdity. And that’s the point. It doesn’t just fire up Democratic partisans—it undermines the aura Trump carefully cultivates with his followers.

Trump’s political brand is built on the projection of strength: the alpha dog who mocks, belittles, and dominates. But ridicule cuts both ways. When Newsom mirrors Trump’s performative bluster, he strips the emperor’s clothes. If Trump can be imitated, he can be mocked. If he can be mocked, he can be diminished. And if he can be diminished, then he can be beaten.

The Democratic Establishment’s Addiction to Losing Gracefully

The problem is not that Democrats lack ideas or policies. The problem is that the party’s establishment has internalized a losing playbook: elevate civility, appeal to norms, trust the process, and hope voters recoil from Republican extremism. Over and over again, this has left Democrats looking weak, reactive, and irrelevant.

The reflexive fear of “becoming like Trump” is a symptom of this addiction to civility politics. But there’s a crucial distinction between embracing Trump’s corruption and embracing his showmanship. Newsom isn’t adopting Trump’s cruelty, nor his authoritarian instincts. He’s adopting Trump’s ability to command attention—and using it to turn the ridicule back on the would-be strongman.

Politics has always been theater as much as governance. Franklin Roosevelt relished sparring with plutocrats, mocking them as “malefactors of great wealth.” Lyndon Johnson bullied, threatened, and cajoled legislators with crude bravado. Even Abraham Lincoln, sainted in retrospect, was a master of cutting wit and public spectacle. The idea that Democrats should limit themselves to soft-spoken appeals to reason is not a noble tradition—it’s a suicide pact.

Why Ridicule Works

There’s a psychological dimension here that Bruni and other hand-wringers miss. Trump’s cult doesn’t respond to policy arguments, fact-checks, or polite condemnations. They respond to strength, and they are addicted to the spectacle of their leader humiliating opponents.

What happens when their leader is the butt of the joke? What happens when he is imitated, mocked, and exposed as ridiculous? The spell weakens. Even if only a fraction of his followers begin to waver, that’s the margin Democrats need in swing states.

This is not about winning over the entire MAGA base—it’s about peeling off just enough to make Trump’s coalition brittle. If 3–5% of Trump voters decide he’s no longer the invincible alpha, the GOP’s electoral strategy collapses. And ridicule, not reason, is what cracks that armor.

The Counter-Argument: Risks and Rewards

Critics will say that fighting fire with fire risks legitimizing Trump’s tactics, lowering the discourse, or alienating independents. These risks are real. But the greater risk is repeating 2024’s mistake: underestimating Trump’s ability to dominate the conversation and failing to seize attention on terms that voters actually recognize.

The Democratic base doesn’t need more policy white papers. It needs candidates who project strength, humor, and relevance. Independents don’t need more lectures about norms—they need to see a Democrat willing to stand in the arena and swing back.

Newsom’s strategy may not be universally appealing, but it is disruptive. And disruption is exactly what the Democratic Party needs if it wants to avoid becoming a permanent minority party of polite losers.

The Coastal Elite Problem

One valid critique is that Newsom embodies the “coastal elite” stereotype. His slick appearance, polished cadence, and California pedigree feed directly into the GOP’s narrative of Democrats as out-of-touch technocrats. That’s a vulnerability.

But that vulnerability doesn’t negate the value of his strategy. In fact, it makes his willingness to brawl even more important. If Newsom leans into parody, self-awareness, and humor, he can offset the caricature. What looks like aloofness in a press conference becomes relatability when delivered through biting mockery of Trump.

Conclusion: Time to Break the Spell

Frank Bruni’s lament about blurred lines reveals a party elite still hypnotized by the fantasy of “contrast politics”—the idea that voters will reward Democrats simply for not being Republicans. That fantasy has been tested to destruction.

Newsom’s trolling isn’t a gimmick; it’s an experiment in breaking the Trump spell. It’s not about firing up Democrats—we’re already engaged. It’s about dislodging a few MAGA voters by puncturing the myth of Trump’s untouchable dominance.

Politics is not a seminar. It’s a street fight. Trump has known that all along. The question is whether Democrats are finally willing to learn it too. If Newsom is the first to pick up that weapon, then good. About damn time.
