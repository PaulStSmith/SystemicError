Dems reveal plan to call Trump's 'bluff' before the 2028 election

Based on an article by Jake Johnson

Paulo SantosNovember 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Sen. Elizabeth Warren urged Democrats to use their reported incoming control of the House and Senate in January to pass an economic agenda immediately, forcing President Donald Trump either to sign broadly popular measures or expose his promised cooperation as empty. In a TIME op-ed highlighted by AlterNet, Warren called this a test of Trump’s “bluff” before the 2028 election—not a plan to wait for that election.

Her priorities included ending the war in Iran, lowering household costs, undoing Republican cuts to Medicaid and nutrition assistance, reversing tax cuts benefiting the wealthy, and confronting corporate abuses. She said Democrats should use budget reconciliation or abolish the Senate filibuster if Republicans obstruct that agenda, then put legislation before Trump despite his veto power.

Sen. Bernie Sanders separately argued that the reported midterm victories were insufficient without action on inequality, the federal minimum wage, artificial intelligence, campaign finance, healthcare and Social Security. Both senators’ message was that Democrats would assume real governing power in January and should be judged by what they do with it.

Power is not a press release

The decisive fact is not that Warren has produced a list of popular policies. It is that Democrats, according to the reporting, are about to control both chambers of Congress. That gives them authority over legislation, budgets, committees and the congressional agenda. Trump retains the veto, but a presidential veto does not prevent Congress from writing bills, holding votes or establishing who blocked what.

Warren is therefore arguing against her own party’s favorite alibi: power is never quite sufficient, the timing is never quite right, and voters must first deliver one more election. Her standard is more serious. Use the authority voters supplied, remove self-imposed procedural barriers and make every officeholder publicly own the consequences.

The filibuster is a choice

Republican obstruction would be an exercise of agency. So would Democratic submission to it. The filibuster is not weather; senators preserve or eliminate it through political decisions.

Warren’s willingness to abolish the 60-vote threshold matters because reconciliation cannot carry every part of the agenda she describes. If Democrats retain a rule that allows a Senate minority to bury legislation, they cannot honestly portray the burial as something that simply happened to them.

The same applies to Trump’s veto. He alone would decide whether to use it. Passing legislation would not guarantee lower costs or restored benefits, but it would separate the actors seeking a policy from the president preventing it. That distinction is politically useful only if Democrats first act.

“Call his bluff” understates the obligation

The language of bluff-calling makes governing sound like campaign strategy: place Trump in an awkward position, record the vote and prepare an argument for 2028. Warren’s stronger point is that working people were promised material results now.

Congressional Democrats must try to produce those results even when defeat is possible. Otherwise, “Trump might veto it” becomes advance permission for congressional inaction. That framing misdirects attention from legislators who control what reaches the president to speculation about what the president may eventually do.

If Trump signs the bills, the policies take effect. If he vetoes them, he owns that decision. If Democratic leaders refuse to send them, they own theirs.

The mandate belongs to policy

Warren and Sanders both locate the reported victories in economic demands rather than partisan branding. Their case is that voters backed candidates promising relief from high costs, concentrated wealth, weakened public benefits and corporate power.

That interpretation is still a political argument, not proof that every voter endorsed every item on their agendas. But it establishes the standard Democrats themselves chose: they claimed to represent working people against a system shaped by wealthy interests. Failure to pursue the promised policies would reveal that claim as electoral positioning rather than a governing commitment.

The system runs on deferred responsibility

The larger pattern is institutional evasion through sequencing. Leaders campaign on urgent conditions, win power, invoke procedural constraints, and ask the same voters to wait for a still more favorable election. Each institution points to another institution, while the people with the least power absorb the delay.

Warren’s proposal is valuable because it strips away that choreography. Congress must legislate. Senators must decide whether to preserve minority obstruction. Trump must sign or veto. Those are separate choices made by identifiable actors with actual power.

The test before 2028 is not merely whether Democrats can embarrass Trump. It is whether they will stop treating their own authority as a future aspiration and start accepting responsibility for its use.
