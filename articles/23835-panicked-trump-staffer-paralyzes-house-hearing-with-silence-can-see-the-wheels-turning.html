Panicked Trump staffer paralyzes House hearing with silence: 'Can see the wheels turning'

The Silence Was the Strategy

Three Minutes to Admit Reality

Joel Borkert, a senior State Department counterterrorism official, needed roughly three minutes of evasions and prolonged silences before acknowledging that right-wing political violence exists in the United States. The hesitation mattered because the administration’s own strategy elevates “violent left-wing extremists” while omitting the killing of former Minnesota House Speaker Melissa Hortman, which Rep. Jared Moskowitz identified as right-wing violence.

Power Was Sitting at the Witness Table

Borkert was not an uninformed bystander caught in a difficult conversation. He was the sole administration witness at a congressional hearing and a deputy coordinator in the State Department’s Bureau of Counterterrorism. His job places him inside the institution responsible for defining threats and directing the government’s response to them.

That makes his refusal to say “yes” more than an awkward performance. A counterterrorism official who can readily affirm the administration’s preferred threat category but struggles to name another is demonstrating how political priorities constrain official analysis.

The Report’s Omission Is the Point

The White House strategy cites the assassination of conservative activist Charlie Kirk as left-wing extremist violence. According to Moskowitz, it does not mention Hortman’s murder. That asymmetry shapes which victims receive federal recognition and which perpetrators become symbols of a national menace.

This is how propaganda can operate through selection rather than fabrication. A government need not invent every fact. It can elevate useful violence, bury inconvenient violence, and present the resulting distortion as a comprehensive security assessment. The document’s one-sidedness is not corrected merely because an official eventually concedes, under pressure, that right-wing extremism also exists.

Fear Produces Obedience, Not Neutrality

Moskowitz described “the wheels turning” as Borkert searched for an answer that would not get him in trouble. The source cannot establish what Borkert was thinking, but the inference is difficult to ignore. His eventual answer immediately pivoted back to the administration’s claim that left-wing terrorism had been a neglected threat.

The institutional context strengthens that reading. The State Department had lost roughly 3,000 employees through firings and buyouts by July 2025, while an anonymous policy adviser described a culture of “keeping your head down.” Retaliation does not need to occur in every exchange once officials understand that professional security depends on political compliance.

Borkert’s earlier role helping transfer USAID’s remaining work into the State Department also places him within the administration’s restructuring of federal institutions. He is not merely enduring the new order. He has participated in implementing it.

Euphemism Launders the Bias

When a direct answer became unavoidable, Borkert first reached for “various” forms and then “all forms.” Those phrases sound inclusive while evading the subject actually under examination. The problem was not whether many kinds of violence exist. It was whether the administration could acknowledge violence associated with its own political flank as plainly as violence attributed to its opponents.

The article presents the episode largely as an official “struggling” to answer. That framing is too gentle. Nothing about the question was intellectually difficult. The difficulty was political: how to preserve an administration narrative under congressional scrutiny without making the censorship of reality look too obvious.

Counterterrorism as Partisan Classification

The larger pattern is the conversion of state security power into a machinery of selective naming. Favored constituencies become invisible; opponents become categories of threat. Victims are sorted by political usefulness, and civil servants learn that accurate language can carry professional consequences.

Institutional cowardice is not incidental to that system. It is one of its operating mechanisms. Once officials hesitate to acknowledge an obvious threat because the answer may offend the president’s political project, counterterrorism analysis stops describing violence consistently. It begins assigning legitimacy—and danger—according to power.
