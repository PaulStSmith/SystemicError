Former military leaders tear into Trump Pentagon’s promotion scandal

Merit, As Long As It Looks Like Him

The Chain of Power

The relevant fact is simple: promotions in the Pentagon are not being blocked by the people whose careers are at stake. They are being blocked by Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, operating inside Donald Trump’s Department of Defense. That is where the power sits, and that is where the decision was made.

According to the reporting, Hegseth intervened to stop a set of one-star admiral promotions that had already been approved, with women and Black officers overrepresented among the targeted names. The source makes the conflict plain: this is not a debate about abstract standards. It is an exercise of institutional control over who gets to rise.

The Story Hegseth Wants Told

The administration’s line is the usual laundering of discrimination through vocabulary. It says skin color and gender are not being considered. But that denial collapses against the pattern described in the reporting: the officers were singled out in a Pentagon already engaged in a broader war on diversity, equity, and inclusion initiatives.

That is not neutrality. That is ideological selection. When the same office attacks DEI, then blocks promotions for officers with ties to pro-diversity efforts, the claim of colorblind merit becomes a cover story, not a principle.

Merit Talk, Chauvinist Practice

The most revealing part of this episode is not the promotions themselves. It is the language around them. Hegseth and his allies invoke merit while producing a system that looks increasingly like preference for white male authority dressed up as rigor.

Frank Kendall’s complaint lands because it names the double standard directly: the assumption that a woman or minority officer who advances must have been carried by identity rather than competence. That assumption is not an accidental bias. It is the operating theory of a bureaucracy deciding which forms of leadership it wants to see and which ones it wants to punish.

Hunter Stires sharpened the point further: this is chauvinism marketed as standards. That is the correct diagnosis. Merit is not being defended here. It is being used as a shield for exclusion.

The Real Damage

The immediate harm is obvious. Officers see that advancement can be slowed or reversed for reasons that are not transparent and not apolitical. That has consequences for retention, trust, and institutional legitimacy. The source notes former officials warning that people will decide not to stay if career progress is throttled by politics.

But the deeper damage is structural. Once promotion lists can be altered to reflect the secretary’s politics, the military’s claim to professional independence starts to rot. A force that tells itself it rewards excellence cannot function cleanly when its leadership pipeline is being ideologically filtered at the top.

What the Pattern Reveals

This is not just one ugly personnel fight. It is part of a broader project: to remake institutions so that formal rules remain in place while actual authority becomes personal, loyalist, and exclusionary. The administration does not need to abolish standards if it can redefine them selectively. It does not need to announce discriminatory policy if it can make promotion decisions do the work quietly.

That is how institutional cruelty usually operates now: through procedure, through euphemism, through the steady conversion of prejudice into management. The Pentagon is not being confused. It is being used.
