'Gonna feel some pain’: Man accused of threatening ‘every single Trump voter’

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 13, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower is not evenly distributedThe state must prove its caseA useful threat, politicallyCruelty converted into spectacleThe systemic error

What happened

AlterNet reports that federal prosecutors accused Howard Gimmey, 47, of Midwest City, Oklahoma, of threatening to kill President Donald Trump and harm Trump voters. Gimmey, described in the report as homeless, allegedly blamed Trump and his supporters for the conditions that cost him his home and warned that any Trump voter who crossed his path would “feel some” pain.

Prosecutors allege that Gimmey knowingly and willfully threatened the president while recklessly disregarding whether his statements would be understood as genuine threats. His public defenders asked the court to require proof that he understood his words would be regarded that way. The reporting provides no evidence that Gimmey attempted or prepared to carry out the alleged violence.

Power is not evenly distributed

Gimmey’s alleged statements are serious, but he did not exercise institutional power. The federal government does. Prosecutors can investigate, charge and imprison him; the president commands an administration with immense coercive and communicative reach. Trump voters, meanwhile, are tens of millions of people—not a decision-making institution and not collectively responsible for one defendant’s alleged conduct.

That distinction matters because political rhetoric routinely collapses individual threats into claims about entire opposition movements. One accused man becomes evidence of a hostile political class, while violence committed by government personnel or organized political actors is treated as a series of disconnected incidents.

The state must prove its case

The decisive action here was the government’s choice to prosecute speech as a true threat. That requires more than proving that the words were ugly or alarming. As AlterNet notes, the dispute includes Gimmey’s mental state: his lawyers want prosecutors required to establish that he understood his statements as threats.

The article weakens its own factual footing by calling him a “would-be assassin” despite presenting no reported attempt, plan or preparatory act. A person accused of making threats is not thereby an assassin, would-be or otherwise. That label converts an allegation about speech into a conclusion about intended action before the government has proved its case.

A useful threat, politically

AlterNet places the prosecution within the Trump administration’s broader effort to portray the president’s opponents as inherently violent. It cites Karoline Leavitt’s 2024 response to a question about comparisons between Trump and Adolf Hitler: she invoked a recent assassination attempt, condemned the media and used the episode to attack its credibility.

That is a recognizable propaganda maneuver. A specific act against a political figure becomes a shield against scrutiny of that figure’s rhetoric and conduct. Criticism is dragged into the moral orbit of assassination, allowing officials to imply responsibility without demonstrating causation.

AlterNet is right to examine that asymmetry, but its counterargument also needs discipline. References to January 6, attacks at rallies and threats against officials can establish a broader record only when each claim remains tied to identified actors and evidence. One person’s alleged threat does not indict every Trump opponent; violence by particular Trump supporters does not make every Trump voter culpable.

Cruelty converted into spectacle

The most politically revealing detail is not the profanity. It is that the accused man reportedly blamed political conditions for losing his home. That claim does not excuse or validate a threat, and the article does not establish that Trump or his voters caused Gimmey’s homelessness. But the framing quickly turns material desperation into an ideological spectacle: a homeless defendant becomes chiefly useful as a villain in a story about danger to the powerful.

The president receives federal protection, prosecutors and an enormous platform. A homeless defendant receives an inflammatory label before adjudication. Political coverage misdirects when it dwells on the menace of a powerless individual while leaving the institutions governing housing insecurity, punishment and public safety largely outside the frame.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is the conversion of violence into partisan property. Officials highlight threats against their own side, minimize violence associated with their coalition and use isolated defendants to stigmatize political opponents. Media outlets then risk reproducing the same distortion while trying to expose it.

The proper line is sharper: investigate credible threats, require the government to prove every element and refuse collective guilt. Institutional power belongs to the administration and prosecutors, not to a homeless man with alleged violent words. Coverage that obscures that hierarchy does more than sensationalize a criminal case. It helps power present itself as the victim while exercising the machinery of the state.
