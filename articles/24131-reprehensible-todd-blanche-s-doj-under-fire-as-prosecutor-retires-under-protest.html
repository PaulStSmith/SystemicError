'Reprehensible': Todd Blanche's DOJ under fire as prosecutor 'retires under protest'

The Justice Department’s Accountability Stops Below the Front Office

A Prosecutor Leaves Under Protest

Veteran federal prosecutor Sheri Mecklenburg retired before a judge considered whether to appoint an independent prosecutor to investigate alleged misconduct in the “Broadview Six” case. Mecklenburg acknowledged that she improperly vouched for the prosecution and spoke with two grand jurors outside the grand jury room, while denying that she improperly excused jurors. But in a letter reported by the Chicago Sun-Times, she also accused U.S. Attorney Andrew Boutros’s leadership team of directing the felony prosecution, inadequately defending her, and threatening discipline or termination if she filed a response on her own behalf. The Justice Department disputes her account of who recommended the felony charge.

Power Sat Above the Counsel Table

Mecklenburg’s admitted conduct deserves scrutiny. It does not, however, settle who controlled the prosecution.

Todd Blanche, then deputy attorney general, publicly announced the charges on October 29, 2025. Mecklenburg alleges that Boutros personally directed the felony case despite her objection that misdemeanor charges were more appropriate. The department says the opposite: that her written recommendation identified conspiracy as the strongest charge. That factual dispute is unresolved in the reporting.

What is not disputed is the institutional hierarchy. Line prosecutors do not set national immigration policy, direct presidential crackdowns, issue department-wide charging priorities, or command a U.S. attorney’s office. Blanche and Boutros possessed the authority. If the prosecution reflected bad judgment beyond Mecklenburg’s conceded errors, responsibility cannot credibly end with the subordinate who appeared before the grand jury.

Silence Is Not Accountability

According to Mecklenburg, a senior official initially saw no problem with her citing a Justice Department manual in a court filing, then reversed course after consulting Boutros’s leadership team. She says she was warned that filing anything in her own defense as a department employee could bring discipline or termination. She retired, she wrote, because doing so was the only way to preserve healthcare benefits earned over decades.

Those are Mecklenburg’s allegations, not established findings. But they identify the central institutional question: whether Justice Department leaders used their employment power to prevent a subordinate from presenting an account that might distribute responsibility upward.

Boutros’s office told Judge April Perry that Mecklenburg did not deserve sanctions. Mecklenburg called that defense late and incomplete. If her account is accurate, leadership tried to occupy both positions at once—assuring the court that she should not be punished while denying her the ability to explain why. That is bureaucratic self-protection dressed as personnel management.

The Convenient Shape of a Scapegoat

Mecklenburg had withdrawn from the case by February and says she learned through media coverage about the May 21 hearing at which Perry rebuked prosecutors over apparent grand-jury misconduct. Boutros then dropped the remaining charges and accepted responsibility in court, according to Capitol News Illinois. Mecklenburg alleges that he later sent an office-wide email assigning responsibility to her.

That sequence does not prove she was scapegoated. Her admitted conduct supplies a legitimate basis for criticism. But scapegoating does not require an innocent target; it requires concentrating institutional failure on someone with less power while insulating those who authorized, supervised, or publicly promoted the underlying operation.

The article’s opening framing—that Mecklenburg crossed the line “under pressure from Trump and Blanche”—goes further than the reported facts clearly establish. The supplied reporting connects the prosecution to an administration crackdown and records Blanche’s public announcement, but it does not demonstrate that either man directed her specific grand-jury conduct. The stronger, supportable indictment is institutional: senior officials owned the policy, hierarchy, and public presentation, while the fiercest consequences appear to have landed lower down.

A Crackdown Needs Prosecutors—and Cover

The six protesters were charged with impeding a federal agent outside an immigration facility during Operation Midway Blitz. President Donald Trump had previewed the operation with militarized “Department of WAR” rhetoric, and Blanche declared when announcing the charges that nobody had the right to obstruct law enforcement.

That political context matters. An administration that advertises immigration enforcement as spectacle creates pressure for prosecutors to convert confrontation into exemplary punishment. The reporting does not establish that the felony case was ordered for propaganda purposes. It does show that senior leadership publicly attached itself to the prosecution when charges conveyed strength, then faced a struggle over responsibility after judicial scrutiny and dismissal.

The political use of prosecution does not require an explicit memo saying, “Make an example of them.” It can operate through incentives: leaders announce aggressive enforcement, supervisors approve ambitious charges, and career employees absorb the legal and professional exposure when the case collapses.

Responsibility Flows Down; Authority Remains Above

The broader pattern is not confusion. It is asymmetric accountability.

Officials at the top control policy, charging culture, public messaging, employment, and the department’s response to the court. Subordinates control far less, yet their individual errors can be isolated from the command structure and presented as the whole failure. That arrangement preserves institutional power by separating authority from consequence.

Judge Perry’s consideration of an independent prosecutor matters because an institution cannot credibly investigate misconduct if its leaders can determine who speaks, who stays silent, and whose version reaches the court. Whether Mecklenburg’s account ultimately holds up is a question for evidence and adversarial review. The systemic error is already visible: the people empowered to direct a prosecution also possess the machinery to define where responsibility ends.
