Trump’s 'strategic breakthrough' with China smells like 'collapse: analysis

“War Room” co-host Natalie Winters noticed national headlines were eager to tout China’s decision to reduce its painful embargo against U.S. soybean farmers as a kind of “win.” But this was no win.

“Soybeans are a solid proxy for how the United States deals with China: small, transactional, immediately legible ‘wins’ pitched as strategic breakthroughs. A shipment resumes, a statistic ticks up, a headline declares progress. Meanwhile, Beijing quietly continues accumulating long-term advantages,” Winters writes in The American Conservative, adding that “Even after this supposed thaw, U.S. soybean exports to China remain down roughly 99 percent from historical levels. Despite all the hype, China is on track to register its lowest share of U.S. soybean exports since 2002.”

When President Donald Trump resumed power last year he immediately re-ignited his tariff trade war with China, sparking yet another soybean exclusion against U.S. farmers. The farmers, who largely voted to elect Trump to both his first and second terms, were furious that their top international buyer of soybeans now gets most of its supply from elsewhere due to Trump’s tariffs.

“This president sold America’s farmers out,” said farmer and rancher John Boyd, who is also the founder of the National Black Farmers Association.

Trump and Chinese leaders only recently hashed out a deal to loosen the Chinese lock-out of American soybeans, but Winters is astounded that administration officials and the press are flagging this as success.

“The soybean theory rests on a simple insight: America evaluates success through events, while China evaluates success through positioning. We ask whether something happened. They ask whether something became permanent,” said Winters. “China doesn’t need to win negotiations. It shapes the environment in which negotiations occur. That means controlling inputs, bottlenecks, and systems that persist regardless of who occupies office in Washington.”

In past negotiations China has pledged to purchase a certain percentage of U.S. goods and services, but in practice, China “never came close to meeting its commitment,” with independent tracking finding that Beijing fulfilled only about 58–60 percent of its promised purchases across 2020 and 2021.

“There were no meaningful penalties. The deal expired. The headlines moved on. China’s industrial assault on America continued,” said Winters, adding that, in any case, soybeans are merely a “symbolic gesture to absorb attention while larger, irreversible gains continue elsewhere.”

This includes China’s growing monopoly on rare earths, which Winters said accounts for “roughly 60 to 70 percent of global rare-earth mining and more than 85 to 90 percent of global processing and refining capacity, giving Beijing leverage at both the extraction and transformation stages.”

“While America negotiates deals and pushes unpopular cultural programs, China accumulates leverage. And through all of this, Washington keeps falling for the soybeans. Still,” said Winters. “Empires do not collapse because they fail to negotiate. They collapse because they lose the ability to distinguish between tactics and strategy, symbols and structure.”

“Nero played the fiddle while Rome burned,” Winters added. “America celebrates soybeans while the architecture of the world is quietly being rearranged.”

Our Analysis:

The Soybean Smokescreen: A Closer Look at U.S.-China Relations

In the grand theater of international politics, the U.S. celebration over China’s reduced embargo on American soybeans, as highlighted by Natalie Winters in The American Conservative, is akin to applauding a pyrrhic victory amidst a broader strategic defeat. This narrative is not just a story of agricultural trade but a metaphor for the systemic missteps in U.S. foreign policy towards China. Let’s dissect the illusion of victory and the reality of strategic oversight.

The Illusion of Victory

Winters astutely points out that the recent “win” for U.S. soybean farmers is hardly a triumph when viewed through a wider lens. Despite the fanfare, the reduction in China’s embargo is a drop in the ocean, or more accurately, a single soybean in a vast field. U.S. soybean exports to China remain catastrophically down by approximately 99 percent from historical levels. This stark statistic shatters any illusion of victory, revealing a mere token gesture rather than a significant breakthrough in trade relations.

Trump’s Tariff Troubles

The re-ignition of the tariff trade war under President Trump’s administration did not just reignite old conflicts; it arguably backfired against the very constituency it purported to protect: American farmers. The farmers’ frustration, as voiced by John Boyd of the National Black Farmers Association, underscores a bitter reality. The tariffs, intended as leverage against China, inadvertently pushed Beijing to diversify its soybean supply, diminishing U.S. market share. This outcome illustrates a critical misjudgment of China’s resilience and strategic flexibility.

China’s Long Game

The core of Winters’ argument lies in the distinction between America’s event-oriented success metrics and China’s positioning-focused strategy. While the U.S. may celebrate the resumption of soybean shipments as a victory, China is playing a longer, more sophisticated game. China’s approach to negotiations—shaping the environment, controlling critical resources, and focusing on long-term positioning—exposes a stark mismatch in strategic depth.

Falling Short on Follow-Through

The failure of China to meet its purchase commitments of U.S. goods and services, with no significant repercussions, is emblematic of a broader issue: the lack of meaningful enforcement mechanisms in trade agreements. This lapse allows China to continue its “industrial assault” on America, with soybeans serving merely as a distraction from more consequential arenas of competition, such as rare earths.

Rare Earths: The Real Battlefield

Winters’ mention of China’s dominance in the rare earths market is particularly alarming. With control over a significant portion of the world’s rare earth mining and processing capacity, China has secured a strategic advantage in a critical sector. This control grants Beijing considerable leverage in the global supply chain for technologies essential to the modern economy, from smartphones to electric vehicles.

Symbols vs. Structure

The soybean saga is emblematic of a larger issue: America’s failure to differentiate between tactical wins and strategic necessities. Empires falter not because they fail to negotiate but because they lose sight of the difference between symbolic victories and structural dominance. The celebration of minor trade adjustments while ignoring the strategic encroachment in critical sectors is akin to fiddling while Rome burns.

Conclusion: Beyond Soybeans

The narrative spun around the U.S.-China soybean saga is a microcosm of a larger strategic misalignment in American foreign policy. As Winters poignantly concludes, the focus on immediate, symbolic wins blinds Washington to the quiet rearchitecture of global power structures by Beijing. The true measure of success in international relations is not found in celebratory headlines but in the strategic positioning that determines long-term global influence. America must recalibrate its priorities, focusing less on the short-lived triumphs of soybean shipments and more on the broader chessboard of global power dynamics.
