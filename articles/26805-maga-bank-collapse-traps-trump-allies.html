MAGA bank collapse traps Trump allies

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 25, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet, citing New York Times reporting, says Georgia gubernatorial candidate Rick Jackson and Ohio gubernatorial candidate Vivek Ramaswamy invested in GloriFi, a MAGA-branded financial company that filed for bankruptcy months after launching its website. Its backers included right-wing billionaires Peter Thiel, Ken Griffin and Jeff Sprecher.

Jackson later invested millions more in the remains of GloriFi and now controls decisions about whom the bankrupt company sues, according to the Times. Former White House ethics counsel Norm Eisen warned that, if Jackson became governor, creditors could appear vulnerable to pressure from an official controlling litigation against them. Jackson’s campaign would not say whether he would relinquish that role if elected. Ramaswamy’s campaign did not answer the Times’ questions.

GloriFi marketed itself to consumers supposedly neglected by “liberal Wall Street,” with pitches built around faith, freedom and culture-war symbolism—including plans for credit cards made from metal used in shell casings. Its collapse left investors and Republican megadonors fighting through bankruptcy litigation.

The power sits with Jackson

The central political issue is not that two candidates made a bad investment. Investors lose money. The consequential fact is that Jackson reportedly acquired decision-making power over litigation involving wealthy creditors while seeking control of Georgia’s executive branch.

That arrangement would give the same person political authority and leverage in a private legal conflict. Eisen’s proposed remedy—an independent trustee and complete separation—is therefore not decorative ethics advice. It addresses the obvious danger created when a prospective governor retains the power to pursue or spare financially and politically connected people.

Jackson’s refusal, through his campaign, to say whether he would surrender that control leaves the conflict unresolved. Silence does not establish misconduct, but it withholds the most basic assurance that public office would be insulated from his private financial campaign.

A grievance brand met financial reality

GloriFi sold political identity as if it were a banking model. Its appeal rested on the claim that conservative consumers had been abandoned by liberal financial institutions. Candace Owens helped pitch the company; Republican donors and Trump allies supplied money and connections.

The company’s rapid bankruptcy matters because it exposes the hollowness of that proposition. Cultural resentment may attract customers and billionaire investors, but it does not substitute for a durable business. The shell-casing card was not merely a gimmick. It captured the enterprise’s priorities: partisan signaling presented as institutional competence.

The failure did not eliminate the underlying power network. It converted a culture-war venture into a contest among rich Republican actors over losses, lawsuits and control.

“Trapped” hides agency

The headline’s suggestion that the collapse “traps” Trump allies is too forgiving. Ramaswamy and Jackson were not powerless customers ensnared by an impersonal disaster. The Times identifies them as investors, and Jackson reportedly chose to commit millions more after the company had already failed.

That distinction is essential. Ramaswamy’s precise role beyond his investment is not established in the supplied reporting, and his campaign’s nonresponse does not prove wrongdoing. Jackson, however, reportedly pursued an additional strategy that placed him at the center of the litigation. He did not merely remain caught in the wreckage; he sought influence over how the wreckage would be divided.

Framing powerful investors as victims shifts attention from their choices to their discomfort. The weaker parties are the creditors subject to decisions made by someone who may soon wield state power.

The unanswered question is the story

Neither candidate’s campaign supplied the transparency the circumstances warranted. Ramaswamy’s campaign did not respond. Jackson’s campaign declined to say whether he would reduce or abandon his litigation role if elected.

Those omissions do not answer whether any legal or ethical violation occurred. They do show that the candidates were unwilling to resolve foreseeable concerns created by their private interests. The relevant standard is not whether allies promise they can manage overlapping loyalties. It is whether the overlap is removed before public authority makes it dangerous.

Politics as a private asset

GloriFi reflects a larger pattern in which partisan identity becomes a commercial product, wealthy political networks finance it, and public ambition follows the money into the aftermath. When the venture succeeds, its promoters sell grievance. When it fails, the same network battles over control—and candidates ask voters to treat those battles as irrelevant to governing.

They are not irrelevant. A governor commands appointments, enforcement priorities and access to state power. A candidate who simultaneously controls litigation involving politically connected creditors carries a private web of incentives toward public office. The systemic error is allowing that entanglement to be treated as colorful campaign baggage rather than a problem of institutional power.
