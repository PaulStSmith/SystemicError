Disagreements rage as White House contemplates risky strikes in country with Russian ties

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

Trump’s Eighth War and the Usual Lie of “Counterterrorism”

The Story, Briefly

The Washington Post reports that the Trump administration is weighing military strikes in Mali against JNIM, an al-Qaeda-aligned group that has expanded across West Africa. Sebastian Gorka is reportedly pushing for action while others urge caution. If approved, Mali would become the eighth country where Trump has authorized military operations, despite his campaign promise to end America’s endless wars.

Power Is the Point

The real actor here is not JNIM. It is the White House, which controls the force package, the authorization, and the political cover. A militant group can threaten a region, but only an American president can decide to turn that threat into another open-ended U.S. military role. The administration is not being “pulled” into Mali by events. It is choosing whether to widen the war map.

That choice matters because Trump has already normalized military escalation while pretending to stand against it. The article notes that this would be his eighth nation for military operations. That is not drift. It is a governing pattern.

The Old Trick: Rebrand War as Prudence

The administration’s internal split is useful theater. Gorka is the hawk, caution is the brake, and the president gets to appear as a reluctant judge between extremes. That framing hides the core fact: the decision structure itself is already tilted toward intervention. Once a counterterrorism director is “pushing hard” for strikes, the policy machine is working exactly as designed.

The language of “options” and “debate” also launders responsibility. No one wants to say the obvious: the U.S. security state keeps generating new justifications for new violence, then packages them as sober management.

Mali Is Not a Blank Slate

The source does at least name the other forces on the ground: Mali’s junta and the Russian mercenaries it hired. That detail matters because it punctures the fantasy that U.S. missiles would enter a clean, controllable battlefield. They would enter a messy contest among armed factions, foreign proxies, and a local regime that is itself part of the problem.

The article also notes that direct U.S. involvement could create hostilities with Russian forces and require deconfliction efforts like Syria. That is not a footnote. It is a warning that Washington is contemplating another arena where escalation can outrun intent. Calling that “counterterrorism” is a euphemism for risk management in someone else’s country.

The Base Is Not the Target

Trump’s political problem is not that he has become too restrained. It is that he is doing what he always does: promising an antiwar posture, then preserving the empire’s machinery once in office. His Iran war has already damaged him with his own base, and another intervention would deepen the split. But that political cost is exactly why the story matters. Even when the president is weakened, the military option remains available, and the national-security class keeps pressing it.

That is the institutional truth here. The damage is not accidental. It is produced by a presidency that treats war as a default executive instrument and by advisers who can always find a threat that justifies one more strike.

The Pattern

This is what U.S. power looks like when stripped of its slogans: a government that claims to reject endless war while steadily extending it, a political press that frames expansion as debate, and a security apparatus that converts chaos abroad into another opportunity for force. Mali is not being “pulled into” Trump’s orbit. It is being considered as the next site where American officials can translate instability into authority.

The larger pattern is not confusion. It is imperial habit with better branding.
