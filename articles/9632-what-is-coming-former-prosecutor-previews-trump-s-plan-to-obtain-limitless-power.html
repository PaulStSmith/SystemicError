'What is coming': Former prosecutor previews Trump's plan to obtain 'limitless power'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 13, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Donald Trump (Gage Skidmore)

A Donald Trump aide recently hinted at the president’s plan to obtain “limitless power,” according to a former prosecutor this weekend.

Ex-federal prosecutor Joyce Vance on Saturday called “When they Bukele the Courts,” in which she talks about Trump following on the path of Nayib Bukele, the President of El Salvador. Bukele previously said, “If you don’t impeach the corrupt judges, you CANNOT fix the country. They will form a cartel (a judicial dictatorship) and block all reforms, protecting the systemic corruption that put them in their seats.”

According to Vance, Trump is following this path of threatening judicial appointees for a reason.

“Why is it important to threaten and intimidate the courts? Because of what is coming. We talk a lot about the audience of one. There’s also an audience of nine, or put more rightly, an audience of six, that must be persuaded to continue to side with the president. As the administration seems to increasingly focus on invoking the Insurrection Act, keeping the Court on its side will be of utmost importance,” she wrote in reference to the Supreme Court.

Vance added, “Responding to a question about the legality of deploying National Guard soldiers to Portland, Oregon, last week, Stephen Miller began to respond that ‘the president has plenary authority’ to do so. He was cut off, either by a technical glitch or some recognition that he was about to say the quiet part out loud, because plenary authority means unlimited power to act.”

“That’s what the Trump administration is advocating for: a unitary presidency that no court has the power to second-guess, which effectively means limitless power,” she said. “That’s a good reason to try to Bukele the courts if you’re the president.”

Our Analysis:

The Slippery Slope to Unlimited Power: A Trump Administration Tale

In a recent revelation that seems ripped from the pages of a cautionary political thriller, we’re confronted with the chilling hint from a Donald Trump aide concerning the president’s aspirations for “limitless power.” This isn’t just a storyline fit for late-night TV drama; it’s a scenario unfolding in real-time, as dissected by ex-federal prosecutor Joyce Vance in her piece, “When they Bukele the Courts.”

Vance draws a parallel between Trump’s tactics and those of Nayib Bukele, the President of El Salvador, who has openly criticized his country’s judiciary. Bukele’s argument that corrupt judges are the linchpin of systemic corruption and must be removed to effectuate real change, is being mirrored in Trump’s approach towards the U.S. judiciary. This isn’t mere speculation. It’s a calculated move, as Vance posits, aimed at ensuring the judiciary remains a pliant tool in the administration’s quest for unchecked authority.

The Strategy: Intimidation and Persuasion

The strategy is as transparent as it is nefarious: threaten and intimidate the courts. Why? Vance answers this succinctly: because of what’s coming. The Trump administration’s increasing focus on invoking the Insurrection Act is a testament to its desire for a unitary presidency, unencumbered by judicial oversight or constitutional checks and balances. This isn’t just about persuading the “audience of nine” on the Supreme Court; it’s about ensuring that the “audience of six” remains firmly in the president’s corner.

Stephen Miller’s almost-admission of the president’s “plenary authority” to deploy National Guard soldiers to Portland, Oregon, is a glaring example of this strategy in action. The interruption—be it a technical glitch or a moment of realization that the veil was being lifted too high—prevented Miller from laying bare the administration’s true intent: a presidency with limitless power.

The Implications: A Judicial Dictatorship?

What Vance outlines is not just a critique; it’s a clarion call to recognize the path towards a judicial dictatorship that the Trump administration seems hell-bent on pursuing. If the courts can be “Bukeled,” or bent to the will of the executive, what stops the United States from descending into autocracy?

This isn’t about judicial reform or combating corruption within the judiciary; it’s about power—pure, unchecked, and boundless. The administration’s advocacy for a unitary presidency, where no court can question its actions, lays bare its desire for a power that is antithetical to the very foundation of American democracy.

Conclusion: A Wake-Up Call

Joyce Vance’s analysis is a wake-up call, urging us to see beyond the political maneuvering to the stark reality of an administration seeking to dismantle the checks and balances that safeguard our democracy. It’s a scenario where the courts are not just referees but players coerced onto one team, leaving the field dangerously tilted.

As we digest Vance’s insights, let’s remember that this isn’t merely a political debate—it’s a battle for the soul and future of American democracy. The administration’s attempt to “Bukele the courts” isn’t just a policy move; it’s a step towards an abyss from which there may be no return.
