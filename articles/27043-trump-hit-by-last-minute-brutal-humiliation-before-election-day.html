Trump hit by last-minute 'brutal' humiliation before Election Day

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 3, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower was on the ballotA verdict, not a humiliationThe limits of the Trump explanationThe systemic error

What happened

AlterNet reports that the “humiliation” was a set of New York Times/Siena College polls showing Democratic Senate candidates ahead in seven battleground states on the Monday before Election Day. The label came from the Daily Beast’s description of the results as a “brutal outlook” for President Donald Trump and the Republican Party.

The reported leads ranged from two points in Alaska to eleven in Michigan. Across the seven states, 38 percent of likely voters approved of Trump’s presidential performance, while respondents favored Democrats on living costs, foreign wars, and the interests of ordinary people. These were polling results, not election results; the article supplies neither methodology nor evidence that Trump alone caused every Republican deficit.

Power was on the ballot

The institutional prize was control of the Senate and, with it, the capacity to constrain or reinforce Trump during the final two years of his presidency. Voters had agency, but candidates, party organizations, donors, and the president possessed the machinery: money, endorsements, rallies, and national messaging.

Trump exercised that power conspicuously. AlterNet says he campaigned for Ken Paxton in Texas, appeared for Ashley Hinson in Iowa, backed Dan Sullivan in Alaska, and held a Republican midterm convention in Dallas. If those candidates were losing ground, the relevant political fact was not that Trump suffered embarrassment. It was that presidential influence had failed to produce public consent.

A verdict, not a humiliation

Calling adverse polling a personal “humiliation” reduces an institutional contest to Trump’s emotional weather. The reported stakes were Senate power, representation, and public judgment about government performance. None of those is clarified by imagining the president embarrassed.

The polling instead suggested a measurable rejection of the governing party’s offer in those states. Respondents reportedly trusted Democrats more on the cost of living, foreign wars, and ordinary people’s interests. Those are assessments of material conditions and governing competence, not merely expressions of distaste for one personality.

The limits of the Trump explanation

AlterNet presents Trump as the common drag on Republican candidates, and the 38 percent approval figure supports that interpretation. But the article also acknowledges that the candidates had distinct weaknesses. It does not establish how much of each polling deficit came from Trump, the nominees, state conditions, or other factors.

That distinction matters because personalization can become a convenient escape hatch for the party around him. Paxton, Hinson, Sullivan, Roger Marshall, Mike Rogers, Jon Husted, and Susan Collins were not passive objects upon whom Trump inflicted polling numbers. They were candidates seeking power under the Republican banner. Their campaigns and records remained their own responsibility.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is a press habit of translating democratic accountability into palace drama. A possible transfer of Senate power becomes a president’s “brutal humiliation”; voters become instruments “taking it out” on his candidates; policy judgments become supporting details in a story about one man’s fortunes.

That framing reverses the hierarchy. Trump’s feelings were politically irrelevant. The consequential actors were voters deciding who would wield legislative authority, and candidates asking to exercise it. Polls could only estimate that decision, not settle it. The systemic error is treating power as personality—and then treating the public’s judgment about power as spectacle.
