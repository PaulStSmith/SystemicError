Bound By Amber

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 20, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

I had this thought while I was writing the piece about JD Vance.

It didn’t arrive fully formed. It crept in sideways, nagging at the edges of the argument. We were dissecting context collapse, grievance politics, the weaponization of old words—and suddenly the deeper question forced itself forward:

Will we always be bound by our past?

Not metaphorically. Literally.

We live in an age that captures data the way amber captures insects. Perfectly preserved. Suspended forever. A post you wrote in a Pokémon forum at twelve—half-formed, impulsive, written by a child who hadn’t yet learned restraint or irony—can be exhumed when you’re fifty and treated as moral evidence. Not as a curiosity. Not as a developmental artifact. As an indictment.

That realization should chill anyone who believes growth is real.

Because if we are bound permanently to our past selves, then growth is a lie. Evolution is cosmetic. Redemption is theater. You are not a process—you are a snapshot.

And snapshots are cruel.

Human Time vs. Machine Time

Human morality evolved under conditions of forgetting. Memory faded. Records decayed. Distance softened judgment. This wasn’t a flaw—it was a feature. It created space for maturation. A person who said something foolish at twenty was not the same person at forty, and society largely understood that without needing to articulate it.

Machine memory has no such grace.

Digital systems do not forget. They do not contextualize. They do not age information. A tweet from 2009 is functionally identical to a press release from yesterday. The system assigns no moral weight to time, intent, or development. Everything is flattened into an eternal present.

That collapse is not neutral. It is violent.

It erases the most basic truth about being human: we change.

When we allow machine memory to dictate moral judgment, we are implicitly accepting a worldview in which personal evolution does not exist. Where learning does not mitigate error. Where apology does not matter. Where improvement is irrelevant.

What remains is not accountability, but permanence.

The End of Moral Arcs

Stories have arcs because people have arcs.

Every moral framework worth taking seriously—religious, philosophical, legal—has recognized this. Christianity has repentance. Judaism has teshuvah. Secular law distinguishes between juveniles and adults, between first offenses and patterns. Even the harshest systems concede that a person is not a static object.

The internet does not.

Online morality has no arcs. It has receipts.

A receipt is not a story. It has no beginning, middle, or end. It has no context. It does not care what came before or after. It exists only to prove that something once happened, and therefore must always matter.

This is why digital outrage feels so hollow and yet so devastating. It bypasses the question of who someone became and goes straight to who they once were—preferably at their least articulate, least informed, or most emotionally volatile.

In that framework, growth isn’t just ignored. It’s punished.

Punishing the Unfinished

There is something uniquely cruel about holding adults morally hostage to their adolescent or early-adult selves.

Children experiment with identity. Teenagers provoke. Young adults posture. That’s not moral failure—it’s development. We learn by saying stupid things and then realizing why they were stupid.

But digital permanence transforms that learning process into a liability. The very evidence of growth—past error—becomes the weapon used to deny it.

The message is clear: you should have known better, even when you couldn’t have.

That standard is not just unreasonable. It is inhuman.

It demands omniscience from children and moral perfection from the inexperienced. It retroactively criminalizes immaturity.

And once that logic takes hold, no one is safe. Not because everyone is secretly monstrous, but because everyone was once unfinished.

Power Loves Frozen People

This is where the politics enter.

A society that allows people to change is dangerous to entrenched power. Change implies unpredictability. It means alliances shift, ideas evolve, hierarchies weaken.

Frozen people, by contrast, are easy to manage.

If every individual can be reduced to a permanent moral record, then politics becomes prosecutorial rather than deliberative. You don’t argue against ideas—you disqualify people. You don’t debate outcomes—you excavate archives.

This is why bad-faith actors love screenshots. They replace engagement with accusation. They turn time into a trap.

The irony is that this tactic masquerades as moral seriousness while hollowing morality out entirely. Ethics becomes forensic. Judgment becomes algorithmic.

And humanity becomes collateral damage.

The Self as Evidence Locker

Under this regime, a person is no longer a self. They are a searchable database.

Your past statements are no longer memories—they are exhibits. Your old jokes are not mistakes—they are tells. Your evolution is irrelevant unless it can be proven with documentation more exhaustive than the accusation.

This creates a culture of defensive living. People learn not to think freely, but to think safely. Not to speak honestly, but to speak blandly. Curiosity becomes dangerous. Risk becomes foolish.

What emerges is not a more ethical society, but a more timid one.

And timid societies do not progress. They stagnate.

Why This Is Dehumanizing

To be human is to be in motion.

Strip that away, and what remains is a thing—a specimen. Something to be labeled, cataloged, and judged, but not understood.

When we deny the possibility of moral evolution, we deny personhood itself. We replace the idea of a living, changing being with a fixed moral barcode.

That is dehumanization in its purest form.

It doesn’t require slurs or violence. It only requires permanence without mercy.

Choosing to Remember Differently

The solution is not amnesia. It is proportion.

Past actions matter. Harm matters. Patterns matter. But time matters too. Growth matters. Context matters.

A society that cannot distinguish between who someone was and who they became is not protecting morality—it is destroying it.

We built machines that remember everything. The mistake was letting them teach us how to judge.

If we allow the past to become an unbreakable chain, then the future collapses inward. No one can become better, only quieter.

I don’t want to live in a world where the worst thing I ever said defines me forever. Not because I’m afraid of judgment—but because I believe, stubbornly, that people are more than their earliest drafts.

Without that belief, we are not a society.

We are an archive.
