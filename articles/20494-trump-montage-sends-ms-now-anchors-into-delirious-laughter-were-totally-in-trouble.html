Trump’s Justice Theater Depends on Memory Loss

The Source Material

MS NOW aired a montage of Donald Trump claiming his administration had ended political prosecutions and restored “fair” justice, right before discussion of Jack Smith describing the precautions he took because retaliation was a live possibility. Jonathan Capehart’s reaction was laughter, because the gap between the claim and the record is so obvious it barely qualifies as a dispute.

Power, Not Performance

The central fact is not that Trump says things. It is that Trump controls the machinery capable of turning his claims into action. That is the power structure. When a president talks about “fair” justice while the same presidency targets opponents and protects allies, the statement is not a policy description. It is a cover story issued from the top of the state.

The Lie Is the Point

The montage exposed the administration’s favorite trick: present domination as neutrality. Trump’s line about never weaponizing state power against opponents is not merely false in the abstract. It is a strategic inversion, meant to launder retaliation through the language of law. Jack Smith’s account matters because it shows the opposite reality: prosecutors and officials had to prepare for abuse from the executive branch itself.

Who Gets Miscast

The weaker actors in this story are not the targets, the commentators, or the people forced to describe the obvious. The weak actors are the institutions that keep acting as if this is a normal disagreement about standards rather than an attempt to convert law enforcement into personal leverage. The article’s framing, even in parody form, points at a larger failure: too much U.S. political coverage still treats deliberate authoritarian conduct as if it were confusion, contradiction, or mere bluster.

The Pattern Is Familiar

This is not just about one montage or one joke that nearly got someone “in trouble.” It is the old pattern of executive impunity: attack enemies, reward loyalists, then insist the whole system is impartial because the people in charge say so. That pattern does not survive on force alone. It survives on repetition, normalization, and the institutional habit of acting surprised when power behaves exactly as designed.

The Real Story

The real story is that Trumpism does not require consistency. It requires control. It can preach impartial justice while preparing retaliation, call itself lawful while bending institutions toward personal power, and count on too many people to translate that into a punchline instead of a charge sheet. That is the political meaning of the clip: not hypocrisy, but an operating manual for state power used as a private instrument.
