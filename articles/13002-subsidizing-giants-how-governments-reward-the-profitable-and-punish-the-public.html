Subsidizing Giants: How Governments Reward the Profitable and Punish the Public

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 7, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

In an age of unprecedented corporate profits, you might expect governments to redirect public resources toward addressing poverty, healthcare, infrastructure, or education. Instead, they continue funneling billions into the pockets of multinational conglomerates that are already raking in record-breaking profits. This is not just an economic contradiction—it's a systemic indictment of modern capitalism's marriage to state power. It is the story of how the rich get public welfare and the public gets austerity.

The Illusion of Necessity

Governments routinely justify subsidies to wealthy corporations under the guise of economic stability, job creation, and national interest. The argument is disarmingly simple: if Big Oil, Big Tech, Big Pharma, or Big Agriculture thrive, then everyone else supposedly benefits through job opportunities, innovation, and growth. But dig past the press releases and economic jargon, and you find a grotesque distortion of reality. These subsidies rarely trickle down. Instead, they reinforce monopolistic power, suppress wages, stifle innovation, and concentrate wealth at the top.

Take oil giants like ExxonMobil and Chevron. Even in years when they report windfall profits, they continue to receive generous tax breaks, grants, and research subsidies. The rationale? Energy security. But these same corporations often turn around and spend their gains on stock buybacks, dividend payouts, and lobbying to prevent climate regulations. Public money is being used not to secure energy for the masses but to secure shareholder wealth.

The Hostage Economy

One of the most insidious dynamics underpinning this system is the concept of corporate blackmail. Companies dangle the threat of relocating jobs or operations overseas unless they receive favorable tax treatment or direct subsidies. This hostage-taking is not subtle. It is baked into site selection processes for factories, data centers, or corporate headquarters.

Amazon's infamous "HQ2 Hunger Games" pitted American cities against one another in a frenzied race to offer the most extravagant incentives. The company, already worth hundreds of billions, extracted promises of tax breaks, infrastructure upgrades, and workforce training programs. All for the privilege of housing jobs that pay warehouse workers barely enough to survive.

This is not competition. This is coercion. And it turns the democratic principle of public accountability into a race to the bottom.

Campaign Contributions and Revolving Doors

The mechanism enabling this brazen wealth transfer is neither invisible nor complex. It is built on campaign finance systems that allow corporations to bankroll politicians who, in turn, reward them with favorable legislation and subsidies. This is not lobbying in the traditional sense; it is legalized bribery.

Many of the policymakers who approve subsidy packages later find themselves on corporate boards or in lucrative consulting gigs with the very firms they "regulated." The revolving door spins smoothly between Capitol Hill and Wall Street, between ministries and boardrooms.

This creates a governance system that doesn't merely tolerate corruption; it institutionalizes it. Decisions are not based on public interest but on donor ROI.

Subsidies for Billionaires, Austerity for the Rest

While governments hand out billions to profitable corporations, they tell the rest of us that there's "no money" for basic services. Public school budgets are slashed. Hospitals are closed. Infrastructure decays. Social safety nets shrink.

Meanwhile, companies like Boeing receive massive government contracts and subsidies even as they lay off workers and outsource manufacturing. Big Pharma reaps billions in taxpayer-funded research and development, only to charge those same taxpayers extortionate prices for the resulting medications. And tech giants like Apple and Google benefit from public investments in internet infrastructure, while using sophisticated accounting tricks to avoid paying their fair share in taxes.

This is a redistribution of wealth—not from the rich to the poor, but from the public to the ultra-rich.

The Myth of Free Market Capitalism

Free-market fundamentalists often decry "government interference," but their outrage is oddly selective. They howl at the idea of food stamps or healthcare subsidies for the poor, yet remain silent when trillion-dollar companies gorge themselves on public money.

The truth is that there is no such thing as a truly free market. Every market is shaped by laws, incentives, and regulations. The question is: who writes those rules, and for whose benefit? In our current system, it's abundantly clear who holds the pen.

A Call for Structural Change

This isn’t just a bad policy choice. It is a reflection of a broken system that prioritizes capital over people, donors over voters, and private profit over public good. Addressing this requires more than tweaking tax codes or increasing transparency.

We need structural reform:

  • Ban corporate subsidies to profitable firms.

  • Enact strict revolving door laws.

  • Institute public financing of elections.

  • Impose wealth taxes that claw back the public money siphoned into corporate coffers.

  • Empower workers and communities to have a say in how public funds are allocated.

Until these changes occur, we will continue living under a system where the government acts not as a steward of the people, but as an ATM for the already wealthy.

Final Word

When governments subsidize record-breaking profits, they are not making economic choices. They are making moral ones. They are choosing to uphold a system of economic apartheid, where the few feast and the many scramble for crumbs. This isn’t governance. It’s collusion.

And it must end.
