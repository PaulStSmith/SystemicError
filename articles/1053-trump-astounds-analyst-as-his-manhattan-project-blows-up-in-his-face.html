Trump astounds analyst as his 'Manhattan project' blows up in his face

Based on an article by Robert Davis

Presidential Vanity, Public Delay

A Brief Source Note

Donald Trump attended Game 3 of the NBA Finals at Madison Square Garden, became the first sitting U.S. president to do so, and was booed inside and outside the arena. The trip forced tighter security, pushed the Knicks’ watch party from outside the stadium to Bryant Park, and left some fans waiting more than two hours to get in.

Power, Not Personality

This is not a story about a man “loving sports.” It is a story about the occupant of the presidency using the state’s reach for a vanity appearance that imposed costs on everyone else. Trump held the actual institutional power. Fans did not choose the security regime, the access restrictions, or the disruption. He did.

That matters because political coverage too often treats this kind of stunt as quirky spectacle. It was not quirky. It was a demonstration of who gets to move through the city and who gets slowed down for it.

Who Paid

The price was paid by ordinary attendees and local organizers. The source says fans waited for upward of two hours, and that the team moved its public watch party away from the arena. That is the core fact pattern: public inconvenience downstream from presidential ego.

The injury is not only wasted time. It is the deeper insult of hierarchy made visible. The president arrived as a private spectator, but he carried public consequence with him. Everyone else absorbed the friction.

The Misdirection Problem

Brian Tyler Cohen’s criticism lands because it names the obvious flaw in Trump’s conduct: self-importance. But even that framing only partially captures the issue. The important question is not whether Trump “cares” about New Yorkers. It is that he had the power to impose his priorities on them anyway.

That distinction gets blurred all the time in political coverage. Deliberate inconvenience gets narrated like accidental fallout. Selfishness gets softened into temperament. The result is a false neutrality that disguises agency.

The Pattern

This is a familiar Trump pattern: maximize visibility, convert public office into personal theater, and let other people carry the disruption. The boos are not the point. The point is that the presidency was used as a shield for an unnecessary spectacle, while the costs were pushed onto fans, staff, and the city.

The larger political lesson is simple. Power does not always announce itself through policy. Sometimes it shows up as delay, inconvenience, security theater, and the expectation that everyone else will adapt.
