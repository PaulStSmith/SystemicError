“The Great Dictator” and the Small Men of Today

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 10, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

When Charlie Chaplin delivered his now-iconic speech at the climax of The Great Dictator in 1940, the world was already engulfed in fascism's flames. The Axis powers were not theoretical threats. They were goose-stepping in real-time, building camps, crushing dissent, and cloaking genocidal ambition in national pride. Chaplin, a comedian by trade, broke from satire to deliver something unflinchingly sincere: a raw, humanist rebuke of tyranny.

Fast forward to today, and the danger hasn’t vanished—it’s evolved. In the United States, the Republican Party, once a bastion of conservatism (flawed but grounded in principle), has morphed into a vessel for authoritarian nostalgia, white grievance politics, and open contempt for democratic norms. Donald Trump—twice-impeached, criminally indicted, and still the undisputed leader of the GOP—is no Hitler. But let’s not kid ourselves: his playbook is dog-eared from the same authoritarian handbook.

Chaplin warned us: "Greed has poisoned men's souls... goosestepped us into misery and bloodshed." Today, greed wears a suit and ties itself to tax cuts for billionaires, deregulation for polluters, and privatization of everything from prisons to schools. The soul-poisoning continues—it just has better PR.

"We think too much and feel too little," he said. Under Trump, the GOP has weaponized cynicism. Facts are optional. Empathy is weakness. Thought is suspect unless it flatters the party line. The cruelty isn't the bug, it's the feature. Immigrant children in cages, COVID downplayed for political gain, climate change dismissed as a hoax—it’s the same machinery Chaplin decried: "Machine men with machine minds and machine hearts."

The real horror is how effective it still is. Chaplin’s line—“Only the unloved hate. The unloved and the unnatural”—should sting. Trump’s cult is built on fear, resentment, and projection. Its engine runs on a fantasy of lost greatness, fueled by white anxiety and evangelical exceptionalism. Its love isn’t for country—it’s for domination.

Let’s not mince words: the GOP has become fascist-friendly. Not just in aesthetics, but in practice. They decry democracy when it doesn’t serve them. They flood local elections with conspiracy theorists. They storm the Capitol and call it patriotism. They target trans people, immigrants, educators, and anyone not safely within the straight, white, Christian mold.

"Dictators free themselves but they enslave the people." It’s a prophecy replaying in slow motion. Trump’s every move has been about consolidating power, evading accountability, and enriching himself. The GOP, far from resisting, has enabled him at every turn—gutting oversight, parroting propaganda, and purging dissenters.

But the most chilling part of Chaplin’s speech is also its most hopeful: "You the people have the power." That power has always existed, but it's squandered when fear trumps solidarity. Chaplin implored his audience to fight—not with weapons, but with unity, kindness, and reason. "Let us fight to free the world, to do away with greed, with hate and intolerance."

The question is whether we still have the courage to do so.

Chaplin's world was on fire. Ours smolders under the weight of apathy and disinformation. But the blueprint he offered remains. His words—delivered through the character of a Jewish barber mistaken for a dictator—resonate because they call us to reclaim our shared humanity. Not as a platitude. As a revolutionary act.

We must name the fascism when it rears its head—even when it wears a red hat.

We must reject leaders who glorify strength over justice, cruelty over compassion, and power over truth.

And we must remember: liberty does not perish unless we let it.

In the name of democracy, let us all unite.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.

Written and published by Paulo Santos for Systemic Error.
