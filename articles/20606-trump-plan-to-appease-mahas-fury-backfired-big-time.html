Trump plan to appease MAHA’s fury backfired big time

Based on an article by Ayurella Horn-Muller, Grist

Paulo SantosJuly 9, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

MAHA Was Never About Purity. It Was About Power.

The farmers were the audience

The source story circles around a familiar scene: farmers and MAHA voters who believed the Trump-RFK Jr. alliance would curb pesticides, only to watch the administration back Bayer, push glyphosate as strategically important, send government lawyers to defend the company at the Supreme Court, and then stage a regenerative-agriculture photo-op to blunt the backlash. That is the whole contradiction in one line, and the contradiction is the point.

Who actually had the power

The people with real institutional power were not the farmers, not the disappointed MAHA moms, and not the activists trying to police the movement’s brand. The power sat with the White House, HHS, EPA, and the legal apparatus the administration used to assist Bayer. Trump signed the executive order. Trump’s administration urged the Court to hear the case. Trump’s administration sent a lawyer to defend the company. The state did not stumble into this outcome. It produced it.

That matters because the story’s soft language makes it sound like a movement drifting out of alignment with its leadership. In reality, leadership is doing exactly what leadership does when it is captured by corporate power: it speaks in the language of reform while using federal authority to protect the industry it claims to be scrutinizing.

The public-relations shell game

The regenerative-agriculture executive order came after the Supreme Court ruling, and the timing was not accidental. It was a damage-control maneuver, a political deodorant sprayed over a chemical-policy stench. The order had no new funding and no new regulation. Experts in the piece say it does not change the equation. That is the substance. Everything else is theater.

Lundgren’s line that the administration needed “farmer faces” to “give the whole thing a spin” is the most honest quote in the story. It describes the actual mechanism of governance here: borrow legitimacy from independent growers, wrap corporate protection in pastoral branding, and hope the base confuses symbolism for policy. The administration was not building an anti-pesticide agenda. It was manufacturing consent for the opposite.

The blame is being pushed downward

The article correctly notes MAHA disillusionment, but it still gives too much room to the fantasy that voters were merely misled by inconsistency. The deeper problem is not voter confusion. It is the habit of turning deliberate policy choices into a personality flaw, a communications issue, or a “schizophrenic” moment. That framing lets the decision-makers off the hook.

The outcome after the Supreme Court ruling was not an accident, and neither was the administration’s posture toward Bayer. Tens of thousands of plaintiffs alleged cancer harms and warning failures. The Court’s ruling narrowed the legal pathways for recourse. That is a material victory for the company and a material defeat for the people living with the consequences. Calling that a messaging problem is a dodge. It is a political decision with bodily consequences.

The movement’s real fracture line

The MAHA coalition is revealing something larger than its own instability. It shows how quickly a supposedly anti-establishment food politics can be absorbed by the same administration it thought would serve it. The movement’s complaint is not that power is abstract. Its complaint is that power refused to obey the story it was sold.

That is why the “blue team or red team” language in the piece is so revealing. It treats partisan identity as the problem, when the real issue is which institutional bloc controls federal action. On pesticide policy, the bloc in charge made its choice. It chose industry protection, legal insulation, and symbolic consolation for the people it had just undercut.

The larger pattern

This is not a one-off contradiction. It is the governing style of contemporary right-wing populism: promise protection from elites, then use state power to defend the elite interests hiding behind national-security rhetoric and farm-friendly branding. The administration’s language of “health,” “resilience,” and “regeneration” functions as cover for maintaining the chemical regime that produces the harm in the first place.

That is the political meaning of the story. The farmers are not the authors of this policy. They are the props in it. The real plot is simpler: corporate agriculture got federal cover, the White House tried to launder the result through MAHA imagery, and the people with the least power were expected to applaud the insult.
