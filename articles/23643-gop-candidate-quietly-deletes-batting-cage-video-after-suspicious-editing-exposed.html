GOP candidate quietly deletes batting cage video after suspicious editing exposed

Based on an article by Matthew Chapman

The Swing Was Fake. The Distraction Was Real.

A Campaign Built for the Clip

Vivek Ramaswamy’s campaign posted—and then deleted—a batting-cage video after his awkward swings became a punchline. An eyewitness alleged that the footage recycled a few successful hits from roughly 25 pitches; the campaign offered no response. Democrats answered with parody videos, masculinity jokes, and a press release built around baseball puns.

That is the entire incident. Its political significance lies in what everyone chose to do with it.

The Candidate Controls the Edit

Ramaswamy is not a powerless victim of internet mockery. He is Ohio’s Republican gubernatorial nominee, tied in polling with Democrat Amy Acton and backed by a campaign operation capable of selecting, editing, and distributing flattering images of its candidate.

If the eyewitness account is accurate, the deception was small but deliberate: manufacture competence from repeated failure, publish the result, and count on the audience never seeing the raw material. The allegation remains unconfirmed, but the campaign’s silence and deletion leave it unanswered. Removing the evidence is not an explanation.

The relevant question is not whether Ramaswamy can hit a baseball. It is whether his campaign regards political communication as an exercise in creating synthetic proof of whatever persona it wants voters to buy.

Masculinity Is the Product

The video mattered because Republican politics routinely sells masculinity as a qualification for power. Candidates pose with guns, machinery, sports equipment, and staged displays of physical command because dominance is part of the brand.

That makes the failed performance revealing—but not in the way the jokes suggest. Weak batting form does not make Ramaswamy unfit for office. Treating contrived masculine imagery as evidence of leadership does make his campaign intellectually fraudulent.

The mockery accepts the premise it should dismantle. It argues that Ramaswamy performed masculinity badly instead of asking why voters are being sold masculinity in place of governing judgment.

Democrats Take the Bait

Ohio Democrats converted the episode into “Swing and a Miss!” messaging. David Pepper used it to attack Ramaswamy’s business record. Representative Chris Deluzio connected his own parody to private equity and youth-sports costs. Those are attempts to move toward substance, but the viral machinery still rewards the visual gag over the underlying critique.

Ramaswamy’s business record, policy agenda, and prospective control of Ohio’s executive branch carry consequences. His batting average does not. When opponents center the joke, they help political campaigns replace scrutiny with content.

The weaker actor here is not the nominee with a professional communications team. It is the electorate being offered an edited spectacle by one party and a meme response by the other.

Power Is Not a Batting Cage

A governor commands agencies, appointments, budgets, and enforcement power. Yet the coverage revolves around whether a candidate looked ridiculous while swinging a bat. That framing shrinks an election for control of state government into a dispute over authenticity in a campaign prop.

The alleged editing is relevant because it illustrates how the campaign handles evidence. But without connecting that behavior to the power Ramaswamy seeks, the story becomes celebrity embarrassment journalism: entertaining, disposable, and politically cheap.

The System Rewards the Decoy

Modern campaigns do not merely endure trivial coverage; they manufacture the conditions for it. They stage an image, opponents ridicule the image, and media coverage follows the conflict because humiliation travels faster than institutional analysis.

Everyone receives something. The candidate gets attention. The opposition gets engagement. Publishers get traffic. What disappears is the structure of the election itself: who wants state power, what they intend to do with it, and whose lives will absorb the consequences.

The deleted video may show a campaign embarrassed by a bad swing. The larger episode shows a political system perfectly comfortable editing power out of the frame.
