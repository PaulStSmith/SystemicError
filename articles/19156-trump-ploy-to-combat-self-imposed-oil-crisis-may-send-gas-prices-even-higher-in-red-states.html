Trump ploy to combat self-imposed oil crisis may send gas prices even higher in red states

The Trump administration revealed a plan on Wednesday to combat the surging oil costs sparked by its own war against Iran, but after running the numbers, one policy think tank said that the move would likely save Americans only pennies at the pump – and could even raise prices for some.

The plan is to impose a 60-day waiver to the Jones Act, a 1920 shipping law that mandates goods transported between U.S. ports be carried only on U.S.-built ships. The waiver, according to White House Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt, would help “mitigate the short-term disruptions to the oil market” and “allow vital resources like oil, natural gas, fertilizer, and coal to flow freely to U.S. ports,” NBC News reported Wednesday.

The public policy think tank Center for American Progress, however, estimated that waiving the Jones Act would only have “limited cost-savings benefits,” and in some cases, actually increase prices at the pump for Americans.

“The measure could have limited cost-savings benefits, decreasing East Coast gasoline prices by just three cents per gallon and could even raise costs on the Gulf Coast,” reads the think tank’s report published last week ahead of Trump’s announcement.

The Gulf Coast is made up of five states: Texas, Louisiana, Mississippi, Alabama, and Florida. All voted for Trump in the three presidential elections since 2016.

“It would also sideline American shipbuilders and workers and allow the oil industry to continue to profit from high prices while reducing transport costs.”

The Trump administration’s attack on Iran has led the Middle Eastern nation todeny accessto U.S.-aligned shipping vessels to the Strait of Hormuz, a crucial shipping route through which 20% of the world’s oil trade flows. Our Analysis:

Dissecting the Response to Surging Oil Costs Under the Trump Administration

The recent plan unveiled by the Trump administration to combat rising oil costs, ignited by its confrontational stance towards Iran, presents a complex scenario where political decision-making and economic consequences intertwine. Analyzing this situation requires a meticulous examination of who holds the power, the decision-making process, and the framing of the issue by various stakeholders.

Institutional Power and Decision-Making

At the heart of this narrative lies the Trump administration’s strategic decisions, both in foreign policy and domestic regulatory adjustments. The administration’s choice to engage in a conflict with Iran directly led to the escalation of oil prices, a result of Iran’s countermeasure to deny access to U.S.-aligned shipping vessels through the Strait of Hormuz. This move, impacting a significant portion of the world’s oil trade, underscores the administration’s central role in precipitating the crisis.

The decision to propose a 60-day waiver to the Jones Act—an attempt to alleviate the resultant economic pressure—is also solely within the administration’s purview. The Jones Act, a century-old maritime law, has long been debated, with critics arguing it leads to higher transportation costs. However, the waiver’s introduction as a solution to a problem of the administration’s own making highlights the critical role of executive decisions in shaping economic landscapes.

Analyzing the Outcome

The effectiveness of the waiver, as critiqued by the Center for American Progress, suggests that the proposed solution may not only be ineffectual but could potentially exacerbate the problem for certain regions. This critique draws attention to the nuanced impact of policy decisions on different parts of the country. The anticipated minimal decrease in gasoline prices on the East Coast, contrasted with potential price increases on the Gulf Coast, reveals the complexity of the oil supply chain and the limited influence of the Jones Act waiver on macroeconomic factors influencing oil prices.

Moreover, this policy move is critiqued for potentially sidelining American shipbuilders and workers, highlighting a scenario where the administration’s attempt to mitigate one problem potentially creates another, impacting a different sector of the domestic economy.

Framing and Responsibility

The framing of the administration’s plan by various actors—particularly the White House and its critics—paints a vivid picture of the contentious debate surrounding the decision. White House Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt’s portrayal of the waiver as a necessary measure to ensure the free flow of vital resources starkly contrasts with the Center for American Progress’s analysis, which underscores the limited benefits and potential drawbacks of the move.

This discrepancy in framing points to an attempt to shift responsibility away from the administration’s initial actions towards a regulatory measure with dubious efficacy. It’s essential to recognize that while the Jones Act waiver is presented as a solution, the root cause of the surging oil costs lies in the administration’s aggressive foreign policy stance.

Conclusion

The Trump administration’s response to the oil price surge, a direct consequence of its aggressive policies towards Iran, exemplifies the intricate interplay between political decisions and economic realities. The decision to engage Iran militarily and the subsequent proposal to waive the Jones Act as a countermeasure reveal a chain of causality rooted in executive action. The administration holds the institutional power and made the decisions leading to the current scenario, and the framing of the Jones Act waiver as a significant remedy misdirects responsibility and overlooks the broader economic implications of geopolitical conflict.

In the final analysis, the situation exposes the limitations of regulatory tweaks in addressing complex economic challenges, especially those born from contentious foreign policy moves. The critique offered by the Center for American Progress, while focused on the waiver’s effectiveness, inadvertently highlights a broader issue: the need for a more coherent and comprehensive approach to both foreign policy and domestic economic strategy, beyond short-term, reactionary measures.
