Trump's scorched-earth feud over Daylight Saving Time just turned personal

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosOctober 31, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower, not sunlightThe conflict the framing obscuresThe false choiceWho absorbs the costThe systemic error

What happened

According to AlterNet, Donald Trump escalated his campaign for permanent daylight saving time by publishing Senator Tom Cotton’s personal cellphone number after Cotton blocked the Sunshine Protection Act in the Senate. Trump urged people to contact Cotton and argued that later evening light would benefit golf, tennis, hunting, farming, and PGA Tour events.

The House had passed the measure 308–117 on July 14, although the report does not specify the year. Cotton defended his opposition by noting that previous experiments with permanent daylight saving time were repealed after the public experienced extremely late winter sunrises.

AlterNet connects Trump’s position to his golf holdings, reporting that he earned nearly $395 million from activity at 16 courses during the previous year. That financial overlap is relevant, but the article does not establish that personal profit caused his position.

Power, not sunlight

Congress holds the power to set the national clock regime. Trump can pressure lawmakers and mobilize supporters, but senators determine whether the House bill advances. Cotton exercised that institutional authority by preventing a vote or passage, according to the report.

Publishing a senator’s private number turns a policy disagreement into targeted personal pressure. Whatever Cotton’s broader politics, the relevant conduct here is straightforward: he used Senate power to obstruct a bill, and Trump answered by directing public attention toward him personally. That tactic substitutes intimidation and mass harassment risk for an argument on the merits.

The conflict the framing obscures

AlterNet portrays this primarily as Trump sacrificing children for golf revenue. The evidence supplied supports a conflict-of-interest concern, not a proven account of motive. Trump owns golf properties, reported substantial golf-related income, and publicly cited golfers and the PGA Tour as beneficiaries. Those facts warrant scrutiny. They do not, by themselves, prove that enriching his businesses drove the campaign.

The article’s strongest case does not require that leap. A politically powerful businessman is advocating a national policy that could benefit his industry while publicly targeting the senator blocking it. The failure is institutional: lawmakers considering the measure must evaluate public-health evidence and private financial interests, rather than treating presidential pressure as a substitute for either.

The false choice

The public is not limited to permanent daylight saving time or disruptive clock changes forever. The research cited by AlterNet distinguishes permanent daylight time from permanent standard time.

The Canadian Medical Association Journal analysis described morning light as the central signal aligning biological clocks. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine likewise favored standard time, according to the report. Data cited in the article associated later sunrise conditions with reduced sleep, poorer mood, lower productivity, and weaker school performance. Research from K12 estimated that permanent daylight time would leave students commuting in darkness every winter morning in 94 of the country’s 100 largest school districts.

That distinction matters because the slogan “stop changing the clocks” conceals the consequential decision: which clock becomes permanent. A popular complaint about seasonal switching can be used to sell the option friendliest to evening recreation while obscuring the option better aligned with morning light.

Who absorbs the cost

The benefits described are concentrated: more usable evening hours for golf courses, sports organizations, and other recreation businesses. The foreseeable burdens are dispersed among children traveling before sunrise, workers starting early shifts, and drivers operating under greater sleep disruption.

AlterNet cites a 6 percent increase in traffic deaths during the week after the spring clock change, a 24 percent increase in heart attacks on the following morning in one hospital study, and higher injury rates among miners after the transition. Those findings support ending the twice-yearly disruption. They do not establish that permanent daylight time is the safe remedy; the evidence presented points toward permanent standard time instead.

The systemic error

This is how a narrow commercial preference acquires the costume of universal convenience. Industry gains are specific and monetizable. Health, sleep, school performance, and morning safety are fragmented across millions of people who have no comparable lobbying apparatus.

The personal attack on Cotton is not the central policy fact, but it reveals the governing method: concentrate pressure on the institutional obstacle while simplifying the choice offered to everyone else. The deeper failure would be Congress allowing that pressure—and an appealing slogan about ending clock changes—to erase the real question of who receives the extra light and who is sent into the dark.
