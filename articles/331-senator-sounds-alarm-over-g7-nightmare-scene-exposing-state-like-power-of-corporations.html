Senator sounds alarm over G7 'nightmare scene' exposing 'state-like power' of corporations

Based on an article by David McAfee

The Table Was the Story

The Photo That Confessed the Crime

At this week’s G7 summit, chief executives of major AI companies sat alongside presidents and prime ministers. Sen. Chris Murphy called it “the nightmare scene” and warned that governments are letting firms with “state-like power” move from the lobby into the room where power is formally exercised. He floated responses that match the scale of the problem: ownership stakes, breakup, or hard regulation.

Who Actually Has Power

The answer is not the elected officials posing as controllers. The article itself shows the real leverage sitting with corporate executives whose systems can shape economies, information, and security at a scale once reserved for states. That is the whole problem. When private firms are seated as equals to heads of government, the fiction of democratic supremacy becomes stage dressing.

The Misdirection Is the Point

The soft framing here is not accidental. Coverage of this kind usually treats corporate capture as a matter of “influence,” as if access were the issue. It is not. Access is the symptom. The substance is that governments are granting legitimacy and deference to companies whose power they supposedly regulate. Calling this a “cozy relationship” underplays the asymmetry. One side writes policy in public; the other bends policy in private and shows up at the summit table to collect the result.

Decision-Makers and Enablers

Murphy is right to identify the executives as central actors, but the enabling force is institutional cowardice. Governments do not accidentally seat industry leaders next to heads of state. They choose to do it. They choose spectacle over accountability and partnership theater over confrontation. That is how private power becomes normal: not by conquest, but by invitation.

State-Like Power, Private Control

Murphy’s language matters because it names the core contradiction. If these companies now exercise state-like power, then treating them like ordinary businesses is a lie that protects them from democratic control. Ownership shares, breakups, and regulation are not radical in this context. They are the minimum tools available once a handful of firms can influence public life at a level that rivals governments themselves.

The Pattern Beneath the Summit

The deeper pattern is familiar: public institutions absorb private power, then describe the arrangement as modernization. First the corporation becomes indispensable. Then it becomes unavoidable. Then it becomes respectable enough to sit at the table. By the time officials admit the imbalance, the imbalance has already been translated into protocol. The summit photo was not a slip. It was the visual record of a political order that has already started to govern on corporate terms.
