Trump’s morning rant continues with series of rage attacks on GOP and Dems

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 13, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power is in the officeBlame as a loyalty testCorruption without particularsThe spectacle obscures the programGovernment as partisan possession

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump spent more than two hours Thursday morning posting attacks on Democrats and Republicans after GOP losses in the 2026 midterms. He blamed the results on his absence from the ballot, accused Democrats of corruption and cheating without elaborating, revived warnings about migrant “caravans,” and faulted Senate Republicans for preserving the filibuster rather than passing the SAVE Act.

Trump also repeated a misleading account of the congressional effort to obtain his tax returns. He claimed investigators “found nothing,” while the inquiry found that the IRS had largely failed to follow its mandatory presidential-audit policy during his first administration. The returns showed little federal income tax paid in several years and none in 2020, though that alone does not establish fraud.

Later, Trump urged Vice President JD Vance to target Democratic-run cities and states, alleged that billions of dollars were being stolen, and abruptly invoked the Iran war and the Strait of Hormuz. AlterNet reports that he provided no details supporting the sweeping corruption claims quoted in the article.

The power is in the office

This was not merely an aggrieved partisan posting through a bad morning. Trump holds the presidency. Vance holds the vice presidency. Senate Republicans control—or, according to Trump’s own taunt after the reported losses, recently lost—legislative power. When the president tells his deputy to “target” jurisdictions governed by the opposing party, the institutional imbalance matters more than the rant’s disordered presentation.

Democratic governors and city officials are not powerless, but they do not command the federal executive branch. Trump does. Treating his statements chiefly as emotional spectacle risks concealing the relevant fact: a president was publicly identifying rival jurisdictions as appropriate objects of federal attention while offering sweeping criminal accusations against their leaders.

Blame as a loyalty test

Trump assigned responsibility everywhere except to himself. Democrats supposedly cheated. Migrant caravans returned as an electoral warning. Senate Republicans supposedly committed a historic error by refusing to eliminate the filibuster. Voters were told for months to behave as though Trump were on the ballot; after the losses, his absence became the excuse.

That contradiction serves a political function. Trump claims personal ownership of Republican enthusiasm when it produces victories and detaches himself from the party when it loses. Republican officials are reduced to instruments whose competence is measured by whether they convert his demands into law.

His message to John Thune and the remaining GOP senators—congratulations on another year of leadership, “Minority though it may be”—was not analysis. It was discipline by humiliation. Trump was telling Republican officeholders that failure belongs to them, while authority within the party remains his.

Corruption without particulars

Trump’s accusations against “Blue States” were maximal and nonspecific: billions stolen every month, criminality embedded in Democratic leadership, corruption sufficient to balance the federal budget if eliminated. The article reports no explanation for those figures or identification of the alleged theft.

This is accusation used as political infrastructure. The charge does not need to resolve into a case; its purpose is to establish that opposition government is inherently illegitimate. Once that premise is repeated often enough, investigations, funding threats, and selective enforcement can be presented as routine cleanup rather than partisan retaliation.

The tax-return grievance follows the same pattern. Congressional scrutiny becomes persecution, while the documented failure of the IRS to apply its presidential-audit policy recedes from view. Oversight of Trump is cast as corruption; unsupported allegations by Trump are cast as self-evident truth.

The spectacle obscures the program

AlterNet’s emphasis on “rants,” “rage attacks,” and the half-hour rhythm of Trump’s posts captures the behavior but weakens the political frame. Anger is the presentation. The program underneath it is clearer: delegitimize election losses, revive immigration fear, pressure senators to dismantle procedural restraints, discredit oversight, and direct federal suspicion toward opposition-led governments.

Calling this confusion would be a category error. The transitions were erratic, but the distribution of blame was consistent. Trump absolved himself, criminalized Democrats, menaced weaker targets, and punished insufficiently obedient Republicans. Disorder in expression does not mean absence of purpose or consequence.

Government as partisan possession

The larger pattern is the conversion of public authority into a loyalty system. Institutions are legitimate when they protect Trump, elections are credible when his party wins, oversight is lawful when aimed at opponents, and federal power is properly used when it disciplines jurisdictions outside his coalition.

That is the systemic error: coverage can become so absorbed by the president’s temperament that it understates the machinery available to him. The consequential fact is not that Trump posted furiously before breakfast. It is that the person making these accusations controls the executive branch, names political enemies as targets, and pressures his party to remove institutional barriers standing between his grievance and government action.
