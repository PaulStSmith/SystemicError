Judge nails glaring omission in brutal ruling against Trump DOJ

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 27, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

A federal judge ruled that President Donald Trump’s Justice Department illegally fired career prosecutor Maurene Comey in July 2025. AlterNet reports that U.S. District Judge Jesse Furman ordered her retroactively reinstated through December 20, 2025—the period before she accepted another job—and awarded back pay.

Comey received no advance notice, opportunity to respond, or explanation tied to her conduct. The government conceded that neither performance nor cause prompted her dismissal. Furman found that the removal violated the Civil Service Reform Act, separation-of-powers principles, and Comey’s Fifth Amendment due-process rights. But the glaring omission remained: the record did not establish who in Washington actually made the decision. The ruling also did not find that Comey was fired in retaliation for her father, former FBI Director James Comey.

Power without a name

The institutional power here belonged to Justice Department leadership. Furman held that the attorney general may remove assistant U.S. attorneys, but must exercise that authority within federal employment law. The administration instead claimed that presidential power under Article II displaced those protections—a position Furman said contradicted nearly two centuries of Supreme Court precedent.

Yet the individual decisionmaker disappeared behind the institution. Manhattan officials reportedly told Comey only that the order came from Washington, while the complaint itself named no responsible official. That absence matters because obscured authorship makes accountability harder even when a court can identify the unlawful act.

A firing in search of a justification

Comey had spent nearly a decade as a career prosecutor, and Furman noted that supervisors and colleagues had consistently praised her work. The dismissal email cited constitutional authority but supplied no performance failure, misconduct, or other cause.

That does not prove the retaliatory explanation Comey alleged. Trump supporters had publicly demanded her removal, and her family connection made the firing politically conspicuous, but Furman did not determine that retaliation was the reason. What the record does establish is narrower and damning enough: officials exercised coercive personnel power without the process the law required and without identifying a lawful basis.

The misdirection of executive supremacy

The Justice Department tried to turn a concrete employment violation into a sweeping constitutional entitlement. That framing shifts attention from what officials did to what they claimed the president could theoretically do.

The weaker actor was a career employee denied notice and a chance to respond. The stronger actor was an executive department invoking presidential authority to escape statutory restraints. Presenting that clash as uncertainty about the boundaries of Article II sanitizes the conduct. Furman’s ruling says the relevant boundary was already written into law.

Accountability stops at the institution

The remedy corrected part of the material harm: back pay and retroactive reinstatement for the period before Comey found other employment. It did not reveal who ordered the firing, why that person did so, or whether anyone inside the department resisted an action the court later found unlawful.

That gap is politically useful to officials. Institutions can act through emails, chains of command, and unnamed Washington authorities while personal responsibility dissolves into bureaucracy. A court may reverse the result, but anonymity protects the decision-making structure that produced it.

The larger pattern

This case shows how executive overreach can operate without dramatic declarations. An administration removes a protected employee, withholds a reason, denies ordinary process, and then argues that presidential power places the act beyond Congress’s limits.

The danger in that structure is not confined to one prosecutor. Career protections exist to prevent public institutions from becoming instruments of personal or factional retaliation. The ruling blocked one unlawful dismissal. The concealed decisionmaker and the maximalist claim of presidential authority reveal the deeper systemic error: power was asserted expansively, responsibility was assigned nowhere, and the burden of enforcing the law fell on the person the government had already harmed.
