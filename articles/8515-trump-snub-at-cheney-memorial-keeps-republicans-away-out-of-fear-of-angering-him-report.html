Trump snub at Cheney memorial keeps Republicans away 'out of fear of angering' him: report

In a time of deep partisan divisions, Thursday’s memorial service for former Vice President Dick Cheney will be revealing — not for who does show up, but for who does not, reports Peter Baker in The New York Times.

Cheney, who died earlier in November at the age of 84, was the architect of America’s “aggressive response to terrorism” after September 11, 2001, and then “championed the invasion of Iraq, making him a hero to Republicans and a villain to many Democrats who thought he went too far,” Baker writes.

But it was Cheney’s late-in-life decision to join his daughter and former Wyoming Republican representative Liz Cheney in speaking out and eventually voting against President Donald Trump that “left him isolated from his own party and re-examined by some of his most vocal critics.”

Former president George W. Bush, under whom Cheney served as VP, will deliver the eulogy at Thursday’s service at the Washington National Cathedral, and former President Joseph R. Biden Jr., who “assailed Mr. Cheney in a successful campaign to succeed him, will attend to honor his predecessor,” Baker writes.

“So will former House Speakers John A. Boehner, a reliable Republican ally during the Bush-Cheney years, and Nancy Pelosi, a staunch Democratic adversary at the time,” he adds.

Also expected to be in attendance: former Vice President Kamala Harris, a year after “Mr. Cheney stunned many Americans by announcing that he would vote for her against Mr. Trump,” Baker writes.

“In our nation’s 248-year history, there has never been an individual who is a greater threat to our republic than Donald Trump,” Cheney said, adding that because of Trump’s “lies and violence,” he could “never be trusted with power again.”

“We have a duty to put country above partisanship to defend our Constitution. That is why I will be casting my vote for Vice President Kamala Harris,” Cheney said.

Three other former vice presidents will be on hand as well: Al Gore, a Democrat, and Mike Pence and Dan Quayle, both Republicans.

Conspicuously left off the list: Trump, which, Baker notes, “is hardly a surprise.” Vice President JD Vance, however, was invited, but hasn’t said if he would attend even though it’s custom for the sitting VP to “honor the service of someone who once held the same office,” Baker notes.

“Trump ordered flags to be lowered to half-staff after Mr. Cheney’s death, as required by law, but issued no statement and has refrained from commenting publicly,” Baker says.

Other Trump administration officials are also staying away, as are those “incumbent Republican members of Congress who still want a political future may stay away as well, out of fear of angering Mr. Trump, who considers Ms. Cheney one of his chief nemeses,” Baker writes.

“Former Presidents Bill Clinton and Barack Obama are not planning to attend, although both released respectful statements after Mr. Cheney’s death praising his service and patriotism,” Baker notes.

Following Cheney’s endorsement of Harris, Trump lashed out on social media, calling him “an irrelevant RINO,” meaning Republican in Name Only. “He’s the King of Endless, Nonsensical Wars, wasting Lives and Trillions of Dollars, just like Comrade Kamala Harris.”

Our Analysis:

The Cheney Memorial: A Microcosm of Partisan Entanglements and Political Legacy

In the intricate dance of American politics, the memorial service for former Vice President Dick Cheney serves as a poignant tableau, reflecting not just the man, but the seismic shifts within the Republican Party and the broader political landscape. The narrative constructed by Peter Baker in The New York Times is replete with the kind of political theater that demands a closer examination, not just for its surface-level intrigues but for what it signals about the current and future state of American political discourse.

Divides Beyond the Grave

At the heart of Baker’s analysis is the assertion that Cheney’s memorial will be “revealing — not for who does show up, but for who does not.” This premise, while evocative, skims over the deeper fissures that Cheney’s life and legacy have carved into the American political fabric. Cheney, described as the “architect of America’s ‘aggressive response to terrorism’” and a “champion of the invasion of Iraq,” is a figure whose political and personal ethos has invariably polarized viewpoints across the spectrum.

The Republican Hero and Democratic Villain: This dichotomy, as framed by Baker, is a reductionist take on a far more complex legacy. Cheney’s role in shaping post-9/11 foreign policy and the invasion of Iraq has consequences that ripple through to today, affecting not just American soldiers and citizens but millions around the globe. His late-life pivot against Trump, while significant, does not erase or mitigate this.

A Study in Political Isolation

Baker’s focus on Cheney’s isolation within his own party, catalyzed by his opposition to Trump, is indeed a compelling narrative thread. It underscores a broader theme of political transformation and realignment within the GOP. However, Baker’s portrayal sometimes veers into the realm of political eulogizing that glosses over the contentious aspects of Cheney’s tenure.

The Eulogy Speakers and Attendees: The choice of George W. Bush to deliver Cheney’s eulogy, with attendees including Joe Biden, John A. Boehner, Nancy Pelosi, and Kamala Harris, amongst others, is emblematic of a political era that, while not devoid of contention, seems almost quaint in comparison to today’s hyper-partisan environment.

The Trump Factor

Donald Trump’s conspicuous absence from the event, as noted by Baker, is “hardly a surprise” but is emblematic of the deep rifts within the Republican Party. Trump’s rebuke of Cheney as an “irrelevant RINO” and his critique of Cheney’s endorsement of Kamala Harris speaks volumes about the current state of GOP politics where allegiance to Trumpism often trumps traditional conservative values.

Legacy and the Lens of History

Baker’s article, while insightful, perhaps inadvertently glosses over the more controversial aspects of Cheney’s legacy. The mention of “lies and violence” attributed to Trump by Cheney could well apply, in the eyes of many critics, to Cheney’s own actions and policies, particularly regarding the Iraq War and its justification.

Conclusion: More Than Mere Attendance

In dissecting The New York Times article, it becomes apparent that the narrative around Cheney’s memorial service is a microcosm of larger political and ideological battles. Cheney’s complex legacy, characterized by significant contributions and equally significant controversies, mirrors the current state of American politics — deeply divided, increasingly polarized, but always reflective of the enduring struggle over the soul and direction of the nation.

In sum, while Baker’s reportage offers a window into the political machinations surrounding Cheney’s memorial, it also leaves untouched deeper questions about accountability, the consequences of political actions, and the ways in which we choose to remember (or forget) the figures who have shaped our world.
