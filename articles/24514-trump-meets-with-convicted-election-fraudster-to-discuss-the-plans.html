Trump meets with convicted election fraudster to discuss ‘the plans'

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 7, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump met in the Oval Office with Tina Peters, the former Mesa County, Colorado, clerk convicted in 2024 of seven offenses arising from a 2021 breach of county voting equipment. Former Justice Department official Ed Martin said they discussed election integrity and “the plans” for the midterm elections.

Martin connected the meeting to Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin’s August 24 warning about foreign-made components in voting machines and to the stalled SAVE America Act, which would impose new citizenship-documentation and identification requirements for federal elections. Mullin’s letter described security risks and tied federal grants to manual ballot audits, but it did not establish that voting systems or vote totals had been compromised.

Neither Martin nor the report identified what “the plans” were. Martin merely suggested that Trump might act if Congress failed to pass the legislation, without specifying what action he meant.

Power was in the Oval Office

Peters did not control the meeting’s significance. Trump did. A sitting president granted Oval Office access to a former election official convicted after prosecutors said she enabled unauthorized access to voting equipment while searching for evidence supporting claims about the 2020 election.

That choice conferred political legitimacy. Peters arrived not as an election-security authority vindicated by evidence, but as a convicted participant in a voting-system breach. The institutional power belonged to the president who received her, the administration officials promoting federal intervention, and the congressional Republicans seeking stricter voting rules.

Suspicion replaced evidence

Martin moved from a legitimate supply-chain concern to the unsupported assertion that voting machines were “compromised.” Mullin’s letter, as described by AlterNet, said foreign-made components posed significant security risks. It did not prove altered votes or compromised elections.

That distinction is the heart of the story. A vulnerability can justify safeguards, audits, procurement standards, or investigation. It does not justify announcing a compromised electoral system as fact. Martin collapsed risk into proof, then used that inflated claim to support federal action.

The ambiguity serves the powerful

“The plans” is ominous precisely because it is empty. AlterNet could not establish what Trump and Peters discussed beyond Martin’s characterization, and Martin did not say what Trump might do if Congress refused to pass the SAVE America Act.

The article is right not to manufacture an answer. But the vagueness itself has political value: it advertises presidential resolve to an audience already primed to distrust elections while withholding a proposal that could be tested for legality, necessity, or effectiveness. The public receives insinuation; the executive retains discretion.

Congress became the alibi

Martin framed congressional failure to enact Republican-backed voting restrictions as a reason the president “is going to have to do what he’s going to do.” Congress declining to pass a bill does not create executive authority. It means the bill has not become law.

The SAVE America Act’s documentation and identification requirements were therefore not merely policy preferences in Martin’s account. They became a pretext for unspecified presidential action. That framing converts legislative defeat into supposed permission for executive improvisation.

The systemic error

This is how election distrust becomes governing infrastructure. Officials identify a real category of risk, strip away the limits of the evidence, elevate a convicted breach participant as a credible adviser, and invoke congressional inaction to enlarge presidential power.

The immediate danger described by the reporting is not a proven foreign manipulation of votes. It is the use of unproven compromise claims to justify opaque planning from the nation’s most powerful office. When evidence runs out, insinuation takes over—and institutional authority gives that insinuation consequences.
