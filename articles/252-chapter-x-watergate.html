Chapter X. Watergate

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 23, 202613 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

On June 17, 1972, five men were arrested inside the Democratic National Committee offices at the Watergate complex.

Adrian Vale read the first wire item at 7:43 that morning in a hotel room in Washington, barefoot, unshaven, and already sick with recognition.

The report was short. Too short for what it carried. A burglary. Five suspects. Photographic equipment. Electronic gear. The Democratic National Committee. Watergate.

There it was.

Not as history yet. Not as scandal. Not as a suffix that would one day attach itself to every lazy headline in America. Just a police item, small enough to be missed by men who did not already know that republics sometimes announced their fevers in the language of trespass.

Adrian sat on the edge of the bed with the paper in his hand and did nothing.

That was the bargain he had made with himself.

He had not warned the Democrats.

He had not warned the police.

He had not called an editor and told him where to look.

He had not sent an anonymous envelope to the Washington Post or the FBI or a judge or a senator with a conscience and a staff large enough to use it.

For months, he had carried the date like a hot coal. June 1972. DNC. Watergate. Burglars. Money. Committee to Re-elect. Cubans. Hunt. Liddy. Mitchell. Nixon. The old chain lay in him almost complete, names and pressures and outcomes pressed into memory by years of documentaries, articles, lectures, jokes, arguments, and late-night rage.

And still he had done nothing before the door was taped.

Because if he prevented the break-in, he might save Nixon from exposure.

Because if he exposed the plot before it happened, he would have to explain knowledge no private research could plausibly hold.

Because if he engineered the story, then every newspaper he had tried to free would become evidence that he had built a better-looking propaganda machine.

Because the Post had to find it.

Because history, if it was going to bend, still needed some events that did not begin in Adrian Vale’s pocket.

He folded the wire item once, then again, then unfolded it because ritual did not make the nausea useful.

The telephone rang.

He let it ring twice.

“Vale.”

“You have seen it,” Roth said.

“Yes.”

“How bad?”

Adrian looked at the word again.

Watergate.

“Potentially presidential.”

Roth was silent for half a second. “You said that too quickly.”

“I know.”

“Is this one of your patterns?”

“Yes.”

“How much do you know?”

“Too much before proof. Not enough for court.”

“That is a miserable category.”

“Most of mine are.”

Roth exhaled through his nose. Adrian could picture him in New York, already reaching for a legal pad, already resenting the shape of the day.

“Do we have people on it?”

“We do not.”

“Do we put people on it?”

Adrian closed his eyes.

There were a dozen tempting answers. Send research teams. Call Claire. Call Ruth Elkins. Call every protected paper in the trust network and tell them to pull every thread attached to the Committee to Re-elect the President. Flood the zone with lawsuits, document requests, source protection, money trails, court watchers, private investigators, legal defense.

He could already feel the machine wanting to move.

“No,” he said.

Roth did not answer.

“Not first,” Adrian said. “The Post gets first run.”

“Why?”

“Because the break-in happened in their city, against a national political target, and their newsroom is already positioned to own the story if they have the nerve.”

“That is an answer you can use outside this call.”

“Because if my papers move first, Nixon calls it a coordinated attack by hidden money.”

“Better still.”

“Because if the Post moves first and my network supports later, the story belongs to journalism, not to me.”

“Best.”

Adrian opened his eyes.

“We monitor. We do not direct. If any of our papers pick up local threads, they follow evidence, not my memory. No one receives private instructions from me about Watergate.”

“You understand that this restraint may cost the country time.”

“Yes.”

“And you understand that moving too visibly may cost the story legitimacy.”

“Yes.”

“I dislike both options.”

“So do I.”

“Good,” Roth said, then caught himself. “Damn you, now I am doing it.”

Adrian almost smiled. Almost.

“There is one thing,” he said.

“Of course there is.”

“Katharine Graham.”

Roth’s tone changed. “What about her?”

“If this becomes what I think it becomes, the Post will face pressure. Legal, financial, political, personal. Advertisers. Shareholders. Board panic. Federal threats. Licensing. The whole weight.”

“You want to buy the Post.”

“No.”

“Say that again.”

“No.”

“Good. I wanted to hear whether you hesitated.”

“I want to fortify Graham’s position if she will allow it. Non-controlling support. Litigation backstop. Retaliation insurance. Shareholder stability. Editorial independence protected. She remains publisher as long as she wants. She chooses and trains her successor. The trust cannot direct coverage.”

Roth was quiet long enough that Adrian heard a car horn through the line from New York.

“You have been thinking about this.”

“Since before Zurich.”

“That is either reassuring or horrifying.”

“Both.”

“When?”

“Not today. Not before the Post commits itself. If we approach too early, it looks like we were waiting with a net.”

“Were we?”

“Yes.”

“Then let us not look like it.”

After Roth hung up, Adrian dressed and went downstairs.

Washington was already hot. The kind of heat that rose from pavement with bureaucratic patience. The hotel lobby was full of men in suits, women with folders, tourists with maps, and staff who had learned not to react visibly to anything short of fire. A newspaper lay folded on a chair beside a potted plant. Adrian picked it up, scanned the front page, and found nothing yet large enough to satisfy memory.

Good, he thought.

Then hated himself for thinking it.

The first Post story appeared the next morning under Alfred E. Lewis’s byline.

Adrian read it twice.

It was small and enormous.

Five Held in Plot to Bug Democrats’ Office Here.

Names. Details. Tools. Address books. One suspect connected to the CIA. A burglary that did not behave like a burglary. The first outline of a door through which the whole country might be forced to walk.

He clipped it carefully and placed it in a folder marked only W.

Woodward and Bernstein would come later, when a burglary became a pattern and a pattern became a trail. For now, the story belonged to Lewis, and Adrian made himself honor that. History did not need him stealing bylines too.

For two days, he did not call anyone at the Post.

That was harder than calling would have been.

Instead, he read. The Post. The Times. The Star. The wire services. Black papers. Local papers. Hostile papers. Friendly papers. Papers he owned and papers he would not touch. He watched who minimized the story, who noticed the names, who laughed, who waited, who understood that burglars wearing suits were sometimes more interesting than burglars with guns.

On the third day, Claire Donnelly called him.

“Watergate,” she said.

“Yes.”

“You know something.”

“I know it matters.”

“That is not what I said.”

“No.”

“Should we put people on it?”

“Only if you find a local thread.”

“That’s oddly restrained for a man who buys fire extinguishers before matches.”

“This one is the Post’s match.”

Claire was quiet.

“You respect them?”

“Yes.”

“Or you remember them?”

Adrian said nothing.

She absorbed that in the way good editors absorbed silence: not as absence, but as material.

“Fine,” she said. “We will watch. But if Brookhaven money touches this thing, I am sending a reporter through the wall.”

“Good.”

“Do not good me.”

She hung up.

By the end of the week, Adrian’s network had begun doing what he had allowed it to do: not lead, but listen.

A paper in Miami assigned a reporter to Cuban exile connections because the names in the arrest report mattered locally. A California paper began examining campaign finance flows connected to Republican committees, not because Adrian told it to, but because its editor had grown allergic to unexplained money. Ruth Elkins wrote a column about political burglary, federal power, and the arrogance of men who assumed offices belonged to whoever could enter them after midnight. The Sentinel-Register ran a short editorial titled BURGLARY IS NOT CAMPAIGNING and managed, somehow, to make Hallowell sound like a disappointed shop teacher with a constitutional theory.

The Post kept digging.

Adrian kept waiting.

Waiting, he discovered, was a form of discipline more painful than action because it offered so many opportunities to mistake impatience for courage.

In July, when the story refused to die, Benjamin Roth arranged the first quiet contact with Katharine Graham’s counsel.

Not Graham herself. Not yet.

“Too early,” Roth said.

“You keep saying that.”

“Because you keep arriving with money before people have admitted they need it.”

The conversation took place in New York, in a law office where the chairs were modern enough to be uncomfortable on principle. Roth did most of the speaking. Adrian sat beside him and said very little. Across the table, two lawyers representing the Washington Post Company listened with expressions ranging from skepticism to professionally concealed alarm.

“Let me be plain,” one of them said. “You are offering a legal-defense and financial backstop to a newspaper you do not own, regarding a story you claim no interest in directing, through trusts whose funding sources you will not fully disclose.”

“That is plain,” Roth said.

“It is also insane.”

“Many useful structures sound that way before fees.”

The other lawyer looked at Adrian. “Why the Post?”

Adrian had prepared this answer carefully because the wrong truth would poison the room.

“Because the President of the United States can survive many things. He should not be able to survive a free newspaper with insufficient cash.”

“The Post is not poor.”

“No.”

“Mrs. Graham is not helpless.”

“Certainly not.”

“Then what exactly do you think you are protecting?”

“Her ability to remain unafraid of consequences that have nothing to do with truth.”

The first lawyer leaned back.

“You do understand how patronizing that sounds.”

“Yes.”

“And still?”

“Still.”

The second lawyer tapped the proposal.

“This compact says any trust support would be non-controlling.”

“Yes.”

“No editorial authority.”

“None.”

“No endorsements.”

“None.”

“No story review.”

“None.”

“No power to hire or fire newsroom leadership.”

“None.”

“But the trust could purchase public shares.”

“Non-controlling, under voting restrictions.”

“And would vote to support Mrs. Graham’s continued control.”

“Against politically motivated panic, yes.”

“Who determines political motivation?”

Roth smiled thinly. “Now we are billing.”

Adrian said, “The structure needs independent review. Your counsel, our counsel, outside trustees. I am not asking Mrs. Graham to trust me. I am asking whether there is a way to make fear less useful to her enemies without making me useful to her newsroom.”

The lawyers exchanged a look.

It was not acceptance.

It was no longer dismissal.

That was enough for a first meeting.

The actual meeting with Katharine Graham came in August.

By then, the story had become a thing with roots. Woodward and Bernstein had names, money trails, calls, committees, denials. The White House had learned the value of contempt and the limits of it. The Post had learned that publishing one story made the next one harder and more necessary. Graham had learned, again, that courage was often described as composure by people who did not feel the knife.

She received Adrian in a private office that did not try to impress him and therefore did. Ben Bradlee was not present. That was deliberate, Adrian assumed. Or a test. Or both.

Katharine Graham was smaller than the force around her, which was true of many people history mistook for symbols. She had a composed face, watchful eyes, and the particular reserve of someone who had spent too many years being underestimated in rooms she owned.

“Mr. Vale,” she said.

“Mrs. Graham.”

“My lawyers tell me you do not want to buy my newspaper.”

“I do not.”

“People who say that often mean they want to buy influence more cheaply.”

“Yes.”

“Was that agreement?”

“It was recognition.”

She gestured to a chair. He sat only after she did.

“You have been buying papers,” she said.

“Yes.”

“A great many.”

“Yes.”

“Quietly.”

“Yes.”

“And now you arrive at my office with money that promises not to behave like money.”

“No. I arrive with money and a proposed structure to make it behave less badly.”

For the first time, a flicker of something crossed her face. Not amusement. Interest, perhaps, made cautious by experience.

“Why me?”

“Because the Post is about to be tested.”

“About to be?”

“Is being.”

“By Watergate.”

“Yes.”

“And you know where it goes?”

Adrian’s mouth went dry.

She saw it.

Of course she saw it.

“I know what it is,” he said.

“What is it?”

“A burglary that touches money, intelligence men, campaign officials, fear, and the President’s habits.”

Graham sat very still.

“That is a dangerous sentence.”

“Yes.”

“Can you prove it?”

“No.”

“Then why say it?”

“Because if I pretend not to understand the stakes, you may mistake the offer for ordinary investment.”

“And it is not?”

“No.”

“What is it?”

Adrian looked at the proposal on the table between them.

“A shield with no handle.”

She did not pick it up.

“Meaning?”

“Legal-defense funds available under terms your counsel controls. Retaliation insurance. Shareholder-stability support through a non-controlling trust. No newsroom command. No story review. No endorsement authority. No power over Mr. Bradlee. No power over your reporters. No power to remove you. The trust votes against political pressure to weaken your authority. It cannot tell you what to print.”

“And what do you receive?”

“The chance that the story survives.”

“Not enough.”

“No.”

“What else?”

“A stronger country.”

She looked unimpressed in a way Adrian respected.

“Men have done terrible things while promising stronger countries.”

“Yes.”

“Why should I believe you are different?”

“You should not.”

That landed.

“Then why are you here?”

“Because you can inspect the structure. Because your lawyers can make it harder for me to abuse. Because if the terms are not good enough, you should refuse them. Because I would rather you mistrust me and remain unafraid of Nixon than trust me and become dependent.”

Graham looked down at the proposal then, finally.

“My position is not yours to fortify.”

“No.”

“My paper is not a fortress you can improve from outside.”

“No.”

“My courage is not for sale.”

“No.”

She opened the folder.

“But litigation is expensive.”

“Yes.”

“And shareholders are not romantic.”

“No.”

“And presidents can be vindictive.”

“Very.”

She read the first page. Then the second. Adrian waited. He had learned that silence was the native language of rooms where people decided whether money would be permitted to touch honor without staining it.

At last she looked up.

“This says I remain publisher as long as I choose.”

“Yes.”

“Subject to law and ordinary fiduciary catastrophe.”

“Roth insisted on the phrase.”

“Lawyers enjoy catastrophes if they are ordinary.”

“So I am learning.”

“This says I choose my successor.”

“Yes.”

“But I must train one.”

“Yes.”

Her eyes sharpened.

“That is presumptuous.”

“Yes.”

“Explain it anyway.”

“No institution should depend forever on one courageous person.”

For a moment, the room changed.

Graham closed the folder halfway.

“You think I am courageous?”

Adrian chose the truth he could afford.

“I think you are becoming so under conditions that should have been kinder.”

She looked away.

Outside the office, somewhere beyond the door, a telephone rang and was answered. A newsroom moved. A city sweated. A president worried. Reporters followed threads that still looked too thin to hang history from.

Graham looked back.

“If I accept any part of this, and you try to steer my paper, I will publish you.”

Adrian felt relief so sharp it nearly became pain.

“That is why I came.”

“No,” she said. “It is why I may let you leave without throwing this folder at you.”

He almost smiled.

She did not.

“My lawyers will speak to Mr. Roth,” she said.

“Thank you.”

“Do not thank me. I have agreed to nothing.”

“Of course.”

“And Mr. Vale?”

“Yes?”

“If this is a shield, I decide where it stands.”

Adrian rose.

“Yes, Mrs. Graham.”

When he left the building, the city had not changed.

That was one of history’s cruelties. A door opened, a line held, a person chose not to be frightened, and outside there were still taxis, heat, exhaust, shoe polish, lunch carts, men in shirtsleeves, women with folders, a boy selling papers with a headline already aging in his hands.

Adrian bought one.

The Post’s Watergate story sat below the fold, still too small for what it was becoming.

He folded the paper under his arm and walked without hurry.

This time, he thought, Nixon would not be fighting one newspaper alone.

But if Adrian had done his work correctly, Nixon would not know that until it was too late to frighten the first one.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
