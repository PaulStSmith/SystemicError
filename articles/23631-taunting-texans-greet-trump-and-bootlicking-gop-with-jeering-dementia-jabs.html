Taunting Texans greet Trump and 'bootlicking' GOP with jeering 'dementia' jabs

Based on an article by David Edwards

Paulo SantosSeptember 19, 20263 min read

The Insult Is Easy. The Machinery Is the Story.

A Convention Built to Prop Up Power

The Republican convention in Dallas is not merely a partisan gathering. It is an institutional intervention on behalf of Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton, the party’s Senate nominee, who is reportedly running roughly even with Democratic state Rep. James Talarico. Republican leaders chose Dallas partly to strengthen Paxton because retaining the seat helps preserve their hold on Texas—and, by extension, their power in Washington.

That is the central fact. The party possesses the money, organization, presidency, and statewide machinery. It is using them to protect a vulnerable nominee.

The Dementia Joke Is a Distraction

Texas Democratic Party chair Kendall Scudder’s crack about Dallas having a memory-care clinic generated an obvious headline. It also handed reporters an escape route from the more consequential claims made at the same news conference.

Mocking Trump’s health does not explain who controls ballot administration, who can pressure state officials, or who benefits when election rules become uncertain. Worse, asserting that Trump “clearly has ailing health” turns an unsubstantiated diagnosis into political theater. It replaces an argument about power with an insult about age and illness.

If Democrats want to identify authoritarian conduct, they should name the conduct precisely. Scudder’s more serious allegation was that the president claimed authority to determine which mail ballots a state would accept while Texas officials could not provide a voting roster. That assertion concerns the attempted concentration of electoral power. The clinic joke concerns nothing beyond applause.

Fear of Trump, Sold at Market Rate

The convention’s pricing exposes the party’s actual hierarchy. A roundtable seat with Trump was offered for $250,000. A commemorative photograph cost another $88,600. Twenty thousand dollars purchased “honorary delegate” status, plus the costume accessories of a Texas shirt and cowboy hat.

Access is the product. Proximity is the reward. The event dresses transactional politics in convention pageantry while party leaders claim to speak for ordinary voters.

Even the reported $25,000 ticket request for House members reveals the arrangement: influence moves upward toward those able to pay, while public-facing lotteries for free seats help fill the arena and manufacture the image of mass enthusiasm. The donors buy access; everyone else supplies atmosphere.

Cowardice Without Defection

Several Republicans in competitive races are staying away. Their absence should not be mistaken for resistance.

Rep. Tom Barrett’s explanation—he cannot control what Trump says, but can control where he is—captures the strategy perfectly. These candidates are not challenging the president’s authority over the party. They are managing photographic evidence of their submission to it.

That is institutional cowardice in its cleanest form: remain inside the structure, accept its support, avoid its least marketable spectacle, and hope voters confuse physical distance with political independence. They want Trump’s coalition without responsibility for Trump’s words.

Democrats Still Prefer the Punchline

Scudder correctly pointed toward a Republican system organized around big business, billionaires, and personal loyalty to Trump. But the Democrats’ presentation diluted that structural indictment with jokes about dementia, Paxton’s charisma, and Dallas Mayor Eric Johnson’s marriage.

This is the recurring failure of opposition politics: describing a machinery of money, electoral control, and enforced loyalty, then packaging it as a roast. The result may be quotable, but it leaves the public with personalities where it should see institutions.

The weak framing does Republicans a favor. It permits a convention designed to consolidate power and sell presidential access to become another story about partisan insults.

The System Beneath the Spectacle

The Dallas convention reveals a party protecting a vulnerable nominee, monetizing access to its leader, and allowing endangered candidates to stage-manage their association with him. Those are decisions made by people with institutional power, not signs of confusion or political disorder.

The larger pattern is a political system in which loyalty is financed, dissent is simulated through strategic absence, and electoral authority becomes another arena for presidential control. The insults are noise. The transaction is the governing principle.
