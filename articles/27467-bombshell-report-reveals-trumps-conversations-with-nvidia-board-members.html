Bombshell report reveals Trump’s conversations with Nvidia board members

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 26, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Rubio’s warning lost to a last-minute reversalNvidia cited government approval the government could not explainExport enforcement and corporate access

President Donald Trump and Vice President JD Vance privately engaged with Nvidia board members and lawyers over Jensen Huang’s reinstatement and participation in a presidential trip to China, while officials responsible for intelligence and export policy were reportedly excluded. CBS News reported that CIA Director John Ratcliffe and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick did not know what was unfolding. Secretary of State Marco Rubio—who had personally warned Trump about intelligence concerning Huang’s relationships in China—was reportedly furious.

The significance is not merely that corporate representatives had the president’s ear. It is that their access appears to have overridden the administration’s own national-security process, leaving senior officials uninformed and producing a decision the attorney general could not—or would not—explain.

Rubio’s warning lost to a last-minute reversal

According to CBS News, Rubio prepared a classified memorandum earlier in the year based on U.S. intelligence, including intercepted conversations involving Alice Huang. The memorandum warned that Jensen Huang had a close relationship with Alice Huang, who was known to communicate with an officer in China’s Ministry of State Security.

Rubio was also reportedly one of two officials who briefed Trump about Huang’s relationship with a Chinese spy. That concern initially kept Huang off the guest list for the China trip. But while Air Force One was refueling in Alaska, Trump added him at the last minute—even after Rubio personally explained why he considered it unsafe to include Huang on the diplomatic mission.

CBS reported that Nvidia told Trump and Vance late Sunday that Huang would be brought back on. One official described the message as a command, not a consultation: “We were told, ‘This is going to happen.’”

That sequence clarifies where the institutional power sat. Intelligence officials could collect information, the secretary of state could prepare a classified warning, and national-security officials could recommend excluding Huang. Trump could erase all of it with a last-minute decision after private discussions involving Nvidia.

Nvidia cited government approval the government could not explain

Nvidia said Monday that its board had received input “from senior levels of the United States government” and was confident Huang’s reinstatement presented no national-security risk. The statement did not reconcile that assurance with Rubio’s reported warning or the exclusion of Ratcliffe and Lutnick from the discussions.

Trump then offered a different and deeply muddled account during a call to CNBC. Asked what the attorney general had told him about Huang’s possible criminal exposure and export-control issues, Trump said an unspecified “they” had conducted a study and “pretty good investigation” that left Huang “exonerated.”

He then shifted to Nvidia’s chip sales, saying Huang could sell older products—apparently referring to “the 200s”—but not Blackwell chips or other advanced technology. Trump suggested future problems might arise if buyers resold Nvidia products, concluding, “Who knows.”

That was not an explanation of who investigated Huang, what conduct was examined, what legal standard applied, or how the reported national-security concerns had been resolved. It was presidential absolution without an identifiable process.

Attorney General Todd Blanche further undermined Trump’s account the following day. When reporters asked about an investigation, Blanche said he did not know what they were talking about and noted that the Justice Department handles hundreds or more export investigations at any given time. Asked specifically whether the Justice Department or FBI had investigated Huang as a security risk, Blanche responded: “What does that mean, for what? I don’t understand the question.”

Trump claimed an investigation had exonerated Huang. His attorney general professed ignorance of it. Nvidia claimed senior government input had eliminated the national-security concern. Senior officials responsible for intelligence and commerce were reportedly outside the conversation. Those accounts do not describe a functioning review; they describe a decision acquiring official justification after the people with direct access had already made it.

Export enforcement and corporate access

The surrounding financial interests make that exclusion more consequential. AlterNet, citing the CBS report, noted that Lutnick’s former company holds significant Nvidia investments, raising questions about his refusal to enforce export requirements involving the company. The supplied reporting does not establish why he declined to act, but it places the administration’s export-control decisions inside a web of corporate access and unresolved conflicts.

Nor has the wider scrutiny disappeared. CBS reporter Jennifer Jacobs said the FBI and the Justice Department’s National Security Division were examining potential export-control violations involving Megaspeed and Super Micro Computer, including who knew what and when. That report concerns ongoing scrutiny, not a finding of wrongdoing by Huang.

The central failure is already visible without prejudging those inquiries. Rubio’s warning reportedly reached Trump. Trump nevertheless reversed the security-based exclusion. Nvidia’s representatives had access to Trump and Vance while key officials remained unaware, and the administration subsequently produced incompatible stories about the review supposedly supporting its decision.

The “bombshell” is therefore not simply that Trump spoke with Nvidia’s board. Presidents routinely hear from corporate executives. It is that private corporate access appears to have displaced the government’s own security apparatus—and that when officials were asked to account for the result, the president invoked an exoneration his attorney general could not identify.
