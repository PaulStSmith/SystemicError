Journalist rips RFK Jr. for ‘rape apologist’ remarks and ‘vendetta against women’

Journalist and blogger Amanda Marcotte laid into Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Monday in an op-ed over what she described as his “special vendetta against the bodily autonomy of women, especially pregnant women,” and compared his rhetoric to that of “rape apologists.”

RFK Jr. has faced renewed scrutiny after his conference last week alongside President Donald Trump, in which he linked autism to the use of Tylenol by pregnant women, a claim that experts say is not supported by existing data. At the event, Trump recommended that pregnant women “tough it out” rather than use Tylenol, among the few over-the-counter pain relievers scientists have said are safe for them.

But for Marcotte, Kennedy’s long-running fixation on identifying a culprit for the rising rates of autism in the United States is less out of concern and more a means to an end — one that Marcotte argues is strictly about “controlling women.”

“Kennedy still offered the most vile comment during the event, when he appeared to casually mock victims of sexual abuse: ‘some of our friends like to say we should believe all women,’ he said, before insisting that the ‘friends’ he mentioned must be hypocrites because they don’t believe women who falsely claim vaccines cause autism,” Marcotte wrote.

“Kennedy, of course, was referencing the slogan ‘believe women,’ which advocates for victims of sexual violence crafted to reflect the studies that show over 90% of rape allegations are true. ‘Believe all women,’ in contrast, is a sarcastic phrase typically deployed by rape apologists to falsely suggest that feminists want men to be imprisoned without evidence.”

Both RFK Jr. and Trump have doubled down on their assertion that Tylenol use among pregnant mothers was directly linked to an increased chance of autism, with Trump declaring on social media, in all caps, that under no circumstance should a pregnant mother use Tylenol “UNLESS ABSOLUTELY NECESSARY,” and that mothers should never give their children the drug “FOR VIRTUALLY ANY REASON.”

At best, the link between autism and Tylenol is inconclusive, with perhaps the largest study on the correlation – which used data from 2.5 million children over a period of 24 years – finding only a .09% increase in autism rates among pregnant mothers who used Tylenol. However, the study did not control for a range of factors, potentially leading the small difference to be contributory to secondary factors, such as mothers in poorer health more frequently using pain medication.

“Feminists have never used the phrase ‘believe all women,’ which is a misogynist insult. In Kennedy’s mouth, it became even more sinister – and not just because he was accused of sexual assault by a family babysitter in the 1990s,” Marcotte wrote.

“The secretary was attempting to frame his attacks on children’s health care as if they were a crusade for women’s rights. Instead, he insulted women’s intelligence by suggesting that a rape victim talking about her own experiences deserves no more respect thanformer Playboy model Jenny McCarthyclaiming to have superior medical knowledge than actual doctors.”

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Vendetta: Kennedy’s Controversial Stance on Women’s Autonomy and Health

In a blistering op-ed, journalist and blogger Amanda Marcotte takes aim at Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy, casting him as an antagonist in the ongoing struggle for women’s bodily autonomy. Kennedy, alongside President Donald Trump, has ignited a firestorm of controversy with claims linking Tylenol use in pregnant women to autism—a stance unsupported by the bulk of scientific evidence. But it’s Kennedy’s alleged mockery of sexual abuse victims and his dubious invocation of women’s rights that Marcotte rightly identifies as particularly insidious.

Kennedy’s Misguided Autism Crusade

Kennedy’s fixation on autism’s causes is not only scientifically dubious but also dangerously simplistic. The claim that Tylenol use during pregnancy significantly increases autism rates in children is, at best, based on inconclusive evidence. Citing a study involving 2.5 million children over 24 years, Marcotte notes that any observed increase in autism rates among Tylenol-using mothers is minuscule and likely attributable to other factors. This fact alone should give pause to anyone tempted to take Kennedy’s assertions at face value.

The Mockery of Sexual Abuse Victims

Kennedy’s rhetoric takes a dark turn when he appears to mock victims of sexual abuse, according to Marcotte. By twisting the phrase “believe all women”—a deliberate misrepresentation of the feminist advocacy for taking rape allegations seriously—Kennedy not only trivializes sexual violence but also positions himself in troubling alignment with rape apologists. Such manipulation of sensitive issues is not just morally reprehensible; it’s a calculated move to undermine the credibility of women’s voices, particularly those who challenge his views.

A False Crusade for Women’s Rights

Perhaps most egregious is Kennedy’s attempt to frame his controversial stance on healthcare as a fight for women’s rights. Marcotte astutely observes that this is nothing but a smokescreen, intended to mask his efforts to control women under the guise of advocacy. By comparing a rape victim’s testimony to Jenny McCarthy’s anti-vaccine claims, Kennedy not only belittles the experiences of sexual assault survivors but also insults the intelligence of women everywhere. This false equivalency between lived trauma and debunked medical theories is a stark illustration of Kennedy’s manipulative tactics.

Conclusion: A Dangerous Distortion of Advocacy

Marcotte’s critique of Robert F. Kennedy is not just a condemnation of one man’s troubling views; it’s a cautionary tale about the dangers of co-opting the language of rights and advocacy for nefarious purposes. Kennedy’s actions, as laid bare by Marcotte, represent a broader challenge to the fight for women’s autonomy and respect. The attempt to control women’s bodies, dismiss their experiences, and distort their advocacy is a stark reminder of the systemic barriers still facing the movement for gender equality.

In the final analysis, Marcotte’s op-ed serves as a critical reminder that the battle for women’s rights is far from over, and that vigilance is required to counteract the efforts of those who would seek to undermine these rights under the guise of health or science. Kennedy’s stance, as dissected by Marcotte, is a clear example of how not to advocate for women’s health or autonomy—making it all the more important for voices like Marcotte’s to continue calling out such systemic errors with clarity and conviction.
