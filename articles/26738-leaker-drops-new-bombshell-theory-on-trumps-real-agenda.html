Leaker drops new bombshell theory on Trump’s real agenda

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 21, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that former Department of Homeland Security chief of staff Miles Taylor believes Donald Trump opposed foreign election interference selectively: not as a matter of national sovereignty, but when he thought it could hurt him politically. Taylor bases that conclusion on his experience in Trump’s first administration, while presenting Trump’s underlying motive as his own interpretation.

Taylor says that in August 2018 he assembled leaders from the FBI, the intelligence community and DHS to warn Russia against interfering in that year’s midterms. According to Taylor, Trump called the press conference “terrible” and asked whether anyone had pressured the officials to hold it. Weeks later at the United Nations, Trump accused China of election interference while saying nothing about Russia, which Taylor says administration officials had identified as the actual concern.

Taylor writes that this discrepancy made Trump’s position “click” for him: foreign meddling appeared objectionable when it threatened Trump or his preferred candidates, but tolerable when it benefited them. AlterNet also recounts Taylor’s argument that this pattern worsened after the 2020 election, when Trump promoted baseless voter-fraud claims and attempted to prevent the transfer of power.

The theory is Taylor’s, but the conduct is concrete

The headline promises a “bombshell theory.” The less inflated story is more useful: a former senior official describes a president reacting with hostility when his own national-security apparatus publicly warned a foreign adversary against election interference.

Taylor cannot prove Trump’s private motive merely by recounting that reaction. He can, however, identify the contradiction he witnessed. Trump reportedly disparaged a warning aimed at Russia, then publicly redirected attention toward China. The theory belongs to Taylor; the selective public conduct belongs to Trump.

That distinction matters because careless commentary can weaken a strong case by overstating it. The evidence presented here does not establish every element of a secret agenda. It does support scrutiny of whether Trump treated election security as an instrument of personal politics rather than a consistent government obligation.

The president held the power

The institutional hierarchy is not complicated. Career officials and agency leaders could assess threats, coordinate warnings and present intelligence judgments. The president could amplify that work, undermine it or redirect the government’s attention.

Trump held the greater power. According to Taylor, officials warned about Russia, while Trump attacked their warning and publicly emphasized China instead. Whatever Trump privately intended, that response placed presidential authority against his own officials’ stated effort to deter interference.

Calling Taylor and his colleagues a “resistance group” risks distorting that hierarchy. They were executive-branch officials trying to perform an election-security function inside an administration led by a president who could overrule, marginalize or remove them. The consequential actor was not the subordinate attempting to preserve institutional practice. It was the president deciding how the government would speak about the threat.

The misdirection was the message

Taylor’s account describes more than inconsistency. It describes political misdirection: suppress attention to the reported threat and substitute an adversary more convenient to the president’s narrative.

That does not establish that every accusation against China was false, nor does the supplied reporting document the basis for Trump’s United Nations claim. It establishes a narrower and still damning point: Trump reportedly declined to foreground the country his own team had warned him about, then accused another country instead.

The consequence is predictable. Election security stops being a rule applied to all foreign powers and becomes a partisan sorting mechanism. Helpful interference is minimized; potentially harmful interference is dramatized. Intelligence is no longer used chiefly to inform the public. It becomes material to be filtered according to the leader’s interests.

The larger pattern is personalized government

Taylor connects the 2018 episode to Trump’s conduct after losing the 2020 election. The common feature is not confusion about election administration. It is the subordination of institutional legitimacy to one politician’s fortunes.

In that model, an election is trustworthy when it delivers the preferred outcome. Foreign influence is intolerable when it disadvantages the leader and negotiable when it does not. Officials are credible when they validate the political line and suspect when they contradict it.

The systemic error is treating this as a clash of personalities or a mystery about Trump’s “real feelings.” Feelings are secondary. Power is exercised through decisions: which threats receive presidential backing, which warnings are disparaged, and which unsupported claims are promoted after defeat. Taylor offers an interpretation of motive. The reported pattern of selective institutional support is the more important indictment.
