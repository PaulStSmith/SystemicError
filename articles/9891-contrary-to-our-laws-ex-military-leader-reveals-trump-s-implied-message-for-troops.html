On Tuesday, Trump and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth spoke to America’s military leaders in a meeting that some have described as an “insult” and a “waste of time.” Trump told the military that they needed to start training in American cities that have the most crime, which retired Lieutenant General Mark Hertling said carried a hidden implication.

Hertling discussed Trump’s speech in a new episode of “The Daily Beast Podcast.”

“You’re putting an infantryman, a tanker, a truck driver in front of the American people,” Hertling said. “That would be like saying you and I could go out and arrest people and quell riots and do all those kinds of things. The military does have its force to support civilian authorities. If manpower is needed, certainly military forces can go to an area and protect guard buildings, put up fences, do the kind of things that you need a lot of people to do.”

“But that razor’s edge into policing activities again is, contrary to the Constitution and contrary to our laws,” he continued. “So it shouldn’t be done. But what I heard the president say yesterday was an implied remark that he was telling everyone in that room is be prepared to do that.”

Hertling added that it seemed Trump was stoking the fires of insurrection to justify deploying the military into American cities.

“That only happens once an insurrection occurs, like during the Civil War,” he said. “So, unless he’s prepared to stoke that kind of insurrection by further dividing the American people and it turns to violence, soldiers are the means of last resort for any kind of police activity, primarily because they’re not trained to do things like that, and secondarily because it’s against the law.”

Our Analysis:

Trump’s Implied Military Maneuver: A Closer Look

In an era where the lines between military operations and police work dangerously blur, recent comments by former President Donald Trump have ignited a firestorm of controversy and concern. During a televised speech aimed at America’s top military brass, Trump seemingly suggested that active duty troops should start training in American cities plagued by crime. This assertion, as unpacked by retired Lieutenant General Mark Hertling on “The Daily Beast Podcast,” is not only alarming but laced with implications that could potentially steer the United States into constitutional crisis territory.

The Insinuation of Military Policing

At the heart of Trump’s speech is an insidious suggestion that military personnel could be repurposed as domestic law enforcement agents in crime-ridden cities. Hertling underscores the absurdity and danger of this notion by comparing it to unqualified individuals attempting to undertake specialized police tasks. The military’s role, while versatile, is sharply delineated from policing activities by both tradition and law. The deployment of military forces within the U.S. to engage in law enforcement activities without clear, extenuating circumstances is a direct violation of the Posse Comitatus Act, a statute that aims to prevent exactly this kind of overreach.

A Dangerous Precedent

Trump’s rhetoric doesn’t merely flirt with the idea of militarizing American streets; it teeters on the precipice of advocating for a de facto imposition of martial law under the guise of restoring order. By insinuating that the military might be needed to quell domestic unrest, Trump is, as Hertling notes, potentially “stoking the fires of insurrection.” This is no small accusation. Suggesting military intervention in civil matters presupposes a level of disorder bordering on insurrection—a state of affairs that historically justifies such drastic measures. Yet, the invocation of insurrection to justify military policing is both a misreading of American law and a misjudgment of the military’s role in society.

The Legal and Ethical Quagmire

Hertling’s analysis points to a fundamental misunderstanding—or perhaps a willful ignorance—of the legal and ethical framework that governs military engagement on American soil. The Posse Comitatus Act, a cornerstone of American civil-military relations, expressly prohibits the use of U.S. military forces to perform the tasks of domestic law enforcement unless expressly authorized by the Constitution or an act of Congress. Trump’s proposition not only blurs these boundaries but seemingly encourages a breach of the very principles that safeguard American democracy.

Furthermore, the suggestion that the military could be deployed to manage crime in American cities undercuts the expertise and jurisdiction of local law enforcement agencies. It also overlooks the significant differences in training and purpose between soldiers and police officers. Soldiers are trained for combat, not community policing, making them ill-suited for tasks that require a nuanced understanding of criminal justice and community relations.

Conclusion

Trump’s comments, as highlighted by Hertling, reveal a troubling vision of America where military might is misappropriated to enforce domestic law. This vision not only challenges the constitutional safeguards against military overreach but also risks deepening the divide between the American public and the armed forces. The implications of Trump’s implied message are a stark reminder of the importance of maintaining a clear separation between military and police roles in society. Ignoring this separation endangers not just the principles of democracy but the very fabric of American community life.
