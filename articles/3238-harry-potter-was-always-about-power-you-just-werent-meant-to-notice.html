Harry Potter Was Always About Power. You Just Weren’t Meant to Notice

The backlash over casting a Black actor as Severus Snape in HBO’s upcoming Harry Potter adaptation has been framed, predictably, as a debate about “fidelity” and “narrative coherence.” That framing is a dodge.

What’s actually being exposed is something far less comfortable: the original story depends on the audience’s willingness to excuse cruelty when it is performed by the “right” characters and filtered through the “right” perspective.

Change one variable—remove the default assumptions that shield the audience—and suddenly the same events read very differently. Not because they changed. Because they can no longer be ignored.

Narrative Insulation: How the Story Protects Its Favorites

The Harry Potter series is not neutral in how it presents morality. It is carefully engineered to guide the reader’s sympathies early and lock them in.

We meet Harry as an abused child. We follow his perspective. We inherit his loyalties. By the time we learn anything meaningful about his father, James Potter, the emotional verdict has already been rendered: James is a hero.

That matters.

Because the story withholds James’ worst behavior until Deathly Hallows, long after the audience has internalized that verdict. When the Pensieve memory finally reveals James publicly humiliating Snape, the moment lands not as a reassessment—but as a disturbance to be managed.

The narrative offers the reader an escape hatch:

  • It’s in the past

  • It’s brief

  • It’s filtered through Snape’s memory

  • James “grew up”

Each of these elements reduces the weight of what is being shown. Together, they function as insulation.

The result is predictable. Readers don’t sit with the cruelty. They contextualize it away.

Pattern vs. Incident: What the Story Shows vs. What We Say It Shows

When confronted with James’ behavior, many readers instinctively reframe it as an isolated phase—teenage arrogance, a few bad moments, nothing systemic.

But the text itself suggests something else.

We are shown:

  • A persistent nickname: “Snivellus”

  • A group dynamic: multiple aggressors targeting one individual

  • Public humiliation carried out for an audience

  • A justification from James himself: “because he exists”

That is not a single incident. That is a pattern.

And patterns do not emerge from nothing. They require repetition, social permission, and a lack of meaningful consequences.

Hogwarts provides all three.

Teachers do not intervene in a way that disrupts the behavior. Peers laugh. The perpetrators are socially rewarded. The target is isolated.

This is not rivalry. It is structured cruelty.

The Power of Point of View

Everything in Harry Potter is filtered through Harry’s perspective. That perspective is treated as morally reliable—even when it is incomplete, biased, or wrong.

Harry distrusts Snape from the beginning. He interprets events through that distrust. The narrative rarely challenges him in real time.

Instead, it validates him retroactively.

This creates a powerful effect: suspicion becomes indistinguishable from insight.

By the time the reader is shown Snape’s memories, the emotional groundwork has already been laid. Snape is not introduced as a victim. He is introduced as an antagonist.

So when his past suffering is revealed, it does not reset the reader’s judgment. It complicates it—just enough to acknowledge the harm, but not enough to re-center it.

What Changes When Snape Is Black

Here is where the casting controversy becomes revealing.

A Black Severus Snape does not alter the events of the story. The same actions occur. The same dialogue exists. The same power dynamics remain.

What changes is the audience’s ability to minimize them.

The bullying that once read as “school rivalry” becomes immediately legible as targeted abuse.

The justification—“because he exists”—stops sounding like childish arrogance and starts echoing something far more familiar: hostility toward someone marked as an outsider.

The insulation fails.

The distance collapses.

And suddenly, the reader is forced to confront the behavior without the usual narrative protections.

The Memory Defense—and What It Avoids

One common defense is that the Pensieve shows Snape’s memory, and therefore may be biased.

This is technically true. Memory is not a perfect recording.

But this argument does something strategic: it shifts the focus from the behavior to the reliability of the victim’s perception.

Instead of asking:

What did James do?

The conversation becomes:

Can we trust how Snape remembers it?

This is not a neutral move. It is a deflection.

Bias can influence interpretation. It does not invent structural dynamics like group humiliation, power imbalance, and social reinforcement.

Those are observable patterns.

The memory may sharpen the cruelty. It does not fabricate it.

Selective Acceptance of “Inclusive Casting”

The argument that some characters can be race-swapped while others cannot collapses under scrutiny.

The characters typically deemed “safe” for such changes share a common trait: they do not disrupt the audience’s moral assumptions.

Snape does.

Changing his race forces a re-reading of:

  • The Marauders’ behavior

  • Harry’s persistent suspicion

  • The moral hierarchy of Gryffindor vs. Slytherin

That re-reading is what generates discomfort.

Not the change itself.

The Real-World Parallel

What makes this worth analyzing is not fandom discourse. It is the pattern it reveals.

The Harry Potter narrative demonstrates a familiar mechanism:

  • Align the audience with a favored perspective

  • Delay or soften evidence of harm

  • Provide contextual excuses

  • Encourage reinterpretation rather than confrontation

This is not unique to fiction.

It is how real-world systems maintain legitimacy in the face of documented harm.

Authority is protected not by denying the facts outright, but by shaping how those facts are perceived.

Final Cut: The Cost of Losing Narrative Comfort

A Black Snape does not rewrite Harry Potter.

It removes the audience’s ability to glide over its contradictions.

The cruelty was always there.

The imbalance was always there.

The story simply made it easy not to notice.

The backlash is not about preserving canon.

It is about preserving comfort.

And once that comfort is gone, the question is no longer whether the story holds up as written.

It is whether the audience is willing to see what it was always doing.
