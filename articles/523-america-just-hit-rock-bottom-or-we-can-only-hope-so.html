America just hit rock bottom — or we can only hope so

The White House as a Paywall and a Cage

Power on the Lawn

Donald Trump did not merely host a UFC event on White House grounds. He converted the most symbolically charged property in American politics into a stage for spectacle, extraction, and humiliation. The reporting is plain: fighters bled on the lawn, White House rooms became locker rooms, and the Executive Office Building was folded into the production. This was not an accident or a lapse in taste. It was an assertion of control over a public institution that was treated as private property.

The important question is not whether the event was “appropriate.” It clearly was not. The question is who had the power to make it happen. Trump did. Everyone else, from the agencies providing labor to the District covering security costs, was downstream from that decision.

Public Expense, Private Appetite

The article’s money trail matters because it exposes the basic deal. The UFC says private funding covered $60 million, but the burden on federal staff and D.C. security was still immense, and the public was left carrying costs tied to a vanity event. That is the familiar Trump model: privatize the upside, socialize the damage, then call it success.

The profiteering was not incidental. Trump owns stock in TKO Group Holdings, which owns and operates the UFC. A Trump super PAC held a $1 million-a-plate fundraiser the day before the event. Trump sold branded UFC medallions. A Trump-family crypto company was an official sponsor and issued stablecoins used as fighter bonuses. This is not corruption in the abstract. It is a network of personal enrichment wrapped in state ceremony.

The Enablers

The real story is not only Trump’s vulgarity. It is the institutional obedience that made the vulgarity workable. Federal agencies supplied labor. D.C. absorbed security costs. The White House grounds were opened, repurposed, and normalized. That is what institutional collapse looks like in practice: not a single dramatic breach, but a series of small permissions granted by people and offices that should have said no.

Paramount Skydance’s livestream, the Ellisons’ proximity to Trump, and the Justice Department’s approval of the Paramount-Warner Bros. Discovery merger only days earlier sharpen the picture. Corporate media and government power are not separate spheres here. They are adjacent enough to bargain, and close enough to flatter.

Blame Displaced Upward

The article also captures a more familiar trick: outrage gets attached to the fight itself, the vulgar chant, or the “event,” when the real scandal is the architecture above it. The winner’s obscene attack on Michelle Obama is ugly, but it is not the organizing principle. It is the predictable byproduct of a setting built to reward degradation. The arena was not just physical. It was political.

That matters because these stories often get narrated as cultural decline, as if the country drifted into this on its own. It did not. A president chose the venue. Agencies complied. Corporate actors cashed in. Media platforms distributed the product. The blame is not diffuse. It is vertically arranged.

The Pattern

The White House lawn has hosted music, sports, and public celebrations before. That history is the point of comparison, and Trump’s use of the space is a deliberate insult to it. He did not inherit a neutral stage. He took a civic symbol and turned it into a branded enclosure for blood sport and self-dealing.

The larger pattern is authoritarian in style and transactional in method: degrade the institution, monetize the degradation, and make everyone else treat it as normal. That is how public life is hollowed out. Not through one grand rupture, but through repeated demonstrations that power can be used to strip meaning from the office and leave only spectacle, compliance, and invoice.
