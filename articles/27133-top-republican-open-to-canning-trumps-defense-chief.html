Top Republican open to canning Trump’s defense chief

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 7, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that House Oversight Committee Chair James Comer said President Donald Trump must decide whether to replace Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, while adding that there are “a lot of questions” about Hegseth and no clear strategy for Iran. Comer linked Republican dissatisfaction over Iran and gas prices to MAGA voters apparently staying home.

Other Republicans, including Sen. Thom Tillis and Reps. Thomas Massie and Don Bacon, also supported removing Hegseth. Massie had introduced articles of impeachment that, according to the report, would force a House vote when members returned the following week. The article does not report that Trump had decided to dismiss Hegseth.

Comer also said Speaker Mike Johnson was stepping down and predicted that Steve Scalise or Jim Jordan would likely succeed him. The report supplies no date for these developments beyond describing Comer’s interview as occurring on Friday, so their present status cannot be established from this account alone.

Power sits above Hegseth

Hegseth runs the Pentagon, but Trump appointed him and retains the power to fire him. Comer acknowledged that hierarchy, then carefully stopped short of demanding action. His posture allows Republican leaders to register voter anger without accepting responsibility for the administration’s Iran policy.

That distinction matters. A defense secretary can execute policy, shape military advice and mismanage a department. But the article does not establish that Hegseth independently chose the Iran strategy Comer now condemns. Replacing him would change the administrator while leaving the president who controls the executive branch in place.

A scapegoat needs a sponsor

AlterNet explicitly describes Hegseth as a proposed scapegoat for the reported failure of the Iran war. The evidence presented supports the existence of a removal campaign, but it does not establish who designed the strategy or whether Trump accepted it.

Still, the political utility is plain. Republicans can assign failure to one removable official, advertise a personnel change as accountability and avoid examining who authorized the underlying course. Firing Hegseth might be justified on grounds not developed here. It would not, by itself, explain the policy or transfer responsibility away from those with greater authority.

Comer names the electoral consequence

Comer’s concern is not abstract oversight. He says loyal MAGA voters are angry about Iran and gas prices, that some apparently refused to vote, and that Republicans must regain their trust. His own account places electoral damage—not a newly discovered institutional principle—at the center of the leadership debate.

That does not make voter anger illegitimate. It exposes the incentive governing the response. Republican leaders tolerated the administration’s chain of command until its consequences threatened their coalition. Accountability arrives here as campaign repair.

The diversion is almost automatic

After discussing Republican leadership failures, Comer pivoted to attacking new Democratic members as socialists. The host cut him off, but the maneuver was revealing: when scrutiny reaches the governing party’s decisions, redirect attention toward a familiar ideological enemy.

The weaker actors in this story are not Democratic newcomers. They did not appoint Hegseth, direct the Pentagon or determine the administration’s Iran policy. Invoking them obscures the actual distribution of authority—and the Republican officials now trying to distance themselves from decisions made within their own government.

Personnel changes can preserve the system

The larger pattern is managed blame. Powerful institutions frequently answer policy failure by sacrificing a subordinate, changing leadership and declaring the matter resolved. The individual may deserve removal, but removal becomes misdirection when it substitutes for tracing who decided what, who enabled it and who benefited from avoiding scrutiny.

The central question is therefore not whether Hegseth keeps his job. It is whether Congress examines the full chain of authority behind the Iran policy Comer says Republicans cannot explain. If lawmakers stop at one expendable official, the firing will function less as accountability than as insulation for the people with more power.
