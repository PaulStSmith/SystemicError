Who’s really to blame for the US’ historic defense blunder

The Arsenal of Denial

A War Without a Ledger

Donald Trump says the war with Iran is necessary to prevent an Iranian nuclear weapon. Meanwhile, the conflict—now in its seventh month—is consuming munitions the United States cannot quickly replace. Critical missile stockpiles are shrinking, domestic production is inadequate, and new supplies could take years to arrive.

That is enough context. The political question is not whether the shortage exists. It is why officials with the power to confront it chose war, denial, and delay instead.

The Commander in Chief Owns the Escalation

Responsibility for decades of industrial decline spans multiple administrations and Congresses. Responsibility for ordering today’s military into unbudgeted combat does not.

Trump holds command authority. His administration continued operations in the Caribbean and entered a prolonged war with Iran without Congress first providing for the resulting weapons demand. Confronted with the shortage, he called it a fiction spread by a “treasonous” media. That is not strategic ambiguity. It is propaganda aimed at making acknowledgment of a material constraint look like disloyalty.

The consequence is immediate: military options narrow as inventories fall. A president cannot plausibly claim that a war is indispensable while dismissing the physical limits on sustaining it.

Congress Funds the Machine—and Avoids the Choice

Congress possesses the spending power and shares responsibility for the military posture it repeatedly authorized. Yet lawmakers allowed weapons inventories to age and manufacturing capacity to wither after the Cold War. Now they are again delaying decisions that require bipartisan votes and explicit tradeoffs.

Calling this “dithering” risks making institutional cowardice sound like absent-mindedness. Legislators have incentives to approve grand strategy, military commitments, bases, salaries, and politically protected programs without admitting that readiness requires sustained industrial planning. They preserve the appearance of limitless force while postponing the bills that make force possible.

Roughly 60 percent of the military budget is already committed to salaries, facilities, training, and exercises. The remaining share must cover modernization, innovation, equipment, and replenishment. That structure was known before the current war. Choosing additional combat without budgeting for it was therefore not confusion. It was evasion.

“Running Out of Bullets” Conceals the Real Failure

The shortage metaphor is vivid but politically incomplete. The United States did not simply misplace its ammunition. White Houses and Congresses chose not to renew aging stockpiles. The government allowed manufacturing capacity to atrophy. Officials continued to promise global responsiveness without maintaining the production system required to support it.

Blame can also be misdirected toward the military itself, as though the armed forces independently designed the budget, selected the wars, and neglected the factories. They did not. Civilian leaders established the commitments, appropriated the money, and accepted the gap between strategic promises and industrial capacity.

Even opening the spending spigot now would not erase those choices. Missile production cannot be summoned by press release; replacement supplies would take at least two years and probably longer. Industrial capacity is a material constraint, not a slogan awaiting better messaging.

Omnipotence as Political Branding

The larger deception is the cultivated belief that American military power has no meaningful limits. Presidents gain authority from projecting boundless reach. Members of Congress gain political cover by funding the image of strength while avoiding the durable investments and public accounting that strength requires.

Trump’s denial sharpens that pattern into something more authoritarian: inconvenient reporting becomes “treason,” and evidence of depleted capacity becomes an attack on the state. The purpose of that rhetoric is not to resolve the shortage. It is to punish scrutiny and protect the leader’s claim of infallibility.

The contradiction is stark. The administration invokes national survival to justify war, then treats evidence about the nation’s ability to fight as politically forbidden.

The System Spends Power Before It Builds It

This is not fundamentally a story about ammunition. It is a story about a governing system that mistakes declarations of dominance for the infrastructure of power.

For decades, presidents and legislators consumed the benefits of military primacy while neglecting its industrial foundations. Trump inherited that failure, then compounded it by committing scarce weapons to new, unbudgeted battles and denying the resulting constraint. Congress enabled the posture and avoided the reckoning.

The missiles are depleted because accountability was depleted first.
