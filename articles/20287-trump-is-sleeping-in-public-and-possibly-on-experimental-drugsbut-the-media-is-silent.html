Trump is sleeping in public and possibly on experimental drugs—but the media is silent

Based on an article by John Stoehr

Paulo SantosJuly 2, 20263 min read

The President Who Can Skip the Work and Still Own the Credit

The Ceremony Was Theater

The source story centers on a housing bill that would become law with or without Donald Trump’s signature, and on his decision to skip the ceremony anyway. That matters because the ceremony was not governance. It was credit assignment. Trump was supposed to stand in front of a bipartisan bill and harvest the optics while doing nothing to change the outcome.

That is the first clue to the political structure here: the institutional work was already done by Congress, while Trump was invited to launder it through his name. He declined the stage, not the power.

Power Is Not the Same as Attendance

Ted Lieu’s comments treat Trump’s absence as a question of health, drug access, and capacity. Those may be fair questions, but they are not the core problem. The core problem is that Trump still sits inside a system that lets him turn personal volatility into institutional weather. If he is erratic, the White House absorbs the noise. If he is unavailable, others manage the optics. If he wants special access to drugs, the article suggests there are channels, doctors, and connections that can make that happen.

That is not accidental dysfunction. It is what protected power looks like when it is built for one man’s convenience.

The Real Misdirection Is Familiar

The piece correctly notices how quickly the attention can be dragged toward Trump’s age, possible medication, or a hidden medical explanation. But the sharper question is who benefits when the conversation stops there. Trump’s damage is rarely treated as a deliberate exercise of power. It gets softened into eccentricity, confusion, or “Trump being Trump.”

That framing is useful to him. It turns accountability into personality gossip. It makes institutional capture look like a mood swing.

Republicans Get Default Legitimacy

Julia Azari’s point is the hardest and most important one in the source: Republicans are treated as people who naturally belong to power, while Democrats are made to prove they deserve it. That difference explains why Biden’s age became a referendum on legitimacy, while Trump’s age can still be filed under the mythology of strength.

This is not neutrality. It is hierarchy. The press and political class do not merely describe power; they recognize some holders of power as more authentic than others. Trump benefits from that arrangement because he represents the existing structure of power, even when he behaves recklessly inside it.

The White Status Quo Knows Its Own

The article reaches for a deeper pattern, and it is right to do so. The source links Trump’s protected status to an older American habit: treating rural white majorities as the nation’s authentic core and treating challenges to that order as suspect. That is why Biden was asked to justify himself while Trump was allowed to perform disorder without losing his place.

In that sense, the problem is not just age or media bias. It is a political culture that still confuses dominance with legitimacy and treats the old hierarchy as the natural one.

The Pattern Behind the Noise

What this story reveals is not that Trump is mysterious. It is that American institutions still bend to protect men who symbolize the inherited order, even when they are visibly deteriorating or behaving erratically. The ceremony, the drug speculation, the aging narrative, the media double standard: all of it points to the same structure.

Power here is not embarrassed by contradiction. It runs on it.
