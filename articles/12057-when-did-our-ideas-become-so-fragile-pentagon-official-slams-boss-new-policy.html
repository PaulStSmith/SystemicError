'When did our ideas become so fragile?' Pentagon official slams boss' new policy

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 5, 20255 min read

Pete Hegseth (Reuters)

There is a war on ideas in the Pentagon, according to a new report from The Atlantic.

The news site noted on Monday that many top defense leaders and generals were headed to the Aspen Security Forum when Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth put a stop to it and blocked further participation in any event unless vetted by his own team.

On social media, DOD spokesperson Sean Parnell claimed that the forum was “hosted by an organization that promotes the evils of globalism, disdain for America, and hatred for our great president, Donald J. Trump.”

Atlantic reporter Nancy Youssef noted that earlier this year, the DOD killed the Office of Net Assessment, which aimed to create internal assessments of U.S. readiness. Hegseth then blocked a shipment of weapons to Ukraine, claiming his own readiness assessment. All of the decisions came from Hegseth's close group of advisors, defense officials said.

The new isolated approach to ideas is new, The Atlantic reported.

"The U.S. military has had a symbiotic relationship with think tanks for years," the report stated. "While government employees and military officers are mired in day-to-day operations and focused on tactical warfare, outside scholars have the time and space for engaging in strategic thinking and coming up with solutions to thorny problems."

Former U.S. assistant secretary of defense for strategy, Mara Karlin, said, “So often in government, you are choosing between awful options. You think you have found the least-bad options, and places like think tanks allow you to test that conclusion."

Retired Army Col. Pete Mansoor told The Atlantic that Hegseth is emphasizing "lethality" over strategic thinking. He said that such coordination and conversation at conferences can foster more innovation.

"The fact that officers stopped thinking strategically and only thought about lethality resulted in a war that was almost lost in Iraq,” Mansoor said. “I’m sure the Russian army also stresses lethality, but they have educated their generals on the basis of a million casualties” in Ukraine.

Barring participation in conferences makes the U.S. just a little more like China and Russia, Youssef said.

“When did our ideas become so fragile that they can’t stand up to someone who has alternate views?” said one official.

Youssef wrote that it was only after the new anti-conference policy was announced that Pentagon officials fully understood how many conferences military staff attended each year. Officials were forced to draft the official policy with guidelines, said one official.

Vice President J.D. Vance went to the Munich Security Conference this year and former Vice President Kamala Harris went to conferences as well. Secretary Pete Hegseth attended the International Institute for Strategic Studies’ Shangri-La Dialogue, in Singapore. Later this year, the Ronald Reagan Presidential Library & Museum will host its own massive national security conference.

The latter is ironic, since Hegseth and Trump frequently cite the Reagan phrase "peace through strength."

"Yet, by Hegseth’s own directive, no one knows whether he or the troops he urges to embrace that approach will be able to attend the conference that celebrates it," Youssef closed.

Our Analysis:

The Pentagon's War on Ideas: A Critical Analysis

In a recent revelation by The Atlantic, the Pentagon's new strategy under Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth has sparked a significant controversy, marking a clear departure from traditional military engagement with external intellectual resources. This piece aims to dissect the multifaceted implications of these actions, highlighting the inherent contradiction within the Department of Defense's (DoD) new approach.

Blocking the Brain Trust

At the heart of the controversy is Hegseth's decision to block top defense leaders and generals from attending the Aspen Security Forum, a move that sends ripples through the foundational relationship between the military and think tanks. For years, this symbiotic relationship has provided a platform for strategic thinking and problem-solving, far removed from the immediacy and tunnel vision of day-to-day operations and tactical warfare. The rationale, as per a DoD spokesperson, is the forum's alleged promotion of "the evils of globalism, disdain for America, and hatred for our great president, Donald J. Trump." This statement, devoid of substantive evidence, raises questions about the broader implications of isolating the military from global discourse.

Strategic Myopia

The decision to eliminate the Office of Net Assessment and block critical weapons shipments to Ukraine under the guise of a "readiness assessment" further exemplifies a narrowing of strategic vision. Hegseth's reliance on a close circle of advisors for these decisions underscores a worrying shift towards echo chamber governance, where dissenting or alternative viewpoints are not just undervalued but actively suppressed.

Lethality Over Strategy

Retired Army Col. Pete Mansoor's observation that Hegseth is prioritizing "lethality" over strategic thinking strikes at the core of this issue. The reduction of military effectiveness to mere firepower, without the balancing act of strategic foresight, risks repeating historical military blunders. The reference to the almost-lost war in Iraq and the Russian military's heavy casualties in Ukraine serve as stark reminders of where such imbalance can lead.

Isolationism's Irony

The irony of Hegseth's actions, especially in light of his and former President Trump's frequent citations of Reagan's "peace through strength" mantra, cannot be overstated. The decision to potentially exclude the Pentagon from the Ronald Reagan Presidential Library & Museum's national security conference is emblematic of the contradiction between the administration's professed ideals and its actual policies.

A Fragile Foundation

The question posed by an official, "When did our ideas become so fragile that they can’t stand up to someone who has alternate views?" encapsulates the crux of the matter. The strength of a nation's military and defense strategy lies not just in its arsenal but in the robustness of its ideas and the diversity of its strategic considerations. The shift towards isolation and intellectual homogeneity not only undermines the United States' position on the global stage but also diminishes its capacity to innovate and adapt.

Conclusion: The Perils of Intellectual Isolation

The Pentagon's war on ideas, as outlined by The Atlantic, is a troubling development that threatens to undermine the very foundation of military excellence: the ability to think strategically and engage with a broad spectrum of ideas. In eschewing the insights and challenge that external think tanks and forums provide, the DoD risks entering a state of strategic atrophy, where might is mistaken for right, and echo chambers dictate defense policy. The true strength of a nation's defense lies in its capacity to engage with and adapt to the complex, evolving nature of global security challenges, a capacity that is severely compromised by the current administration's insular approach.
