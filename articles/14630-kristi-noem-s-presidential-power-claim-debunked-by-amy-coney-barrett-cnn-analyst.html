Kristi Noem's presidential power claim debunked by Amy Coney Barrett: CNN analyst

President Donald Trump's Homeland Security secretary's idea of the government's power to suspend habeas corpus has been discredited, CNN fact-checker Daniel Dale told anchors on Tuesday — by a Supreme Court justice Trump himself appointed.

Noem was grilled earlier in the day by senators over the issue of habeas, or the right of a prisoner — migrants singled out for deportation, in this case — to ask a judge to review whether their detention is lawful.

"I believe that if any president that was able to do that [suspend habeas] in the past, it should be afforded to our current day president," Noem said. "This president has never said he's going to do this ... but I do think the Constitution allows them the right to consider it."

But Sen. Andy Kim (D-NJ) had a swift response. "Do you know what section of the Constitution, the suspension clause of habeas corpus? Article I. Do you know which branch of government Article I outlines the tasks and the responsibilities for? Congress."

Kim's interpretation, while not universal, is broadly favored, Dale noted.

"So that's correct," he said. "This power to suspend the writ of habeas corpus is in Article I of the Constitution, which outlines and provides limitations on the powers of Congress, not the president. Now, the clause doesn't specifically say that it is Congress or the president that has the power. It kind of leaves this undefined."

However, he continued, "It's not just most legal scholars who say that it is Congress who has the power. A certain judge named Amy Coney Barrett, who, of course, went on to be appointed to the Supreme Court by President Trump, wrote in an article in the National Constitution Center website. She said the clause does not specify which branch of government has the authority to suspend the privilege of the writ, but 'most agree that only Congress can do it.' So this is a pretty widespread consensus."

"Now, I think it's also worth noting the actual text of this so-called suspension clause," Dale added. "It says the privilege of the writ of habeas corpus shall not be suspended 'unless when in cases of rebellion or invasion, the public safety may require it.' So one, it requires rebellion or invasion, very much unclear to be generous ... and number two, it says even in those cases, public safety needs to require it. So does it currently require it? When the president is boasting that the border is basically closed and no one is getting in? That's a pretty high hurdle to clear in the courts."

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Constitutional Controversy: Habeas Corpus and Presidential Power

In a recent revelation that could only be described as a constitutional facepalm, President Donald Trump's Homeland Security secretary has found herself at the center of a discredited idea that the government has the power to suspend habeas corpus. This idea was effectively debunked by a Supreme Court justice appointed by Trump himself. The irony is not lost on us, but let's dive deeper into the murky waters of this constitutional misinterpretation.

The Misguided Notion

The secretary, in a misguided attempt to argue for expanded executive powers, suggested that the president should have the right to consider suspending habeas corpus, a fundamental legal safeguard against unjust detention. This proposition is not only alarming but flies in the face of constitutional law and historical precedent. It's akin to suggesting that the executive branch should have a free pass to bypass the bedrock principles of justice and liberty. Absurd, right?

Congress vs. President: The Constitutional Showdown

Senator Andy Kim's response to this bewildering claim was nothing short of a constitutional mic drop. Citing Article I of the Constitution, he pointed out that the power to suspend habeas corpus lies with Congress, not the president. This isn't a matter of opinion but of constitutional fact. The suspension clause's placement within Article I, which outlines the powers and limitations of Congress, makes it clear who holds the reins on this issue.

CNN fact-checker Daniel Dale's input further discredits the secretary's argument, highlighting how even Amy Coney Barrett, a Trump-appointed Supreme Court justice, has stated that most agree only Congress can suspend habeas corpus. This consensus among legal scholars and justices alike underscores a fundamental misunderstanding or intentional misrepresentation of constitutional powers by the secretary.

The High Hurdle of Justification

Even if we were to entertain the notion of suspending habeas corpus (which, to be clear, is a daunting prospect), the conditions under which it can be considered are stringent. It requires a scenario of rebellion or invasion where public safety necessitates such an extreme measure. Given the current administration's claims of having a "basically closed" border, the argument for a public safety requirement is, at best, a far-fetched fantasy.

Conclusion: A Dangerous Misstep in Constitutional Interpretation

The attempt to argue for the suspension of habeas corpus by the president is not just a dangerous misinterpretation of the Constitution; it's a glaring example of the lengths some will go to expand executive power at the expense of civil liberties. The fact that this argument was so easily dismantled by factual constitutional interpretation and the words of a conservative Supreme Court justice is a testament to its fundamental flaws.

In the end, this episode serves as a stark reminder of the importance of understanding the Constitution and the protections it affords against the overreach of any branch of government. It's a lesson in the value of habeas corpus as a safeguard of personal freedom and a warning against the perils of misusing constitutional arguments for political ends. Let us not forget the wisdom in the checks and balances that define our democratic republic, ensuring no branch holds too much power, especially when it comes to the fundamental rights of individuals.
