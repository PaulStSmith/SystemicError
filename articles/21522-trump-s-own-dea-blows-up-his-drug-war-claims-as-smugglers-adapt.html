Trump's own DEA blows up his drug war claims as smugglers adapt

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Paulo SantosAugust 16, 20263 min read

Trump’s Boat War Didn’t Stop Cocaine. It Just Started Killing People.

The Claim, and the Damage

A DEA assessment, a closed congressional briefing, and interviews with officials have now punctured the administration’s preferred fantasy: months of strikes on suspected smuggling boats have not reduced cocaine flowing into the United States. Prices and purity stayed basically flat. Traffickers adapted. The market kept moving. The boats kept getting hit anyway.

That is the core fact, and it matters because the policy was never sold as theater. It was sold as dominance. Trump claimed repeated, implausible victories. The evidence says something simpler and uglier: the state killed people, declared success, and failed to change the underlying flow of drugs.

Who Had Power

The power here sits with the White House, not with smugglers, not with DEA analysts, and not with the coastal patrols whose bodies and judgments get used as props after the fact. Trump’s administration chose the escalation, ordered or endorsed the strikes, and then defended them after the results collapsed under scrutiny.

That is the political structure the story reveals. The people with authority to launch lethal force also get to narrate its meaning. The people forced to absorb the consequences are treated as interchangeable targets, statistics, or “bad actors.” That is not confusion. It is a governing choice.

The Violence Was the Policy

At least 221 people have been killed in strikes on more than 67 boats since September. That is not a side effect. That is the policy’s visible output.

Legal analysts calling these attacks extrajudicial killings are not reaching for drama; they are naming what the administration is trying to launder through the language of counter-narcotics. If the government can kill suspected traffickers at sea without proving cases, then the point is no longer interdiction. It is the normalization of unreviewed state violence wrapped in the moral cover of drug war rhetoric.

The White House calls this deterrence. The facts describe something much closer to publicly managed killing with no demonstrated strategic gain.

Misdirection as Governance

The administration wants the public to watch the boats and ignore the machinery. That is the trick. It turns a transnational supply chain into a morality play about enemy smugglers, then pretends the body count is proof of seriousness.

But the DEA assessment says the smugglers adapted quickly: larger vessels, coastal routes, clandestine aircraft, bribery, and shifts through Colombia and Venezuela. That is not a sign of triumph. It is a sign that violent disruption pushes trafficking into different channels without touching the larger system. The government keeps swinging at the visible symptom and then congratulates itself when the symptom moves.

That is how scapegoating works in national security politics. The weakest links get destroyed, the headline gets made, and the structural problem survives untouched.

The Real Machine

The more important finding is not just that cocaine prices stayed stable in the United States. It is that global production is at record highs, cultivation and manufacturing have surged over the past decade, and experts tie that boom to rising overdose deaths. The decisive terrain is production, supply networks, corruption, and demand, not the handful of boats the administration can turn into burnt wreckage.

Even worse, the strikes may be degrading the government’s own intelligence capacity. Informants who once helped investigators build trafficking cases are reportedly pulling back because they fear getting killed in a strike. That is what happens when a government confuses fear with strategy. It burns the human infrastructure needed for actual enforcement, then uses the resulting blindness as another excuse for more force.

What This Story Really Shows

This is not a story about one failed anti-drug operation. It is a story about a political class that reaches for lethal spectacle when it has no serious answer to a structural problem. Trump gets to perform toughness. The White House gets to declare resolve. The dead get erased into policy language. The drug flow barely changes.

That is the deeper pattern: authoritarian politics prefers visible punishment to measurable results. It selects targets it can kill, not systems it can solve. And when the evidence shows the violence has failed, it does not retreat. It doubles down and asks the public to confuse cruelty with competence.
