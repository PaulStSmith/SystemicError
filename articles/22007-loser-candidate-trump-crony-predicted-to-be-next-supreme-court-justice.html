Loser candidate Trump crony predicted to be next Supreme Court justice

Trump Is Building a Court of Personal Attorneys

Trump’s latest promotion of Will Scharf, his White House staff secretary and former criminal defense lawyer, is being treated as a parlor-game prediction about a future Supreme Court vacancy. That framing is too small. The real story is that Trump keeps elevating lawyers who have already proven their usefulness to him personally, not to any neutral legal standard. Scharf has worked on Trump’s defense, worked on the immunity case, and now sits even closer to the center of executive power.

Loyalty, Not Law

Josh Orton’s central point is the one that matters: Trump does not appear to be selecting legal talent for public service. He is selecting loyalists for personal protection. Scharf’s résumé matters only insofar as it shows proximity to Trump’s private legal needs. In this system, the credential is not independence. The credential is obedience.

That is not a minor insult to institutional norms. It is the operating principle. A president who treats the legal branch as a staffing pipeline for his own defense team is not respecting the law as an institution. He is repurposing it as a shield.

Where Power Actually Sits

The institutional power here is obvious: the president decides the staffing, shapes the White House legal shop, and controls the path to any eventual Supreme Court nomination. Scharf’s rise is not the story of a brilliant jurist ascending through merit. It is the story of a loyal operative moving upward through proximity.

The article’s own details make the pattern plain. Scharf served on Trump’s criminal defense team from 2023 to 2025. He worked on the presidential immunity case before the Supreme Court in 2024. He lost a Missouri attorney general primary. None of that is disqualifying in Trumpworld. It is qualification by another name: proven allegiance under pressure.

The Credential Theater

The CNN panel’s question about what credentials Trump might cite if he nominated Scharf is exactly the kind of polite Washington deflection that keeps the deeper point at arm’s length. Credentials are not the issue. They are the costume.

The real question is what kind of judge Trump wants. Orton says it directly: not someone well-credentialed, not someone smart, but someone loyal. That should not be treated as a joke, even if the panel laughed. It is a blunt description of patronage politics moving through the machinery of constitutional appointment.

The Normalization Racket

The softer frame is that this is just Trump being Trump, a familiar blend of favoritism and impulse. That framing is too forgiving. Deliberate loyalty-picking is not randomness. It is institutional design. Trump has already shown that he understands the courts as a site of enforcement for his own project, not as a check on it.

That matters because the judiciary is one of the last places where personal allegiance can be disguised as jurisprudence. A loyalist justice is not just another conservative vote. It is a durable investment in impunity, one more node in a structure built to absorb legal scrutiny and return it as permission.

The Pattern

This is what authoritarian politics looks like when it is still wearing a suit and speaking in the language of qualifications. The operative is promoted. The lawyer becomes the loyalist. The loyalist is floated as a future justice. And the media is invited to ask whether he has enough credentials, as if the problem were insufficient polish rather than captured purpose.

The larger lesson is simple: when power becomes personal, every institution is pressured to become personal too. Trump does not merely want allies. He wants translated institutions, staffed by people whose first obligation is to him. That is the real story here, and it is not a hypothetical. It is already happening.
