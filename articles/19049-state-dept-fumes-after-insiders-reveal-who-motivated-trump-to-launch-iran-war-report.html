President Donald Trump’s decision to kick off the U.S. war against Iran last month was motivated, in part, by “pressure from outside allies,” multiple insiders told Bloomberg in its report Saturday, a revelation that sparked a fierce rebuttal from the Trump administration.

Speaking with Bloomberg on the condition of anonymity, the insiders claimed that Trump was under pressure to strike Iran from at least two individuals outside his administration: Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu – who’s wanted by the International Court of Justice for alleged war crimes – and Rupert Murdoch, the billionaire conservative media mogul and architect of Fox News.

“[Murdoch] communicated with Trump several times as he urged the president to take on Tehran, according to one person briefed on their interactions,” Bloomberg’s report reads.

“Meanwhile, some of Trump’s closest advisers were more muted about the prospect of an armed conflict, including Vice President JD Vance, Secretary of State Marco Rubio and White House Chief of Staff Susie Wiles, the people said.”

When asked for comment, a State Department spokesperson accused Bloomberg’s sources of “not knowing what they are talking about,” and “pretending that they do.”

“There is no division,” said State Department spokesperson Tommy Piggot, speaking with Bloomberg. “President Trump is making the world safer, and the entire administration is lockstep in that effort.”

In spite of Piggot’s claim, division did exist within the Trump administration over the Iran war, made evident with the recentresignation of Joe Kent, the head of the National Counterterrorism Center, who stepped down from his position in protest and alleged that Trump was manipulated byIsraelinto launching the conflict.

Our Analysis:

Unraveling the Threads: The Decision to Wage War on Iran

The Bloomberg article on President Donald Trump’s decision to initiate a war against Iran is a complex narrative interwoven with allegations of external pressure, internal division, and vehement denials from the administration. At the heart of this story are claims that Trump was swayed by Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and media mogul Rupert Murdoch. This analysis aims to dissect the institutional power dynamics, decision-making processes, and the framing of the article to provide a clearer understanding of where responsibility lies.

Institutional Power and Decision-Making

Who Holds the Power?

The power to declare war, under the U.S. Constitution, is a shared responsibility. While Congress has the power to declare war, the President, as Commander-in-Chief, has significant leeway in military engagements. However, the decision-making process in this context seems to have circumvented the broader institutional checks and balances, focusing instead on the influence of external allies and advisers.

Who Made the Decision?

The article directly attributes the decision to engage in war with Iran to President Trump. Despite the presence of internal dissent within his administration, as evidenced by the resignation of Joe Kent and the muted responses from key advisers like Vice President JD Vance, Secretary of State Marco Rubio, and Chief of Staff Susie Wiles, the ultimate decision rested with Trump. This aligns with the institutional powers vested in the presidency but raises questions about the influence of external actors on such critical decisions.

External Influences vs. Internal Decision-Making

The framing of the Bloomberg article suggests a significant role played by Netanyahu and Murdoch in persuading Trump to take military action against Iran. While the President indeed has the final say, the emphasis on these external pressures overlooks the internal dynamics and Trump’s own agency in the decision-making process. It is crucial to distinguish between influence and decision-making authority; while Netanyahu and Murdoch may have advocated for action against Iran, the choice to proceed lay solely with Trump and his administration.

Misdirection and Responsibility

Framing and Misdirection

The article’s narrative potentially misdirects responsibility by emphasizing the ‘pressure’ from Netanyahu and Murdoch. This framing could lead readers to attribute the decision more to external influences than to Trump himself, who, despite any pressures, had the ultimate authority and responsibility to decide on matters of war.

Scapegoating vs. Accountability

There is a thin line between recognizing influence and absolving internal decision-makers of their responsibility. While it is essential to understand the context and factors leading to such a pivotal decision, it is equally crucial not to allow this understanding to morph into a scapegoating mechanism that shifts blame away from those with actual decision-making power.

Conclusion

The decision to wage war against Iran, as reported by Bloomberg, underscores the complex interplay between external influence and internal decision-making authority. While it is clear that President Trump faced pressures from figures like Netanyahu and Murdoch, the responsibility for the decision rests with him and his administration. The framing of the article, while shedding light on these pressures, should not distract from the central fact that institutional power and the capacity to make such a decision resided within the White House. Recognizing influence should not equate to absolving internal actors of their responsibilities. In the analysis of political narratives, it is imperative to maintain a clear distinction between those who can persuade and those who ultimately decide.
