Trump’s budget rips nearly $3 billion from swing state’s coffers

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 16, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Wisconsin could forgo as much as $2.76 billion in state income-tax revenue from 2028 through 2037 if lawmakers adopt deductions for tipped and overtime income resembling federal policy. Trump’s federal tax law does not itself remove that money from Wisconsin’s treasury; the projected loss depends on a separate state decision.

Forward Analytics, the nonpartisan research arm of the Wisconsin Counties Association, found that benefits would vary by occupation and income source. In its example, a waiter earning $25,000 in wages and $15,000 in tips would save $716 more annually than an administrative assistant earning the same $40,000 entirely through wages.

Democratic Gov. Tony Evers vetoed Republican-backed exemptions in April, arguing that they offered no permanent relief and would tether Wisconsin’s budget to shifting federal policy. Yet Evers had proposed eliminating state income tax on cash tips in his 2025–27 budget, a provision Republican legislators later removed in committee.

The headline assigns power badly

Trump and congressional Republicans created the federal template and the political slogan. They did not enact the Wisconsin tax change behind the $2.76 billion estimate.

That distinction is not trivia. AlterNet’s framing says Trump’s budget “rips” money from Wisconsin, while its own evidence says Wisconsin “could lose” the revenue only if state officials pass similar legislation. The institutional power over this projected state-budget loss rests with Wisconsin’s Legislature and governor.

A political system cannot be analyzed accurately by collapsing federal advocacy, state legislation and contingent projections into one act. Doing so lets the officials holding the decisive lever disappear behind a more nationally recognizable villain.

A slogan disguised as worker policy

“No tax on tips” sounds like a straightforward raise. It is actually a selective deduction whose value depends on how income is classified. Workers with identical annual earnings can receive unequal tax treatment solely because one person’s compensation is labeled tips.

The policy also does not ordinarily increase a worker’s weekly paycheck immediately, according to the reporting. Eligible taxpayers would generally claim the benefit when filing annual state returns. That gap between campaign language and tax administration matters: Republicans can advertise instant economic solidarity while delivering a delayed, uneven benefit.

The waiter-versus-administrative-assistant example exposes the policy’s arbitrary architecture. It does not systematically target low income, hardship or need. It privileges particular forms of compensation.

Wisconsin Republicans own the choice

Republican legislators largely supported the exemptions Evers vetoed, but they also removed his narrower proposal for cash tips from the state budget. That inconsistency suggests the dispute is not simply over whether tipped workers deserve relief. It is also over who controls the policy, how broadly it applies and who receives political credit.

Evers is not outside that contradiction. He opposed the Republican bills while having advanced his own tipped-income exemption. His stated objection concerned permanence and dependence on federal policy, not an absolute rejection of preferential treatment for tips.

The relevant conflict is therefore not benevolent tax cutters against hostile opponents. It is a contest among officeholders promoting different versions of a politically attractive carveout while the long-term fiscal cost remains unresolved.

The missing side of the giveaway

A revenue reduction of as much as $2.76 billion over a decade is not an abstract accounting adjustment. If enacted, it would leave Wisconsin officials to replace the money, reduce spending, draw on other resources or accept a narrower fiscal margin. The reporting does not establish which consequence would follow, and it should not pretend otherwise.

It also does not identify what public functions that sum currently supports. That omission makes the tax benefit vivid and the cost nearly invisible. Individual savings receive a concrete example; the services, taxpayers or future budgets absorbing the loss do not.

The article further invokes an unreleased IRS report and voter dissatisfaction with the economy, but it does not establish how either changes Wisconsin’s legislative authority or the Forward Analytics estimate. Those details enlarge the partisan atmosphere without clarifying the decision at issue.

The system rewards selective arithmetic

This is the familiar machinery of tax politics: advertise the beneficiary, obscure the financing and postpone the institutional consequences. Federal Republicans supplied the slogan. Wisconsin lawmakers decide whether to import its fiscal design. Evers has exercised veto power while supporting a narrower version of the same basic preference.

The larger pattern is not that one president can simply raid a swing state’s treasury. It is that elected officials at multiple levels can market targeted deductions as uncomplicated relief, while political coverage blurs who has authority to enact them and who will be accountable for the resulting hole. Precision about power is not a courtesy to those officials. It is the minimum requirement for assigning responsibility.
