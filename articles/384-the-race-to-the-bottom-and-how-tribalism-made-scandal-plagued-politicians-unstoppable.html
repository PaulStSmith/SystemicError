The race to the bottom — and how tribalism made scandal-plagued politicians unstoppable

Based on an article by The Conversation

Paulo SantosJune 21, 20262 min read

Parties Reward Rot, Then Blame the Voters

The Setup

The source story is simple enough: Texas Republicans nominated Ken Paxton, an impeached attorney general facing corruption and bribery accusations, over John Cornyn. Maine Democrats nominated Graham Platner, a political novice carrying allegations of sexual misconduct and a SS tattoo scandal, over Governor Janet Mills. The article treats this as a puzzle about voter psychology. It is also a story about institutions choosing damage because they think damage is still useful.

Who Actually Has Power

The real power in both races does not sit with the most principled candidate, or even the median voter. It sits with the party machinery, the donors, the elected officials who rally behind the nominee after the fact, and the primary electorates shaped by years of partisan conditioning. Paxton did not become the Republican nominee by accident. Platner did not become the Democratic nominee in a vacuum. Party networks and political elites create the conditions where scandal becomes survivable if the candidate is useful enough.

The Cover Story

The article leans hard on polarization and negative partisanship, which are real forces. But that framework can become a polite way to hide agency. Voters do not spontaneously produce these outcomes in a sealed laboratory. Parties choose candidates, clear fields, defend them, and then tell everyone the system made them do it. That is misdirection. The problem is not just that Americans dislike the other side. The problem is that party leaders keep presenting contemptible people as the price of admission to power.

Scandal As a Feature

Paxton’s baggage was already known, including impeachment by a Republican-led state House. Platner’s scandals were also known, including the alleged misconduct and the Nazi-linked tattoo he claims he did not understand. These are not hidden defects discovered by surprise. They are public liabilities that the parties decided were acceptable collateral. The article frames this as a product of fear: the other side might win. That is only half the story. The deeper truth is that both parties have learned to treat scandal as manageable if the candidate is loyal, mobilizable, and useful for partisan warfare.

What The Framing Hides

The article is strongest when it describes how partisans harden around their side after scandals. It is weakest when it implies that this is mainly a psychological drift rather than a political strategy. The more important story is institutional cowardice. Leaders who know better still bless the nominee, because backing away would require admitting the party’s standards are performative. They would rather normalize the rot than risk a loss. That is not confusion. It is choice.

The Larger Pattern

These races are not anomalies. They are what happens when parties become less interested in selecting competent public servants than in weaponizing loyalty against the other side. Once winning becomes the only real standard, scandal stops disqualifying people and starts sorting them. The result is a political system that keeps rewarding damage, then asks voters to feel guilty for noticing.
