Republican Senators Pretend They’re Spectators While the Pentagon Bleeds Talent

The Power Is Not Missing

The source is simple enough: Pete Hegseth is under fire for pushing out experienced military officials during a mounting war with Iran, and Sen. Thom Tillis has already said the Pentagon needs new leadership. When Kristen Welker put the question to Sen. John Kennedy, he performed the oldest trick in Washington: act like a senator has no authority over the administration his party installed.

Kennedy’s Dodge Exposes the Game

Kennedy’s line, “It’s not my place to have confidence in him,” was not humility. It was evasion. Senators confirm cabinet officials, oversee them, fund their departments, and can pressure the White House when a secretary becomes a liability. Kennedy knows that. The dodge matters because it reveals the Republican posture toward Trump’s cabinet: maximum protection, minimum accountability, and just enough public wobble to look serious.

The Real Admission

The tell came at the end. Kennedy said he was “not worried about the personalities” and would “probably vote for him again.” That is the governing philosophy in one sentence. Republicans are not evaluating whether Hegseth can manage the Pentagon during a war. They are asking whether he remains useful inside a loyalty-driven regime. Competence is secondary. The personnel damage is treated as a side effect, not a failure.

Misdirection as Cover

Tillis at least named the problem: “inept management” and the loss of “experienced military talent.” But even that critique stays too polite if the outcome is people being pushed out while the country is in a live conflict. The scandal is not just that Hegseth is unpopular. It is that Republican power brokers keep treating catastrophic management as a personality dispute. That framing launders political responsibility. It turns deliberate degradation into mere turbulence.

The Senate’s Cowardice Has a Pattern

This is what institutional decay looks like when it is dressed up as oversight. Republican senators retain the power to constrain a dangerous cabinet and choose not to use it. They preserve their procedural dignity while backing the same machine that empties expertise out of government. Then they act offended when asked to defend the result. That is not governance. It is complicity with a grift of state capacity.

What This Story Really Shows

The larger pattern is familiar: Trump-aligned officials hollow out institutions, Republican lawmakers avert their eyes, and public language is engineered to make that collapse sound like ordinary personnel churn. The damage is concrete. The military loses experienced people. The Pentagon is managed by a former television personality under heavy criticism. And senators who could intervene settle for smirking disclaimers and private excuses. The system is not confused. Its enablers are.
