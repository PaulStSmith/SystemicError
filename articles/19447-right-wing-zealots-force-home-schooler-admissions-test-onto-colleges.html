Right-wing zealots force 'home-schooler' admissions test onto colleges

Indystar reports Indiana’s Gov. Mike Braun has signed a new bill forcing state colleges and universities to include a “classics-based” examination embraced by religious colleges in Republican states.

For decades, the ACT and the SAT have been the gatekeepers and the standard-bearer of college admissions to measure a student’s aptitude in core subjects like math, science and reading. But proponents said the test “would better assess students who received a classical education, typically offered to homeschooled students or at private or charter classical schools,” according to the IndyStar.

The newly required Classical Learning Test aims to promote the “Western intellectual tradition” that right-wing supporters claim has been abandoned by existing standardized tests.

The change stems from a legislative bill that also “requires schools to teach a 2000s-era anti-poverty theory involving waiting until marriage to have kids as part of schools’ good citizenship instruction,” reports IndyStar. “It passed mostly along party lines amid criticism that the CLT could disadvantage students who are less accustomed to Western ideas.”

“It has baked-in prejudices that would make students who come from less diverse backgrounds appear to have done better than students from more diverse backgrounds,” said Joel Hand, a lobbyist for the American Federation of Teachers for Indiana, during Senate committee testimony in January.

Classic Learning Test creator Jeremy Tate applauded the bill’s passage in Indiana in an X post Feb. 24.

“While Tate has said the test is not partisan, his company’s expansive Board of Academic Advisors include administrators from religious colleges and right-wing figures like Kevin Roberts of the Heritage Foundation and PragerU CEO Marissa Streit,” reports IndyStar. “It’s also been promoted by Sen. Jim Banks, who called it the “standard for academic excellence.”

PragerU is not a university, but a conservative nonprofit known for producing wildly inaccurate educational videos. One PragerU video from its history series depicts abolitionist and former slave Frederick Douglass arguing that the founders’ decision not to abolish U.S. slavery was worth it because it helped convince the Southern colonies to join the Union.

“Our system is wonderful, and the Constitution is a glorious liberty document. We just need to convince enough Americans to be true to it,” the video depicts Douglass saying.

Teachers’ unions and academics also doubt the value of the Classic Learning Test considering its concepts and biased arguments.

Our Analysis:

Indiana’s Educational Shift: A Step Backwards?

In a move that can only be described as regressive, Indiana’s Governor Mike Braun has thrust the state’s educational system into the spotlight for all the wrong reasons. By signing a bill that mandates state colleges and universities to adopt a “classics-based” examination, Braun has essentially championed a vision for education that privileges a narrow, ideologically driven understanding of knowledge over a broad, inclusive, and modern curriculum. This decision does not just raise eyebrows; it sets off alarm bells about the direction in which education policy is headed in Republican-led states.

The Problem with “Classics-Based” Assessments

Let’s dissect the core of this policy: the imposition of the Classical Learning Test (CLT) over the universally recognized ACT and SAT. The CLT, according to its proponents, is tailored to students who have received a “classical education”, a phrase that in this context, is coded language for an education steeped in a Eurocentric, conservative worldview. This isn’t just a shift in educational standards; it’s a blatant attempt to reshape what educational excellence looks like through a politically and culturally conservative lens.

The promoters of this test, including Jeremy Tate, the creator of the CLT, tout it as a return to the “Western intellectual tradition”. Yet, one cannot help but question whose “tradition” we are returning to when the advisory board is peppered with figures from religious colleges and right-wing organizations like the Heritage Foundation and PragerU. The latter, it’s worth noting, is infamous for its misleading and often factually incorrect educational content. To align Indiana’s educational assessment with an organization that distorts history to fit a conservative narrative is not just irresponsible; it’s dangerously revisionist.

The Implications of a Narrow Curriculum

The IndyStar article rightly points out the criticism surrounding the CLT’s inherent biases. By privileging a “classics-based” examination, Indiana is not just sidelining students who come from diverse educational backgrounds; it’s actively disadvantaging them. This isn’t merely about different educational philosophies; it’s about creating a hierarchy of knowledge that values certain histories and cultures over others. Joel Hand’s testimony underscores a critical point: the CLT is likely to exacerbate educational inequalities by making students from less privileged backgrounds appear inferior.

Moreover, the bill’s requirement for schools to teach a 2000s-era anti-poverty theory that emphasizes waiting until marriage to have kids as part of good citizenship instruction is another example of how education policy is being used as a vehicle for conservative social agendas. This isn’t just about promoting good citizenship; it’s about imposing a moral and social framework on students that aligns with a particular ideological stance.

A Call to Action

The situation in Indiana should serve as a wake-up call. The adoption of the CLT is a step back into a past where education was more about indoctrination than enlightenment. It’s a move that prioritizes a narrow, exclusionary view of knowledge over a diverse, inclusive understanding of the world.

Educators, parents, and policymakers must resist this backward slide. Education should be about opening minds, not closing them to the rich diversity of human thought and experience. Indiana’s students deserve better than a curriculum that prepares them for a world that no longer exists—if it ever did. The fight for a fair, inclusive, and forward-thinking educational system is more crucial now than ever. It’s time to stand up against policies that seek to narrow the scope of knowledge under the guise of classical education. The future of our students, and indeed our society, depends on it.
