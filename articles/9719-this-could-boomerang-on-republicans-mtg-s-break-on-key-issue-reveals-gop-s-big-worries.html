​'This could boomerang on Republicans': MTG's break on key issue reveals GOP's big worries

By Paulo Santos

Marjorie Taylor Greene (Photo by Evelyn Hockstein for Reuters)

Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene’s break with her party on the key issue driving the government shutdown is a leading indicator of the political blowback Republicans are risking in the funding fight.

The Georgia Republican called for action on expiring Obamacare subsidies, which she complained would cause insurance premiums to double next year for her constituents, as well as her own adult children, and a former GOP lawmaker told “CNN This Morning” the far-right MAGA firebrand was signaling anxiety within the conservative coalition.

“Ordinarily the party making the policy demand typically gets blamed,” said former congressman Charlie Dent. “However, in this era of Trump with, you know, with the president himself saying a lot of good can happen in a shutdown, [budget] director] Russ Vought threatening to run wild and massively lay off federal employees. This could boomerang back on Republicans. But I don’t think anybody really wins this thing. I don’t think will have an impact on the midterms, either.”

Democratic strategist Antjuan Seawright wasn’t so sure, arguing the numbers at stake for Affordable Care Act participants were too big to ignore.

“Part of this truth decay has become a large component of this conversation,” Seawright said. “This conversation, this disagreement is not about illegals receiving health care or illegal immigrants, because you have to have a Social Security number in order to qualify for the ACA subsidies. What Republicans are conflating is Ronald Reagan’s law that says if you go to a hospital, you have to be seen, by law. Republicans have not rolled back that clause. But let me tell you what this is about: 18.2 million Americans in red states receive those ACA subsidies, 5.8 [million] in blue states, 76 percent of those who receive those subsidies are in places where Trump won.”

House Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA) and Senate Majority Leader John Thune (R-SD) are demanding Democrats cave on the health care subsidies and reopen the government first, but Seawright argued that Greene’s break showed that Republicans are worried about the issue.

“This is a bipartisan nonpartisan conversation, and if you are a working-class American in this country paying $350 to $450 a month for ACA, that’s going to go up to about $1,500 to $1,800 a month,” Seawright said. “So this idea that Republicans have been intentional about defunding health care and deprioritizing health care is an argument that I think Democrats should continue to elevate. Why? Because it will come back to haunt Republicans in the midterms.”

Our Analysis:

The Political Theatre of the Government Shutdown: A Closer Look

In a recent development that reads more like a plot twist in a political drama than the sober reality of governance, Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene, a figure often synonymous with the far-right faction of the Republican Party, has seemingly diverged from her party’s script. The issue at hand, the potential expiration of Obamacare subsidies, has forced Greene to publicly break ranks with her party colleagues. This move is not just a crack in the GOP façade but a glaring spotlight on the political quagmire that the government shutdown has become.

The Irony of Opposition

Rep. Greene’s outcry over the expiration of Obamacare subsidies—subsidies her party has historically loathed—is nothing short of ironic. It’s a testament to the complex reality of political ideologies versus the practical needs of constituents. When the rubber meets the road, or more accurately, when policy impacts pocketbooks, ideological purity becomes a luxury that even the most fervent political firebrands cannot afford. Greene’s concern over insurance premiums doubling, affecting not just her constituents but her own family, peels back the layers of political posturing to reveal the underlying truth: health care policy impacts real lives, transcending political affiliations.

The Blame Game and the Shutdown

Former congressman Charlie Dent’s analysis that the party making the policy demand typically gets blamed for a shutdown rings true in most conventional scenarios. However, these are not conventional times. The Trumpian era of politics, characterized by a certain fondness for chaos and disruption, has upended traditional political calculus. The notion that “a lot of good can happen in a shutdown” is a dangerous gamble, one that could backfire spectacularly, especially when the well-being of millions of Americans is at stake.

A Misconstrued Debate

Democratic strategist Antjuan Seawright’s comments highlight a critical aspect of the current debate: the deliberate misrepresentation of the issue at hand. The argument, as Seawright correctly points out, is not about “illegals receiving health care” but about the 18.2 million Americans in predominantly red states who stand to lose their ACA subsidies. This deliberate conflation of issues is a tactic as old as politics itself, yet it remains effective in muddying the waters of public discourse. It’s a stark reminder that truth is often the first casualty in the battleground of political rhetoric.

The Potential Political Fallout

The fear expressed by House Speaker Mike Johnson and Senate Majority Leader John Thune, demanding Democrats to cave on health care subsidies, underscores a palpable sense of worry within the Republican ranks. This worry is not unfounded. The numbers don’t lie: the majority of ACA subsidy recipients reside in states that supported Trump. This demographic reality should serve as a wake-up call to the GOP. Ignoring the needs of their own base could lead to political repercussions that resonate far beyond the current shutdown.

Conclusion

The government shutdown, driven by a clash over health care subsidies, is a case study in political brinkmanship where ideology collides with reality. The surprising dissent from Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene serves as a reminder that, at the end of the day, political figures are accountable to their constituents, not just their party ideologies. As this saga unfolds, it’s clear that the real losers are the American people, caught in the crossfire of a battle that seems more about scoring political points than addressing their needs. The potential for this issue to “come back to haunt Republicans in the midterms” is not just a possibility; it’s a likely outcome if the current trajectory continues. One can only hope that reason prevails over rhetoric, for the sake of millions of Americans whose lives hang in the balance.
