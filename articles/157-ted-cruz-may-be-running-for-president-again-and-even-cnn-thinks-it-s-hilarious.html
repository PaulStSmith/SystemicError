Ted Cruz may be running for president again —and even CNN thinks it's hilarious

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosJune 26, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Cruz Isn’t Running for President. He’s Running for a Surname Slot.

The Real Power Is Not in the Field

The article’s own premise gives the game away: Ted Cruz is not so much building a campaign as circling a succession fight inside Donald Trump’s movement. That is the only power struggle that matters here. Cruz, JD Vance, and Marco Rubio are not competing as independent political actors; they are auditioning for proximity to the man who still treats the movement like personal property.

The Source Treats Court Politics as News

The reporting briefly notes Cruz’s early-state travel, his 2016 run, and the speculation that he is “probably” running again in 2028. That is the factual skeleton. But the frame drifts immediately into amusement, personality jokes, and the old Washington ritual of ranking who is most hated. That is not analysis. It is costume drama. The article spends more time on Cruz’s personal abrasiveness than on the institutional reality: he is trying to position himself for a party that now runs on loyalty tests, factional alignment, and Trump’s inheritance logic.

Who Enabled This Outcome

Trump created the market. He turned the Republican Party into a movement where future ambitions are measured by deference to him and by performance inside his orbit. The article says Trump’s likely “deference” has been Vance or Rubio. That is the key fact. Nobody in this story is waiting for voters to define the next era. They are waiting to see which figure Trump blesses, or at least stops punishing.

Cruz knows this, which is why his anti-Iran-war posture matters more than his old presidential ambitions. Vance, in contrast, has been carrying Trump’s line on the war. That is not ideology in the abstract. It is succession positioning.

The Misdirection Is the Point

The piece leans on Cruz’s reputation for being disliked, as if loathing were the central political fact. It is not. Personal contempt is a convenient distraction because it turns a structural story into a personality story. The deeper issue is that a senator who once ran as a constitutional conservative is now auditioning for relevance inside a personalized factional machine.

The secret recording of Cruz trashing Vance and Trump is not just gossip. It shows the kind of opportunism this system rewards and then pretends to disdain. Public loyalty and private contempt are not contradictions in this political culture. They are standard operating procedure.

The Larger Pattern

This is what post-ideological party politics looks like when one leader captures the brand: not policy debate, but inheritance contest; not principle, but signal management; not institutions, but a court. Cruz’s movements in Iowa and South Carolina are less a campaign than an attempt to survive the next phase of Trumpism without disappearing into the pile of failed courtiers.

The broader pattern is simple. The press keeps describing factional succession as if it were ordinary electoral competition, which lets the party’s real structure hide in plain sight. The subject is not whether Ted Cruz runs. The subject is what happens when a political movement trained to obey one man starts sorting through his replacements like property.
