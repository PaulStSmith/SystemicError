The biggest lie about America’s economic crisis is hiding in your paycheck

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Affordability Is a Wage Problem With Powerful Authors

The Price Story Hides the Pay Decision

“Affordability” is usually presented as a problem of expensive groceries, housing, and borrowing. That framing describes the injury while obscuring who controls the distribution of income.

The source article’s strongest point is not its sweeping account of inflation. It is the simpler political fact that employers set pay, workers bargain from positions shaped by labor law, and Congress sets the federal minimum wage. Prices do not have to fall for living standards to improve. Wages and benefits can rise, bargaining power can shift, and public policy can stop rewarding business models built on poverty-level compensation.

Power Sits Above the Checkout Line

Consumers do not determine the federal minimum wage. Nonunion employees generally do not decide what share of the value they produce becomes compensation. Workers receiving Medicaid or food assistance did not design corporate payrolls or tax policy.

The institutions with leverage are identifiable: Congress, presidential administrations, employers, shareholders, and courts and agencies that enforce labor law. According to the article, the federal minimum wage has remained $7.25 for seventeen years, while private-sector union density has fallen to roughly 6 percent. Those outcomes are not mysterious market weather. They reflect decisions about whose bargaining power government protects and whose income it allows to stagnate.

The article assigns primary responsibility to Republican administrations and congressional Republicans, beginning with Ronald Reagan’s 1981 firing of striking air traffic controllers and decertification of their union. That episode matters because presidential power gave employers a blunt signal: organized labor could be confronted aggressively from the top of the state.

Inflation Is a Convenient Alibi

The article weakens its case when it treats inflation almost entirely as dollar depreciation and suggests that falling prices occur only in depressions. Its own examples show why that formulation is too absolute: individual prices move for many reasons, including disease, tariffs, war, supply conditions, and corporate decisions. The political argument does not need an oversimplified economic theory.

What inflation rhetoric can do, however, is dissolve responsibility. If hardship is blamed on an impersonal force called “the economy,” then frozen wage floors, union suppression, tax choices, and employer compensation practices disappear from view. Workers are invited to resent the price of bread while the institutions governing their pay escape scrutiny.

That is the misdirection. The weaker actor—the household trying to stretch a paycheck—is made to experience affordability as a personal budgeting crisis. The stronger actors get to portray compensation as fixed, assistance as generosity, and low wages as nobody’s deliberate policy choice.

Public Benefits, Private Payrolls

The article reports that in Nevada in 2024, 48.4 percent of Amazon workers and 29.3 percent of Walmart workers were enrolled in Medicaid. It also cites a 2026 Government Accountability Office report as finding large numbers of workers at major low-wage employers receiving Medicaid or SNAP, including people working full time. Those figures require their stated geographic and program limits; they do not establish that every worker or company fits one national pattern.

But the political arrangement they illustrate is clear. When full-time compensation is low enough that workers qualify for public assistance, taxpayers absorb part of the cost of maintaining the workforce. The employer keeps the labor. Owners retain the gains. The public pays part of the household’s survival bill.

Calling those programs subsidies for low-paid workers is incomplete. They also stabilize employers whose compensation practices leave workers eligible for aid. That is not evidence, by itself, of an illegal scheme or a secretly coordinated plan. It is the foreseeable result of allowing profitable companies to externalize labor costs onto public programs.

The Media’s Cowardly Abstraction

The article alleges that corporate media avoid the wage question because outlets fear appearing partisan and may share the interests of wealthy owners. The supplied evidence does not establish those motives across the industry, so they should not be presented as proven.

The observable framing problem is damning enough. Coverage that endlessly catalogs prices while treating wages, union power, and statutory wage floors as secondary is not politically neutral. It privileges the variables executives and lawmakers would prefer to call inevitable. It turns a distributional conflict into consumer anxiety.

“Prices are high” requires no named decision-maker. “Congress left the minimum wage at $7.25” does. “Workers are struggling” invites sympathy. “Employers pay wages low enough for some full-time workers to qualify for public benefits” identifies an institutional arrangement. The first formulation fills airtime; the second threatens interests.

Affordability Is a Distribution of Power

The larger pattern is the conversion of political choices into economic inevitabilities. Workers lose bargaining power, lawmakers permit the wage floor to erode, employers retain more of the value labor produces, and public programs cover part of the resulting shortfall. Then the hardship is marketed back to the public as “inflation.”

The remedy proposed in the article—raising the federal minimum wage and making union organization easier—correctly targets power rather than sentiment. Whether every statistic or historical claim in the source withstands independent scrutiny is beyond the supplied material. The essential distinction remains: households experience affordability at the register, but the terms of that experience are set in workplaces, boardrooms, legislatures, and enforcement agencies.

An affordability crisis without named authors is propaganda by omission.
