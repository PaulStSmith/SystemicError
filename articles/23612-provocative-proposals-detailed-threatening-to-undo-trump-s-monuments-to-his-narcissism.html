Trump’s Architecture of Impunity

An Arch Built With State Power

Donald Trump’s proposed 250-foot granite-and-gold “Triumphal Arch” is not merely an exercise in architectural vanity. Interior Secretary Doug Burgum has announced imminent excavation near Arlington National Cemetery, even though the National Park Service found that the project would damage the setting of existing monuments and disrupt the sightline between the Lincoln Memorial and the cemetery.

That is enough context. The political story is not that Trump likes gold. It is that federal power is being used to force his personal mythology into the nation’s physical landscape.

The Administration Is the Actor

Trump conceived the monument. Burgum is advancing it. The National Park Service documented adverse effects, but that finding has not stopped the machinery of government.

Coverage that lingers on garish aesthetics risks mistaking vulgarity for the offense. Taste is incidental. The operative fact is that an administration can press ahead with a president’s self-glorifying project despite the government’s own assessment of the damage. The arch’s intended message is permanence: Trump’s image, preferences and authority rendered in stone at a site already dense with national symbolism.

Calling the structure “narcissistic” may be accurate as interpretation, but it is politically incomplete. Narcissism becomes consequential when institutions supply the land, authority and labor.

Erasure Is Doing More Work Than Construction

The arch belongs to a broader project described in the source: Trump’s name attached to institutions and federal initiatives, geographic and departmental names changed, a White House ballroom pursued, and agencies including USAID and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau weakened.

These acts differ in substance, but they share a governing instinct. Public institutions are treated as presidential property: available to be renamed, repurposed, diminished or branded. The goal appears less like ordinary policy revision than the production of a political reality in which the state continually reflects one man back at the country.

The monument is therefore the most visible part of the project, not necessarily the most damaging. An impaired agency can affect millions of people without producing a dramatic skyline. Bureaucratic destruction rarely photographs as well as a gold-plated arch.

Reversal Is Not Reconstruction

Paul Waldman’s proposed response—demolish the arch, remove Trump’s name, reverse his renamings and eliminate his branded programs—correctly rejects the idea that presidential self-commemoration must become permanent through inertia. A future administration would make a choice by preserving these projects just as surely as Trump’s administration made a choice by creating them.

But demolition is the easy part. Restoring an agency’s name is not the same as restoring its personnel, expertise or capacity. Removing installed officials from the Justice Department does not automatically repair institutional norms. Rebuilding USAID or the CFPB would require budgets, staff, legal authority and political endurance.

A wrecking ball can erase a symbol. It cannot reconstruct a state.

The Executive-Power Trap

The proposal becomes more troubling when it recommends using Trump’s expansive executive-power precedents to undo Trump’s work. That tactic has an obvious appeal: if one administration exploited presidential authority to impose these changes, its successor could use the same authority to reverse them.

But this would also preserve the underlying system in which public institutions are radically remade by presidential command. The occupant changes; the concentration of power survives. Today’s corrective precedent becomes tomorrow’s instrument of retaliation, branding or demolition.

That does not mean a successor should leave deliberate damage untouched. It means reversal should not be confused with reform. Repair requires constraints capable of binding the next president too.

The Monument Behind the Monument

The arch may eventually fall. The more durable structure is the network of officials, legal doctrines and institutional weaknesses that allowed it to rise despite documented harm.

Trump’s physical imprint on Washington is not simply an aesthetic embarrassment awaiting a better decorator. It reveals how quickly public space and public administration can be subordinated when appointees enable the president, oversight fails to restrain him and unilateral action becomes its own justification.

The systemic error is not the belief that stone lasts forever. It is the belief that removing the stone will remove the power that put it there.
