'Guaranteed death': Another GOP senator comes out swinging against Trump's demands

Based on an article by Bennito L. Kelty

Paulo SantosAugust 16, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Filibuster Is a Veto Machine, Not a Virtue

Sen. John Curtis says he will not alter the filibuster, and he is not alone. Sen. Lisa Murkowski and Senate Majority Leader John Thune are also resisting Donald Trump’s demand that Republicans gut the rule to force through his stalled SAVE America Act, a controversial voting bill. That is the surface story. The deeper story is a fight over who gets to control Senate choke points, and over how far Republicans will go to bend procedure around a president who still wants the machinery of government to obey him.

Power Sits With the People Holding the Rules

The article frames this as a clash between Trump and a few “defiant” senators. That is too clean. Trump has influence, but the Senate rules are held by senators, not by a presidential slogan. Curtis’s real point is not principle in the abstract; it is that individual senators can block, delay, and bargain because the chamber is built to let them do it. He is defending a power arrangement that lets a minority of lawmakers overrule a majority.

That is not accidental. The filibuster is not a neutral civic safeguard. It is a mechanism for freezing power in place and preserving leverage for whoever already has enough of it to obstruct.

The Bill Is the Point

The source says Trump wants this rule change to move a voting bill. That matters. This is not some harmless housekeeping dispute over floor procedure. When the pressure is being applied to force through voting legislation, the question is not whether Senate independence is being protected. The question is why the bill needs procedural demolition to survive.

Trump’s demand is revealing because it treats institutional restraint as an obstacle to be smashed, not a limit to be respected. The controversy is not that the filibuster exists. The controversy is the agenda that cannot make it through without breaking the glass.

“Minority” Is Doing a Lot of Work

Curtis argues that the filibuster protects the minority, including any senator who dissents from leadership. That sounds noble until you notice how often the “minority” in Senate rhetoric means a tightly organized conservative bloc with enough procedural weapons to frustrate majority rule for years.

This is the filibuster’s favorite camouflage: it dresses up obstruction as constitutional balance. It turns a veto point into a moral principle. It lets senators describe their own power as democratic restraint, even when that power exists primarily to stop legislation that elected majorities might otherwise pass.

Republican Resistance Is Not Democratic Redemption

There is a temptation to read this as proof that some Republicans still care about norms. That is too generous. What Curtis, Murkowski, and Thune are resisting here is not Trumpism as a political project. They are resisting one specific demand that would make the Senate easier for Trump to use on his terms.

That is a narrower conflict. It is also a telling one. When Republicans defend procedure, they are often defending the parts of the system that still give them leverage. That can occasionally produce the right outcome for the wrong reason. It does not turn Senate self-protection into democratic courage.

The Pattern

This story is about how authoritarian pressure works inside formal institutions: not always by open takeover, but by demanding that rules be broken in the name of “getting things done,” then calling the people who refuse “obstructionists.” The source article mostly treats this as an intraparty dispute over Senate procedure. That framing is too timid for what is actually happening.

The real pattern is older and uglier. Power that cannot win cleanly tries to rewrite the rules. Power that benefits from those rules calls them sacred. And voting, the one thing that should never be treated as a procedural inconvenience, becomes the object of the fight.
