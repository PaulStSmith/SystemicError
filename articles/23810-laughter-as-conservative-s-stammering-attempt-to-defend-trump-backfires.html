Laughter as conservative's stammering attempt to defend Trump backfires

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Paulo SantosSeptember 20, 20263 min read

First Came the War. Then Came the Excuse.

The Sequence Matters

The Strait of Hormuz was open before President Donald Trump launched military action against Iran on February 28. Iran then closed the strait, disrupting a route that carries a fifth of the world’s oil. That chronology is not a footnote. It is the central political fact.

Yet Tea Party Patriots co-founder Jenny Beth Martin described the resulting crisis as evidence that Washington must “make sure we have control” of the strait. The administration’s war creates the immediate conditions for disruption; the disruption is then invoked to justify greater American control. Cause becomes pretext.

Power Sits in Washington

Iran’s conduct may be dangerous, but Iran did not order the United States into this war. The Trump administration did. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth helped wield the military power now under scrutiny, and Representative Thomas Massie’s impeachment push targets an official with authority—not a spectator staging a rhetorical dispute.

Calling that effort a “political stunt” serves a specific purpose: it trivializes accountability for the people who made or executed the decision. Martin’s framing shifts attention away from state power and toward the supposed unseriousness of anyone attempting to constrain it.

A Crisis Laundered Into Necessity

Martin cited fertilizer, grocery prices, energy production, mines, missiles, and decades of hostility. Each point widens the frame until the initiating decision disappears inside a fog of accumulated grievance.

This is how war propaganda often works. Officials and their allies do not need to prove that escalation was unavoidable. They need only describe the consequences of escalation as new reasons to continue it. The administration’s choice becomes background; retaliation becomes the beginning of the story.

That is not confusion. It is political laundering.

The Claim That Collapsed

Martin insisted the strait was under U.S. control and that ships were still passing through. CNN’s Audie Cornish asked the question the claim required: Did Martin actually believe shipping companies considered the route under American control?

The value of Cornish’s intervention was its simplicity. She did not bury the assertion beneath procedural language or competing quotations. She tested it against observable consequence. If commercial shippers do not behave as though Washington controls the strait, declaring control is not strategic analysis. It is branding.

The article’s focus on Martin’s stammer risks reducing the episode to a television embarrassment. The more important failure belongs to a political system in which an activist can repeat an administration’s assertion of control without having to explain what that control means, what it costs, or why the war that supposedly secured it instead produced a global fuel crisis.

Accountability Is Not the Stunt

Massie’s impeachment effort may succeed or fail, and the source provides too little information to judge its full merits. But dismissing oversight as theater while treating a war’s self-inflicted consequences as justification for more power reverses the proper burden.

The officials who initiated military action owe an account of the decision, its legal basis, its objectives, and its consequences. Their critics do not become unserious merely because accountability is politically inconvenient.

Institutional cowardice begins when scrutiny is treated as disruption and executive violence as the natural order of things.

The Escalation Machine

The larger pattern is brutally efficient: initiate force, detach the fallout from the decision, blame the adversary for the entire sequence, and cite the resulting instability as proof that still more force is required.

Under that logic, failure never discredits policy. Failure expands its mandate. Higher prices, blocked shipping, and wider conflict become arguments for the same officials to acquire greater control over the crisis they helped produce.

The television exchange exposed a weak surrogate. The deeper systemic error is the machinery behind her: an administration able to turn consequence into permission, and a political culture trained to call accountability a stunt before asking who started the war.
