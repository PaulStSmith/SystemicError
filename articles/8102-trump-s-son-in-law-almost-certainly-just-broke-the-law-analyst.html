Trump's son-in-law almost certainly just broke the law: analyst

President Donald Trump’s son-in-law likely broke the law this week, one analyst wrote Wednesday.

Jared Kushner traveled to Moscow on Tuesday to participate in high-stakes foreign policy negotiations with Russian President Vladimir Putin over a potential peace deal with Ukraine. The U.S. delegation consisted of Kushner, Special Envoy Steve Witkoff, and an interpreter.

“Kushner’s participation in the Moscow meeting — and the similar role he played in the Gaza negotiations — likely violates the law,” wrote Judd Legum for Popular Information.

As a representative of the Trump administration, he technically qualifies as a Special Government Employee — a designation that requires specific legal guidelines.

However, “Trump has not named Kushner an SGE,” noted Legum, even though a 1977 Department of Justice opinion suggests that performing governmental functions under presidential direction can constitute an official capacity.

“Here, Kushner is engaged in activities that can only be conducted by government officials,” wrote Legum.

Private citizens are banned from unauthorized foreign negotiations under the Logan Act. Furthermore, accepting payments from foreign governments is restricted under the Foreign Emoluments Clause. But since leaving the White House four years ago, Kushner has raised nearly $5 billion for his private equity firm. Nearly 99% of that funding has come from foreign sources.

“As a de facto SGE with substantial authority, the Foreign Emoluments Clause of the Constitution prohibits Kushner from accepting ‘any present, Emolument, Office, or Title, of any kind whatever, from any King, Prince, or foreign State,’ noted Legum.

The Saudi government pays Kushner about $25 million a year, while other investors, such as the governments of Qatar and the United Arab Emirates, pay up to 2% a year.

“Kushner is continuing to collect these fees as he serves in a top foreign policy role for the Trump administration. This is precisely the kind of behavior the Foreign Emoluments Clause was designed to prevent,” he said.

Kushner notably pledged last year that he would not resume a foreign policy role in a potential second Trump administration. He stressed his commitment to being a private investor and keeping away from governmental affairs.

Our Analysis:

Kushner’s Legal and Ethical Quagmire: A Deep Dive

Jared Kushner’s Moscow Adventure: Legal Skating on Thin Ice

In a recent piece that cuts through the diplomatic fog with the precision of a scalpel, Judd Legum of Popular Information lays bare a scenario that should have alarm bells ringing across the spectrum of American governance and ethics. Jared Kushner, son-in-law of President Donald Trump and a figure whose governmental role has often seemed as amorphous as it is expansive, has ventured into territory that not only raises eyebrows but likely crosses legal boundaries.

The Moscow Meeting: A Diplomatic Misstep or A Legal Misfire?

Kushner’s journey to Moscow, under the guise of negotiating peace with Ukraine, alongside Special Envoy Steve Witkoff and an interpreter, is not just another diplomatic rendezvous. It’s a stark embodiment of the blurred lines between private interest and public office that has characterized much of his tenure in the Trump administration. The delegation’s composition itself—a mix of official and unofficial elements—begs the question: where does Kushner stand?

Special Government Employee (SGE) or Not? The Legal Labyrinth

Legum’s analysis underscores a crucial point: Kushner, by dint of his actions, might qualify as a Special Government Employee (SGE). This designation, however, comes with a labyrinth of legal expectations and restrictions, none of which seem to have been formally applied to Kushner. The 1977 Department of Justice opinion, suggesting that performing governmental functions under presidential direction constitutes an official capacity, wraps Kushner’s actions in a cloak of officialdom—unofficially.

The Logan Act and Foreign Emoluments Clause: Legal Guardrails Ignored?

The Logan Act’s prohibition against unauthorized foreign negotiations by private citizens and the Foreign Emoluments Clause’s restrictions against accepting payments from foreign governments are not just legal formalities; they are foundational guardrails designed to protect American sovereignty and prevent conflicts of interest. Kushner’s financial entanglements—nearly $5 billion raised from foreign sources for his private equity firm, with substantial yearly payments from foreign governments—don’t just flirt with these legal boundaries; they dance merrily across them.

A Pledge Forgotten: Kushner’s Duality as Investor and Diplomat

Kushner’s pledge to remain a private investor and steer clear of governmental affairs in a potential second Trump administration seems, at best, a forgotten promise, or at worst, a deliberate obfuscation of his ongoing dual roles. His current actions—notably, the Moscow meeting—underscore a troubling disregard for the ethical and legal standards expected of those in or near the seats of power.

Concluding Thoughts: A Call for Accountability

Kushner’s foray into Moscow, underpinned by a complex web of financial and political interests, is not just a story of diplomatic overreach; it’s a glaring spotlight on the systemic issues of accountability, transparency, and conflict of interest that plague American politics. As citizens and stewards of governance, the onus is on us to demand higher standards and clearer boundaries. Kushner’s actions, as outlined by Legum, are not merely questionable; they represent a systemic error that requires immediate correction. The silence and inaction in response to such flagrant disregard for legal and ethical standards speak volumes about the current state of American political accountability—or the lack thereof.
