History warns a catastrophic economic crash could be next

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosNovember 8, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power is concentratedDemocrats still have agencyThe crash framing misdirectsThe real warning

What happened

AlterNet does not establish that a catastrophic economic crash is next. It argues that such a rupture is possible, using Peter Turchin’s theory of political disintegration and Neil Howe’s generational-cycle framework to interpret an election the article describes as occurring “this week.” Its central claim is that extreme wealth concentration, declining state capacity, and working-class economic insecurity resemble conditions that preceded earlier crises.

The article says Elon Musk spent nearly half a billion traceable dollars supporting right-wing candidates, while billionaire-funded groups and AIPAC poured money into other races. It maintains that this spending failed to overcome voter anger in some contests, then urges Democrats to reject the party’s neoliberal faction, embrace progressive economic policies, and confront Donald Trump rather than cooperate with him. The piece supplies no economic forecast, crash date, or evidence that collapse is imminent.

The power is concentrated

The institutional power described here belongs primarily to wealthy donors, corporate owners, elected officials, judges, and party leaders. Billionaires can finance campaigns and control major communications platforms. Congress writes tax and spending law. Presidents direct the executive branch. Courts determine which campaign-finance and labor restrictions survive. Democratic leaders decide whether their party will challenge that structure or accommodate it.

Ordinary voters possess electoral agency, but they do not enter politics with equivalent leverage. Treating a ballot cast amid concentrated media ownership, enormous campaign spending, weakened unions, and economic precarity as an isolated consumer preference hides the machinery shaping the available choices.

The article is strongest when it names that imbalance. It is weaker when it compresses decades of institutional change into a single story of “the morbidly rich.” Wealth is not self-executing. Tax reductions, benefit cuts, anti-union laws, deregulation, and campaign-finance rules require officials to enact them and judges to sustain them. Oligarchic influence works through public institutions whose occupants retain responsibility for their decisions.

Democrats still have agency

The piece correctly refuses to portray Democratic leaders as helpless spectators. According to AlterNet, figures such as Chuck Schumer and Claire McCaskill represent a choice to seek cooperation or ideological moderation, while politicians including Bernie Sanders, Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, Ro Khanna, Mark Pocan, and Abdul El-Sayed represent a more confrontational economic program.

That dispute is not merely about messaging. Party leaders control committee assignments, legislative priorities, campaign resources, endorsements, and the boundaries of acceptable policy. When they favor affluent donors or dilute broadly popular economic proposals, they are exercising power—not bowing to an unexplained political weather system.

But the article overstates what polling alone can prove. Support for Medicare for All, free college, higher taxes on the wealthy, or climate investment does not automatically translate into votes for every candidate carrying those policies. Election outcomes also cannot, by themselves, validate a historical-cycle theory. Democrats need a material program, but numerology about 40- and 80-year intervals is not a substitute for organizing, governing, or counting votes.

The crash framing misdirects

The headline converts a political argument into a prophecy the reporting cannot support. Turchin’s sequence—popular impoverishment, excess elite competition, and state breakdown—is presented as a warning model, not a demonstrated forecast of the next economic event. Howe’s generational framework is likewise an interpretation of history, not proof that the United States is scheduled for collapse.

That distinction matters because inevitability can conceal agency. Crashes do not arrive because a historical clock strikes midnight. Legislators choose fiscal policy. Regulators permit or constrain risk. Employers suppress or accept unionization. Courts expand or limit the political reach of money. Party officials decide whose interests receive protection.

The article also assigns vast historical upheavals to concentrated wealth with more certainty than its evidence here can carry. Economic inequality can intensify instability, but revolutions, civil wars, and democratic erosion have distinct causes and institutional histories. A forceful class analysis becomes less persuasive when analogy is made to do the work of proof.

The real warning

The consequential pattern is not that history mechanically repeats every eight decades. It is that concentrated wealth can purchase political access, weaken countervailing institutions, and redirect government toward asset holders while insecurity spreads below them. The article attributes that trajectory to Reagan-era policies continued across administrations: weaker unions, lower taxes on the wealthy, global labor arbitrage, and reduced public provision.

That account identifies a genuine political mechanism within the story’s own evidence. Economic pain erodes confidence when people repeatedly encounter institutions that protect wealth more reliably than wages, housing, healthcare, or food assistance. Demagogic politics can then redirect justified anger toward convenient targets while preserving the arrangements that produced the anger.

The danger of the crash metaphor is that it makes a chain of decisions sound like fate. The more precise conclusion is harsher: powerful people built the conditions AlterNet fears, public officials enabled them, and Democratic leaders still must decide whether to dismantle that structure or manage its decline. History offers no automatic progressive renewal. Institutions change when organized power forces the people controlling them to choose differently.
