Your Mistake, Your Problem. The Government’s Mistake? Still Your Problem.

How America’s permitting system demands absolute obedience from citizens while allowing the government to escape responsibility for its own decisions.

There is an old legal principle that almost everyone has heard:

Ignorance of the law is no excuse.

File your taxes incorrectly, and the IRS may come knocking.

Miss a regulatory deadline, and you may face penalties.

Build something without the required permit, and the government may order you to tear it down.

Violate a zoning ordinance, and you might find yourself paying fines while lawyers argue over exactly what you're allowed to do with your own property.

The rules are the rules.

The law is the law.

And the government expects you to know both.

Fair enough.

But here's a question that apparently hasn't occurred to enough people:

What happens when the government doesn't know its own rules?

More specifically, what happens when you follow every procedure the government requires, obtain its approval, spend your own money acting on that approval, and then discover that the government made a mistake?

In far too many cases, the answer is astonishingly simple.

You pay.

Not the official who made the mistake.

Not the agency that approved your application.

You.

The citizen who did exactly what the government told you to do.

And somehow, we've accepted this as a reasonable arrangement.

First, They Make You Ask Permission

In an earlier Systemic Error article, we examined the American housing crisis and the extraordinary power zoning regulations give existing residents over development.

In cities such as San Francisco and Los Angeles, restrictive land-use rules, discretionary approvals, and neighborhood opposition can make building desperately needed housing an expensive legal obstacle course.

Want to replace a single-family house with a small apartment building?

Not so fast.

Perhaps the zoning doesn't allow it.

Perhaps you need a variance.

Perhaps the neighbors object.

Perhaps the project requires another environmental review.

Perhaps someone files an appeal.

Perhaps that appeal becomes a lawsuit.

Perhaps the project spends years in bureaucratic limbo while the cost of housing continues climbing.

The absurdity is difficult to overstate.

People complain that housing is unaffordable while supporting regulations that make constructing additional housing more difficult and expensive.

And the system frequently gives disproportionate influence to people who already own homes, at the expense of people who would like to have one.

But the problem extends far beyond housing.

The underlying principle is the same: property ownership does not necessarily convey a predictable right to use the property.

Instead, the owner must navigate a labyrinth of permissions.

And once those permissions are obtained, there's another problem.

The permission itself may not be reliable.

Then, They Change Their Minds

Consider the case of Panhandle Bike Ranch in Idaho, which we explored in our second article.

Scott and Jennifer Kalbach purchased approximately 170 acres of wooded land to develop a mountain bike park.

They didn't secretly build an illegal facility.

They hired a land-use consultant.

They submitted their plans.

They applied for the required conditional-use permit.

Bonner County approved the project in 2024, and county commissioners subsequently upheld approval after appeals.

The couple proceeded to develop the property, bringing in professional trail builders and investing approximately $2.5 million.

Then, just eleven days before the park's scheduled June 2025 opening, a judge vacated the permit.

Why?

Among other problems, the county had failed to provide adequate findings supporting its decision to classify the proposed operation as a recreational facility rather than a commercial resort.

The county had also failed to adequately establish water availability for fire suppression.

Notice what the court did not establish.

It did not find that the owners had fraudulently obtained their permit.

It did not determine that the park's trails were inherently dangerous.

It did not establish that a wildfire was imminent.

The decision identified deficiencies in the government's justification for granting permission.

Now, judicial review is important. Government agencies must follow the law, and affected parties have a legitimate right to challenge unlawful decisions.

But consider the consequences.

The applicants submitted their proposal to the authority designated to review it.

That authority approved it.

They invested millions in reliance on that approval.

Then they lost their authorization because the authority's decision was legally deficient.

The government made the mistake. The private citizens absorbed the financial consequences.

And that brings us to the central argument of this article.

A Permit Should Be a Contract, Not a Lottery Ticket

I propose a rather straightforward principle.

Once the government grants a permit, that permit is final. Period.

There should be one exception: fraud or material deception by the applicant.

If you falsify documents, conceal important information, or deliberately misrepresent your intentions to obtain approval, you have forfeited the protection that approval would otherwise provide.

You lied. The government relied on your lie. The consequences are yours.

Perfectly reasonable.

But if you submitted accurate information, followed the prescribed procedures, and obtained approval in good faith?

That's it.

The permission is granted.

The government cannot subsequently revoke it because an official overlooked a regulation, interpreted a provision incorrectly, failed to complete an inspection, or neglected to document a finding.

Government error must not invalidate an innocent citizen's legitimate reliance on government approval.

Think about what a permit actually represents.

The government requires you to submit detailed information about a proposed activity.

It claims the authority to evaluate that activity against applicable laws and regulations.

It charges fees for performing that evaluation.

And it issues an official document declaring that you are authorized to proceed.

If that document doesn't create a binding obligation on the part of the government, what exactly have you purchased with your application fees?

An opinion?

A suggestion?

A temporary expression of bureaucratic optimism?

A lottery ticket that might turn out to be worthless after you've invested your life savings?

That is not how a reliable regulatory system should function.

When a bank guarantees a financial obligation, that guarantee has consequences.

When a company signs a contract, that contract creates enforceable commitments.

When a citizen signs a declaration to the government, the citizen can be held accountable for its accuracy.

But when the government issues an authorization, its ability to escape responsibility for mistakes can be remarkably broad.

That asymmetry needs to end.

“But What If the Government Approves Something Dangerous?”

This is the predictable objection.

What if a building is approved and subsequently discovered to have a serious structural defect?

What if a permitted activity creates an imminent danger?

Surely the government must be allowed to revoke the permit?

No.

And before anyone starts screaming about public safety, allow me to explain.

The government must be allowed to correct a dangerous situation. What it should not be allowed to do is transfer the cost of its own failure to the innocent person who relied on its approval.

These are two completely different questions.

Imagine that you hire licensed engineers and architects to design a building.

You submit the plans to the building department.

The department reviews them.

The inspectors examine the technical specifications.

The government grants the construction permit.

You construct the building exactly as approved.

A year later, inspectors discover a structural defect that should have been identified during the permit review.

The building needs substantial reinforcement.

Under the system I'm proposing, the answer is not to revoke the permit and demand that you demolish or repair the building at your expense.

The answer is:

The government fixes it. The government pays for it.

If the building presents an immediate danger, the authorities can evacuate it and perform whatever emergency measures are necessary to protect human life.

But the authorization remains binding. The government must finance the corrections needed to fulfill the legitimate expectations created by its original approval, including appropriate compensation for losses caused by the intervention.

The owner submitted the plans.

The government approved the plans.

The owner built according to those plans.

The government missed the defect.

So why should the owner be financially punished for a failure in the very review process the government required?

Now, if the owner secretly substituted inferior materials, departed from the approved design, or concealed defects, that's different.

The government never approved those deviations.

But where the owner acted honestly and complied with the authorization, the government should bear responsibility for mistakes within its review.

That's not an argument against public safety.

It's an argument for making the institution responsible for certifying public safety accountable for its certification.

You Don't Fight a Forest Fire With a Garden Hose

The Idaho mountain bike park provides another illustration.

One of the issues raised in the litigation concerned inadequate documentation of water availability for fire suppression.

Let's remember what we're discussing.

Approximately 170 acres of wooded, mountainous terrain.

A forest.

Anyone familiar with wildland firefighting knows that controlling a large forest fire is fundamentally different from extinguishing a burning kitchen.

Wildfires require specialized crews, vegetation management, firebreaks, heavy equipment, and sometimes aerial support.

Certainly, a recreational facility should provide appropriate emergency access, evacuation procedures, and fire protection for buildings.

But those are matters for the permitting authority and responsible fire district to evaluate before granting permission.

The county's original approval already contemplated fire-risk assessments, mitigation planning, and emergency-services requirements.

If those provisions were inadequate, the government should have identified the deficiencies before approval.

And if it failed to do so?

Then it should correct its failure.

At its expense.

The taxpayers might reasonably be unhappy about funding corrections to an improperly approved project.

But that's precisely the point.

Government mistakes should have consequences for the government.

When the financial cost of incompetence is transferred entirely to private citizens, public authorities have weaker institutional incentives to avoid errors.

When mistakes affect public budgets, there is at least an opportunity for taxpayers, auditors, elected officials, and oversight bodies to demand explanations and reforms.

Government accountability should not consist of issuing another order to the person who trusted the government's previous order.

“But the Taxpayers Would Have to Pay!”

Yes.

They would.

And that objection deserves a serious answer.

Government agencies don't possess magical pools of money.

When they pay damages or finance corrective work, those expenses ultimately come from public resources.

But consider the alternative.

Suppose a government agency makes a costly mistake affecting a private business.

Why should the entire financial burden fall on the one business owner unlucky enough to receive the defective approval?

That person pays taxes, too.

That person followed the law.

That person paid the required fees.

That person had no authority over the internal workings of the permitting agency.

Yet somehow, they are expected to shoulder the consequences.

The public collectively establishes the government, grants it regulatory authority, funds its operations, and benefits from its services.

There is a reasonable argument that the public should collectively bear the financial risks of government errors rather than imposing them arbitrarily on whichever innocent individual happens to be affected.

And if those errors become expensive, the public has every reason to demand better administration.

That means proper training.

Clearer regulations.

Competent technical reviews.

Auditable decisions.

Independent oversight.

And consequences for officials whose negligence or misconduct causes substantial public losses, subject to appropriate due process.

In other words, actual accountability.

Not merely another layer of bureaucracy designed to insulate the bureaucracy from accountability.

Won't This Make Permits Harder to Obtain?

Possibly.

And this is where the proposal needs another essential component.

If every approval carries binding financial responsibility, agencies might respond by delaying decisions indefinitely.

They could demand endless studies, additional inspections, more documentation, and increasingly expensive technical reports.

The government, fearful of making a mistake, could simply refuse to make a decision.

We've already seen what happens when discretionary land-use approvals become an obstacle course.

So a binding-permit system must also have binding decision deadlines.

The government should receive a reasonable, clearly defined period to evaluate a complete application.

Within that period, it must approve the application or reject it with specific, documented reasons grounded in existing law.

If additional information is genuinely necessary, the agency must identify exactly what is missing.

It should not be allowed to restart the process indefinitely or manufacture new procedural requirements simply because a project is controversial.

And applicants must retain access to prompt, meaningful review when an agency rejects an application or fails to act.

The goal is not to force officials to approve unsafe projects without adequate review.

The goal is to prevent administrative caution from becoming administrative paralysis.

The government must have enough time and resources to do its job, but it must actually do the job.

This also requires adequate technical staffing and competent inspectors. Financial liability alone cannot substitute for the expertise necessary to make defensible decisions.

What About the Neighbors?

There's another objection worth addressing.

What happens when neighbors oppose a proposed development?

Shouldn't they have a say?

Of course they should.

But having a right to be heard is not the same thing as having a right to dictate what someone else does with their property.

During the approval process, neighbors should be able to raise legitimate, demonstrable concerns.

Will the development exceed the capacity of existing roads?

Will it create noise above established limits?

Will it contaminate groundwater?

Will it interfere with drainage or create measurable environmental hazards?

Will emergency vehicles have adequate access?

These are pertinent questions. They concern actual impacts that can be measured, evaluated, and addressed.

But other objections have no legitimate place in the decision.

“I don't want it here.”

Not a valid reason.

“It will change the character of our neighborhood.”

Not without identifying an actual impact that violates a legitimate standard.

“What about my property value?”

Your property is an investment, not a government-guaranteed asset. You don't acquire authority over neighboring land simply because someone else's lawful development might affect what buyers are willing to pay for your house.

And then there's the particularly revolting variation:

“I don't want those types of people living/working here.”

Whether dressed up as concerns about “neighborhood character,” “community compatibility,” or “preserving our way of life,” prejudice doesn't become a legitimate planning objection merely because someone presents it at a public hearing.

The government should not give discriminatory preferences the dignity of regulatory consideration.

Nor should it allow personal dislikes to masquerade as technical objections.

If someone claims that a proposed apartment building will cause unacceptable traffic, require evidence about traffic.

If someone claims a mountain bike park will create dangerous noise levels, require measurements or credible projections against applicable standards.

If someone claims a development will destroy property values, that alone should not be grounds for denying permission.

The burden should be on the objector to identify a genuine, legally relevant impact—not on the applicant to prove that every neighbor will be happy.

And once the government has evaluated those concerns and granted approval, the matter is settled.

The permit is final.

If the government subsequently discovers that it overlooked a legitimate danger, it must address that danger at its own expense, without imposing the financial consequences of its mistake on an innocent permit holder.

The neighbors retain their right to protection against actual harm.

The property owner retains the right to rely on the government's authorization.

And the government assumes responsibility for the decision it made.

That's how the balance should work.

Your right to enjoy your property does not include the right to control mine.

And your fear that my property might attract people you don't like is not a planning consideration.

It's your problem.

Not mine.

We Already Demand This Standard From Citizens

Now let's return to the fundamental double standard.

If you make an error on your tax return, the government generally doesn't respond:

“That's understandable. Mistakes happen. We'll absorb the consequences.”

You may owe additional tax, interest, and, depending on the circumstances, penalties.

If you fail to obtain a required permit before constructing a building, you can face enforcement action even if you genuinely misunderstood the rules.

If you miss an important filing deadline, you may lose rights or opportunities.

The government expects diligence.

It expects accuracy.

It expects compliance.

It frequently demands these things regardless of whether you intended to do anything wrong.

And there are good reasons for many of those requirements. A legal system cannot function if every citizen is free to disregard the rules whenever compliance becomes inconvenient.

But why shouldn't the same fundamental expectation apply to the government itself?

Why should an ordinary citizen be expected to understand and comply with complicated regulations while the agency responsible for administering those regulations can make mistakes without necessarily bearing the financial consequences?

Why should an entrepreneur have to hire consultants to navigate zoning rules while the government retains the ability to issue a legally deficient approval?

Why should a property owner lose millions because an agency failed to produce adequate findings?

There is a simple principle of fairness here:

If ignorance of the law is no excuse for the citizen, incompetence in administering the law should be no excuse for the government.

That doesn't mean every government employee who makes an honest mistake should be personally ruined.

It means the institution must be accountable for the decisions it makes.

The state cannot demand strict compliance with its rules while reserving the right to disclaim responsibility for its own authorizations.

This Is Not the Law Today. It Should Be a Serious Reform Proposal.

American law already recognizes concepts such as vested rights, equitable estoppel, and government liability in certain circumstances.

But their application varies across jurisdictions, and they do not provide the universal protection proposed here.

Courts frequently distinguish between permits lawfully issued and approvals granted in violation of applicable law.

Government immunity doctrines can further restrict compensation for administrative errors.

The result is that an innocent applicant may discover that even substantial reliance on an official approval provides less protection than expected.

The principle proposed here is deliberately stronger.

It would require legislative changes and, in some jurisdictions, potentially constitutional adjustments.

It would establish that good-faith reliance on a government permit creates an enforceable obligation.

It would prohibit revocation based solely on mistakes attributable to the issuing authority.

It would require the government to pay for necessary corrections to dangers arising from defects in its approval process, rather than imposing those costs on innocent permit holders.

It would preserve remedies for fraud, deception, and unauthorized deviations from approved conditions.

And it would establish clear deadlines and accountability mechanisms so agencies cannot escape responsibility simply by refusing to decide.

There would be difficult implementation questions, particularly where correcting an unsafe approval requires substantial changes to property or activity. Compensation, temporary safety interventions, disputes over causation, and the responsibilities of private engineers or contractors would all require careful legal design.

But those are reasons to develop the principle into workable legislation, not reasons to abandon the underlying question.

Why should government authorization create binding obligations for the citizen but not for the government?

The One-Way Street of Accountability

There is something deeply revealing about a society that celebrates private property and entrepreneurship while tolerating a permitting system in which official approval can prove financially unreliable.

We tell people to invest.

We tell them to create businesses.

We tell them to build housing.

We tell them to create jobs.

And then we subject them to regulations so complicated that even the agencies administering them can fail to apply them correctly.

When citizens make mistakes, we demand accountability.

When the government makes mistakes, we often demand patience from the citizens affected.

Patience while another hearing is scheduled.

Patience while another appeal proceeds.

Patience while another official reviews another document.

Patience while investments sit idle and legal bills accumulate.

And, sometimes, patience while an entire business opportunity disappears.

The Kalbachsʼ mountain bike park is one particularly visible illustration.

But the larger issue affects anyone who needs government authorization before making a substantial investment.

A homeowner building an addition.

A small business opening a restaurant.

A developer constructing affordable housing.

A farmer installing infrastructure.

An entrepreneur creating a recreational facility.

All of them are required to trust the government's decision-making process.

That trust should carry reciprocal obligations.

A permit should mean that the government has completed its review and stands behind its decision.

If the applicant lied, revoke it.

If the applicant disregarded its conditions, enforce them.

But if the government made the mistake, the government must correct it—and bear the cost.

No retroactive excuses.

No casually shifting financial losses onto innocent applicants.

No treating official permission as something that can evaporate after the citizen has already paid the price of relying on it.

We cannot build a serious culture of accountability on rules that operate in only one direction.

The government insists that citizens answer for their mistakes.

It's time to insist that the government answer for its own.

You made the rules. You reviewed the application. You granted the permission. You own the consequences.

Because accountability is not a one-way street.

And a government that demands obedience to its decisions should be required to stand behind them.
