MAGA has lost the culture war it started when President Donald Trump entered office in 2016, and it could cost the president his legacy, one GOP analyst declared on Monday.

Rick Wilson, co-founder of The Lincoln Project, argued in a new Substack essay that MAGA’s meltdown over the Super Bowl LX halftime show by Puerto Rican artist Bad Bunny proved that MAGA had lost the culture war. However, the MAGA crowd can’t seem to shake the “grievance act” because it’s the only schtick they know, he added.

“The culture war is over, and MAGA lost; QED Bad Bunny and the NFL,” Wilson wrote. “They know the grievance act is wearing thin. But like a gambler who’s down a hundred grand at the craps table, they just keep doubling down on the same losing hand.”

Since Trump entered politics in 2016, he has consistently preached a message of bringing America through a revival moment that would make the country feel “great” again to the people who voted for him. That message largely resonated with wealthy white voters who saw the country changing more rapidly than they could accept.

“You can’t shout ‘fake news!’ at a deposition,” Wilson added. “You can’t meme your way out of a federal investigation. The mythology is cracking, and the panic in the Trump White House is palpable enough to track on radar.”

Our Analysis:

MAGA’s Alleged Culture War Defeat: A Closer Look

Rick Wilson’s claim that MAGA has lost the culture war, particularly highlighted by the reaction to Bad Bunny’s Super Bowl LX halftime show, is a statement loaded with implications, assumptions, and a hefty dose of schadenfreude. Let’s dissect this with the precision of a surgeon and the bluntness of a sledgehammer.

The Premise of Loss

Wilson posits that the MAGA movement, spearheaded by Donald Trump since his 2016 presidential campaign, has been engaged in a culture war that it has now ostensibly lost. This conclusion is drawn from the MAGA movement’s “meltdown” over a halftime show performed by Puerto Rican artist Bad Bunny. By focusing on this single event as evidence of a broader cultural defeat, Wilson is engaging in a reductionist argument that oversimplifies the complex and multifaceted nature of culture wars.

The Grievance Act

Wilson argues that the MAGA crowd clings to a “grievance act” because it’s the only schtick they know. While it’s true that grievance politics have been a significant part of Trump’s appeal, to claim it as their only tactic is to ignore the broader strategies employed by the movement, including judicial appointments, deregulation efforts, and a focus on economic nationalism. The critique here isn’t that Wilson points out the grievance politics; it’s that he does so with a brush so broad it could paint a barn in one stroke.

The Mythology of MAGA

The assertion that MAGA’s mythology is cracking under the weight of legal and political pressure is an interesting one. Wilson states, “You can’t shout ‘fake news!’ at a deposition,” highlighting the limits of MAGA’s rhetorical strategies in the face of legal realities. This is perhaps the most salient point Wilson makes, touching on the tangible consequences faced by the movement’s figurehead and, by extension, the movement itself. However, to equate legal challenges with a total cultural defeat is to conflate different arenas of influence and impact.

Wealthy White Voters and the Great Again Promise

The portrayal of MAGA’s base as primarily “wealthy white voters” is a simplification that fails to capture the diversity within Trump’s electoral support. While it’s undeniable that Trump’s message resonated with segments of the population feeling alienated by rapid social changes, it’s reductive to frame this solely in terms of wealth and race. The narrative of feeling left behind in a changing country is more nuanced, involving factors like geography, education, and cultural identity.

Conclusion: The Culture War Continues

Wilson’s declaration of MAGA’s defeat in the culture war is premature and overly simplistic. Culture wars, by their very nature, are protracted, evolving, and far from binary in their outcomes. While MAGA may be facing significant challenges, both legally and in the court of public opinion, to declare a definitive end to their influence on American culture is to misunderstand the nature of cultural conflict.

In essence, Wilson’s essay serves more as a victory lap for his ideological cohort than a nuanced analysis of the current cultural landscape. The reality is that the so-called culture war is far from over; it is merely entering a new phase. As observers and participants in this ongoing conflict, it behooves us all to approach these developments with a critical eye and an understanding of the complexities at play, rather than declaring premature victories based on wishful thinking.
