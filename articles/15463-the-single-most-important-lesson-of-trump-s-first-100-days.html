The single most important lesson of Trump's first 100 days

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 30, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Myth of the First Hundred Days: A Critical Analysis

REUTERS/Carlos Barria/File Photo

Ever since Franklin D. Roosevelt’s first hundred days, “the first hundred days” of a presidency has become the first test of presidencies.

It’s been assumed that, like FDR, a successful president in the first hundred days gets a lot done and aggregates lots of power to do it.

Trump has now revealed the fatuousness of that view.

No modern president has done as much as Trump has done in his first hundred days — to trash human rights, undermine our alliances, threaten the independence of our universities and the press, stymie progress on climate change, decimate our civil service, shaft the poor, harm the working class, worsen the economy, and leave much of our government in tatters.

And his efforts to amass power have ridden roughshod over the Constitution, ignored the federal courts, usurped the authority of Congress, and turned the Justice Department into a sewer for personal vengeance.

The most important lesson of Trump’s first hundred days is that the test of a successful president after 100 days has nothing to do with how much he gets done or how much power he accumulates.

The real test is how much better off are the people, and how much stronger is our democracy.

By these measures, the second term of Trump is beyond a doubt the worst travesty in American history.

Our Analysis:

The Myth of the First Hundred Days: A Critical Analysis

The concept of the first hundred days as a measure of presidential success has long been a staple in American political discourse, tracing its origins back to Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal era. However, as the recent presidency of Donald Trump has glaringly illustrated, this benchmark is not only outdated but dangerously misleading.

The Fallacy of the First Hundred Days

The article poignantly underscores the absurdity of equating the success of a presidency with the sheer volume of actions taken within the first hundred days. Under Trump's administration, the nation witnessed an unprecedented onslaught against the very pillars of democracy and human rights. From eroding the independence of critical institutions such as universities and the press to exacerbating the climate crisis and dismantling the civil service, Trump's first hundred days were less about constructive governance and more about the systematic dismantling of the state's fabric.

A Legacy of Destruction

Trump's tenure exemplifies how a presidency focused on amassing power and undermining democratic norms can inflict long-lasting damage on the country. The claim that "No modern president has done as much as Trump has done in his first hundred days" to harm the nation is a stark reminder of the consequences of unchecked executive power. Trump's efforts to subvert the Constitution, bypass federal courts, and politicize the Justice Department represent a frontal assault on the rule of law.

Redefining Presidential Success

The article rightly argues that the true measure of a presidency's success lies not in the accumulation of power or the frenetic pace of policy changes but in the tangible improvement of citizens' lives and the strengthening of democratic institutions. By these standards, Trump's presidency, particularly his first hundred days, emerges as a catastrophic failure, marked not by progress but by regression on every front.

A Historical Travesty

Comparing Trump's record to some of the darkest periods in history, the article doesn't mince words in categorizing his presidency as "the worst travesty in American history." This comparison serves not as hyperbole but as a sobering reflection on the depths to which American governance sank under his leadership. The mention of Hitler in the context of discussing presidential first hundreds of days is a controversial but telling choice, highlighting the dangers posed by authoritarian tendencies in a democratic leader.

Conclusion: A Call for Reevaluation

The article, penned by Robert Reich, a seasoned observer of American politics, is a clarion call to fundamentally reevaluate how we measure presidential success. The fixation on the first hundred days as a period of rapid achievement ignores the more essential criteria of governance: the welfare of the populace and the integrity of democratic systems. In the wake of Trump's presidency, it becomes imperative for the American electorate and its leaders to prioritize these values over the superficial allure of quick legislative victories. Only then can the nation hope to avert a repeat of the recent past's errors and ensure that its leaders serve the true interests of the people and democracy itself.
