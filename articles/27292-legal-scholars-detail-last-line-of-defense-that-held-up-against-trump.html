Legal scholars detail ‘last line of defense’ that held up against Trump

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosNovember 16, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

An executive power grab, rejected in courtThe judiciary’s split recordA safeguard is not a governing plan

Federal judges blocked the Trump administration’s attempts to seize powers the Constitution assigns to states and Congress, handing it at least 34 defeats in election-related cases and another 28 losses in Justice Department lawsuits seeking complete state voter rolls. According to legal scholar David Wippman and historian Glenn C. Altschuler, those lower courts—not the Supreme Court—became the “last line of defense” against presidential interference in this year’s midterms.

That defense held. The underlying vulnerability did not disappear.

An executive power grab, rejected in court

Writing in The Hill, Wippman and Altschuler said Trump repeatedly tried to extend federal control over voter registration, ballot distribution and vote counting. Those efforts collided with the Constitution’s Elections Clause, which gives state legislatures authority over the “times, places and manner” of congressional elections while allowing Congress—not the president—to alter those rules.

The executive branch may enforce election law. It does not get to rewrite the division of power because the president alleges fraud. Federal judges appointed by Democratic and Republican presidents, including Trump himself, repeatedly enforced that boundary.

The Justice Department separately went to court 28 times demanding complete voter-registration lists from states, according to the scholars, and lost every case. Critics believed the administration wanted the records to construct its own roster of eligible voters. That purpose remains an attributed concern, not an established fact in the supplied reporting. What is established is the administration’s sustained effort to obtain the lists and its unbroken run of courtroom defeats.

Calling this merely an unsuccessful political strategy would obscure the institutional stakes. The president attempted to exercise election powers his office does not possess. The Justice Department supplied the litigation machinery. Judges stopped them.

The judiciary’s split record

Wippman and Altschuler’s praise for the lower courts comes with an indictment of the Supreme Court. They argued that the justices have spent years weakening the ability of elections to reflect voters’ preferences, pointing to Citizens United in 2010 and this year’s Louisiana v. Callais decision.

The pair described Citizens United as unleashing corporate money in politics and said Callais gutted important Voting Rights Act protections. Their larger point is that the federal judiciary did not act as a single democratic safeguard. Trial judges constrained presidential power while the institution at its summit helped create an election system more vulnerable to concentrated money and diminished voting-rights enforcement.

That distinction matters. “The courts held” is too flattering and too imprecise. Particular judges applied a clear constitutional allocation of authority despite pressure from the executive branch. Other judges, wielding greater power and facing fewer meaningful checks, produced the precedents that Wippman and Altschuler say warped the system in the first place.

A California federal court captured the danger in U.S. v. Weber: “the taking of democracy does not occur in one fell swoop; it is chipped away piece-by-piece until there is nothing left.” The administration’s losses prevented some of that erosion. They did not repair what earlier rulings had already removed.

A safeguard is not a governing plan

Wippman and Altschuler warned against making constitutional government depend on individual judges resisting presidential pressure and unsupported fraud claims. That is the central lesson of the litigation record. Thirty-four defeats demonstrate that the legal barrier worked repeatedly; they also show how repeatedly the administration tested it.

The scholars therefore placed the next responsibility on congressional Democrats and their new majorities. They called for limits on partisan gerrymandering, stronger Voting Rights Act protections and regulation of dark money, while acknowledging that some changes may have to wait for a different president or a changed Supreme Court.

That agenda identifies where power now sits. Judges can reject an unlawful demand presented in a particular case. They cannot, through gratitude alone, close the structural openings that invite the next demand. Congress can legislate, but only if its members use their majorities rather than treating favorable court rulings as proof that the system will automatically defend itself.

The lower courts preserved the constitutional line this time. The more revealing fact is that a president crossed toward it over and over, enlisted the Justice Department in the effort, and forced individual judges to become the barrier Congress had not yet built into law.
