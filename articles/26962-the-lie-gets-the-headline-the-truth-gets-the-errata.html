The Lie Gets the Headline. The Truth Gets the Errata.

There is an old joke that The Washington Post once built a mechanical counter to keep track of Donald Trump’s lies.

Imagine an old analog gauge: numbers around the edge, a single hand in the middle, and every falsehood advances the hand another six degrees.

Help fuel the work. Independent analysis doesn’t fund itself:
☕ Buy us a coffee

Eventually Trump talks so much that the hand starts spinning continuously.

At that point it is no longer a counter.

It is a fan.

The joke works because The Washington Post really did attempt something almost as absurd. During Trump’s first term, its Fact Checker catalogued 30,573 false or misleading claims, averaging roughly 21 per day. The pace accelerated dramatically as his presidency continued.

Eventually, however, the absurdity stopped being funny.

Not because Trump stopped lying.

Because everyone became accustomed to it.

And that may be one of the most dangerous consequences of the modern information system: the extraordinary can become ordinary simply through repetition.

A statement that would once have produced screaming headlines can eventually generate little more than a tired shrug.

Another falsehood.

Another outrage.

Another day.

But Trump did not create the information environment that allows this to happen.

He merely discovered how perfectly suited it was to him.

When Everyone Watched the Same News

For much of the twentieth century, American news operated through bottlenecks.

There were newspapers, radio stations and television networks, certainly, but the number of genuinely national information sources was relatively small.

Millions of Americans watched the same evening newscasts.

They read the same wire stories.

They saw broadly the same front pages.

They could disagree ferociously about what those facts meant, but at least they were usually arguing over approximately the same collection of facts.

That distinction matters.

A functioning political culture does not require everyone to agree.

It requires people to have enough shared reality that disagreement remains possible.

“You think unemployment of 6% justifies stimulus; I don’t.”

That is political disagreement.

“Unemployment is 6%.”

“No it isn’t.”

Now we have a different problem.

Then Came Cable

The fracture did not begin with Facebook, Twitter, TikTok or YouTube.

It began decades earlier.

CNN launched in 1980 as the first 24-hour television news network.

That was revolutionary, and in many ways genuinely beneficial. News no longer had to wait for the evening broadcast. Events could be covered as they happened.

But cable also introduced something enormously consequential: audience segmentation.

By 2000, Pew was already describing American news audiences as fragmented between broadcast-only viewers, cable-only viewers and people consuming both, with those groups displaying different interests and demographics.

Once there were dozens, then hundreds, of channels competing for attention, mass media began becoming niche media.

Then came the internet.

And the niches became microscopic.

From Three Networks to Eight Billion Front Pages

The internet removed the physical scarcity that had constrained publishing.

A newspaper required presses, trucks, reporters, editors and distribution.

A television network required studios, transmitters, affiliates and enormous amounts of capital.

A website required considerably less.

A blog required almost nothing.

A social media account required literally nothing except an account.

The result was an explosion of outlets.

Some perform original reporting.

Many do not.

Instead, modern news often resembles a gigantic game of telephone.

A reporter uncovers something.

A wire service summarizes it.

A newspaper rewrites the wire story.

An aggregator summarizes the newspaper.

A partisan site rewrites the aggregator.

A YouTuber explains the partisan article.

A TikTok account clips the YouTube video.

Someone posts a screenshot of the TikTok caption.

Then twenty thousand people argue about the screenshot.

At every stage information can be shortened, reframed, sensationalized, misunderstood, stripped of context or simply mangled.

By the time the story reaches its final audience, the original reporting may barely be visible.

And algorithms have pushed fragmentation even further.

Cable gave us channels for different audiences.

Social media gives us something approaching a different channel for every individual human being.

Not literally, of course. There are not eight billion newsrooms.

But functionally, every person receives a different mixture of headlines, clips, commentators, memes, friends, advertisements and recommendations.

We have moved from three television networks delivering broadly similar front pages to something approaching eight billion personalized front pages.

The Echo Chamber Became a Business Model

Worse, audiences increasingly want it that way.

A 2025 Pew study found that 55% of Americans considered it at least somewhat important that their news sources share political views similar to their own. Republicans and Democrats gave almost identical answers: 56% in each group.

That is extraordinary.

We have culturally normalized the idea that news should not merely tell us what happened.

It should make us comfortable with what happened.

And the partisan split in media consumption is enormous.

Pew found that 57% of Republicans regularly obtained news from Fox News, while Democrats relied on a much broader collection of outlets including CNN, NBC, ABC, CBS, NPR, PBS, the Associated Press and The New York Times. Trust patterns were similarly divided: 56% of Republicans trusted Fox while 64% of Democrats distrusted it; 58% of Democrats trusted CNN while 58% of Republicans distrusted it.

These are not merely disagreements over interpretation.

They are different information ecosystems.

Two Americans can wake up on the same morning, consume “the news” for an hour, and emerge with entirely different understandings not merely of what events mean, but of which events even happened.

The old echo chamber metaphor may actually be too gentle.

An echo chamber suggests everyone entered the same room and then began hearing their own voices repeated back.

We increasingly occupy separate rooms.

Separate microphones.

Separate acoustics.

Sometimes separate buildings.

Then Trump Arrived

Into this environment stepped Donald Trump.

His peculiar political talent is not merely telling falsehoods.

Politicians have been lying since politics existed.

What distinguishes Trump’s documented record is partly volume.

When a president produces thousands upon thousands of false or misleading claims, something strange happens to journalism.

Editors cannot put every one of them on the front page.

Television cannot devote twenty minutes to every fabrication.

Readers cannot sustain outrage indefinitely.

Eventually yesterday’s extraordinary behavior becomes today’s baseline.

Something that would have produced a political earthquake under another administration becomes:

“Trump said another thing.”

Page 30.

Below the fold.

Maybe.

That is normalization through saturation.

The conduct has not become less abnormal.

The observer has adapted to the abnormality.

It is similar to living beside a railroad line.

At first every train wakes you.

Eventually you sleep through them.

The trains did not become quieter.

Your brain learned not to react.

Reality Has to Compete for Distribution

Now combine that normalization with media fragmentation.

Trump makes a demonstrably false claim.

One outlet fact-checks it.

Another outlet presents it neutrally.

Another defends it.

Another ignores it.

A commentator argues that the fact-check itself is partisan.

A clip strips away the context.

A meme reduces the clip to six words.

Algorithms deliver each version to whichever audience is most likely to engage with it.

And suddenly the question is no longer:

“Is this statement true?”

It becomes:

“Which team’s version do you believe?”

That is much more dangerous.

Because evidence itself becomes tribalized.

Government statistics become “their statistics.”

Court findings become “their courts.”

Journalists become “their media.”

Scientists become “their experts.”

Once that happens, a lie does not necessarily need to defeat the truth.

It merely needs a sufficiently large distribution network of its own.

The Lie Gets the Event. The Truth Gets the Postmortem.

None of this began with social media either.

Newspapers perfected one of its central mechanisms more than a century ago.

A sensational false story appears under a six-inch headline across multiple columns.

Everyone sees it.

Everyone discusses it.

Everyone remembers it.

Three days later, perhaps the newspaper discovers that the story was wrong.

So somewhere near the bottom of page 32 appears:

Correction: In Tuesday’s edition, we incorrectly stated…

Six-point type.

Half a column.

Next to the crossword puzzle.

The lie got the headline.

The truth got the errata.

Psychology makes the imbalance even worse. Research on the “continued influence effect” has repeatedly found that misinformation can continue affecting reasoning even after people remember and accept a correction. Simply negating the original claim does not necessarily erase the mental model created when the misinformation was first presented.

The internet took that ancient problem and industrialized it.

Today the original falsehood may reach five million people before anyone produces a correction.

The correction reaches 200,000.

And they may not even be the same 200,000 people.

The lie travels through one social network.

The correction travels through another.

By the time the truth emerges, the conversation has moved somewhere else entirely.

Which gives us another useful formulation:

The lie gets the event.
The truth gets the postmortem.

And a truth delivered after the consequence is sometimes little more than an autopsy report.

Even the Old Saying Proves the Point

There is a famous saying:

“A lie can travel halfway around the world while the truth is still putting on its shoes.”

Or boots.

Or sandals.

Depending on which version you encountered.

It has been attributed to Mark Twain.

And Winston Churchill.

And Thomas Jefferson.

And various anonymous ancient cultures.

Which is almost comically appropriate, because the quotation itself demonstrates the phenomenon it describes.

The attribution spreads because it sounds right.

The correction arrives later.

Quietly.

To a smaller audience.

We Are Part of This Machine Too

It would be dishonest to write this without acknowledging something else.

Systemic Error exists inside exactly the same ecosystem.

We do not operate a global network of investigative bureaus.

We read original reporting, government documents, studies and other publications, then interpret them, analyze them and publish commentary.

We are another layer in the information chain.

The difference worth defending is not some imaginary purity.

It is whether the chain remains traceable.

Can the reader find the underlying source?

Can fact be distinguished from interpretation?

Can a claim be checked?

Can an error be corrected prominently rather than quietly buried?

Can we admit when evidence does not support what we expected to find?

Fragmentation itself is not inherently evil.

Thousands of voices can expose stories that three television networks might never touch.

Independent journalism can investigate institutions large organizations ignore.

Experts can publish directly without waiting for editors.

Minority communities can speak for themselves rather than hoping someone notices them.

The democratization of publishing has produced enormous benefits.

But democratizing publication also democratized bullshit.

And algorithms discovered that outrage, certainty and tribal validation are extraordinarily profitable commodities.

The Most Dangerous Stage

That brings us back to our imaginary Trump lie counter.

At first the lies are shocking.

Then there are enough of them that someone builds a counter.

Then there are enough that the counter becomes a joke.

Then there are enough that the hand spins so rapidly it becomes a fan.

Then people become accustomed to the breeze.

That last stage is the dangerous one.

Because democracy does not require universal agreement.

It does not require ideological harmony.

It does not require everyone to watch the same television channel.

But it does require some common ground beneath the argument.

You can debate whether 3.4% inflation is acceptable.

You cannot meaningfully debate whether the number exists.

You can argue about what a court decision means.

You cannot sustain civic discourse if one side simply declares that the decision never happened.

You can disagree about policy responses to an event.

But first, both sides need to inhabit a world in which the event itself exists.

Once every citizen receives a personalized reality feed, that common ground begins disappearing beneath our feet.

And eventually the greatest threat is no longer that people believe a particular lie.

It is that society loses the machinery required to agree that truth is a category at all.

The lie will always have advantages.

It can be simpler.

More exciting.

More emotionally satisfying.

It can be invented instantaneously while truth must be investigated, documented and verified.

So yes, eventually the truth may get its shoes on.

It may even catch up.

But by then the lie has already crossed the finish line, collected the trophy, given the victory speech, sold the merchandise, and been converted into six thousand TikTok clips.

The truth arrives afterward.

Someone points toward page 32.

Near the bottom.

There is a correction.
