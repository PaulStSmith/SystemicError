SYSTEMIC ERROR / COMMENTARY

New book reveals who’s really calling the shots in Trump’s White House

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosJune 24, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Center of PowerExpertise Was Kept OutMisdirection as MethodThe Theater of DistractionGut Instinct, Public DamageThe Pattern

Rule by the Unbriefed

Jonathan Swan and Maggie Haberman’s account describes a White House where major national decisions were handled by a tiny circle around Donald Trump, while key officials were left outside the room until the consequences were already in motion. The result, in their telling, was not transparency but concentrated power, sealed off from expertise and insulated from fallout.

The Center of Power

The first fact worth naming is simple: the power sat with Trump and the handful of aides and loyalists around him. The people actually shaping the Iran war planning were not a broad national-security team but a tight inner circle that included Trump, JD Vance, Susie Wiles, John Ratcliffe, David Warrington, Steven Cheung, Karoline Leavitt, Pete Hegseth, Marco Rubio, and Dan Caine.

That is not coordination. It is gatekeeping. The White House did not merely streamline decision-making; it narrowed it to the point where the relevant institutions were sidelined.

Expertise Was Kept Out

The source makes the institutional failure plain. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, Energy Secretary Chris Wright, and Director of National Intelligence Tulsi Gabbard were reportedly still out of the loop one day before the war launch. That matters because this was not a ceremonial omission. If officials are excluded from planning the moment a military action could trigger the largest supply disruption in the global oil market, then the administration is not managing risk. It is manufacturing it.

The exclusion is the point. These are the offices meant to translate military action into fiscal, energy, and intelligence reality. Leaving them uninformed is how you get surprise, then denial, then public cleanup.

Misdirection as Method

The article’s framing leans toward the familiar fantasy that secrecy is a virtue when it comes from the top. It isn’t. The source describes a White House that talks about being “the most transparent” while operating as a closed shop. That is not a contradiction by accident. It is a political technique: claim openness, practice concealment, then present the fallout as unforeseeable.

Even the internal handling of Trump’s volatility follows the same logic. Aides reportedly do not tell him bad news because he is “not receptive” to it. That is not prudence. It is a bureaucratic workaround for a leader who treats evidence as an insult. When staff filter reality to avoid displeasing the boss, the institution starts serving his mood instead of the country.

The Theater of Distraction

The detail about Trump choosing trees and designing a “grand ballroom” while the Iran war was underway is not just a character note. It is evidence of what this system rewards: spectacle over stewardship, vanity over competence, self-image over consequence. The same presidency that could gamble on military escalation was also occupied with landscaping and branding.

That contrast is the moral architecture of the administration described here. The state takes immense risks, but the president’s attention drifts to decorations. The costs are socialized; the gratification is personal.

Gut Instinct, Public Damage

The book’s language about Trump as a “walking moral hazard” is blunt, and it should be. He is described as taking breathtaking risks while rarely carrying the costs. That is the structure of impunity: the decision-maker is protected from the consequences that others absorb.

The source also notes the larger political stakes Trump was willing to gamble with: his presidency, the Republican Party, and the world. That is not hyperbole. It is a recognition that the people closest to power are often the least accountable to the damage it causes. In that environment, reckless choices do not get corrected. They get normalized.

The Pattern

This story is not really about one war plan or one president’s mood swings. It is about a governing style that concentrates authority, sidelines expertise, hides bad news, and then treats the resulting crisis as an accident. That pattern is older than Trump, but he sharpens it. He turns institutional cowardice into a system of government.

The deeper lesson is ugly and familiar: when power is held by a small, insulated circle and staffed by people who manage the boss more than the country, policy becomes improvisation, secrecy becomes branding, and the public pays for the damage after the fact.
