Hunters tear apart Trump admin's 'blatant misinformation' in monuments' fight

Trump Shrinks the Monuments, Then Shrinks Reality

Power First, Facts Later

Donald Trump did not merely announce a land-ordering decision. He used presidential power to slash Grand Staircase-Escalante and Bears Ears national monuments by about 90%, with Utah Republicans standing beside him to bless the move. That is the real story: federal executive authority, backed by state and congressional allies, overriding public-land protections in the name of “access” and “multiple use.”

The source article is brief on the policy mechanics because the administration made the theater louder than the substance. That is itself revealing. When officials move this aggressively, the question is not whether they were confused. It is who benefits from the confusion.

The Lie at the Podium

Trump claimed the monuments were places where you “can’t do anything,” including hunting, fishing, or even walking. That was false. Hunting and fishing are allowed in the two monuments, and hiking is permitted in all of them. His own deputy interior secretary endorsed the falsehood in real time.

This is not a harmless mistake. It is a deliberate distortion designed to convert public land protection into a culture-war grievance. The point is to make conservation sound like confiscation, and to recast legal access rules as elite exclusion. It is propaganda for extraction, not an honest debate about land use.

Utah’s Enablers

The Utah delegation, Gov. Spencer Cox, and House Speaker Mike Schultz were not bystanders. They were the local political cover for a federal land grab dressed up as common sense. Rep. Celeste Maloy praised Trump for “listening to the people of Utah,” which is a convenient way to launder a decision that came from the top down and aligns neatly with industry interests.

Supporters argued the original boundaries were too expansive and that the cuts would improve access while protecting valuable sites more “targetedly.” That phrasing is doing a lot of evasive work. What the source makes clear is that the administration is pushing faster mining permits and moving to weaken roadless protections. “Access” here is not democratic access. It is access for roads, rigs, trucks, and extraction.

What the Hunters Actually Noticed

The hunters and anglers quoted in the story were not demanding ideology. They were asking what happens next: weed control, wildfire prevention, wildlife habitat, road traffic, mining pressure. That is the level of reality the administration wants to skip.

Ryan Callaghan of Backcountry Hunters & Anglers put it plainly: the issue is not sentimental scenery, but stewardship of public resources. The organization’s concern is practical and immediate. More roads and more mining do not preserve habitat; they fragment it. More access for industrial use is not the same thing as access for the public who actually uses and values the land.

The Pattern Beneath the Spin

This is how authoritarian politics often works in miniature. The powerful make the decision. The loyalists repeat the lie. The public is handed a fake premise and told to argue within it.

Here the false premise is that monument protections are a barrier to ordinary people, when the real barrier is Trump’s drive to open land to more aggressive commercial use. Utah’s Republican leadership is helping translate that agenda into local legitimacy. The result is not a cleaner policy debate. It is a managed deception that protects power and transfers risk onto the landscape, the wildlife, and the people who use the land responsibly.

The Real Conflict

The conflict is not between “use” and “lockup.” It is between stewardship and extraction, between public goods and the political machinery that treats them as inventory. Trump’s orders show exactly how this machinery works: lie about the existing rules, empower allies who will repeat the lie, and use federal force to rewrite the terrain before the public can name what was taken.

The deeper lesson is not about one pair of Utah monuments. It is about a governing style that treats truth as disposable and public land as collateral.
