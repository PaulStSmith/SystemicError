Hegseth's killing fantasy is an admission his treason attacks were dead wrong

Based on an article by Sabrina Haake

What happened

RawStory argues that Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth undercut his own accusation that media reporting on depleted munitions amounted to treason. In a Wednesday address at Quantico, Hegseth attacked journalists for discussing weapons stockpiles, then cited the vulnerability of costly “legacy platforms” to cheap, mass-produced weapons as justification for creating an Autonomous Warfare Command.

Hegseth said the four-star command would oversee artificial intelligence, robotics, drones, and counter-drone systems, with a target operational date of October 1, 2027, subject to congressional authorization. He also announced Project Meridian, a 120-day study of future warfare led by Elon Musk, Newt Gingrich, and Anduril co-founder Palmer Luckey.

RawStory contends that the appointments create conflicts because SpaceX and Anduril already hold major Pentagon contracts. The article also condemns Hegseth’s rhetoric about “lethality” and autonomous weapons, although several of its broader allegations are asserted without enough supporting evidence in the supplied text to treat them as established fact.

The contradiction is the story

Hegseth’s memorandum recognized a basic military problem: inexpensive systems produced at scale can exhaust or defeat far costlier weapons. That is substantially the same vulnerability journalists expose when they report that munitions are being consumed faster than they can be replaced.

Calling such reporting treason does not rebut it. It redirects attention from the officials who determine procurement, deployment, and expenditure toward the people describing the consequences. Hegseth holds institutional power over Pentagon policy; reporters do not decide how many interceptors exist or how quickly commanders use them.

The word “treason” is especially revealing here. It converts scrutiny of military management into alleged betrayal while leaving management itself beyond scrutiny. The article is strongest when it identifies that maneuver, not when it substitutes insults for evidence.

Power follows the procurement authority

The proposed command would not merely speculate about future combat. According to RawStory’s account, it would receive personnel, budgets, and acquisition authority designed to accelerate deployment outside traditional channels. That is concentrated bureaucratic power over which technologies are purchased, tested, and placed into military use.

Congress still matters because Hegseth said authorizing legislation would be required. But the executive branch is setting the agenda, defining urgency, and selecting the advisers before lawmakers act. Those choices can narrow the eventual debate: once a weapons program is presented as the necessary answer to an emerging threat, resistance is easily recast as weakness.

Project Meridian compounds the problem. Musk and Luckey are not detached futurists if companies associated with them sell relevant systems to the Pentagon. Their commercial interests do not prove that their recommendations will be corrupt, but they create an obvious need for disclosure, independent evaluation, and enforceable safeguards. The supplied reporting does not establish what ethics rules will apply, so it cannot support the categorical claim that their work “won’t” be properly vetted.

Machines do not erase human agency

“Autonomous warfare” sounds like a technical domain operating beyond politics. It is nothing of the kind. Officials choose the systems, contractors design them, commanders establish their permissible uses, and elected branches decide whether meaningful human control is required.

That chain of responsibility becomes more important as decision-making accelerates. A machine may identify or engage a target, but institutions determine the conditions under which it can do so. Automation can diffuse accountability rhetorically without eliminating it materially.

The central risk described by this story is therefore not a robot independently seizing power. It is powerful officials using technological complexity and wartime urgency to make lethal policy harder to examine and responsibility easier to evade.

The article overreaches where scrutiny should sharpen

RawStory weakens its case by presenting grave peripheral allegations—including claims of mass deaths and murders—as settled facts without supplying the evidence needed to assess them here. Its language about “killer robots” conveys the stakes but also collapses important distinctions among remotely operated, semi-autonomous, and fully autonomous weapons.

Those excesses do not vindicate Hegseth’s attack on the press. They show why precise reporting matters. A serious critique should distinguish documented policy, announced intentions, foreseeable risks, and unproven accusations. Otherwise, officials can use an article’s weakest claims to avoid answering its strongest ones.

The strongest unanswered questions are concrete: What targeting decisions could these systems make? Who would authorize lethal force? What testing, legal review, and congressional oversight would apply? And how would advisers with defense-industry interests be prevented from converting national strategy into a customer acquisition plan?

Militarism protects itself through misdirection

The larger pattern is institutional: leaders who control military policy portray scrutiny as sabotage, invite vendors to help define future needs, and then use the resulting sense of urgency to weaken ordinary restraints. The public is told to fear disclosure while procurement decisions move faster and become less accountable.

Hegseth’s own rationale exposes the trick. If cheap systems can strain expensive arsenals, then stockpile reporting concerns a real strategic weakness—not a betrayal invented by journalists. The responsible response would be to defend procurement choices, explain operational tradeoffs, and submit new weapons authorities to rigorous oversight.

Instead, the critic becomes the enemy and the contractor becomes the strategist. That is how power avoids an accounting: not by answering the charge, but by criminalizing the messenger and outsourcing the future to people positioned to profit from it.
