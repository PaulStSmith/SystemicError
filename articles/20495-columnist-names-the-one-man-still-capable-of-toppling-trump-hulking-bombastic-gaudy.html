Columnist names the one man still capable of toppling Trump: 'Hulking, bombastic, gaudy'

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

Personality as Cover for Power

The Wrong Fight

The reporting premise here is a mismatch: it turns a structural fight over democracy into a fantasy matchup about two former presidents. The source centers Ezra Klein’s argument that Barack Obama would be the ideal counterweight to Donald Trump, with the 22nd Amendment standing in the way. That is a neat media object. It is also a distraction from the actual question: who is using state power, party power, and spectacle to dominate public life right now.

Obama is not the answer because he has the right silhouette or the right temperament. Trump’s power does not come from his physique, his branding, or his performance art. It comes from the institutions, donors, media ecosystems, and political enablers that continue to protect him and translate his grievance politics into government force. Treating the story as a personality duel shrinks the problem until it looks manageable.

What Power Actually Looks Like

The article, as summarized, contrasts Obama’s restraint with Trump’s appetite and discipline with chaos. That framing flatters liberal nostalgia, but it misses the mechanism. Actual institutional power is not won by virtue ethics or gym metaphors. It is exercised by people who can command parties, shape narratives, and normalize abuse while pretending it is merely style.

Trump’s strength is not that he is “gaudy” or “unrestrainable.” It is that he has trained a political coalition, and too much of the press, to accept corruption as character and cruelty as strategy. The problem is not that he has too much personality. The problem is that large parts of the Republican apparatus have made his authoritarian instincts administratively usable.

Obama as Symbol, Not Solution

Obama’s remarks at the presidential center, as cited here, present self-government as a radical democratic project. That is the useful part of the source. It names the core conflict correctly: rule by the many versus domination by the few. But Klein’s move turns that principle into a branding exercise, as if the answer to Trumpism were a better emblem.

Democrats do not need a resurrection of Obama as a campaign mascot. They need a coalition that can actually govern in defense of democratic institutions, materially and aggressively. Nostalgia for Obama’s approval ratings is not strategy. It is a comforting way to avoid asking why the party that can still claim the democratic center so often behaves as if symbolism is a substitute for organizing, enforcement, and confrontation.

Misdirection as a Media Habit

The source leans hard on contrast: lean versus hulking, disciplined versus bombastic, approval versus disapproval. That is not analysis. It is a personality scrapbook with polling data taped to it. It converts political damage into aesthetic judgment and asks readers to admire the elegance of the frame.

That is how elite commentary often misdirects blame. It treats authoritarian politics as a problem of tone, presentation, or candidate quality instead of a deliberate project of power. It leaves the machinery intact while debating whether the right man has the right posture. The result is a politics of spectatorship, where the audience is invited to rank leaders rather than confront the system that keeps elevating predators.

The Democratic Task

If the source implies anything useful, it is that Democrats need a sharper theory of power than “we are not Trump.” Obama’s language about self-government matters because it points to collective agency, not because it supplies a nostalgia product. Democrats should be building institutions that can survive demagogues, not waiting for the perfect contrast figure to appear.

That means refusing the lazy comfort of elite commentary that treats democratic decline as a contest of vibes. It means naming the Republican project for what it is: organized anti-democratic politics wrapped in entertainment and grievance. It also means holding Democrats accountable when they retreat into symbolism, consultation, and caution while the other side weaponizes every lever available.

The Real Lesson

The deeper pattern here is simple: American political media keeps trying to personalize systemic conflict because personalities are easier to package than power. But democratic erosion is not a charisma problem, and it will not be solved by finding a better foil for Trump. It will be solved only when the coalition against authoritarianism stops mistaking aesthetic contrast for political force.
