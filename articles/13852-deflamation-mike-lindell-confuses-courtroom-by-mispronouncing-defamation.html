'Deflamation': Mike Lindell confuses courtroom by mispronouncing 'defamation'

By Paulo Santos

Mike Lindell speaking with attendees at the 2020 Student Action Summit. (Photo by Gage Skidmore)

MyPillow CEO Mike Lindell confounded attorneys by repeatedly mispronouncing "defamation" at his trial.

The 2020 election denier took the stand during the second week of trial after being sued by former voting machine executive Eric Coomer.

At one point, Lindell confused the courtroom, "Next" on 9 News correspondent Kyle Clark reported.

"Lindell pronounces defamation as 'deflamation,' causing the Plaintiff's counsel to ask Lindell to confirm they are talking about the same thing," Clark wrote on X.

During Monday's hearing, Lindell was asked about his earlier statements, calling Coomer "treasonous" and "disgusting."

However, the pillow executive tried to distance himself from the remarks by claiming he was angry about being blackballed by right-wing outlet Newsmax.

"Minutes later from the stand, Lindell likens Coomer to 'the driver of the getaway car in the biggest heist in history," Clark reported. "If Lindell's attorneys were hopeful he wouldn't continue his attacks on Coomer from the stand, as Austin Powers said, that train has sailed."

Our Analysis:

The Farce Unfolds: Mike Lindell's Trial Spectacle

In what reads like a script torn from the pages of a poorly written comedy, MyPillow CEO Mike Lindell's performance at his defamation trial transforms the courtroom into a theater of the absurd. The central figure in this circus? A man who can't seem to pronounce "defamation" correctly, opting instead for a peculiar rendition: "deflamation."

Mispronunciation or Misdirection?

Lindell's consistent mispronunciation could be dismissed as a harmless quirk, if not for the gravity of the accusations at hand. He faces a lawsuit from Eric Coomer, a former voting machine executive, for defamation—a term Lindell ironically mangles into "deflamation." This linguistic stumble prompts the plaintiff's counsel to question if they're even discussing the same matter. One can't help but wonder if this is a tactless attempt at humor or a poorly veiled effort to diminish the seriousness of the proceedings.

A Trail of Denials and Accusations

The trial, which unfolded during its second week, saw Lindell taking the stand, only to further entangle himself in a web of contradictory statements and unfounded allegations. Describing Coomer as "treasonous" and "disgusting," Lindell later attempts to backtrack, blaming his incendiary comments on his frustration with being ostracized by the right-wing outlet Newsmax. This claim, however, is quickly overshadowed by Lindell's subsequent comparison of Coomer to "the driver of the getaway car in the biggest heist in history."

The Unraveling of Credibility

Lindell's credibility, much like the pronunciation he opts for, is in shambles. His attempts to distance himself from previous remarks only serve to highlight a pattern of reckless accusation and a disturbing lack of accountability. The spectacle of a CEO, embroiled in a legal battle over defamation, unable to pronounce the term correctly, is emblematic of the farcical nature of his defense.

Conclusion: A Ship Without a Sail

The trial's proceedings, as reported, bring to mind the quote, "that train has sailed," a fitting metaphor for Lindell's disjointed and seemingly directionless defense. If his attorneys harbored any hope that Lindell would present a coherent, restrained front, those expectations have been thoroughly dashed. Instead, what emerges is a portrait of a man out of his depth, floundering in a sea of his own making, where even the basic lexicon of his accusations eludes him.

The trial of Mike Lindell is more than a legal battle; it's a stark reminder of the perils of unchecked rhetoric and the importance of accountability in public discourse. As this theatrical saga unfolds, one can't help but ponder the cost of such defamation—sorry, "deflamation"—on the very fabric of truth and justice.
