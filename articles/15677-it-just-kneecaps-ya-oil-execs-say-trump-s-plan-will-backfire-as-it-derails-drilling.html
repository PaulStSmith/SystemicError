Despite Donald Trump's exhortation to "drill baby drill," oil industry executives are throwing up their hands at the president giving them free rein to forge ahead while at the same time making it unprofitable to do so.

The problem? Tariffs that have made buying needed equipment too expensive, as well as the threat of a looming recession, which would decrease demand.

According to a report from NPR, in the energy-rich Permian Basin, the remaining drilling companies were optimistic about Trump's re-election, but then came his trade war and the reality of higher costs that have discouraged new drilling.

ALSO READ: 'We’ve made a mistake': Trump’s trade war sends GOP into frenzy

According to Sean Dugan, president of Dugan Production, "Your polypipe, which is what these pipelines are made out of now. That all comes from the Asian markets," and that has become a major obstacle.

The report notes, "Dugan says he wakes up every morning and checks the news on tariffs. He used to spend about $80,000 on a load of pipes that come from South Korea. Now, he figures it could be up to $120,000. His company was one of the few locally to avoid mass layoffs at the start of the pandemic in 2020 when oil prices tanked."

"It just kneecaps ya when all this uncertainty and volatility is in the air," he explained.

George Sharpe, investment manager for Merrion Oil and Gas, agrees, and citing a possible recession conceded, "You know, drill baby drill and lower oil prices are not simpatico," before adding, "I think the whole tariff thing is going to backfire on Trump."

Our Analysis:

The Irony of 'Drill, Baby, Drill': A Tale of Tariffs and Troubles

In an almost Shakespearean twist of fate, the very administration that championed the battle cry of "drill, baby, drill" has managed to hamstring the oil industry it sought to liberate. The culprit? A concoction of tariffs and trade wars that have left oil executives grappling with the harsh reality of escalating costs and a looming recession. This narrative isn't just a series of unfortunate events; it's a masterclass in the unintended consequences of haphazard policy-making.

The Tariff Trap

The oil sector, once buoyant with the promise of deregulation and support from the Trump administration, finds itself in a quagmire of economic impracticalities. The introduction of tariffs on imported equipment has directly contradicted the ethos of unfettered exploration and drilling. Sean Dugan, president of Dugan Production, lays bare a stark example: the cost of a load of pipes, essential for the lifeblood of this industry, skyrocketing from $80,000 to an eye-watering $120,000. This isn't just an inconvenience; it's a financial straitjacket that stifles expansion and innovation.

The Recession Specter

As if the tariff-induced price hikes weren't enough, the specter of a recession looms large, threatening to further depress demand. The oil industry, inherently cyclical and sensitive to economic downturns, finds itself at the mercy of broader market forces. This looming threat compounds the challenges faced by the sector, rendering the Trump administration's encouragement of rampant drilling not just ineffective but counterproductive.

Clash of Ideologies

The irony of the situation is palpable. The administration's policies, ostensibly designed to bolster the oil industry, have instead created a labyrinth of economic hurdles. George Sharpe, investment manager for Merrion Oil and Gas, succinctly captures this contradiction: "Drill baby drill and lower oil prices are not simpatico." The reality is that the administration's approach has resulted in a policy paradox where the intended support has morphed into an inadvertent stranglehold.

The Backfire

The sentiment that the tariff strategy might "backfire on Trump" is not just a passing concern; it's a looming reality. The very base that once celebrated the promise of a revitalized energy sector now grapples with the consequences of policies that have, in effect, turned allies into adversaries. This isn't just a policy misstep; it's a cautionary tale of the dangers inherent in pursuing economic nationalism without a nuanced understanding of global supply chains and market dynamics.

Conclusion

The tale of tariffs, trade wars, and their impact on the oil industry serves as a stark reminder of the complex interplay between policy decisions and economic realities. The administration's "drill, baby, drill" mantra, while catchy, has failed to account for the nuanced, interconnected nature of global markets. As the industry reckons with these challenges, one thing becomes clear: simplistic slogans make for poor policy guides. In the end, the oil sector's struggles may just be the canary in the coal mine, signaling broader economic repercussions from a misguided trade war.
