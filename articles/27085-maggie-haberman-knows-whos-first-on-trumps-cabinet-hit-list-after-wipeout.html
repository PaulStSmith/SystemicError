Maggie Haberman knows who’s first on Trump’s Cabinet hit list after wipeout

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 5, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Maggie Haberman identified Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth as the most obvious potential scapegoat for the Republican midterm defeat, but she did not report that President Donald Trump had decided to fire him. Speaking with MS NOW’s Stephanie Ruhle, Haberman suggested Trump could dismiss Hegseth, blame him for the Iran war and then present the move as an opening for cooperation with Democrats.

Haberman emphasized that the war was ultimately Trump’s decision, even though Hegseth was reportedly its most optimistic advocate and other advisers had reservations. Asked by Fox News anchor Bret Baier late Tuesday about possible Cabinet changes, Trump rejected the idea, saying the losses had “nothing to do with my Cabinet” and attributing them to a passing public mood.

She also predicted that Trump would confront a changed political environment in January while retaining two years in office. Haberman doubted Republican lawmakers would suddenly challenge him openly, although she expected pressure on them to produce results and adopt more populist rhetoric.

The president owns the decision

The institutional hierarchy is not complicated. Hegseth runs the Defense Department, but Trump is commander in chief. Haberman’s most important contribution is her refusal to let Cabinet personnel obscure that chain of authority: whatever Hegseth advocated, Trump made the decision to go to war.

That distinction matters because Washington routinely converts presidential choices into staffing stories. A secretary can be dismissed. An adviser can be condemned for bad counsel. The president who possessed final authority can then pose as the official who discovered and corrected somebody else’s failure.

Hegseth’s reported enthusiasm does not absolve him of responsibility for his advice or administration of the war. It does, however, define the limits of that responsibility. Removing a subordinate would change the cast, not the authorship of the decision.

Scapegoating as political management

Haberman described a possible maneuver, not an announced plan. Her scenario is nevertheless politically coherent: concentrate public anger on a vulnerable appointee, remove him and advertise the personnel change as accountability.

That would be accountability stripped of its actual subject. Trump selected his defense secretary, heard his advisers and exercised presidential power. If the Iran war contributed to the electoral backlash, firing Hegseth could redirect attention from the decision-maker to the subordinate most easily discarded.

The additional suggestion that such a firing might generate “comity” with Democrats exposes another familiar transaction. Bipartisan harmony can become a reward for sacrificing an official while leaving the underlying exercise of power unexamined. A Cabinet reshuffle is not a reckoning with presidential judgment.

A wipeout without an explanation

Trump’s own account was deliberately thin: there was simply “a mood in the air.” That description turns voters into weather and political consequences into atmospheric change. It avoids identifying any policy, war or exercise of power that might have produced the result.

Haberman instead said voters had delivered a clear rejection of overreach. The AlterNet report does not provide election totals, voter interviews or other evidence establishing which issues drove the losses, so that interpretation should remain attributed to her. The article’s headline also outruns the reporting: Haberman offered an obvious candidate for blame, not evidence of a settled presidential hit list.

The sharper fact is the contrast between the two explanations. Haberman traced responsibility upward to a presidential decision. Trump dissolved responsibility into mood. One account identifies power; the other makes power disappear.

Republican dependence is the constraint

The story’s central political question is not whether Republican lawmakers privately understand the defeat. It is whether they will use their institutional authority against presidential overreach. Haberman was skeptical, with reason grounded in the repeated failure of predictions that Republicans are finally about to break publicly with Trump.

Her expectation that they may seek “results” and lean into populist messaging describes adaptation, not independence. Legislators can change their slogans while continuing to protect the person who controls the party. They can pressure Cabinet officials, demand tactical adjustments and still refuse to contest presidential responsibility.

That arrangement leaves weaker actors carrying the visible consequences while the dominant actor retains command. It is not confusion about who decided. It is a political incentive structure that rewards everyone around the president for pretending the hierarchy is less obvious than it is.

Personnel is not accountability

The larger pattern is government by displacement: presidential decisions produce damage, electoral pressure follows, and the system searches for a removable subordinate. The official may deserve criticism. The removal may even be justified. Neither fact transfers the president’s authority retroactively to his appointee.

Real accountability begins by naming who held the power when the choice was made. In this case, according to Haberman’s account, advisers advised, Hegseth advocated and Trump decided. Any political narrative that reverses that order is not an explanation of power. It is a mechanism for protecting it.
