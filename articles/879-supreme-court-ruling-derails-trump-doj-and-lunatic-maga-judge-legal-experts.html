Supreme Court ruling derails Trump DOJ and 'lunatic MAGA judge': legal experts

The Venue Trick: When Prosecutors Shop for Power, the Court Finally Noticed

A Technical Ruling With Political Teeth

The case began with a former Twitter employee and the transfer of private information about Saudi dissidents to a staffer for Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman. The Supreme Court’s ruling in Abouammo v. United States turned on venue: Justice Elena Kagan wrote that a defendant charged under 18 U.S. Code § 1519 must be tried where the falsification happened, not where investigators built the case.

That sounds procedural only if you ignore what procedures are for. Venue is not a clerical detail. It decides which court, which jury pool, and which local legal environment gets to wield state power.

Who Actually Held Power

The institutional power here sat with the Department of Justice and, above it, the Supreme Court. The alleged defendant had none of the structural advantages. Prosecutors choose charges, choose forums, and choose the terrain on which a case will be fought. The Court’s ruling limits that leverage, at least in this instance.

That is why the decision matters beyond one former employee. As Cato argued in its amicus brief, prosecutors should not be able to weaponize venue rules to engineer favorable outcomes. That is not a theoretical concern. It is the machinery of state power selecting its battlefield.

The Real Story Is Forum Shopping

The article’s strongest implication is also its most important one: the Justice Department has been using geography as a prosecutorial tool. Barbara McQuade warned the ruling could derail DOJ’s “grand conspiracy” probe in Florida if the conduct occurred in Washington, D.C. Mark Joseph Stern pointed to a Rhode Island hospital case being pushed in Texas, where DOJ appears to be seeking the help of a favorable judge.

That is the pattern. The issue is not that prosecutors are confused about where crimes happen. The issue is that they may be choosing districts to tilt the odds. When the state can decouple a case from the community where the conduct occurred, it does not just file charges. It manufactures advantage.

Blame Is Being Laundered Through Procedure

This kind of coverage can hide agency by making prosecutorial overreach sound like a venue dispute. It is cleaner, and more dishonest, to call it a technicality than to admit the government may be shopping for judges and juries. The article’s framing is useful only if the goal is to keep the machinery invisible.

Stern’s description of the Texas filing as an effort to secure the help of a “lunatic MAGA judge” is not a neutral legal diagnosis, but the underlying point is plain enough: the forum is being selected for its politics. If that is what DOJ is doing, the problem is not clerical error. It is institutional cowardice dressed up as legal strategy.

The Larger Pattern

This case reveals a broader political habit: powerful institutions preserve their authority by presenting coercion as neutrality. Prosecutors call it venue. Courts call it doctrine. Commentators call it procedure. But the consequence is plain: the state gets to choose the most favorable terrain while the target bears the full weight of the law.

That is how institutional power protects itself. It does not always need to rewrite the rules. Sometimes it just needs to pick the courthouse.
