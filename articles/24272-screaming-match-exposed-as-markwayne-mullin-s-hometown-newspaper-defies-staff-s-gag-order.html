Screaming match exposed as Markwayne Mullin's hometown newspaper defies staff's gag order

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosSeptember 30, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin shouted at former Edmond Mayor Randel Shadid during a September 28, 2026, Edmond Chamber of Commerce meeting after Shadid asked how Mullin reconciled his Christian faith with mass deportations and alleged abuse of migrants by Department of Homeland Security agencies. Mullin invoked the duty to obey national laws; police then escorted Shadid from the event.

According to The Oklahoman, Mullin’s staff declared the gathering “off record” even though the chamber had admitted journalists and the event was open to its members. A staffer reportedly told a photographer that nothing could be published—not even Mullin’s attendance. The newspaper rejected that attempted restriction and published audio of the exchange.

RawStory connects the episode to earlier concerns about Mullin’s temperament, including footage shown during his confirmation process of confrontational remarks he made as a senator. The report does not establish the underlying allegations of migrant abuse raised in Shadid’s question.

The secretary had the power

Mullin was not merely another angry participant. He headed the department carrying out the enforcement policy under challenge. His staff tried to control reporting, and police removed the person questioning him. Whatever the precise reason for that removal, the institutional hierarchy was unmistakable: the secretary kept the room while his critic lost access to it.

That imbalance matters more than the spectacle of raised voices. “Shouting match” suggests two comparable combatants. The reported sequence instead describes a public official shouting from a position protected by staff, police and federal authority at a citizen questioning his administration of state power.

“Off record” is not a magic spell

Officials do not create an off-record agreement by announcing one after journalists have been admitted. The restriction described by The Oklahoman was broader still: Mullin’s staff reportedly sought to suppress even the fact that he appeared.

That was not a court order or an enforceable gag order, based on the account provided. It was an attempted assertion of control over independent reporters. The newspaper’s consequential act was simply refusing to let a powerful official’s staff dictate what could be published about an accessible civic event.

Temperament is the softer story

Framing the episode primarily as a question of Mullin’s “temperament” reduces an exercise of public power to a personality defect. A short fuse may explain the volume; it does not explain the press restriction, the removal of the questioner or the refusal to answer allegations about agencies under Mullin’s command.

The operative political question was not whether Mullin became emotional. It was whether the DHS secretary would account for deportation policy and alleged mistreatment by his department. His reported response shifted the argument to obedience to law, as though invoking legality answered a question about how state agents use their authority. It did not.

The machinery of insulation

The episode shows how political insulation works at close range. A senior official faces an adversarial question. Staff attempt to narrow what journalists may report. Police escort the questioner away. Coverage then gravitates toward the official’s demeanor rather than the substance he evaded.

The Oklahoman’s publication disrupted that machinery by preserving the exchange. But the larger pattern remains: institutional power can turn scrutiny into a dispute over decorum, access or personality while the conduct of government agencies recedes from view. The shouting was revealing. The effort to decide who could record it—and whose question could remain in the room—was the deeper story.
