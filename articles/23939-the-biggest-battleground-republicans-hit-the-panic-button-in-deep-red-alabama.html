'The biggest battleground': Republicans hit the panic button in deep-red Alabama

Based on an article by Ralph ChapocoAlabama Reflector

Alabama Republicans Aren’t Losing Control. They’re Losing the Luxury of Apathy.

The Panic of the Powerful

Alabama Republicans control the state government, hold a legislative supermajority, and have not lost a governor’s race since 1998. Yet weak Republican primary turnout and increased Democratic participation have party candidates openly nervous about November. That anxiety is especially sharp in the 2nd Congressional District, where Republicans redrew the seat earlier this year to favor their party but election analysts subsequently moved the contest from “Likely Republican” to “Leans Republican.”

This Is Not an Underdog Story

The Republican Party is not confronting an institutionally stronger opponent. It is confronting the possibility that its own voters may stay home while Black voters and other Democratic constituencies participate at higher rates.

The distinction matters. Alabama Democrats remain a legislative superminority. Republicans benefit heavily from straight-ticket voting: in 2024, roughly two-thirds of Alabama ballots were straight-ticket, and about two-thirds of those favored Republicans. One political scientist cited in the reporting noted that the closest statewide race in the 2022 midterms still ended 67% Republican to 29% Democratic.

This is entrenched power worrying that its margins may narrow—not a party being dispossessed.

Republicans Changed the Map; Voters May Change the Calculation

The clearest exercise of power in the story is the Republican Legislature’s decision to redraw the 2nd District to favor a Republican. The reporting also says the change was part of broader Republican efforts that spring to draw Representative Shomari Figures and other Black members of Congress out of their seats.

The foreseeable response is increased Black political participation. Analysts expect Black turnout to rise across the South, and Figures could potentially offset weaker rural Republican turnout with stronger support in Montgomery.

That is not mysterious “enthusiasm.” It is political cause and effect. Officials used institutional power to alter electoral boundaries; affected voters may answer with the power still available to them—the ballot.

The Horse Race Conceals the Power Structure

The article’s turnout framing is too narrow when it treats Republican and Democratic “engagement” as comparable campaign problems. Democrats are trying to compete from a position of long-term institutional weakness. Republicans are trying to protect control reinforced by legislative dominance, favorable district lines, and straight-ticket voting.

Republican candidates themselves acknowledge where responsibility lies. State Auditor Andrew Sorrell said Republican losses occur when Republican voters stay home, and added that motivating them is “on us as candidates.” That is unusually direct: the people with power do not lack electoral machinery. They fear that their coalition may not appear without Donald Trump on the ballot.

Even Trump’s reported appeal at a Texas convention—telling supporters they would “go to hell” if they did not vote—reveals the dependence. A movement organized heavily around one leader has created a turnout problem for candidates who need his voters but cannot place him on every ballot.

Participation Is Not Yet Realignment

The Democratic share of Alabama primary ballots reportedly rose from 22% four years earlier to 43% in May. Votes in the Democratic gubernatorial primary increased from almost 170,000 in 2022 to more than 365,000 earlier this year, about 117%. Those figures establish increased primary participation, not a statewide partisan transformation.

The general-election consequences remain uncertain. Primary electorates differ from general-election electorates, and Alabama’s recent statewide margins strongly favor Republicans. The most plausible immediate effects are in the redrawn 2nd District and smaller, more competitive races—particularly legislative contests in Madison County.

Republican alarm may therefore exceed the statewide danger. But it accurately identifies where dominant parties are vulnerable: not necessarily when voters change ideology, but when people previously treated as politically containable decide to participate.

Dominance Produces Its Own Fragility

Alabama Republicans have spent years governing without meaningful statewide competition. Now their candidates must generate enthusiasm amid inflation concerns, Trump’s reported unpopularity, and anger that Democratic organizers attribute to attacks on voting rights, civil liberties, and governing norms.

The larger pattern is not that Democrats have suddenly conquered Alabama. It is that durable one-party power encourages its holders to mistake structural advantage for permanent consent. Republican officials drew favorable lines and accumulated overwhelming institutional control. What they cannot legislate into existence is enthusiasm.

Their fear is revealing: when participation rises among people whose representation lawmakers tried to weaken, even a fortified political order has to notice them.
