'My first observation': Editor who interviewed Trump flags a 'truly odd' obsession

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 2, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Joe Scarborough, Jeffrey Goldberg (MSNBC screenshot)

Appearing on MSNBC on Thursday morning, Jeffrey Goldberg, editor-in-chief of The Atlantic, singled out his first moments speaking with Donald Trump for an interview that appeared in the magazine this week that he found notable for explaining the president's state of mind.

Speaking with "Morning Joe" co-host Joe Scarborough, Goldberg –– who is at the center of the "Signalgate" scandal that has rocked the White House and put Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth under further scrutiny –– claimed the current president seemed overly eager to talk about his predecessor in the Oval Office, Joe Biden.

"My very first observation and this, this happened repeatedly in the interview: we walk in and the first thing he says, apart from like there's kind of like this knowing like, 'Oh my god, you're here, I'm here. How fun is this sort of thing?' The first thing he said was, 'I bet Joe Biden would never do this.'"

He then recalled, "And then about 6 or 8 times during the interview, he refers to Joe Biden. 'Joe Biden doesn't do this, Joe Biden is dumb. Joe Biden is sleepy.' Whatever, it's like, it's truly odd. Like, he's gone! It's like 'You're the president.' It's just this, this crutch and you see it in all his speeches that was noticeable."

Our Analysis:

The Echoes of Obsession: Trump's Biden Fixation

In an MSNBC appearance that could be mistaken for a study in political psychology rather than a mere interview recap, Jeffrey Goldberg, the editor-in-chief of The Atlantic, unveils yet another layer of the enigmatic persona of Donald Trump. The occasion? A recent interview published in The Atlantic, offering insights not just into Trump's policies or decisions, but into a psyche seemingly haunted by the specter of Joe Biden.

First Impressions: A Telling Prelude

Imagine the scene: the President of the United States, arguably the most powerful person on the planet, greets a journalist with what can only be described as a schoolyard taunt aimed at his predecessor. "I bet Joe Biden would never do this," Trump proclaims, setting the tone for what is to come. This is not just an offhand remark; it's a window into an obsession. Goldberg's observation here is crucial – it's not just the content of the comment but its immediate emergence that's telling. The presidency, with all its responsibilities and challenges, and Trump's first order of business is to belittle Joe Biden?

The Crutch of Comparison

Throughout the interview, Trump's fixation on Biden is relentless. According to Goldberg, Trump's references to Biden are not just frequent; they are a crutch. This isn't political commentary; it's personal derision masquerading as political discourse. Trump's remarks – ranging from calling Biden "dumb" to "sleepy" – offer little in the way of substantive critique and much in the way of schoolyard bullying.

The Striking Irony

The irony is as thick as the walls of the Oval Office. Trump, sitting at the pinnacle of political power, seems unable to let go of his predecessor – or rather, the man who would become his successor. It's not just odd; it's a vivid illustration of Trump's priorities and, perhaps, insecurities. For a president with a myriad of pressing issues at hand, this preoccupation with Biden is not just a crutch; it's a full-blown diversion.

A Reflection of State of Mind

This fixation, as Goldberg aptly notes, speaks volumes about Trump's state of mind. It's not just about political rivalry; it's a glimpse into what drives Trump's public persona and, potentially, his policy decisions. The constant need to compare, to belittle, to assert superiority over Biden is a telling trait of Trump's leadership style – one that prioritizes personal vendettas over policy substance.

The Bigger Picture

Goldberg's recounting of these moments is not merely anecdotal; it is a critical piece of the puzzle in understanding Trump's presidency. This obsession with Biden, as trivial as it might seem in isolation, is emblematic of a broader pattern of behavior that prioritizes personal grievances over governance.

In conclusion, what Goldberg unveils is more than just a quirky anecdote from an interview. It's a stark reminder of the personal preoccupations that can influence the highest office in the land. As Trump fixates on Biden, one can't help but wonder: what real issues are being neglected in the shadow of this obsession? The implications are as profound as they are troubling, painting a picture of a presidency caught up not in policy, but in pettiness.
