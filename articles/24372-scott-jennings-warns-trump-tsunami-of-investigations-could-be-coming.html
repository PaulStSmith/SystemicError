Scott Jennings warns Trump 'tsunami' of investigations could be coming

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 3, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe presidency is the productJennings warns the sellerOversight should not depend on an electionThe soft language protects hard powerA system of optional accountability

What happened

AlterNet reports that CNN commentator Scott Jennings warned Donald Trump and his associates to expect a “tsunami” of congressional investigations if Republicans lose either chamber in November. His warning came during a discussion of a promotion promising leading holders of Trump’s memecoin access to a Nov. 22 dinner at Trump National near Washington, with VIP treatment and Trump-branded gold watches for the highest-ranked investors.

Jennings said he lacked enough information to defend the arrangement and merely assumed White House lawyers had reviewed Trump’s private business activity. He nevertheless called the use of a sitting president’s name in a venture where investors “could get hurt” troubling. AlterNet reports that the coin plunged in value while Trump reportedly earned more than $600 million from it; Senate Democrats called the latest promotion another Trump “grift.” Organizers said attendees would not receive private meetings with Trump and could not give him gifts.

The presidency is the product

This is not principally a story about cryptocurrency volatility. It is about presidential power being used to increase the appeal of a private financial venture bearing the president’s identity.

The dinner contest makes the hierarchy plain: investors compete by acquiring and holding more of the asset, while proximity to a sitting president functions as the prize. Even without a private meet-and-greet, the advertised setting and political celebrity give the promotion value unavailable to an ordinary memecoin operator.

Trump holds the institutional power. The buyers assume the financial risk. That imbalance matters more than the promotional fine print.

Jennings warns the seller

Jennings framed congressional scrutiny as the danger Trump must prepare to survive. His advice was to document everything, anticipate investigations and be ready for transparency “when that day comes.”

That formulation reverses the proper order of concern. The immediate issue described by the reporting is that people may lose money in a president-branded venture while Trump reportedly profits. Yet the warning is directed upward, toward protecting the powerful actor from future investigators, rather than outward, toward protecting buyers exposed to the scheme.

Jennings did acknowledge that people could be hurt. But his primary prescription was compliance preparation. Political accountability became a weather event—a “tsunami”—instead of a foreseeable institutional response to conduct Congress may have reason to examine.

Oversight should not depend on an election

Jennings’s warning also exposes a structural failure: meaningful investigation may depend on which party controls Congress after November.

Republican control, in this account, is treated as insulation. A Democratic takeover would activate scrutiny. That is not neutral oversight; it is partisan contingency governing whether a president’s private financial arrangements receive sustained examination.

The article does not establish that any law was broken, and Jennings did not claim one was. But Congress does not need a criminal conviction before asking whether presidential status is enriching a private venture, who is financing it and what access those financial relationships appear to purchase.

The soft language protects hard power

Calling the arrangement merely “uncomfortable” shrinks a question of public power into a matter of personal taste. Assuming unnamed White House lawyers handled it properly substitutes faith in internal review for evidence.

The organizers’ assurances address narrow optics: no private meeting and no gifts accepted. They do not answer the central question raised by the promotion—whether access to a presidential venue and association with the president are being used to stimulate demand for an asset tied to his financial interests.

That is the misdirection at work. Attention shifts from the officeholder monetizing his political identity to the investors who might make poor choices, and from present accountability to hypothetical investigations after an electoral change.

A system of optional accountability

The larger pattern is not confusion. It is an accountability system designed to become energetic only when partisan control changes hands.

A president can attach his name and political aura to a speculative product; allies can treat legal vetting as sufficient; congressional scrutiny can remain conditional; and commentators can describe investigation as the looming hazard. Every institution then discusses the risk surrounding the transaction without squarely confronting who possesses the power to create it.

The scandal is not simply that investors may get burned. It is that presidential influence can be converted into private commercial leverage while oversight waits for permission from the next election.
