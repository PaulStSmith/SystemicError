Why houses cost 10 times your salary today — and who is to blame

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Reagan’s Real Legacy Was Power Theft, Not Growth

The Story in Plain English

The source article makes one useful point and buries it under its own rhetoric: the collapse of the middle class was not an accident. It traces housing costs, wage stagnation, vanishing unions, weakened antitrust enforcement, and the Clinton-era turn toward corporate-friendly “Third Way” politics back to a deliberate transfer of power away from workers and toward capital.

The People Who Actually Won

The real beneficiaries were not some abstract “market.” They were the corporations, oligarchs, and conservative institutions that understood exactly what they were buying: lower labor costs, weaker unions, and a political system starved of working-class money. Reagan did not merely govern within that system. He accelerated a project already aimed at breaking the Democratic Party’s financial base by crushing unions, the one institution that could fund labor-aligned politics at scale.

That is the point the article gets right most clearly. Union destruction was not just an economic strategy. It was political warfare. Remove union density, and you do not just depress wages. You also cripple the infrastructure that made mass-democratic politics possible.

Misdirection Masquerading as History

The mainstream habit is to narrate these decades as a blur of “globalization,” “modernization,” and inevitable change. That language is political anesthesia. It strips agency from the people who chose the policies and blurs the difference between accidental drift and organized class attack.

The article refuses that fog, but it sometimes replaces it with its own simplifications. Reagan did not alone invent every mechanism of decline, and Clinton did not wake up one morning and casually decide to betray the New Deal out of personal whim. The deeper truth is uglier: the political class adapted to donor power, and donor power was increasingly organized against labor. That is not confusion. That is capture.

Clinton’s Bargain Was Not Neutral

The article is strongest when it names the Democratic surrender for what it was: a retreat from labor politics under the pressure of money. Clinton’s “Third Way,” NAFTA, and the refusal to rebuild labor rights were not clever updates to an outdated party. They were a capitulation to the donor class after unions had been beaten down enough to stop serving as a counterweight.

This is where “moderate” politics reveals its real function. It does not merely compromise with power. It reorganizes the party around the absence of working-class leverage. Once Democrats decided they could survive without unions, they became easier to buy, easier to discipline, and easier to hollow out.

The Middle Class Was Built on Collective Power

The article’s historical instinct is correct: the middle class was not conjured by austerity, investor discipline, or corporate benevolence. It was built by organized labor setting wage and benefit floors that employers had to match. When that floor collapsed, living standards did too. Houses, healthcare, college, and wages all moved in the wrong direction because bargaining power moved in the wrong direction.

That matters because it exposes a lie that still structures elite debate: that prosperity is something capital generates and labor merely receives. In reality, labor created the conditions that made broad prosperity possible, and labor’s defeat made that prosperity reversible.

What the Present Actually Requires

The article is right to center unions as the strategic problem. Without unions, there is no durable middle class, no serious counterweight to oligarchy, and no broad-based democratic infrastructure capable of resisting authoritarian politics. That is why the right has spent decades attacking them and why institutions like Heritage keep returning to the same project under new branding.

The Democratic Party’s choice now is simple, even if its donors hate the simplicity of it: rebuild labor power or keep managing decline for people who will never be satisfied with managed decline. If Democrats want to be more than a donor-adjacent opposition brand, they have to stop treating unions as a nostalgic constituency and start treating them as the core of democratic power.

Systemic Error

This is not a story about one president, one election, or one policy package. It is a story about institutional capture: capital identifying the source of democratic resistance, then spending decades breaking it. That is the pattern. That is the lesson. And until labor is rebuilt as a source of money, organization, and leverage, every promise of middle-class renewal will remain a glossy fraud stapled onto the same power structure that broke it in the first place.
