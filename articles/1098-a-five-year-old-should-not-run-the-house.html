A Five-Year-Old Should Not Run the House

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 11, 20267 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The headline is engineered to stop the scroll: “I’m terrified that one day my violent five-year-old will kill me”. It is horrifying, obviously. A mother describes a child who attacks her, threatens her, injures her, frightens his siblings, and turns the family home into a place where everyone is managing the weather system of one small boy’s rage.

But beneath the shock of the story is an even more uncomfortable question: how did a five-year-old become the person everyone else in the house has to survive?

That is not a rhetorical flourish. It is the central problem. A five-year-old is not a domestic tyrant by nature. A five-year-old is not a sovereign citizen of the living room. A five-year-old does not get to decide, by force, whether the household is safe. If a child that young has learned that screaming, striking, threatening, grabbing, throwing, and terrorizing will reorganize the adults around him, then something has gone catastrophically wrong.

And no, that does not mean the child is evil. It means the child is five.

Which is exactly why the adults cannot afford to be passive.

The Adult Is Supposed to Be the Authority

Modern parenting culture has developed a strange allergy to authority. Not cruelty. Not abuse. Authority.

Those are not the same thing, though plenty of bad advice depends on pretending they are. Authority is the adult’s obligation to set the boundaries a child cannot set for himself. It is the calm, consistent, immovable fact that the grownups are in charge because the grownups are responsible for everyone’s safety.

A five-year-old does not have the neurological equipment to govern a household. He does not get to make safety policy. He does not get veto power over bedtime, dinner, school drop-off, or whether his siblings can exist without fear. He certainly does not get to assault his mother while the official response becomes an endless bureaucratic shrug about referrals, parenting courses, and waiting lists.

The adult is, or should be, the authority in the house. Not because adults are more important than children, but because children are children. They need structure before they can internalize self-control. They need consequences before they understand limits. They need adults who can say no and mean it.

When a child is violent, the answer cannot be a household built around avoidance. Avoidance teaches the child that violence works. It teaches siblings that their safety is conditional. It teaches the targeted parent that endurance is the price of love.

That is not parenting. That is a slow surrender.

Discipline Is Not a Dirty Word

The most predictable response to any argument like this is the immediate collapse into caricature: so you want to beat the kid?

No. That dodge is too convenient.

Discipline does not have to mean adult violence. In fact, when a child is already violent, adult violence can become a stupid and dangerous form of instruction. Hit a dysregulated child and the lesson may not be “respect authority.” The lesson may be “bigger people get to use force when they are angry.” That is not discipline. That is escalation with a moral alibi.

But the opposite failure is just as real: treating discipline as if it means nothing more than soothing language, therapeutic vocabulary, and the hope that a child eventually runs out of energy before someone gets badly hurt.

Hard discipline means the line is real. It means violent behavior ends the activity immediately. It means dangerous objects are removed. It means access to privileges, screens, toys, and choices is earned through safe behavior, not demanded through chaos. It means the child is not allowed to terrorize siblings. It means the adults coordinate instead of improvise. It means consequences are immediate, predictable, and boringly consistent.

It also means getting serious outside help quickly when the behavior is beyond ordinary parenting. Assessment. Occupational therapy. Behavioral support. Clinical evaluation. Safety planning. Real intervention, not a leaflet and a waiting list.

What it cannot mean is a parent being told to absorb violence quietly because any firm physical intervention might be judged more harshly than the child’s attacks.

If a five-year-old is choking, stabbing, throwing, or threatening, the question is not whether everyone has used sufficiently gentle language. The question is how fast the household can be made safe.

The Future Is Coming

Here is the part polite society hates saying out loud: five becomes fifteen.

Not magically, not inevitably, and not without chances to intervene. But time moves. Bodies grow. Strength increases. Habits harden. A child who learns at five that violence controls the home may become a teenager who no longer needs to throw blinds down the stairs to frighten people.

This is why the urgency matters.

A small child can still be redirected. A small child can still have patterns broken before they become identity. A small child can still learn that anger is survivable, that frustration is not an emergency, that other people’s bodies are not tools for emotional discharge.

But that window is not infinite. Every month a violent pattern is accommodated, explained away, or left untreated is a month of training. The child is learning. The siblings are learning. The parents are learning. The whole house is learning who must bend.

If the answer is always “everyone except the violent child,” then the household is not healing. It is rehearsing.

Institutions Have Chosen Cowardice

The ugliest part of the story is not only the child’s behavior. It is the thinness of the adult world’s response.

The mother is told to wait. She is directed toward courses. She is advised not to react. She is told not to restrain. She is left to install cameras, hide knives, lock cupboards, protect other children, document injuries, manage school, manage shame, manage fear, and somehow continue being the compassionate parent of the same child who is hurting her.

This is not support. This is abandonment with professional vocabulary.

There is a recurring institutional habit of pretending that naming a framework is the same as solving a problem. “Trauma-informed.” “Neurodivergence.” “Self-regulation.” “Co-regulation.” These words can describe real things. They can guide useful care. But when they become substitutes for safety, they turn obscene.

A mother with bruised ribs does not need a system that congratulates itself for nuance while leaving her alone in the blast radius. A child who is violent at five does not need adults tiptoeing around the fact of violence. He needs help, yes. He also needs limits. He needs intervention firm enough to interrupt the pattern before the pattern becomes the family.

Love Is Not Submission

Parents are especially vulnerable to this trap because love makes the obvious feel cruel.

Of course a parent wants to see the wounded child beneath the violent behavior. Of course a mother remembers the sweet moments. The bedtime cuddles. The tears after the rage. The small hand reaching for comfort. Of course she wants to believe there is a way through that does not involve admitting how frightened she is.

But love is not submission.

Loving a child does not mean allowing that child to dominate the home. Loving a child does not mean sacrificing siblings to the most volatile person in the room. Loving a child does not mean pretending that violence is merely communication with bad optics.

Sometimes love has to become a wall.

Not a wall of cruelty. Not humiliation. Not revenge. A wall of reality: you may be angry, but you may not hit. You may be overwhelmed, but you may not threaten. You may be dysregulated, but you may not rule the house. We will help you, but we will not surrender to you.

That is the distinction too many experts blur. Compassion without authority is not kindness. It is neglect wearing a soft sweater.

Stop Romanticizing Helplessness

There is something deeply broken in a culture that can look at a five-year-old terrorizing a family and respond primarily by managing the mother’s expectations.

No. The goal is not for parents to become better victims. The goal is for the violence to stop.

That requires discipline fast. It requires discipline hard. But “hard” must mean serious, immediate, structured, and unavoidable. It must mean adults who reclaim authority without turning into abusers themselves. It must mean systems that stop leaving parents to choose between being injured and being accused.

A five-year-old should not run the house. A five-year-old should not be allowed to terrorize siblings. A five-year-old should not learn that threats and attacks are effective tools of power.

And a mother should not have to whisper in an emergency room because the truth sounds too absurd to say out loud: the person hurting her is the child the world expects her to control, protect, understand, and survive.

The adult world owes that child help. It owes his family safety. And it owes everyone involved the courage to say the simplest thing plainly:

The child is not in charge.
