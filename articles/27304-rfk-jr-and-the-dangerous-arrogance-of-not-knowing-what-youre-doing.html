RFK Jr. and the Dangerous Arrogance of Not Knowing What You’re Doing

How America’s Health Secretary turned public-health prevention into a budgetary inconvenience — and why Senator Raphael Warnock’s interrogation exposed something more troubling than ignorance.

There is a particular kind of institutional danger that arises when someone is given enormous authority over a subject they do not understand.

Ignorance, by itself, is not the problem. Everyone is ignorant about something. Even the most accomplished scientists spend their careers confronting the limits of their knowledge.

The danger begins when ignorance is combined with power, ideological certainty, and an apparent inability to recognize why expertise matters.

Enter Robert F. Kennedy Jr., the United States Secretary of Health and Human Services.

In September 2025, Senator Raphael Warnock confronted Kennedy during a Senate Finance Committee hearing over proposed cuts to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. The exchange recently resurfaced in a video uploaded in November 12, 2026, bringing renewed attention to a confrontation that was already troubling when it first occurred. We criticized Kennedy’s approach to public health then, and the passage of time has done nothing to diminish the significance of what that hearing revealed. This is not an attempt to manufacture fresh outrage over old news. It is a reminder that the concerns raised more than a year ago deserve continued scrutiny, particularly in light of what has happened since.

What followed was a remarkable demonstration of the distance between political talking points and the practical responsibilities of running a national public-health system.

And the most revealing part was not what Kennedy didn't know.

It was how he defended decisions affecting systems whose purpose he appeared not to understand.

The Disease That Barely Exists — Because We Prevent It

Warnock began with rabies.

Rabies is one of the most terrifying infectious diseases known to medicine. Once clinical symptoms appear, the disease is almost invariably fatal.

Fortunately, modern medicine has an extraordinarily effective response: identify potential exposures and administer appropriate post-exposure prophylaxis before symptoms develop.

The United States consequently records very few human rabies deaths annually.

This is an achievement of veterinary medicine, vaccination, surveillance, public-health infrastructure, and timely medical intervention.

Warnock explained that the CDC historically provided round-the-clock expert consultation to clinicians confronting complicated rabies exposures.

He then stated that staffing for this service had been reduced to a single person.

Kennedy acknowledged that he did not know the staffing figure.

Later, Kennedy defended the arrangement by pointing out that the United States experiences only one to three human rabies cases annually. He suggested that a single full-time employee could probably handle that volume.

Stop.

Read that reasoning again.

The Secretary of Health and Human Services used the number of people who develop rabies to estimate the workload of a service intended to help doctors ensure that people do not develop rabies.

It is the administrative equivalent of looking at a fire department, noticing that relatively few buildings have burned down, and concluding that the firefighters must be sitting around doing nothing.

Or looking at commercial aviation, observing that catastrophic crashes are exceptionally rare, and deciding that aircraft maintenance and air-traffic control are unnecessary extravagances.

You cannot measure the value of prevention by counting only the disasters it failed to prevent.

A rabies consultation service does not merely advise physicians treating confirmed human rabies.

It helps evaluate potential exposures, determine whether treatment is necessary, interpret complicated cases, and guide decisions before an irreversible infection occurs.

The relevant workload is not simply the number of fatal human cases.

It includes the consultations and interventions that prevent them.

And Kennedy's response did not address that distinction.

The secretary responsible for protecting American public health was confronted with a question about preventive medical infrastructure.

His answer was a death count.

Congratulations. Your Prevention Program Worked. Now You're Fired.

Warnock articulated the underlying problem with unusual clarity.

CDC professionals, he explained, protect Americans from threats that most people never see.

They receive little recognition precisely because their work prevents things from happening.

This is the central paradox of successful public health.

Averted epidemics do not generate dramatic television footage.

Prevented infections don't produce hospital bills.

Children who never contract preventable diseases don't make the evening news.

And the family whose physician received expert advice after a suspicious animal bite may never realize how important that consultation was.

Successful prevention is largely invisible.

That invisibility makes it politically vulnerable.

The people responsible for preventing disasters can be made to look expendable precisely because they have been effective.

An administrator who understands the system recognizes this problem.

An administrator who doesn't may mistake low mortality for low workload.

And a government determined to advertise budget cuts can transform that misunderstanding into policy.

The absence of catastrophe is not evidence that safeguards are unnecessary. It may be evidence that the safeguards are working.

Prions, Hunters, and the Curious Economics of Catastrophe

Warnock moved to another subject: prion diseases.

These are devastating neurological illnesses caused by misfolded proteins. They include Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease and its variant associated with bovine spongiform encephalopathy.

They also include diseases affecting animals, such as chronic wasting disease in deer and elk.

Human prion diseases are rare, generally fatal, and exceptionally difficult to treat.

Warnock described the importance of the CDC's prion surveillance work and challenged a budget proposal affecting the office responsible for it.

Kennedy again emphasized rarity.

He also cited federal debt and the need for agency-wide spending reductions.

Now, government budgets are legitimate subjects for scrutiny. Every public institution should be able to explain what it does, how much it costs, and whether its programs are effective.

But there is a difference between eliminating waste and eliminating expertise that exists to detect rare but potentially catastrophic threats.

A surveillance program does not become pointless because the disease it monitors is uncommon.

Indeed, rare diseases often require specialized centralized expertise precisely because ordinary healthcare facilities encounter them so infrequently.

A physician might spend an entire career without seeing a particular prion disease.

That makes a national reference and surveillance capability more valuable, not less.

And the apparent argument that a disease is too rare to deserve attention becomes particularly troubling when that disease is invariably fatal.

Public-health priorities certainly require judgments about probability, severity, cost, and opportunity.

But those judgments require evidence.

They cannot be replaced with a shrug and an observation that the disease doesn't happen very often.

You Don't Have to Know Everything. You Have to Know Whom to Listen To.

Let's dispense with one possible defense of Kennedy.

No, the Secretary of Health and Human Services does not need a medical degree.

No, the secretary does not need to memorize every organizational chart across the CDC, NIH, FDA, and the rest of the federal healthcare bureaucracy.

And no, failing to recall the name of a specialized office during a Senate hearing does not, by itself, establish that someone cannot administer a department.

The job is not a medical trivia competition.

It is an executive leadership position.

The secretary must understand what the department's institutions do, what resources they require, what consequences follow from changing their operations, and how to incorporate specialized advice into decisions.

Most importantly, the secretary must understand the difference between personal conviction and scientific evidence.

Kennedy's testimony raises a serious question about those responsibilities.

When confronted with concerns about specialized functions, he did not supply convincing workload analyses or explain how the affected services would retain their capabilities.

He emphasized financial constraints and the relative rarity of the diseases.

The hearing cannot establish how much time Kennedy privately spends studying departmental operations.

But his public responses are fair evidence by which to judge how he explains and defends his decisions.

And the difference matters.

An executive can learn an unfamiliar subject.

An executive can consult specialists.

An executive can revise a mistaken assumption.

What an executive cannot responsibly do is treat specialized public-health functions as expendable without adequately accounting for the work those functions perform.

That is not a dispute about medical credentials.

It is a dispute about the obligations of leadership.

When Scientists Become Obstacles

The controversy becomes more serious when we leave the budget hearing and examine what happened inside the CDC itself.

In August 2025, CDC Director Susan Monarez was removed after only weeks in her position.

During testimony before the Senate Health, Education, Labor and Pensions Committee on September 17, Monarez described an extraordinary confrontation with Kennedy.

According to her sworn testimony, Kennedy demanded that she agree in advance to approve recommendations from the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices, regardless of the scientific evidence.

She also testified that he directed her to dismiss career officials responsible for vaccine policy without cause.

Monarez refused.

Kennedy disputed her account of the demand for advance approval. His department challenged her characterization of the events.

But Kennedy acknowledged during the earlier Warnock exchange that he had demanded the dismissal of career scientists or public-health experts.

Consider the implications of Monarez's allegation.

Scientific review is supposed to examine evidence and then determine whether a recommendation is supported.

Precommitting to a recommendation before reviewing the evidence reverses that process.

It turns scientific evaluation into a ceremonial endorsement of a decision already made.

Under such an arrangement, an independent scientist becomes inconvenient precisely when they perform the job correctly.

The difference between scientific leadership and ideological control is not whether leaders ever disagree with their experts.

Disagreement can be productive.

The difference is whether evidence retains the power to change the decision.

If an administrator has already decided what the scientists must conclude, the scientific process has been reduced to theater.

And if scientists who refuse to cooperate can be removed, the consequences extend far beyond a single personnel dispute.

Science does not become more trustworthy when the people responsible for reviewing evidence are expected to agree with their political superiors before seeing it.

The Institution He Leads Is the Institution He Attacks

Then there is Kennedy's extraordinary description of the CDC itself.

During Warnock's questioning, Kennedy was asked whether he had characterized the CDC as the most corrupt federal agency in the history of the world.

He denied using that precise formulation.

He then said that he regarded it as the most corrupt agency within HHS, and perhaps within the federal government.

This is a remarkable assessment from the person responsible for overseeing the institution.

Of course, federal agencies are not sacred. They can make mistakes. They can become bureaucratic, inefficient, or resistant to legitimate criticism.

The CDC, like any institution, should be subjected to rigorous oversight.

But allegations of institutional corruption require evidence, specificity, and an explanation of what conduct allegedly constitutes corruption.

Sweeping condemnation is not a substitute for identifying actual misconduct.

And it certainly cannot substitute for understanding the functions an agency performs.

Kennedy has framed his changes as necessary to restore scientific integrity and rebuild public trust.

That stated objective deserves to be examined against the actual decisions being made.

If restoring trust means stronger evidence standards, greater transparency, and better accountability, those are measurable goals.

If it means dismissing experienced scientists, reshaping advisory bodies, and demanding adherence to predetermined policy positions, the result is something very different.

It is not enough to declare that an institution has lost credibility.

A responsible leader must demonstrate that the replacement process is more reliable than the one being dismantled.

Otherwise, the word “reform” becomes little more than political packaging.

The Tragedy of Preventable Disease

Near the end of the hearing, Warnock invoked measles deaths among American children.

It was a reminder that public-health decisions are not merely abstract disputes between politicians and bureaucrats.

Measles is highly contagious. Vaccination dramatically reduces the risk of infection and serious complications, while high community vaccination coverage helps prevent outbreaks.

When vaccination coverage declines, the danger increases.

That does not mean every outbreak or death can simply be attributed to a single official. Public-health outcomes emerge from many interacting causes, including vaccination rates, local conditions, exposure patterns, and healthcare access.

But federal leadership helps shape the information, resources, recommendations, and institutional trust on which disease prevention depends.

Those responsibilities carry consequences.

The CDC is not a public-relations operation whose job is to validate the secretary's beliefs.

It is a scientific and public-health institution whose work can determine whether people receive effective protection against dangerous diseases.

Its success depends on evidence, professional independence, and the preservation of institutional knowledge.

Destroying those capacities is far easier than rebuilding them.

A specialized laboratory can be closed with a budget decision.

An experienced epidemiologist can be dismissed with a personnel order.

A surveillance team can be dismantled in an organizational restructuring.

But rebuilding the accumulated expertise, relationships, and systems that made those operations effective can take years.

And pathogens do not suspend their activity while governments reorganize.

Ignorance Is Correctable. The Refusal to Be Corrected Is the Danger.

There is an important distinction between being unfamiliar with a subject and governing as though unfamiliarity imposes no obligation to learn.

The first is an ordinary human limitation.

The second is a serious institutional risk.

A competent decision-making process permits a leader to discover that an initial assumption was wrong.

Perhaps a seemingly expensive department is preventing enormous downstream costs.

Perhaps a rarely used specialist service is essential precisely because emergencies cannot be scheduled.

Perhaps a scientific recommendation conflicts with the administrator's expectations because the evidence supports a different conclusion.

Those possibilities are not embarrassments to be suppressed.

They are reasons to conduct a proper review.

Kennedy's responses in the Warnock hearing provide an especially clear example of what happens when a discussion about public-health capability is reduced to headline numbers.

One to three rabies cases.

A rare prion disease.

A national debt figure.

A mandated percentage reduction.

Each number may be relevant to some part of the conversation.

None, standing alone, establishes that an essential preventive function can be safely dismantled.

What matters is the relationship between resources, workload, expertise, risk, and outcomes.

That relationship was precisely what Warnock was trying to examine.

And it is precisely what Kennedy's responses failed to adequately explain.

The Failure Was Designed

This controversy is larger than one Cabinet secretary's uncomfortable hearing.

It reflects a recurring weakness in political attacks on public institutions.

First, portray specialized government work as bloated, corrupt, or unnecessary.

Then reduce the staffing and expertise that make the work possible.

When the institution struggles, point to those struggles as further evidence that it cannot be trusted.

The process can become self-reinforcing.

A weakened agency is less capable of fulfilling its mission. Its diminished performance can then be used to justify additional cuts.

Meanwhile, the people most dependent on the agency's invisible services may not recognize what has disappeared until they need it.

Nobody wants to discover that a specialized infectious-disease consultation service has been hollowed out while waiting for advice about a potentially fatal exposure.

Nobody wants to learn that surveillance capacity was eliminated after an unfamiliar disease begins spreading.

And nobody wants vaccine recommendations to become a contest between political loyalty and scientific evidence.

The central obligation of public-health leadership is not to make every institution smaller, nor to defend every existing program indefinitely.

It is to preserve and improve the systems that protect the public, using evidence to distinguish what works from what does not.

That demands expertise, humility, accountability, and a willingness to change course.

Warnock concluded that Kennedy should resign or be fired.

That was Warnock's political judgment.

The underlying policy question is broader and more enduring:

What happens when the people empowered to dismantle a prevention system do not adequately account for the disasters that system exists to prevent?

The answer may not become visible immediately.

That is the nature of preventive infrastructure.

Its failures often reveal themselves only after the protections are gone.

And by then, the consequences are no longer theoretical.
