Glaring signs spotted GOP barreling toward ‘worst midterm’ of the century

Based on an article by Erik De La Garza

Trump Made the Midterms About Himself. Voters May Oblige.

The Numbers Are an Indictment, Not a Transfer of Power

CNN’s September 25 analysis found Donald Trump polling worse on several measures than Republican presidents did before the party’s major congressional defeats in 2006 and 2018. One CNN poll placed his approval 34 points underwater among U.S. adults; on the economy, 27 percent approved and 73 percent disapproved. Opposition to the Iran war and growing interest in casting an anti-Trump ballot compound the damage.

Those figures measure public rejection. They do not, by themselves, remove anyone from office, restrain presidential authority, or guarantee Republican defeat in November 2026. Institutional power remains with the officials who hold it until elections—and the structures governing those elections—produce an actual change.

Trump Claimed the Ballot

Trump has explicitly told Republican voters to behave as though he is on the ballot. That is not incidental campaign rhetoric. It makes Republican candidates answerable to a presidency whose unpopularity CNN describes as historically severe heading into a midterm.

Republicans still retain agency. Candidates, party officials, and allied institutions can embrace Trump’s demand, distance themselves from it, or attempt the familiar maneuver of defending his power while treating his consequences as somebody else’s problem. If they choose loyalty, the resulting electoral exposure is not a weather event. It is the price of a political decision.

The “Democratic Catch” Is Doing Too Much Work

CNN identifies an eight-point Democratic advantage on the generic congressional ballot, then emphasizes that the margin is smaller than Democratic leads at the same stage before the 2006 and 2018 elections. The comparison is relevant. Calling it a “catch,” however, risks turning a lopsided political environment into another story about Democratic inadequacy.

Democrats’ brand may indeed be poor, as the analysis says. But that does not erase the central fact: the president and his party face deep public disapproval, including overwhelming disapproval of his economic performance. The weaker opposition party’s limitations should not become an alibi for the governing figure’s failures.

Disapproval Is Not Accountability

Forecast language—“could,” “appears,” “looking like”—matters here. Polling cannot establish what voters will do, how district-level contests will break, or whether an eight-point national advantage will translate into control of Congress.

It also cannot substitute for accountability. A president can be politically toxic and remain institutionally powerful. His party can suffer reputational damage while its candidates continue organizing themselves around him. Treating terrible polling as punishment confuses public sentiment with material constraint.

The System Protects Power Until Votes Actually Move It

The broader pattern is not merely that an unpopular president may drag down his party. It is that American political coverage often jumps from evidence of public anger to speculation about electoral consequences, skipping the machinery that converts—or fails to convert—that anger into power.

Trump has asked Republicans to nationalize the election around him. Republican candidates must decide whether to accept that bargain. Democrats must turn dissatisfaction into victories rather than mistake favorable polling for possession of Congress. Until then, the headline is not that voters have disciplined power. It is that power remains in place while the country registers its disgust.
