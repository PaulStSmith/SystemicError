GOP’s Jack Smith 'face plant' blew up Republican midterm chances: insider

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 1, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power was behind the daisAn accusation built for televisionThe frame outran the evidenceLoyalty as legislative purposeThe system rewards the attempted hit

What happened

AlterNet reports that Republican Senator Eric Schmitt tried to accuse former Special Counsel Jack Smith of perjury during a Wednesday Senate hearing, only to reveal that his supposed evidence concerned the wrong basketball game. Schmitt claimed text messages placed Smith at an Atlanta Hawks game with Fulton County District Attorney Fani Willis on February 3, 2024; the messages reportedly referred instead to the University of Iowa Hawkeyes.

According to conservative commentator Charlie Sykes, the failed confrontation exposed Republican incompetence and the party’s continuing devotion to President Donald Trump. He argued that the spectacle would damage Republicans shortly before the midterms. The reporting provides no polling or electoral evidence establishing that the hearing actually changed the party’s midterm prospects.

The power was behind the dais

Smith was the witness, but Senate Republicans controlled the confrontation. They had the institutional platform, the prepared accusation and the ability to turn a congressional hearing into televised political theater.

Schmitt exercised that power recklessly. Before resolving the basic facts, he displayed the messages on a poster, suggested Smith had already committed perjury and called him a “dirtbag.” Senator John Kennedy helped present the exhibit. This was not a misunderstanding between equal participants; it was an attempted public prosecution conducted by senators who had not adequately checked their evidence.

An accusation built for television

The hearing, as described, was designed to recast investigations of Trump as partisan wrongdoing by investigators. That project required Smith to become the suspect and Republican senators to become the arbiters of legitimacy.

The factual collapse matters because the accusation came before the verification. Schmitt’s confidence, insult and visual prop supplied the performance of guilt; the evidence was expected to catch up. It did not. Institutional authority was used to manufacture a damaging moment, and elementary scrutiny dismantled it almost immediately.

The frame outran the evidence

AlterNet’s headline adopts Sykes’ political conclusion more firmly than the article supports. His argument that the episode harmed Republican midterm chances is plausible commentary, not a demonstrated electoral result. One disastrous hearing does not, by itself, establish changed votes or “blown” chances.

That exaggeration is unnecessary. The documented failure is serious enough: senators used a formal hearing to level a perjury accusation based on a mix-up between a professional team in Atlanta and a college team in Iowa. Inflating that into a settled forecast repeats the same underlying error—substituting a satisfying narrative for evidence.

Loyalty as legislative purpose

Sykes identifies the larger political priority: defending Trump by discrediting those who investigated him. The hearing apparently produced no substantiated perjury case. Instead, it showed Republican senators spending public authority on a retaliatory narrative whose centerpiece could not survive basic fact-checking.

The consequence extends beyond embarrassment. Congressional oversight loses force when lawmakers treat accusation as proof and hearings as content production. Every failed stunt teaches political operatives that the institution can be used for smears first, with correction arriving only after the spectacle has done its work.

The system rewards the attempted hit

This episode reveals a political structure in which the people wielding coercive institutional power can pose as victims while placing investigators on trial. The misdirection runs downward: scrutiny of Trump becomes alleged persecution by prosecutors, and a senator’s evidentiary failure becomes merely a comic mishap.

It was more than a gaffe. It was oversight hollowed out into partisan enforcement—public power deployed to punish an adversary, with accuracy treated as optional. The electoral effect remains unproven. The institutional damage was visible on the poster board.
