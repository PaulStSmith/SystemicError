SYSTEMIC ERROR / COMMENTARY

The longest decade of our lives

Based on an article by D. Earl Stephens

Paulo SantosNovember 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet columnist D. Earl Stephens argues that the two years since Donald Trump’s reported 2024 reelection have felt like “the longest decade” because opponents have remained emotionally and politically engaged rather than tuning out. Writing ahead of Tuesday’s midterm elections, he urges those readers to vote Republicans out of office and presents the election as their clearest available means of restraining the administration.

Stephens traces his anger through Trump’s 2024 victory, the January 6, 2021 attack on the Capitol, and what he regards as the Biden administration’s failure to secure timely accountability. He also levels sweeping allegations about actions during Trump’s new term, but the column supplies little supporting evidence for those claims. Its central message is exhortation, not reported verification: Trump’s opponents were right to resist, Stephens says, and must keep doing so regardless of Tuesday’s results.

Power is not evenly distributed

The column’s readers may possess votes, organizing capacity, and the ability to sustain opposition. They do not control the executive branch, Congress, federal prosecution, or the Supreme Court. Those institutions hold the power to enforce laws, deploy government personnel, shape elections, and determine whether official misconduct produces consequences.

Stephens correctly locates much of that power among Trump, Republican officials, and conservative justices. But his repeated use of “we” sometimes blurs the decisive distinction between citizens who failed to stop an outcome and officials empowered to cause or prevent it. A voter can protest impunity. An attorney general can decide whether and how aggressively to pursue a federal case. Those are not comparable forms of agency.

The decision behind the impunity

Stephens reserves particular fury for Joe Biden and former Attorney General Merrick Garland. His argument is that delayed accountability after January 6 allowed Trump to return stronger. The column, however, does not establish which prosecutorial decisions Garland made, what legal constraints applied, or whether different timing would have prevented Trump’s return. The causal claim is forceful but not demonstrated here.

The underlying institutional criticism remains sharper than the personal denunciation: a system that moves cautiously against powerful officeholders can make delay function like protection, whatever the stated rationale. Procedures imposed in the name of institutional legitimacy lose credibility when their practical result is that accountability arrives too late to affect political power.

Anger is not evidence

The article is most persuasive when it identifies officials, offices, and failures of accountability. It is weakest when accusation substitutes for documentation. Claims that people were “murdered in our streets,” troops sent to die “for his profit,” or environmental protections destroyed for personal gain require dates, decisions, victims, legal records, and evidence of motive. None is provided in the supplied column.

That omission matters politically. Trump’s opponents do not strengthen their case by treating outrage as self-authenticating. A publication confronting abuses of power must document executive orders, appropriations, court rulings, deployments, enforcement actions, and measurable consequences. Otherwise, a column condemning propaganda adopts one of propaganda’s central habits: demanding that emotional certainty carry facts it has not established.

Voters are responsible, but not sovereign

Stephens directs justified attention toward people who knowingly supported Trump after January 6. Elections confer power, and voters are not absolved from the foreseeable consequences of their choices. But the column’s fixation on their moral character risks making ordinary supporters the final explanation for an organized political project.

Party leaders recruited candidates, financed campaigns, normalized election denial, and converted personal loyalty into institutional discipline. Wealthy donors supplied resources. Media organizations supplied attention and euphemism. Officeholders possessed opportunities to impose consequences and often declined to do so. Blaming voters without mapping that machinery mistakes the fuel for the engine.

The system rewards delay

The larger pattern is not simply that one faction behaves badly while another suffers nobly. It is that American institutions repeatedly grant powerful actors time, procedural deference, and opportunities for rehabilitation that weaker defendants rarely receive. The public is then told that the resulting impunity reflects complexity, polarization, or national division—as though no identifiable official made a choice.

Stephens’s column captures the exhaustion produced by that cycle, but exhaustion is not the analysis. The analysis is that power protects itself through delay, diffused responsibility, and selective caution. Elections can interrupt that protection, but voting alone does not explain who built it, who maintained it, or why accountability so often arrives after its political value has expired.
