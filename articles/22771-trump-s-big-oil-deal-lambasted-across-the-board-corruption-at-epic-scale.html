Trump's big oil deal lambasted across the board: 'Corruption at epic scale'

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

Paulo SantosAugust 30, 20262 min read

The Oil Grab Wearing a Flag

What Happened

Trump announced a deal to control 65 billion barrels of Venezuelan oil after the U.S. military captured Nicolás Maduro and Delcy Rodríguez emerged as acting president in January, according to the source. The instant reaction from senators, journalists, economists, and even some conservatives was to argue over whether the move was corruption, socialism, imperialism, theft, or all of the above.

Power Is the Story

The important fact is not the vocabulary people reached for. It is who holds force. The U.S. president, backed by the military, is the actor with real leverage here. Venezuelans are not being offered a fair political choice; they are being confronted with a fait accompli created by military capture and executive announcement. That is not diplomacy. That is coercive state power applied to extract resource wealth.

The Label Fight Is a Distraction

The commentary quoted in the source fractures into predictable camps: corruption, imperialism, socialism, Nazi analogies. Those labels are not equal, and the right-wing ones are especially dishonest. Calling this "socialism" because Trump is associated with state ownership is a childish evasion. State ownership is not the same thing as democratic control, public accountability, or even coherent policy. What is on display here is not social ownership. It is authoritarian plunder with a corporate dividend.

Who Benefits

The source itself points to the beneficiaries: Trump family interests, Big Oil, and the political class that turns war powers into bargaining chips. Even the criticism from Democrats in the piece is strongest when it names the actual mechanism: soldiers placed at risk so a president can deliver oil wealth to private allies. That is the line worth keeping. Not abstract outrage. Not symbolic moralism. The concrete transfer of public force into private gain.

Imperialism With Marketing

Michael McFaul is right about the imperial pattern, even if the usual Washington habit of speaking in grand civilizational terms softens the blade. This is empire in the old sense: force first, extraction second, justification third. The source’s talk of 19th-century or 1700s politics is useful only insofar as it exposes how little has changed when the executive branch gets access to military power and a resource prize. The century changes. The method does not.

The Real Lesson

The deeper story is not that Trump is uniquely reckless. It is that U.S. political institutions still allow presidents to convert military power into commercial advantage and then sell the result as strategy. That is the rot: not confusion, not overreach, but a governing system built to make resource seizure look like policy. The names change. The machinery stays intact.
