It’s Just a Box — And That’s the Problem

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 20, 20267 min read

In Calvin, Oklahoma, Candi Mentink and Todd Collard run a print shop. They do not embalm bodies. They do not conduct funerals. They do not transport the deceased. They do not provide grief counseling, refrigeration, or mortuary services of any kind.

They decorate caskets.

For years, when a family asked for something personal — a racing theme, a favorite color scheme, something that reflected a life actually lived — Candi and Todd would purchase a plain casket from a wholesaler and wrap it in custom vinyl graphics. The same technology used to wrap cars, storefront windows, and commercial signage.

Then in 2021, at the Tulsa State Fair, an investigator from the Oklahoma Funeral Board shut them down. They were told they were operating illegally. They were fined thousands of dollars. They were informed that, in order to sell caskets in Oklahoma, they would need a funeral director’s license.

To sell a box.

The Licensing Wall

In Oklahoma, obtaining a funeral director’s license is not a paperwork exercise. It requires:

  • Two years of mortuary science education

  • Passing multiple professional exams

  • Completing a year-long apprenticeship

  • Overseeing funerals

  • Paying substantial fees

  • Installing embalming facilities

Candi and Todd do none of those things. They never touch a body. They never perform embalming. They operate a print machine.

Yet the state insists that unless they undergo years of training unrelated to their actual work, they cannot sell a burial container.

The absurdity becomes sharper when you realize this: Oklahoma does not regulate what a person may be buried in. A family can legally choose a cardboard box. A cloth shroud. Or no container at all.

The container itself is not treated as a public health device. It is not classified as hazardous equipment. There is no inspection regime for burial boxes. No sanitation certification.

And yet the sale of one requires years of mortuary credentials.

This is not health regulation. This is economic gatekeeping.

The Monopoly in Plain Sight

The modern funeral industry is vertically integrated. Funeral homes often provide embalming, services, facilities, transportation — and caskets. Caskets carry significant markups. When independent sellers enter the market offering lower-cost or customized alternatives, that markup comes under pressure.

Requiring a funeral director’s license to sell caskets ensures that competition must first join the guild before it can compete with the guild.

Oklahoma is one of only three states that still maintains this requirement. The fact that forty-seven other states function without it — without public health collapse, without burial chaos — tells you everything you need to know about whether this barrier is necessary.

It is not about safety.

It is about incumbency.

The Lawsuit

Candi and Todd partnered with the Institute for Justice (IJ), a public interest law firm that has long challenged occupational licensing regimes that appear disconnected from public safety. Their lawsuit argues that Oklahoma’s licensing requirement violates constitutional protections for economic liberty.

The legal argument is straightforward: If the state cannot demonstrate a rational connection between the licensing requirement and legitimate health or safety concerns, then the regulation is arbitrary.

Courts have historically been deferential to occupational licensing. Under “rational basis” review, the government only needs to show that a law is conceivably related to a legitimate purpose. But when the state allows burial in cardboard while criminalizing the sale of a decorated wooden box absent mortuary schooling, the rationality claim strains credibility.

This case forces a court to confront an uncomfortable question: At what point does licensing become naked protectionism?

The Stakes

This fight is not really about caskets.

It is about whether the state can require years of unrelated training to enter a market simply because an established industry prefers fewer competitors.

Occupational licensing in the United States has expanded dramatically over the past half-century. What was once limited to high-risk professions — medicine, aviation, structural engineering — now covers interior designers, florists in some jurisdictions, hair braiders, and countless other trades.

Licensing can serve an important role. When asymmetric information and real risk are present, regulation protects consumers. You want surgeons licensed. You want commercial pilots licensed. You want nuclear plant operators licensed and aggressively regulated.

But wrapping vinyl around a casket does not implicate life-or-death safety concerns.

When licensing ceases to track risk, it ceases to be a safety tool and becomes a barrier to entry.

Barriers to entry increase prices. They reduce innovation. They consolidate power.

In the funeral market — where families are often grieving, vulnerable, and making time-sensitive decisions — price competition is not trivial. It matters.

When Licensing Makes Sense

It is important not to collapse into anti-regulatory reflex. The lesson here is not that licensing is inherently bad.

Licensing makes sense when:

  • There is substantial risk of physical harm.

  • Consumers cannot easily evaluate competence.

  • Errors carry irreversible consequences.

  • Technical complexity requires standardized training.

A surgeon who lacks training can kill someone. A structural engineer who miscalculates can cause buildings to collapse. An anesthesiologist’s error is fatal.

Those professions not only justify licensing — they justify ongoing oversight, inspection, and discipline.

Similarly, industries that handle infectious materials, hazardous waste, or critical infrastructure warrant strong regulatory frameworks.

The public interest demands it.

When Licensing Becomes Protectionism

Licensing becomes suspect when:

  • The regulated activity poses minimal risk.

  • The required training is unrelated to the activity.

  • The primary beneficiaries are existing license holders.

  • Consumers face higher prices without measurable safety gains.

The Oklahoma casket rule checks every box.

Mortuary science does not teach vinyl wrapping. Apprenticeships at funeral homes do not train print shop operators. Installing an embalming room does not improve the quality of a graphic design.

The requirement is not merely excessive. It is irrelevant.

And irrelevance is the tell.

When the credential does not track the task, the purpose is exclusion.

The Speech Dimension

After enforcement began, Candi and Todd were reportedly told they could not even discuss caskets with customers who walked into their shop.

At that point, regulation drifts into suppression.

The state is no longer regulating conduct tied to health risks. It is controlling who may speak commercially about a product.

That is a dangerous expansion of power.

Economic Liberty and Modern Courts

Economic liberty has long occupied an uneasy space in American constitutional law. After the Lochner era, courts grew wary of striking down economic regulations. Deference became the norm.

But deference is not abdication.

If rational basis review becomes a rubber stamp for any barrier that benefits incumbents, then constitutional protections become ornamental.

Cases like this test whether courts are willing to distinguish between legitimate regulation and economic favoritism.

The Broader Pattern

This story echoes across industries:

  • Hair braiders required to obtain cosmetology licenses unrelated to braiding.

  • Food entrepreneurs blocked by commercial kitchen mandates even when producing shelf-stable goods.

  • Small-scale artisans restricted by certification regimes designed for industrial operators.

Each case follows the same structure: A low-risk activity is swept into a high-cost licensing scheme designed for a different profession.

The justification is “professional standards.”

The outcome is reduced competition.

The Consumer Impact

Funeral expenses in the United States are notoriously high. Caskets alone can cost thousands of dollars.

Allowing independent sellers increases options. It encourages customization. It pressures pricing.

When only licensed funeral directors may sell caskets, the market narrows. When independent print shops can compete, families gain leverage.

The ultimate losers in protectionist licensing are not just entrepreneurs. They are consumers.

A Line That Must Be Drawn

Society needs regulation.

We need building codes. We need food safety inspections. We need aviation standards.

But regulation must align with risk.

If a burial container itself is unregulated in substance — if cardboard suffices — then requiring mortuary science credentials to sell one cannot plausibly be about safety.

It is about preserving a revenue stream.

The law should distinguish between industries that demand rigorous oversight and trades that pose minimal harm.

A surgeon should be licensed and monitored.

A nuclear engineer should be licensed and monitored.

A commercial airline pilot should be licensed and monitored.

A print shop owner wrapping a box should not need two years of mortuary school.

What This Case Represents

Candi and Todd’s lawsuit is narrow in subject but broad in implication. If courts uphold the licensing barrier, it signals that states may impose unrelated credentialing requirements without meaningful scrutiny.

If the barrier falls, it reinforces a principle: Regulation must be connected to real risk.

“A casket is just a box” sounds simplistic.

It is meant to.

Because when the state demands embalming rooms and funeral apprenticeships to sell a decorative container — while permitting burial in cardboard — the mismatch becomes impossible to ignore.

The question before the courts is not whether funerals matter.

It is whether protectionism may hide behind the language of professionalism.

And whether the Constitution tolerates that disguise.
