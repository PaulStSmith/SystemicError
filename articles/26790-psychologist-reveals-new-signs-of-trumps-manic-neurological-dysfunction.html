Psychologist reveals new signs of Trump’s manic 'neurological dysfunction'

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 24, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe diagnosis outruns the evidenceThe presidency is the power centerSpectacle replaces accountabilityEvidence must match the stakes

What happened

AlterNet reports that psychologist and body-language commentator John Garrison interpreted Donald Trump’s conduct at Wednesday rallies as evidence of possible neurological dysfunction. Garrison pointed to an abrupt vocalization, facial and arm movements, a brief interruption in speech, repetition, and apparent trouble recalling names.

Garrison suggested several possible explanations, including a focal aware seizure, but the report supplies no examination, diagnosis, medical records, or corroborating clinical assessment. It does document verbal errors: Trump apparently referred to “Lake America,” could not clearly name the lake he was discussing, and substituted the Pulitzer Prize for the Nobel Peace Prize. Garrison characterized Trump’s subsequent behavior as unusually uninhibited, grandiose, and “almost manic,” and predicted further deterioration.

The diagnosis outruns the evidence

The observable facts are limited: Trump made an unusual sound, moved oddly, hesitated, repeated himself, and confused prominent names. Those incidents can legitimately prompt questions about his fitness. They do not, by themselves, establish a neurological disorder, seizure, mania, or progressive decline.

Garrison repeatedly moves from describing behavior to assigning clinical significance without direct evaluation. Even his most specific explanation is presented as one possibility among several. Yet AlterNet’s headline compresses that uncertainty into a psychologist “revealing” new signs of dysfunction. Nothing was revealed in the medical sense. A commentator watched clips and offered an interpretation.

That distinction matters because weak evidence does not become stronger when attached to technical vocabulary. “Motor dysfunction,” “vocal dysfunction,” and “disinhibition” sound diagnostic, but the article provides no clinical basis for determining their cause.

The presidency is the power center

Trump holds the institutional power in this story. A president can direct the executive branch, shape foreign policy, command the military, appoint officials, and dominate the national agenda. Any credible evidence that a president cannot reliably perform those duties deserves serious scrutiny.

But the report does not examine how presidential capacity is actually monitored. It identifies no assessment by a treating physician, no formal review, no disclosure decision by White House officials, and no action by officials empowered to respond to incapacity. The people around a president control access, information, scheduling, and much of the public account of his health. They are largely absent here.

That omission leaves a structural question unanswered: not whether a remote commentator can label a video clip, but whether institutions responsible for evaluating presidential fitness are providing reliable evidence to the public.

Spectacle replaces accountability

The article’s framing invites readers to treat Trump’s strangest moments as a diagnostic puzzle. That is a form of misdirection. Trump’s confusion over names and his boasts about power are politically relevant without requiring a speculative neurological explanation.

When coverage medicalizes public conduct, it can blur agency. False statements, reckless decisions, authoritarian rhetoric, and abuses of office must be assessed as actions by a powerful political actor unless evidence establishes that incapacity caused them. An unsupported diagnosis can inadvertently turn responsibility into pathology: the problem becomes what may be happening inside Trump’s brain rather than what he does with presidential authority.

It also gives defenders an easy escape. They can dismiss the amateur diagnosis and pretend that the documented behavior—and the legitimate question of fitness—disappears with it.

Evidence must match the stakes

The larger pattern is a degraded system of presidential accountability. Official health disclosures are often managed political products, while partisan media fill the resulting vacuum with certainty they have not earned. One side minimizes visible problems; the other converts clips into diagnoses. Neither practice supplies the public with a trustworthy mechanism for judging capacity.

A president’s health is not gossip because presidential power is not symbolic. But the answer to institutional opacity is evidence, not remote clinical theater. AlterNet had material for a defensible story about unusual public behavior and unanswered questions. By presenting speculation as revelation, it weakened the very scrutiny that concentrated power requires.
