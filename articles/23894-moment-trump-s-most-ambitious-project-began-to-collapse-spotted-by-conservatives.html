Moment Trump's 'most ambitious project' began to collapse spotted by conservatives

Declaring Trump Defeated Is How Institutions Excuse Their Surrender

The Victory Lap Comes Too Early

Peter Wehner and Jonathan Rauch argue that Donald Trump’s second-term effort to remake American society has already failed. They cite resistance to the immigration crackdown in Minneapolis, court defeats, sinking approval ratings, and fractures within MAGA as evidence that civil society proved harder to dominate than the administration expected.

That may describe a real political setback. It does not justify converting partial resistance into a completed victory.

The State Still Holds the Weapons

The power imbalance is not complicated. Protesters can refuse cooperation. Universities, law firms, and news organizations can resist intimidation. Judges can block executive initiatives. But the Trump administration controls the executive branch and has used federal authority against immigrants, cities, and institutions it regards as hostile.

Operation Metro Surge was not a cultural misunderstanding that Minneapolis eventually corrected. It was an immigration crackdown carried out with federal power. If resistance forced a retreat, the credit belongs to those who imposed political costs—not to a system that functioned automatically.

The administration chose coercion. Federal officials executed it. Institutions that complied, hesitated, or remained silent helped establish its reach.

Retreat Is Not Disarmament

Wehner and Rauch identify Minneapolis as the point where Trump’s power began to unravel. That conclusion may prove accurate, but the evidence described supports a narrower claim: the administration encountered limits.

A blocked initiative does not erase the intimidation that preceded it. A lower approval rating does not restore speech chilled by government threats. A fractured coalition does not disable the presidency. Even the authors acknowledge that Trump continues targeting universities and free expression. That admission sits uneasily beside their assurance that the country is now less dangerous.

Authoritarian politics rarely requires total victory. It can succeed by making institutions calculate the cost of opposition, forcing vulnerable people to absorb state violence, and establishing that punishment is available even when a particular campaign later retreats.

Conservative Reassurance Serves a Purpose

The essay’s framing shifts attention from what the administration did to whether Trump achieved his grandest ambition. That is a convenient standard. If total social transformation is the test, almost any failed authoritarian project can be recast as institutional resilience.

This is misdirection by scale. The weaker actors—immigrants subjected to enforcement, protesters confronting federal force, universities and newsrooms placed under pressure—bear the immediate consequences. The powerful actor is then judged not by the harm it deliberately inflicted, but by whether it conquered everything.

Calling Trump’s project a failure may also offer conservative institutions an escape from accounting for their own role. The Republican coalition enabled his return to power. Elite organizations had repeated opportunities to oppose coercion before resistance in Minneapolis allegedly changed the political weather. Celebrating civil society now risks laundering earlier accommodation into retrospective courage.

Fear Is Not a Polling Metric

The claim that Trump “no longer inspires the fear he once did” confuses national political mood with the position of those directly exposed to state power. A president can lose popularity while immigration agents remain empowered, universities remain vulnerable to retaliation, and targeted speakers still face institutional pressure.

Fear is distributed according to power. Commentators may perceive decline from approval ratings and courtroom losses. People facing detention, removal, censorship, or federal force encounter the administration differently. Their danger does not disappear because the governing coalition has become less cohesive.

The System Resisted After It Bent

The larger pattern is not a simple triumph of American pluralism. It is a cycle of institutional submission, uneven resistance, political overreach, and elite declarations that the emergency has passed.

Resistance matters. Courts, organized communities, and independent institutions can restrict executive power when they are willing to use the authority they possess. But resilience is not a national personality trait. It is the accumulated result of specific people refusing specific demands—and of powerful institutions deciding, often belatedly, that compliance has become costlier than defiance.

Trump’s project may be weaker. The machinery that enabled it remains. Declaring the battle won before that machinery is dismantled is not analysis. It is permission for the institutions that bent to congratulate themselves for never breaking.
