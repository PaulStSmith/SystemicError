'Stories don't stack up': Pete Hegseth's latest spin on boat attack blown out of the water

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 5, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:A Masterclass in Absurdity: Parsing the Pentagon’s Latest Spin

Pete Hegseth (Reuters)

The latest explanation coming out of the Pentagon over the secondary attack on survivors on a purported drug boat in response to “Kill them all” orders from Defense Department Secretary Pete Hegseth was thoroughly dismantled on MS NOW on Thursday morning.

Late Wednesday, the Wall Street Journal reported that Adm. Frank Bradley is poised to tell lawmakers that it was his belief that the two survivors clinging to the side of the flaming boat “were attempting to continue their drug run, making them and the already-damaged vessel legitimate targets for another attack.”

Not one member of the “Morning Joe” panel bought the story, with co-host Joe Scarborough blurting, “That’s today’s explanation” over the constantly evolving attempt to put out the firestorm.

“The explanation we’re hearing in a little bit is that maybe these two guys who survived were attempting to get back on the boat and somehow continue the mission, this flaming, smoking vessel,” co-host Willie Geist reported to laughter from the panel.

“Exactly. That these guys were going to climb back on board and continue their drug run,” he added.

Calling the latest spin “insane,” Scarborough interjected, “Oh, does that mean now the new sort of rules of warfare is if you have a prisoner and they’re indefensible and they have a radio, you can shoot them in the head. That’s now the new Hegseth policy: if they have a radio, you can shoot them in the head.”

After the “Morning Joe” co-host added, “They’re making s—t up,” Mika Brzezinski contributed “Utterly ridiculous.”

“Sorry, Pete,” Scarborough ranted. “None of these stories stack up. You were so sure that it was destroyed and incapacitated that you left the room. That’s your story now, are you so stupid that you don’t know when a boat is destroyed and when a boat’s not destroyed? Pete, you can’t have it both ways. You can’t say, ‘Oh, well, it was still on its drug run, and. Oh, well, I saw it get blown up, so I just left the room to go play Parcheesi.”

Our Analysis:

A Masterclass in Absurdity: Parsing the Pentagon’s Latest Spin

The most recent narrative contortion from the Pentagon, concerning a “secondary attack on survivors on a purported drug boat,” is not merely unconvincing—it borders on the farcical. As reported by the Wall Street Journal and subsequently torn apart on MS NOW, we have been gifted with a tale that plummets past the bounds of credulity and splashes into the murky waters of outright absurdity.

Defense Department Secretary Pete Hegseth’s orders to “Kill them all,” aimed at survivors clinging to a debris-laden drug vessel, have sparked a firestorm requiring a battalion of spin doctors to quench. Yet, the Pentagon’s explanation, as relayed by Adm. Frank Bradley, only fans the flames. Bradley’s assertion that the two survivors were “attempting to continue their drug run,” thereby rendering them “legitimate targets,” is a narrative leap that would make Evel Knievel balk.

Not a Single Buyer in Sight

The skepticism isn’t just palpable; it’s unanimous. The “Morning Joe” panel’s reaction ranged from incredulous laughter to outright dismissal, with co-host Joe Scarborough’s incredulity boiling over into the declaration, “They’re making s—t up.” This isn’t merely skepticism; it’s a wholesale rejection of the Pentagon’s attempt to retrofit logic onto a situation that seemingly defies it.

The Hegseth Doctrine: A Policy of Absurdity

Scarborough’s critique escalates to highlight the dangerous precedent this rationale could set, mockingly dubbing it “the new Hegseth policy.” The suggestion that possession of a radio—or, by extension, any minimal capability to pose a threat—could justify execution is chilling. It’s a policy derived not from strategic military reasoning but from a place of desperation, attempting to shield a high-level official from the consequences of a potentially unlawful order.

The Art of the Impossible Excuse

The Pentagon’s narrative crumbles under its own weight. The notion that survivors of a missile strike, clinging to the wreckage of their vessel, would attempt to “continue their drug run” is so implausible it would be comedic if the stakes weren’t so tragically high. This isn’t a storyline fit for a military strategy room; it’s more suited to a satirical sketch, lacking only the canned laughter to punctuate its absurdity.

A Question of Competence—or Lack Thereof

Scarborough’s rhetorical question to Hegseth, questioning his ability to discern whether a boat is “destroyed and when a boat’s not destroyed,” strikes at the heart of the issue. This isn’t just about a failure to communicate effectively; it’s about a fundamental failure to operate within the bounds of reason and accountability. The ludicrous nature of the Pentagon’s defense suggests not just a moral bankruptcy but a breathtaking incompetence.

In conclusion, the Pentagon’s latest attempt to justify the unjustifiable is not only unconvincing; it’s an insult to the intelligence of the public. The narrative offered up by Adm. Frank Bradley, under the apparent direction of Pete Hegseth, doesn’t just stretch credulity—it obliterates it. As the MS NOW panel rightly identifies, this isn’t just a flawed policy or a miscommunication; it’s a fabrication, a desperate bid to mask a deeply troubling incident. The only thing more alarming than the Pentagon’s story is the possibility that they believed it would be accepted.
