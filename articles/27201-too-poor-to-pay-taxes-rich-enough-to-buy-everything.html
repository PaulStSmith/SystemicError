Too Poor to Pay Taxes, Rich Enough to Buy Everything

By Paulo Santos

How billionaires turn stocks into spending money, tax deductions, and political influence — while insisting their fortunes aren't taxable income.

There is a peculiar creature in American economics: the billionaire who is simultaneously too poor to pay taxes and wealthy enough to purchase almost anything.

Ask how much money this person has, and the answer is billions. Ask how much income they have, and suddenly the conversation becomes complicated.

“That’s not income,” we're told. “It's unrealized appreciation. They don't actually have that money.”

Fair enough.

Except when they want to buy a yacht.

Or another mansion.

Or an entire company.

Then, miraculously, those supposedly inaccessible billions become useful again.

And when the billionaire decides to donate some of that wealth to charity, the tax system performs yet another remarkable transformation: assets that supposedly weren't income can suddenly support a charitable deduction based on their market value.

Welcome to the wonderful world of American billionaire taxation, where wealth is real when it provides purchasing power, unrealized when taxation comes knocking, and charitable when it generates favorable publicity.

The contradictions are not necessarily evidence of illegal behavior.

They're features of the system.

The Billionaire Who Doesn't Need a Paycheck

Most Americans understand taxation through a relatively straightforward process.

You work. You earn money. The government takes its share.

Your employer doesn't generally give you the option of declaring that your paycheck represents unrealized potential and therefore shouldn't be taxed.

Billionaires, however, often accumulate their fortunes differently.

Consider someone who owns $10 billion in company stock. Those shares appreciate to $15 billion. Their net worth increases by $5 billion, but under the ordinary American income-tax rules, that appreciation generally isn't taxable until the shares are sold.

There are legitimate economic and administrative reasons for distinguishing unrealized gains from realized income. Markets fluctuate. Valuations can change. Selling assets to meet a tax obligation can create liquidity problems.

But something interesting happens when we introduce borrowing.

Our billionaire approaches a financial institution and offers a portion of that appreciated stock as collateral for a loan.

The bank evaluates the shares, accepts the collateral, and provides hundreds of millions of dollars in cash.

The billionaire can now spend that money.

The transaction generally doesn't trigger income tax because borrowed money creates an obligation to repay. A loan isn't ordinarily income.

And that distinction makes perfect sense in isolation.

What becomes controversial is the arrangement that emerges when someone possesses enough appreciating assets to borrow repeatedly, finance an extravagant lifestyle, and avoid selling substantial holdings.

The arrangement has a name.

Buy, Borrow, Die.

Buy appreciating assets. Borrow against them instead of selling. Eventually die, leaving heirs who may benefit from a step-up in the assets' tax basis.

Under existing rules, that final step can eliminate federal income tax on substantial appreciation that occurred during the deceased owner's lifetime.

The underlying wealth was genuine enough to secure loans.

The borrowed cash was genuine enough to purchase luxury goods.

But the appreciation itself may never have appeared as taxable capital gains.

It's a remarkably convenient distinction.

The Liquidity Paradox

Whenever someone proposes taxing billionaires based on their enormous holdings, a familiar defense appears.

“They don't have billions sitting in a checking account!”

Of course they don't.

Nobody seriously imagines that Jeff Bezos keeps his fortune in a gigantic savings account, occasionally walking into a bank branch to withdraw enough money to purchase a cruise ship.

The argument deliberately confuses liquidity with economic capacity.

An individual who owns $20 billion in readily tradable securities is not economically equivalent to someone with $20 in their wallet merely because neither has sold those securities today.

Banks understand this perfectly.

They routinely evaluate assets as collateral. They recognize that stocks have economic value even before their owners sell them.

The tax system recognizes this value, too, in numerous contexts.

And that gives us a useful principle:

If an asset's value is sufficiently real to support hundreds of millions in personal borrowing, policymakers can reasonably examine whether using that value should trigger some recognition of previously untaxed appreciation.

This doesn't require declaring every loan taxable income.

A family borrowing against its home to repair the roof isn't the same economic situation as a billionaire borrowing hundreds of millions against a massive stock portfolio to finance personal spending.

A sensible policy can distinguish between the two.

The objection that billionaires “don't have the money” cannot, by itself, settle the taxation question when financial institutions are already willing to provide that money against their assets.

Enter the Philanthropist

Now comes the second act.

Comedian Alex Falcone recently examined billionaire philanthropy in a video asking whether the entire enterprise might be a scam.

His presentation is deliberately humorous, but it raises questions that extend well beyond individual billionaires behaving badly.

Consider what happens when our hypothetical billionaire decides to donate $100 million in highly appreciated stock.

If the shares meet the applicable requirements, the billionaire can transfer them directly to a qualified charitable organization without first selling them and realizing the appreciation.

Depending on the recipient, the type of stock, and the applicable limitations, the donor may also qualify for a charitable deduction based on the shares' fair market value.

Let that sink in.

When the billionaire wants to spend against the assets, they're valuable collateral.

When the government considers taxing their appreciation, the gains haven't been realized.

And when the billionaire wants a charitable deduction, the donated shares have a recognized market value.

Legally, these are different transactions, governed by different provisions of the tax code. The distinctions aren't imaginary, and the tax deduction isn't automatically equal to the donation's value.

But together they reveal something important: the same financial wealth receives remarkably different treatment depending on what its owner decides to do with it.

And the story gets considerably more interesting when the charitable recipient isn't an independent organization delivering immediate assistance.

What happens when the billionaire gives the money to a foundation associated with the billionaire?

The Charity That Keeps the Money

Private foundations can perform important charitable work. They finance medical research, education, disaster relief, scientific programs, and countless other activities.

Their legal structure also allows assets to remain invested while charitable spending is distributed over time.

Most U.S. private nonoperating foundations are subject to an annual minimum-distribution regime, generally calculated around 5% of relevant investment assets, with adjustments and qualifying expenses.

That matters because it means the commonly repeated claim that foundations have no obligation to spend anything is inaccurate.

But it also means a foundation can ordinarily preserve a substantial portion of its assets in any given year.

Imagine donating $1 billion to a foundation you establish.

You no longer personally own that money. The foundation is legally separate, subject to charitable-purpose requirements and restrictions on self-dealing.

Those restrictions are important.

Nevertheless, the donor and associates may continue to exercise considerable influence over the foundation's priorities through its governance.

The money can remain invested, potentially generating returns, while the foundation distributes qualifying amounts over time.

The donor receives recognition for a billion-dollar charitable commitment even though the benefits to operating charities or intended beneficiaries may emerge over many years.

This isn't necessarily fraudulent.

It's an entirely different arrangement from handing a billion dollars directly to organizations serving people today.

And American public discourse routinely treats the two as interchangeable.

The billionaire gets the headlines now.

Society may receive the benefits later.

Philanthropy Without the Philanthropist's Name

There is another charitable mechanism worth examining: the donor-advised fund.

A donor-advised fund, or DAF, allows someone to contribute assets to a sponsoring charitable organization while retaining advisory privileges concerning future distributions and investments.

The sponsor legally controls the donated assets. The donor generally cannot simply reclaim them for personal use.

Yet the donor can receive an eligible tax deduction when making the contribution, even if the assets don't immediately reach an operating charity.

Unlike private nonoperating foundations, individual donor-advised fund accounts generally aren't subject to a comparable annual payout requirement.

And donor-advised funds can provide an additional benefit: privacy.

The ultimate recipients of charitable grants may receive money from the sponsoring organization without the original donor necessarily being publicly identified.

Privacy isn't inherently sinister. Some donors have entirely legitimate reasons for avoiding publicity.

But donor anonymity also complicates efforts to understand who finances organizations engaged in political, ideological, or controversial social campaigns.

The public may learn that a charitable organization received funding without knowing which extraordinarily wealthy individual originally supplied it.

Once again, the question is not whether every such donation is abusive.

The question is whether the public receives sufficient accountability in exchange for the substantial tax advantages offered to donors.

If the public treasury effectively subsidizes charitable giving through forgone tax revenue, should the public have no meaningful way to understand the concentration of influence behind that giving?

When Charity Meets Business

Falcone's video offers a particularly revealing example involving Elon Musk.

An October 2026 investigation by Mississippi Today documented millions of dollars donated to the Southaven Police Department by Musk-associated business interests and the Musk Foundation.

According to the reporting, the Musk Foundation provided $1.85 million for police equipment, including Flock surveillance cameras and an autonomous drone system.

The donations occurred amid the development of a nearby data-center project associated with Musk's businesses.

Local officials characterized the foundation's contribution as a public-safety grant, and the reporting described a separate business donation agreement explicitly disclaiming any exchange of benefits.

Those details matter. The available reporting does not establish that the foundation purchased government approval.

But it does establish a relationship worth examining.

A billionaire's private business interests and charitable activities operated in the same community, around the same time, as local officials were dealing with the consequences of a major industrial development.

And some of the philanthropic resources supported expanded police surveillance capabilities.

Consider the questions this raises.

Who determines what constitutes a public benefit?

Who decides whether more surveillance equipment is preferable to additional teachers, expanded healthcare, or infrastructure improvements?

Should private philanthropic funding be allowed to reshape local public-service priorities without the level of democratic deliberation normally associated with public spending?

The billionaire may regard the donation as generous.

City officials may appreciate resources that their budgets otherwise couldn't provide.

Residents, however, still live with the consequences of the decisions made.

A charitable donation does not automatically answer whether the funded project serves their interests.

And the existence of a tax advantage makes that question more—not less—important.

When Generosity Becomes Governance

Here is the issue that too often disappears beneath the celebration of billionaire philanthropy.

Tax deductions are not free.

When the government allows a taxpayer to reduce taxable income through qualifying charitable contributions, it generally forgoes revenue it otherwise might have collected.

That does not mean every donated dollar would otherwise have become a dollar of tax revenue. Deduction values depend on rates, limits, and individual circumstances.

It does mean charitable deductions represent a public policy choice.

Society has decided to encourage particular activities through favorable tax treatment.

The resulting private charitable spending can provide genuine benefits.

But it also gives donors substantial discretion over which purposes receive support.

And the wealthier the donor, the more consequential that discretion can become.

An ordinary worker generally cannot redirect their federal tax liability into a personally selected charitable initiative.

A billionaire can sometimes make an enormous qualifying donation, reduce their tax bill, and continue influencing how the charitable assets are deployed.

The two individuals aren't exercising comparable economic power.

Wealth already translates into influence over corporations, markets, media, and politics.

Charitable structures can add another channel through which private wealth shapes public life.

This raises an uncomfortable democratic question:

When public policy subsidizes private philanthropy, how much public influence should one extremely wealthy individual be allowed to purchase with the resulting tax advantages?

Not every dollar of philanthropic influence is improper. Not every charitable foundation is a vanity project. And publicly funded programs are hardly immune to waste, corruption, or misguided priorities.

But pointing out government imperfections doesn't eliminate the accountability problem.

The billionaire's personal preferences are not automatically a substitute for democratic decision-making.

A Tax System That Recognizes Economic Reality

The obvious response is not to declare all philanthropy fraudulent, outlaw borrowing, or tax every fluctuating dollar of paper wealth as though it were cash.

The challenge is to design rules that recognize how substantial wealth actually functions.

Consider several reforms.

First, address borrowing against enormous appreciated holdings.

For ultrawealthy taxpayers, substantial personal borrowing secured by appreciated financial assets could trigger a narrowly defined tax event or a prepayment against future capital-gains liability.

The system would need thresholds, exclusions, protections against double taxation, and rules distinguishing ordinary financing from arrangements primarily used to access untaxed appreciation.

The purpose would not be to tax the entire borrowed amount as though it were wages.

It would be to prevent indefinite borrowing from functioning as a permanent substitute for realizing taxable gains.

Second, reconsider the step-up in basis at death.

Under current rules, inherited assets often receive a new tax basis reflecting their value at the owner's death.

That can erase federal income tax on appreciation accumulated over decades.

Congress could instead require recognition of gains at death, carry over the original basis, or adopt a hybrid system with exemptions and deferrals for genuinely illiquid family businesses and other appropriate circumstances.

There is no compelling economic reason to assume that appreciation disappears merely because its owner does.

Third, distinguish charitable giving from charitable warehousing.

If a donor receives significant tax benefits immediately, the charitable structure should provide meaningful assurance that those assets will serve qualifying public purposes within a reasonable period.

This is especially relevant to donor-advised funds, where an individual account can lack a statutory annual distribution requirement.

Reforms could introduce payout deadlines, stronger disclosure rules, or different tax treatment depending on when assets actually reach charitable programs.

Fourth, scrutinize transactions where philanthropic spending and private business interests overlap.

Not every such transaction is improper.

But when a billionaire's charitable organization funds projects in communities where the billionaire's businesses seek approvals, infrastructure, or other advantages, stronger transparency can help establish whether public benefits are being independently evaluated.

Charity should not become an opaque substitute for corporate expenditures or public accountability.

The Question Isn't Whether Billionaires Give

Some billionaires contribute significant resources to causes that genuinely improve people's lives.

That deserves acknowledgment.

A medical breakthrough financed by philanthropic donations remains valuable regardless of the donor's tax bracket.

A child receiving treatment doesn't become less healthy because the funding came from a wealthy person's foundation.

The benefits are real.

But useful philanthropy does not automatically justify every tax preference surrounding it.

Nor should society treat charitable donations as compensation for weaknesses elsewhere in the tax system.

A billionaire can fund a hospital and still be subject to legitimate questions about how their wealth is taxed.

A foundation can do important work while still exercising disproportionate influence over public priorities.

And someone who gives away millions can remain a beneficiary of arrangements that allow enormous appreciation to escape income taxation.

These propositions aren't mutually exclusive.

Philanthropy and taxation serve different purposes.

One allows individuals to support causes they choose.

The other finances collectively determined obligations.

A functioning society needs room for both.

What it doesn't need is a system that allows private charitable activity to become a rhetorical shield against scrutiny of how enormous fortunes accumulate, finance consumption, and transfer across generations.

The Money Was Always Real

Return to our supposedly cash-poor billionaire.

They own billions in stock.

They borrow against it to finance personal expenditures.

They transfer some of it into a charitable structure and potentially receive substantial tax benefits.

They retain influence over the distribution of donated assets, subject to the applicable legal restrictions.

And when the conversation turns to taxing their vast appreciation, we're reminded that the wealth isn't really income.

There is a legitimate legal distinction between owning appreciated property and realizing taxable gains.

But a legal distinction is not a commandment carved into stone.

Tax law is a set of policy choices. Those choices can be evaluated against economic reality, changed when they produce undesirable incentives, and redesigned when they no longer serve their stated purposes.

The debate shouldn't be reduced to whether billionaires have enormous piles of cash sitting in bank accounts.

Their economic power doesn't depend on that.

Their borrowing capacity demonstrates it.

Their philanthropic commitments demonstrate it.

Their ability to influence entire industries and communities demonstrates it.

The real question is whether the tax system should continue allowing that economic power to generate substantial private benefits while portions of the underlying appreciation remain untaxed indefinitely.

If wealth is real enough to borrow against, real enough to buy companies with, and real enough to generate charitable tax deductions, it is real enough to be considered when designing a fair tax system.

The money was never imaginary.

Only the insistence that it couldn't possibly be taxed was.
