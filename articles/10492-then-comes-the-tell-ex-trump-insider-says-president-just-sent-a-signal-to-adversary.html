'Then comes the tell': Ex-Trump insider says president just sent a 'signal' to adversary

Soviet-born Lev Parnas, who worked closely with Rudy Giuliani during Trump's first administration, was purportedly sent to Ukraine to help Trump and Giuliani make contacts there for the purposes of digging up negative info on Hunter Biden. Parnas was later convicted of campaign finance violations, and today reports on Trump from outside the administration.

Parnas late on Saturday published an article called "Trump’s NATO Letter Exposes His Deal with Putin," in which he argues that Trump's "reckless letter reveals he’ll only act against Russia if it benefits him, not democracy or freedom."

In his letter, Trump wrote in part, "I am ready to do major Sanctions on Russia when all NATO Nations have agreed, and started, to do the same thing, and when all NATO Nations STOP BUYING OIL FROM RUSSIA... I believe that this, plus NATO, as a group, placing 50% to 100% TARIFFS ON CHINA, to be fully withdrawn after the WAR with Russia and Ukraine is ended, will also be of great help in ENDING this deadly, but RIDICULOUS, WAR."

Trump then added, "China has a strong control, and even grip, over Russia, and these powerful Tariffs will break that grip. This is not TRUMP’S WAR (it would never have started if I was President!), it is Biden’s and Zelenskyy’s WAR. I am only here to help stop it, and save thousands of Russian and Ukrainian lives (7,118 lives lost last week, alone. CRAZY!)"

For Parnas, though, the letter can be seen as "extortion" or even "blackmail."

"Trump just posted a so-called 'letter' addressed to NATO and the world. And once again, it’s not just reckless—it’s dangerous," Parnas wrote. "He says NATO should slap 50% to 100% tariffs on China, but not as a long-term strategy—only until the war ends. That’s not foreign policy. That’s a shakedown. He’s treating a global war like one of his real estate deals, bargaining with tariffs as if the lives of Ukrainians are chips at his poker table."

Parnas also highlighted what he says was a "tell" in the letter.

"And then comes the tell: Trump repeats his old line—'this is not Trump’s war, it’s Biden’s and Zelenskyy’s war.' That is the Kremlin’s narrative word-for-word," according to Parnas. "Trump is telling NATO allies that Ukraine’s survival, and Russia’s aggression, don’t matter unless they serve his personal agenda. He even throws out fake casualty figures, pretending concern for '7,118 lives lost last week alone,' while making it clear he’ll only act if NATO bends to his demands. That’s not leadership. That’s blackmail."

Parnas then translated that tell into what it means:

"This is what I’ve been warning you about. Trump is not serious about stopping Putin. He’s not serious about NATO. He’s not serious about saving lives. He is serious about leverage—about using this war to strengthen his power, weaken the alliance, and tie Ukraine’s survival to his trade wars with China. And while he postures about 'ending' the war, what he’s really signaling to Putin is simple: I got your back."

After declaring that Putin is "listening" to the signals, Parnas writes, "I know how these backchannel games work—I was born in the Soviet Union. I grew up in this world. I know how it works because I lived it."

"And later, I was sent by Donald Trump himself to put pressure on President Zelensky, to deal directly with Russian oligarchs," the ex-Trump insider added. "I saw firsthand how these oligarchs and foreign operatives operate, how they treat lives as nothing more than bargaining chips. That’s why when I read Trump’s so-called 'letter' to NATO, I don’t see diplomacy—I see extortion."

Our Analysis:

Trump's Dangerous Gambit: A Closer Look at the NATO Letter

In a recent revelation that should surprise exactly no one, Donald Trump has once again found himself at the center of controversy—this time, over a so-called "letter" to NATO that screams of extortion rather than diplomacy. Soviet-born Lev Parnas, a figure with his own checkered past and deep ties to Trump's first administration, has come forward with scathing criticism of Trump's communication, labeling it a clear "signal" to Vladimir Putin. Let's dissect this latest debacle and what it signifies about Trump's approach to international politics.

The Art of the Shakedown

Trump's letter, as detailed by Parnas, is a masterclass in manipulation, treating a global conflict as nothing more than a high-stakes poker game. The proposal to impose 50% to 100% tariffs on China—supposedly to leverage an end to the Russia-Ukraine war—is ludicrous at best and dangerously naive at worst. Trump's assertion that "this is not TRUMP’S WAR" parrots the Kremlin's narrative with alarming precision, revealing a disposition that's less about global leadership and more about personal vendettas and political posturing.

The so-called strategy? It's simple: Do nothing that doesn't directly benefit Trump. The letter blatantly states that action against Russia is contingent on NATO's compliance with Trump's demands, including the cessation of oil purchases from Russia and the imposition of tariffs on China. This isn't foreign policy; it's extortion on an international scale.

A Puppeteer's Tell

Parnas's analysis spotlights the "tell" in Trump's letter, a moment of revealing honesty in an otherwise calculated message. Trump's regurgitation of Kremlin talking points—blaming Biden and Zelenskyy for the war—exposes his true intentions. He's not interested in ending the conflict for the sake of peace or saving lives (his claim of "7,118 lives lost last week alone" is as dubious as it is manipulative). Instead, Trump's letter is a thinly veiled attempt to use the war as leverage to advance his personal agenda and curry favor with Putin.

The Real Danger: Signal to Putin

Perhaps the most alarming aspect of this entire episode is what Parnas describes as the underlying signal to Putin: "I got your back." In doing so, Trump not only undermines NATO and the international effort to support Ukraine but also emboldens Russian aggression by showing a willingness to turn a blind eye in exchange for geopolitical gains.

Parnas, with his unique insight into the machinations of both Trump and Russian oligarchs, sees through the facade of diplomacy to the core of Trump's message—an extension of autocratic, transactional politics to the global stage. This isn't about saving lives or ending a war; it's about leveraging a conflict for personal and political gain.

Conclusion: A Dangerous Game

What we're witnessing is a dangerous game being played by a former president who views international conflicts through the lens of personal benefit. Trump's letter to NATO is not a solution to the Russia-Ukraine war; it's a strategy for exploitation, a bid for power that risks exacerbating the conflict rather than resolving it.

In his own words, Parnas reminds us of the stakes involved, not just for Ukraine but for the very fabric of international relations. As readers and citizens, we must ask ourselves: Is this the kind of leadership we want influencing global politics? The answer, if we care about democracy, freedom, and the sanctity of human life, should be a resounding no.
