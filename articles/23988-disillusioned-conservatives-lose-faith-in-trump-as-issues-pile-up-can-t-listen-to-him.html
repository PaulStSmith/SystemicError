Disillusioned conservatives lose faith in Trump as issues pile up: 'Can't listen to him'

Based on an article by Matthew Chapman

Paulo SantosSeptember 23, 20263 min read

Distrust Is Not the Actor. It Is the Aftermath.

The Story Behind the Polling

The reporting describes former Trump supporters abandoning him over the handling of the Epstein files, the reported war in Iran, rising diesel prices, and economic claims they no longer believe. A New York Times/Siena College survey found that 60 percent of likely white voters without a college degree disapproved of Trump’s handling of inflation, while the share of Republicans who said the economy had improved over the previous year fell from 72 percent in January to 29 percent at the time of the survey.

Power Did Not “Scramble” Politics

“Pervasive distrust” is an effect, not an independent political force. Distrust did not launch a war, tell the public to move on from the Epstein files, make claims about the economy, or promise $5,000 checks if Republicans retain Congress. According to the reporting, Trump did those things or bears presidential responsibility for them.

Presenting the resulting backlash as a mysterious national mood obscures that chain of agency. The president holds institutional power. His supporters quoted here do not. They can withdraw their votes, but only after decisions have already been made in their name and consequences have reached their wallets.

The Betrayal Is Concrete

Ryan Wagner’s disillusionment is tied to specific events: doubts about the Charlie Kirk murder investigation, the reported war in Iran, and Trump’s economic messaging. Paul Carroll likewise contrasts Trump’s promise to avoid war with the war the article says he began, while connecting his anger to higher diesel prices.

That distinction matters. These voters are not merely exhausted by partisan conflict. They believe Trump sold restraint and delivered military escalation, sold economic competence and delivered conditions they reject, or promised disclosure and then demanded acquiescence. Their alienation follows perceived breaches of political commitments.

The Weakest Actors Get the Spotlight

The article centers voters deciding whether to participate in November, which risks making their disillusionment the political problem. But nonvoting is their limited response to decisions made far above them. Treating disengagement as the engine of instability shifts attention from officeholders exercising power to citizens reacting after the fact.

Jeff Sieting’s expectation that Democrats will sweep the midterms is still only his prediction. His account of Trump-supporting friends planning to stay home is anecdotal. But the institutional imbalance is clear: disappointed voters possess a ballot; the president possesses the machinery of the executive branch and the platform to define what supporters are told to accept.

A Transactional Movement Meets Its Limit

Trump’s reported promise of $5,000 checks if Republicans hold Congress is especially revealing. Sieting calls it insulting and pathetic because he reads it not as governing policy but as an attempt to purchase renewed loyalty after trust has been spent.

The larger pattern is a political movement colliding with the limits of personal allegiance. When loyalty is organized around one leader, contradictions are tolerated until they touch a supporter’s defining expectation—peace, disclosure, or material security. Then the same centralized authority that once made the movement feel powerful leaves supporters with no internal mechanism for correction.

Distrust Is Manufactured by Unaccountable Power

Pew Research Center’s figure—17 percent of Americans trusting the federal government at the time reported, compared with more than 70 percent during the 1950s postwar boom—establishes a long decline, not its cause. The article does not prove that Trump created that history, nor that today’s disillusionment will produce bipartisan solidarity.

What it does show is more specific: distrust grows when powerful officials demand belief after their words and reported actions diverge. Calling that “scrambled politics” turns accountable decisions into atmospheric disorder. The system is not being disrupted by voters noticing the contradiction. The contradiction was produced by power; the voters are merely refusing, belatedly, to pretend otherwise.
