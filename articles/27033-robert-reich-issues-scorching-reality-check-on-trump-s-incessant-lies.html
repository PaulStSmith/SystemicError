Robert Reich issues scorching reality check on Trump's 'incessant lies'

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosNovember 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that former Labor Secretary Robert Reich urged voters to reject Donald Trump and Republican candidates in an election scheduled for the Tuesday referenced in his commentary. Reich argued that Trump’s “incessant” lying, attacks on opponents and the press, scapegoating of minority groups, and personal use of presidential power warranted a decisive vote of no confidence.

Reich also said Republicans controlled every branch of the federal government and had subordinated the party to Trump. He warned that, without electoral restraint, they would pursue cuts to major social programs and further tax reductions for wealthy people and large corporations. The supplied text gives neither a publication date nor the election result, so it does not establish whether voters delivered the rejection Reich sought.

The power is not rhetorical

Trump is the subject of Reich’s condemnation, but Reich locates the operative power in an alliance: a president commanding a Republican Party that controlled the federal government. That distinction matters. Presidential demagoguery becomes policy only when legislators, officials, donors, and party institutions convert it into appointments, budgets, enforcement priorities, and law.

Calling those Republicans “enablers” is accurate but incomplete. They were not spectators who failed to restrain one man. According to Reich’s account, they possessed institutional authority of their own and chose to place it behind him. Trump supplied the political brand; the party supplied the machinery.

Scapegoats are governing tools

Reich identifies a familiar substitution. Republicans promoted supposed dangers associated with Muslims, Mexican and Latino Americans, transgender Americans, allegedly fraudulent voters, and “globalists,” while neglecting climate change, economic inequality, unregulated artificial intelligence, and a costly, ineffective healthcare system.

This is not merely a dispute over which problems deserve attention. Scapegoating redirects anger away from officials and economic interests capable of shaping public policy and toward groups with less power to defend themselves. It turns political failure into a story about contamination by outsiders. The targets become more visible; the decision-makers become less accountable.

AlterNet oversells the confrontation

The headline promises a “scorching reality check” on Trump’s lies. The text provides something broader and less evidentiary: Reich’s election appeal. He lists patterns of conduct and prospective policy consequences, but he does not examine particular false statements or demonstrate, claim by claim, how they collide with reality.

That framing reduces an argument about organized political power to a verbal clash between Reich and Trump. It is clickable, but politically thin. The important issue is not that a former cabinet secretary produced forceful language. It is that, in Reich’s account, an entire governing party accepted Trumpism because doing so preserved its control and advanced its agenda.

The ballot is leverage, not absolution

Reich puts the immediate burden on voters, urging them to “throw the bums out.” Elections are the public’s most direct instrument for removing officeholders, but voter responsibility does not erase elite agency. Party leaders selected their alliances. Legislators chose which proposals to advance. Officials decided whether power would restrain abuse or facilitate it.

Blaming “the judgment of the American people” alone would therefore reproduce the same misdirection Reich otherwise exposes. Citizens cast ballots within a system shaped by parties, concentrated wealth, media incentives, and officeholders who possess far more capacity than any individual voter to determine what government actually does.

The system rewards the decoy

The larger pattern is a governing coalition using cultural targets to secure authority for material decisions. Fear is directed downward; benefits flow upward; institutional actors describe their choices as obedience to a political movement they helped construct.

That arrangement survives when coverage treats politics as a contest of personalities and insults. Reich’s argument is strongest where it points beyond Trump: cruelty can mobilize a constituency, but institutions make it consequential. The systemic error is to stare at the provocateur while the people holding legislative, administrative, and financial power quietly turn provocation into government.
