Alarm as Trump's secretive court claims its first victim: 'It defies logic'

A Secret Court Gives the Government a New Deportation Weapon

The Precedent Was the Point

Nazira Haji Zada, an Afghan lawful permanent resident, became the first person removed through the federal Alien Terrorist Removal Court, a long-dormant tribunal revived by the Trump Justice Department. The government accused her of assisting relatives tied to a foiled 2024 shooting plot, relied on classified information to label her an “alien terrorist,” and secured her consent to removal without an appeal.

That outcome matters beyond one deportation. The Justice Department has now demonstrated that it can place a lawful permanent resident before a specialized terrorism court, conceal much of the evidence, obtain removal, and avoid an appellate ruling on whether the process is constitutional.

Power Belonged to the Government

The institutional imbalance is stark. The Justice Department chose the forum, controlled the classified evidence, applied the terrorism designation, and pursued removal. Zada could contest the government’s case only from the weaker position of a noncitizen facing evidence she could not fully examine.

Her formal ability to resist does not erase that structure. A waiver produced inside a process dominated by secret government claims is not the same thing as public judicial validation of those claims. Zada accepted the designation and waived appeal, according to prosecutors. That establishes what she agreed to procedurally. It does not independently establish that the tribunal was fair or that its powers survived meaningful constitutional scrutiny.

“Rule of Law” Without an Evidentiary Test

Attorney General Todd Blanche called the result a victory for national security and the rule of law. That rhetoric reverses the central problem.

The government did not prove the legitimacy of this system through open adversarial testing. It obtained the result before the constitutional question could be resolved. Zada’s public defenders explicitly said her consent should not be treated as an endorsement of the court, and argued that forcing lawful permanent residents to answer secret evidence violates due process.

Calling that arrangement the “rule of law” launders executive advantage through legal vocabulary. A proceeding does not become accountable merely because a judge signs an order. If the accused cannot see the government’s case, the courtroom risks functioning less as a check on state power than as its administrative seal.

Terrorism Is the Exceptional-Power Switch

The terrorism label performs political work before it performs legal work. It encourages the public to treat ordinary procedural protections as indulgences and secrecy as proof of seriousness. Once the designation is accepted as sufficient justification for a concealed case, the government gains a reusable mechanism whose reach depends heavily on its own assertions.

The administration’s emphasis on a “landmark” victory reveals its interest in the machinery, not merely this defendant. Former Justice Department attorney Jess Dawgert said she could not imagine officials reviving the court, obtaining a quick concession, and then declining to use it again. Immigration attorney Eric Lee likewise doubted the process would remain narrowly confined amid Trump’s aggressive immigration crackdown.

Those are warnings, not confirmed predictions. But the institutional incentive is obvious: an executive branch seeking faster removals now has a tested venue offering secrecy, stigma, and limited public scrutiny.

The Misdirection Is the Individual Case

Coverage can easily collapse this story into a question about whether Zada deserved removal. That framing gives the government the advantage it wants. It centers an accused individual’s alleged conduct while pushing the state’s expansion of power to the margins.

The more consequential decision was made by the Justice Department: revive an unused terrorism tribunal and establish it as a viable deportation route. The weaker actor is then made to carry the entire controversy, while the officials constructing the precedent present themselves as passive guardians responding to danger.

They were not passive. They selected this mechanism. They invoked classified evidence. They celebrated the result as a model. The precedent belongs to them.

Secrecy Converts Accusation Into Infrastructure

Authoritarian systems are rarely built in one declaration. They are assembled through procedures that make state claims harder to challenge and exceptional powers easier to reuse.

This case does not prove how broadly the Alien Terrorist Removal Court will be deployed. It does show that the Justice Department has converted a dormant statute into functioning infrastructure without first enduring a decisive constitutional test. That is the larger pattern: executive power expands through the first uncontested case, then cites its own successful expansion as precedent for the next one.
