The Reckoning: How Lindsey Graham’s ominous warning about Trump finally came true

Based on an article by Alec MacGillis, ProPublica

Paulo SantosNovember 8, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Lindsey Graham’s 2016 warning—that Republicans would be “destroyed” if they nominated Donald Trump—finally materialized in the 2026 midterms. Democrats won the national congressional popular vote by 13 percentage points, flipped more than 40 House seats, gained at least seven Senate seats, captured five governorships and took as many as 12 state legislative chambers. Republicans also lost Graham’s former South Carolina seat after his reported death in July.

The losses reached deep into Trump territory. Democrats won Ohio’s races for governor and Senate, captured four Republican-held House districts in Pennsylvania and prevailed in states where they had not won Senate contests for decades. AlterNet portrays the result as a delayed reckoning with Trump’s second-term record, including high prices, social-welfare cuts, aggressive immigration enforcement, alleged self-dealing and a war with Iran that the outlet says had already cost taxpayers more than $47 billion.

At campaign stops in Ohio and Pennsylvania, the reporter found former Trump supporters turning against Republican candidates while some loyalists redirected responsibility toward Congress, markets or conspiracy theories. Trump, meanwhile, offered digressions, insults and self-praise at a diminished Ohio rally as voters described material pressures his administration and Republican allies had the power to address.

Power had nowhere left to hide

The governing hierarchy is not ambiguous. Trump held the presidency. Republicans controlled both houses of Congress. Republicans had governed Ohio since 2010. Their candidates could not credibly present themselves as outsiders confronting somebody else’s system.

Yet that is precisely the political habit Trumpism had cultivated: exercise state power while speaking as though an unnamed establishment remains in charge. Jacob Hawk blamed Congress for supposedly preventing domestic drilling, overlooking that Trump’s party controlled Congress. Debbie Shock blamed “the markets.” The confusion protected the officials making policy from the consequences voters were experiencing.

The central decision identified by AlterNet is Trump’s choice to enter the war with Iran. The article connects that decision to higher fuel prices and reports that Netanyahu and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth encouraged Trump’s swagger. But influence is not authority. Trump made the decision; Republican legislators enabled his administration; Republican candidates inherited the consequences.

The scapegoats were weaker than the rulers

Trump’s rally rhetoric pushed attention downward and outward: Haitians in Springfield, Somali people, transgender people, protesters, Democrats and Barack Obama’s supposed failure to understand “cleanliness.” None of those targets controlled the presidency, Congress or the war policy burdening the voters gathered before him.

That is not an accidental mismatch. The speech’s political function was displacement, whatever Trump’s personal intent: turn economic pain into resentment against immigrants, minorities and cultural enemies while the officials holding institutional power escape scrutiny. Even Jeffrey Epstein became material for partisan inversion, with Trump declaring himself exonerated and blaming Democrats rather than addressing the documented questions that had made the subject politically dangerous.

The result outside the arena showed the foreseeable consequence of such politics. Accusations of “pedophiles” flew between opposing groups, and a Trump supporter knocked a protester to the pavement. The article does not establish that Trump ordered or intended the assault. It does show a rally organized around humiliation and accusation producing a space where political hostility became physical violence.

Material failure broke the spell

The decisive opposition described in Pennsylvania was not a single ideological conversion. Voters named lost SNAP benefits, threatened Medicaid coverage, inflation, diesel prices, harsh ICE arrests, the Iran war and alleged self-dealing by Trump’s family and associates. One three-time Trump voter, identified only as Bill, voted Democratic to impose “gridlock” after concluding that Trump’s promises to drain corruption and avoid wars had been false.

This matters because personality politics endured until policy consequences became difficult to rationalize away. Loyalty survived rambling speeches, conspiracy theories and cruelty toward designated enemies. It weakened when voters could connect government decisions to grocery assistance, health coverage, fuel bills, foreign war and abuses of public office.

AlterNet’s strongest evidence is not that every voter reached the same moral judgment. It is that Republican power produced many distinct injuries, which converged electorally. The rout was cumulative accountability.

Graham warned, then enabled

Graham correctly predicted political destruction in 2016, according to the tweet quoted by AlterNet. He then joined much of his party in accommodating Trump. That history makes the warning less an act of courage than an abandoned diagnosis.

Republican officials understood the danger and chose proximity to power anyway. The party survived earlier opportunities for accountability: Trump’s narrow 2016 victory, the January 6 assault on the Capitol and Joe Biden’s decision to seek reelection in 2024. Each reprieve taught Republican officeholders that submission carried fewer immediate costs than resistance.

The 2026 defeat did not vindicate Graham as a dissident. It exposed him as part of the enabling structure he had once described. Foreseeing a wreck does not absolve someone who later helps keep the vehicle moving.

The reckoning was institutional

The temptation is to frame this as voters finally discovering Trump’s true character. That is too comforting and too narrow. The larger failure belonged to a party that possessed governing power, refused responsibility for its decisions and relied on scapegoats to absorb public anger.

The same system that elevated Trump also furnished compliant legislators, candidates willing to defend cruelty, officials prepared to redirect blame and voters trained to see every failure as sabotage by somebody weaker. The midterm results disrupted that arrangement, but an electoral rout is not self-correction. It is a penalty imposed after years of institutional consent.

Graham’s warning came true only after his party spent a decade ensuring that the eventual bill would be larger. The destruction was not fate, and Trump did not accomplish it alone. Republican leaders repeatedly chose access over restraint, obedience over accountability and propaganda over governing. The voters merely ended the grace period.
