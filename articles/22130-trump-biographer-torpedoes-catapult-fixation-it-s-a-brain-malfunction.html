Trump biographer torpedoes catapult 'fixation': 'It's a brain malfunction'

When a Presidential Mood Becomes a Procurement Order

The Real Power Is Not the Opinion

The source story is simple enough: the Navy may spend billions redesigning aircraft carriers to satisfy Donald Trump’s preference for a more “retro” look, including a return to older steam-powered catapults. Michael Wolff and Joanna Coles turn that into a debate about Trump’s brain, his fixation, and whether he is acting strategically or irrationally.

That framing is useful only if you want to avoid the harder question: who has the institutional leverage to turn one man’s taste into a multi-billion-dollar defense decision?

Trump Is Not the Whole Story

Trump is the obvious instigator. He is the person making the demand, and the report says the demand carries real consequences for ship design, cost, and time. But the deeper political fact is that his preference is being treated as a governing input.

That is the part worth naming plainly. This is not just a president with a bizarre aesthetic. It is a military bureaucracy and a defense apparatus organized so thoroughly around executive whim that the whim can become policy. The problem is not merely that Trump wants the Navy to look like World War II. The problem is that anyone in the chain of command has to take that seriously enough to discuss implementation.

Pathology Is a Convenient Detour

Wolff’s “brain malfunction” theory may satisfy the cable-news appetite for personality diagnosis, but it also serves as a distraction. If the entire story becomes Trump’s mental state, then the institutions around him get to disappear into the background.

That is how power protects itself. It invites commentary about symptoms instead of decisions. It turns a governance failure into a character study. It lets the people with actual administrative authority hide behind the spectacle of a president who cannot stop fixating on steam catapults.

The Cost Is the Point

A former official told the Post the cost and time would be extraordinary. That is the political substance here. The Navy is not being asked to make a small cosmetic adjustment. It is being pressured toward an expensive technological reversal because the president likes the old look.

This is how authoritarian aesthetics enter policy: first as taste, then as symbolism, then as budget line. The language of “retro” makes the demand sound harmless, even quaint. It is neither. It is an attempt to force public infrastructure to flatter the vanity of one man.

Media Framing Softens the Damage

Coles’s suggestion that Trump may be acting strategically, because modern technology can seem too complicated to people, is another form of misdirection. It gives the stunt a rational gloss it has not earned. If the purpose is persuasion, then the substance is still corruption by image management: making the state conform to a projection of strength rather than actual capability.

The press keeps getting pulled toward the least useful explanation. Either Trump is a genius playing some hidden game, or he is mentally unraveling. Both frames are too comfortable. The more important truth is that the system keeps adapting itself to him.

The Pattern

This story is not mainly about one president’s fixation on old machinery. It is about how institutions absorb authoritarian vanity and translate it into procedure. The Navy, the bureaucracy, the press, and the broader political class all participate when they treat a dangerous preference as an administrative question instead of a warning sign.

That is the pattern: private impulse becomes public cost, and the people with real power pretend the problem is confusion rather than obedience.
