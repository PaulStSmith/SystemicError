'That’s absolutely not true': GOP pundit's blame game gets pounced by panel

Inflation as a Talking Point, Not a Cost of Living

The Facts on the Screen

The exchange on CNN was simple enough: Scott Jennings tried to argue that inflation under Trump was somehow better than under Biden, and Kaitlan Collins and Karen Finney corrected him on the spot. Collins pointed to Trump’s own promise to lower inflation, then noted that inflation was higher after he took office than when he entered it. Jennings pivoted to the usual evasions: the “rate” of inflation, job growth, manufacturing, anything except the fact that people still pay more for gas, groceries, and health care.

Who Actually Holds Power

This is not a debate between equals. Trump holds the presidency, and with it the institutional power to shape economic policy, set the agenda, and take credit or blame for outcomes. Jennings is not a decision-maker; he is a political cleaner, sent in after the fact to launder a broken promise into a semantic argument.

That matters. The story is not that pundits are confused. The story is that a governing coalition makes a promise, fails to deliver, and then deploys media loyalists to argue about definitions instead of consequences.

The Blame Shift Is the Point

Jennings’ move was classic misdirection. He tried to turn inflation into a statistical abstraction, as if voters pay the “rate” at the checkout line. Collins kept dragging the discussion back to the actual consequence: families still paying more for food, fuel, and health care.

That is the core trick. Convert material harm into a chart dispute. Recast broken promises as a technicality. Treat the public’s pain as a communications problem. It is the same pattern politicians use whenever they cannot defend results: change the subject, narrow the metric, and hope the audience gets tired.

What This Framing Hides

The framing also shelters responsibility. Jennings floated “inartful” language from Trump, which is a generous way to describe a president taking ownership of inflation rhetorically while producing nothing that helps. This is not confusion. It is deliberate insulation.

When people are “losing their health care,” when gas is “over $6,” when grocery prices are “exploding,” the issue is not whether a spokesperson can contort himself into a comparison with 2023. The issue is whether the people in charge made things better. According to the exchange itself, Jennings had no serious answer to that.

The Larger Pattern

This is how modern political power operates when it cannot justify its record: it outsources accountability to commentators, replaces policy with spin, and asks the public to accept verbal gymnastics as governance. The cruelty is bureaucratic in form and propagandistic in execution.

The real pattern here is not inflation. It is impunity. Power makes a promise, the promise fails, and the defense is not correction but distortion.
