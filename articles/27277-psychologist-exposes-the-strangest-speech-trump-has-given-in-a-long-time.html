Psychologist exposes 'the strangest speech' Trump has given in a long time

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosNovember 15, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A halting performance becomes a remote diagnosisThe relevant institution is the presidencySensational framing obscures the unanswered questions

AlterNet’s headline promises an exposure. What its report actually provides is narrower: clinical and forensic psychologist John Paul Garrison watched clips from President Donald Trump’s Wednesday Veterans Day speech and argued in a Friday YouTube video that Trump appeared unable to read, improvise, or speak clearly. Garrison called it Trump’s strangest speech in some time and suggested the performance could help explain what he described as the president’s reduced participation in informal press appearances after the midterms.

The speech may warrant scrutiny. The diagnosis remains Garrison’s interpretation, not an established medical finding.

A halting performance becomes a remote diagnosis

According to AlterNet, Trump stumbled almost immediately and had particular difficulty whenever he departed from his teleprompter. Garrison said the president struggled to articulate words and sometimes appeared not to understand the prepared text.

The psychologist highlighted several passages. Trump referred to the “jungles” of Afghanistan while recounting a soldier’s service, called the Taliban an “evil group,” followed that with the fragment “terrorists all,” and had trouble pronouncing “eardrums.” Garrison interpreted the remarks as disconnected verbal fragments rather than ordinary misstatements.

“What you’re seeing is that he’s just saying whatever random fragments of words come to his mind,” Garrison said. He later suggested that Trump’s difficulty speaking could reflect “some sort of event” interfering with his speech.

That interpretation is serious, but AlterNet’s reporting does not establish it as a diagnosis. Garrison did not examine Trump for the analysis described here; he assessed recorded public behavior. His broader suggestion that Trump may have experienced a stroke is therefore speculation, however firmly he presents it. A specialist’s credentials do not turn video commentary into a neurological examination.

The report weakens its case further by noting Trump’s lack of his usual “glowing orange makeup,” which Garrison called “highly unusual.” A change in cosmetics may be visually conspicuous. On the facts presented, it is not meaningful evidence of neurological impairment.

The relevant institution is the presidency

Trump’s health is not merely personal because he holds presidential power. If a president repeatedly cannot comprehend prepared material, communicate coherently without a script, or participate in unscripted questioning, that has consequences for governance and public accountability. The proper response, however, is documented scrutiny—not diagnosis by montage.

Garrison said Trump appeared to be doing fewer press gaggles after the midterms and favoring prerecorded interviews. AlterNet reports that observation as something he could determine, not as a confirmed change ordered by the White House or supported by a stated schedule comparison. Nor does the account establish why any reduction occurred. The speech may suggest a possible explanation; it does not prove one.

Still, the distinction between live questioning and prerecorded appearances matters. A teleprompter can stabilize a speech. Editing and controlled interview conditions can conceal confusion, remove prolonged mistakes, and limit follow-up questions. If presidential aides are increasingly steering Trump toward those formats, they possess the schedules, access controls, and institutional authority to do it. That would make their conduct part of the story—not because the report proves a concealment effort, but because gatekeeping determines what the public can observe.

Sensational framing obscures the unanswered questions

Calling the remarks “the strangest speech” supplies an irresistible clip-driven hook. It does less to clarify whether Trump’s public performance has changed, whether any change is sustained, and whether the White House has restricted unscripted access in response.

Those are questions about presidential capacity and institutional transparency. They cannot be settled by a YouTube psychologist declaring that a verbal stumble “seemed” to reflect an unspecified neurological event. Nor should they be displaced by chatter about makeup.

The report supports a concrete, limited conclusion: Trump delivered a Veterans Day speech containing halting delivery, verbal difficulty, and several peculiar phrases, and Garrison interpreted those moments as signs of serious neurological decline. It does not establish that Trump suffered a stroke, that his appearance schedule changed because of his health, or that aides deliberately hid a condition.

If Trump’s handlers are replacing open questioning with controlled recordings, the revealing evidence will be in the pattern of access and the decisions behind it. The president’s “orange makeup” is a distraction from that exercise of power.
