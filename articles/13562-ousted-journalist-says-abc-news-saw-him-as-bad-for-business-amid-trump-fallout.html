Ousted journalist says ABC News saw him as 'bad for business' amid Trump fallout

The Bulwark’s Tim Miller with former ABC News journalist Terry Moran, June 16, 2025 (The Bulwark YouTube screenshot)

Former ABC senior national correspondent Terry Moran, who was fired after calling top Trump aide Stephen Miller "a world-class hater," said he believed the network made a "business decision" to let him go.

"It was their calculation," Moran said of his former bosses during an interview with The Bulwark's Tim Miller. "It was a business decision. From my perspective, it looked like a business decision. And I became bad business, it feels like."

The controversy began when Moran posted to social media, "The thing about Stephen Miller is not that he is the brains behind Trumpism. Yes, he is one of the people who conceptualizes the impulses of the Trumpist movement and translates them into policy. But that's not what's interesting about Miller. It's not the brains. It's the bile. Miller is a man who is richly endowed with the capacity for hatred. He's a world-class hater."

Moran said he doesn't regret the sentiment "because I thought it was true." Earlier, he told The New York Times, “I don’t think you should ever regret telling the truth. And I don’t."

"You don't sacrifice your citizenship as a journalist, and the job is not to be objective," Moran told Tim Miller. "There is no Mount Olympus of objectivity where a Mandarin class of wise people have no feelings about their society. We're all in this together. What you have to be is fair and accurate."

Moran added, "I will also say, while very hot, is an observation, a description, that is accurate and true."

Shortly after posting his observation, ABC News released a statement saying it did "not reflect the views of ABC News" and "violated" their standards. Moran was first suspended, then fired several days later.

Moran's social media bio now says he's an "independent journalist" writing on Substack.

Our Analysis:

The Unvarnished Truth: Terry Moran's Exile from ABC

In a media landscape that often tiptoes around the fiery pits of political controversy, Terry Moran’s blunt assessment of Stephen Miller as a “world-class hater” was akin to a verbal Molotov cocktail – explosive and bound to cause damage. The aftermath? Moran, a former senior national correspondent for ABC, found himself on the receiving end of what he describes as a "business decision" to fire him. This incident raises disturbing questions about the state of journalistic freedom, the influence of corporate interests on news coverage, and the battle between personal conviction and professional constraints.

The Cost of Candor

Moran's characterization of Miller wasn’t pulled from the ether but was a conclusion drawn from a career spent analyzing the political landscape. His assertion that Stephen Miller is not merely the architect behind Trumpism but a purveyor of hatred speaks to a deeper, more systemic issue within the political discourse. However, ABC's swift action to suspend and then terminate Moran underscores a chilling reality: in today’s media environment, truth-telling can be a punishable offense if it conflicts with corporate interests or alienates a segment of the audience.

A "Business Decision"?

Let’s dissect the term "business decision." In essence, Moran suggests that his former employer prioritized the network's financial interests and public image over journalistic integrity. This isn’t a new phenomenon; media outlets, being businesses, often face the delicate task of balancing truth-telling with audience retention and advertiser satisfaction. However, when this balance tilts too far towards the latter, the media's role as the Fourth Estate – a watchdog of democracy – is compromised. The implication here is stark: ABC’s decision was less about upholding journalistic standards and more about safeguarding its bottom line.

The Mirage of Objectivity

Moran’s parting shot at the notion of journalistic objectivity is both poignant and provocative. He argues, convincingly, that objectivity in journalism is a myth – a lofty ideal that, in practice, is unattainable. Journalists are not devoid of personal convictions, and pretending otherwise is not only disingenuous but also detrimental to the pursuit of truth. Moran’s stance is a call to embrace fairness and accuracy over the unachievable standard of objectivity. It’s a reminder that journalists, though tasked with reporting facts, cannot – and should not – divorce themselves from the societal implications of their work.

The Road Ahead

In the wake of his dismissal, Moran has embraced the title of “independent journalist,” a move that, while fraught with its own challenges, offers a silver lining. Freed from the constraints of corporate media, Moran now has the opportunity to explore journalism in its purest form – unfiltered, unabashed, and unafraid. This transition to platforms like Substack is indicative of a larger trend, where journalists seek refuge in spaces that value substance over style, truth over traffic.

Conclusion: A Systemic Error

The case of Terry Moran is not an isolated incident but a symptom of a larger, systemic problem within the media industry. The suppression of dissenting voices and the commodification of news not only undermine journalistic integrity but also erode public trust in the media. As Moran charts a new course in the uncharted waters of independent journalism, his story serves as a stark reminder of the need for a media ecosystem that champions truth, champions diversity of thought, and resists the allure of the “business decision.”

In an era where the boundaries between truth and profit are increasingly blurred, the saga of Terry Moran is a clarion call for a reevaluation of the values that underpin our media institutions. It’s time to ask ourselves: What kind of journalism do we want to champion in our society? The answer to that question will shape not just the future of journalism, but the future of our democracy.
