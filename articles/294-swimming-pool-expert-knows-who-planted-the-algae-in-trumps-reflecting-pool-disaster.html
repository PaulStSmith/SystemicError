Swimming pool expert knows who 'planted the algae' in Trump’s Reflecting Pool disaster

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosJune 23, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Sabotage as Spectacle, Maintenance as Crime

The Source Story

CNN gave airtime to a pool expert, “Swimming Pool Steve,” to explain the algae bloom and the claimed “vandalism” at the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool. His basic point was plain: algae in an open-air pool is expected, the liner is tough commercial-grade material, and the alleged damage would require serious equipment. The White House was also pressed to release footage of the supposed vandalism.

Who Actually Holds Power

The meaningful power in this story sits with the White House, which can define the event, name the culprit, and frame the damage before evidence is public. Trump’s claim of a “300-foot gash” is not a side detail. It is the central act of power here: turning an infrastructure problem into a political narrative before the facts are settled.

That matters because public institutions do not merely respond to events. They decide what counts as an event at all. A pool maintenance issue becomes “vandalism” only if those in authority insist on that frame and get enough media oxygen to make it stick.

The Decision That Shapes the Story

The decisive move, as described in the source, is not algae growing or a duck dying. It is the choice to present the Reflecting Pool problem as deliberate sabotage. That choice redirects attention away from maintenance, materials, and remediation and toward a theatrical enemy.

The expert’s comments undercut that framing. He says algae is expected in an open-air environment, that fixing it requires draining and remediation, and that the liner is robust enough that the alleged gash would take concerted effort and advanced equipment. That is not proof of innocence. It is evidence that the simplest explanation is not the one Trump is selling.

Where the Blame Gets Misdirected

The story’s most revealing feature is how easily responsibility is displaced. Nature becomes the culprit. A bird is joked into the role of origin point. Chemical treatment and wildlife safety get folded into the conversation after the fact. A dead duck becomes another prop in a drama that should be about stewardship and evidence.

That is classic misdirection. When institutions fail at maintenance or communication, they often reach for sabotage language because it is politically cleaner than admitting neglect, delay, or incompetence. Alleged enemies are more useful than boring truths.

The Media Problem

The article’s framing is weak because it lets the spectacle sit too close to the facts without fully naming the power arrangement behind it. The useful information is not that CNN found a pool builder with a YouTube channel. The useful information is that an expert with practical knowledge is being used to puncture a narrative already pushed by the highest office.

Reporting becomes institutionally timid when it treats a presidential claim and a technical explanation as equivalent “sides.” They are not equivalent. One is a claim backed by state power. The other is a description of how pools work.

The Larger Pattern

This is how modern political damage control operates: inflate ordinary failure into hostile action, demand attention before verification, and let media clutter obscure accountability. The goal is not truth. The goal is narrative dominance.

The Reflecting Pool story is small on its face, but the pattern is not small at all. Power prefers sabotage stories because sabotage absolves power. It converts negligence into conspiracy, management failure into persecution, and ordinary institutional decay into a drama that can be blamed on someone else.
