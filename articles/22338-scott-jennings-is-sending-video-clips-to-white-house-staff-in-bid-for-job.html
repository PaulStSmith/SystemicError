Scott Jennings is sending video clips to White House staff in bid for job

Trump’s Communications Post Is a Loyalty Test, Not a Job

The Real Contest

The reported fight over Karoline Leavitt’s job is not about press strategy. It is about proximity to Donald Trump, the right to flatter him in public, and the confidence of the people around him that they can manage the story without losing control of the room. Scott Jennings is not “campaigning” for a communications role in any ordinary sense. He is auditioning in front of the boss’s ecosystem, where access matters more than competence and performance matters more than truth.

That is the actual institution here: not the White House briefing room, but the Trump orbit’s private hierarchy of approval.

The Useful Distraction

The spark was Jon Ossoff’s comment about Trump traveling with Natalie Harp and surrounding himself with staff who function as a “security blanket.” That became the target of outrage. But the controversy is not that a senator noticed a staffer’s outsized role. The controversy is that the White House and its allies treat any exposure of Trump’s dependency as an offense requiring immediate counterprogramming.

Jennings’ defense of Harp is a familiar maneuver: turn scrutiny into cruelty, and turn character into evidence. “Good person” and “cancer survivor” are not responses to the underlying power question. They are shields. They are meant to make the actual issue disappear: why this staffer matters so much, why insiders are nervous, and why the president’s circle is leaking about her in the first place.

Who Enabled the Story

The source makes one thing plain: the most revealing leaks are coming from inside Trump’s own operation. White House staff have reportedly been feeding information about Harp for months. That matters more than Ossoff’s comment, because it shows where the real conflict sits. The people closest to Trump are not defending a stable process. They are waging a quiet internal campaign over influence, access, and succession.

Jennings’ videos spreading through the White House because he sent them to senior staff is not a quirky detail. It is the operating system. These people are not building a communications shop around public accountability. They are circulating audition tapes and watching who gets noticed by the leader and his gatekeepers.

Gender As Cover

NOTUS reported that some close Trump advisers prefer a man for the press secretary job, partly because the last four press secretaries have been women and they think that would end comparisons to Leavitt. That is not neutral staffing logic. It is image management dressed up as organizational preference. The role is being treated as a gendered costume change, as if swapping the face in front of the cameras will change the politics underneath.

It will not. If the goal is to stop comparisons, that already tells you the comparisons are probably doing useful work. The problem is not the optics of one press secretary. The problem is the administration’s need to manage perception by replacing one loyal messenger with another, while the underlying power remains concentrated in Trump and his coterie.

The Party of Personal Rule

The source also says Trump was open to the idea but not sold on Jennings, and that he spent days calling people to ask whether they liked him for the job. That is not how a serious press operation functions. It is how a personal court functions. Appointments are filtered through intimacy, reputation, and the boss’s mood, not through institutional standards.

That is why the story keeps drifting away from policy and toward personality. The important actors are not the reporters asking questions or the senator making a pointed remark. The important actors are the staffers leaking, the advisers maneuvering, and the president deciding whether a surrogate is useful enough to promote.

What This Actually Shows

The story reveals a durable pattern: Trump-world treats the state as an extension of emotional management. Staff are there to reassure him, defend him, and absorb embarrassment. When that arrangement becomes visible, the machine does not correct itself. It recruits more performers.

That is the point Osborne’s line accidentally exposed and Jennings is now trying to bury. The issue is not whether Natalie Harp is nice, or whether Scott Jennings can win a cable-news cage match. The issue is that this White House runs on dependency, leakage, and self-protective theater. The press secretary fight is just the latest reminder that in Trump’s political order, the job is not to explain power. It is to flatter it, conceal its fragility, and help it keep pretending to be normal.
