Trump eyes move that would lock in his gilded Oval Office makeover for future presidents

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Paulo SantosOctober 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that Donald Trump said he wants the Oval Office designated a federal landmark to preserve both the room and his gilded redesign. Asked whether a successor could remove that decor under his proposal, Trump answered, “No.” The article does not establish that any designation has been created or that Trump has legal authority to bind future presidents this way.

Trump also claimed Secret Service officials proposed demolishing and rebuilding the Oval Office for security reasons and that he refused. The White House declined further comment, and the Secret Service did not immediately confirm his account. The White House is already a National Historic Landmark, but Congress has exempted it from the National Historic Preservation Act, allowing presidents to make major alterations without the usual review.

The power is in the exemption

The central institutional fact is not Trump’s taste in gold. It is that Congress left the White House outside the ordinary preservation process. That exemption concentrates unusual control over a nationally significant public building in the sitting president.

Trump has used that discretion to remake the Oval Office. He now says he wants preservation rules that would prevent a successor from undoing his work. That is not a neutral defense of history. It is an attempt—so far only stated—to convert one officeholder’s preferences into a restraint on the next.

Preservation as personal entitlement

Historic protection ordinarily concerns the significance of a place, not the vanity of its temporary occupant. Trump’s answer makes his objective unusually plain: the proposed designation should protect his decor from his successor.

That turns stewardship upside down. The Oval Office belongs to the presidency as an institution, not to the president as a proprietor. Trump’s position treats public space as personal legacy property and future elected administrations as trespassers who might disturb it.

A proposal is not a fait accompli

The headline’s “move” remains an aspiration. Trump said he had been asking chief of staff Susie Wiles for six months to provide a statement about saving the room and making it a federal landmark. RawStory reports no completed designation, legal mechanism, formal order, or congressional action.

That distinction matters. Presenting Trump’s wish as though it already “would lock in” the makeover gives political theater the weight of settled authority. The relevant questions are who could legally impose such restrictions, through what process, and whether they could survive a future administration. The reporting does not answer them.

Security claims need verification

Trump’s account of the Secret Service proposal is also unconfirmed. He says officials urged replacing the Oval Office; the agency did not immediately respond, and the White House supplied no additional evidence.

The anecdote nevertheless serves his argument neatly: security professionals supposedly proposed destruction, while Trump cast himself as the defender of a “special place.” Without confirmation or details, that contrast remains Trump’s framing—not an established institutional dispute.

The office becomes the incumbent’s possession

The larger pattern is the personalization of state power. A president exercises broad discretion over a public institution, marks it with his own aesthetic, and then seeks rules that would deny comparable discretion to whoever follows him.

The gold is decoration. The consequential idea is that temporary control should become permanent ownership. When institutional authority is treated as a tool for preserving an incumbent’s image, public stewardship gives way to dynastic self-commemoration.
