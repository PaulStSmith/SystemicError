Shortening Dying: Law, Dignity, and the Honesty We Owe the Terminally Ill

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 17, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There are moments when politics briefly stops pretending and confronts the human condition head‑on. New York’s decision to legalize medical aid in dying is one of those moments — not because death is new, but because honesty is.

Governor Kathy Hochul’s framing of the Medical Aid in Dying Act cut through decades of euphemism with surgical clarity: this is not about “shortening life,” but about shortening dying. That distinction matters more than any theological objection or partisan slogan, because terminal illness has already answered the question of mortality. The only unresolved issue is how much suffering the state is willing to mandate in the name of moral purity.

The Lie at the Center of the Debate

Opponents of assisted dying speak as if the alternative were life itself — vibrant, conscious, meaningful life. It isn’t. The alternative is often a slow neurological erasure, organ failure measured in weeks, or pain controlled only through sedation so deep it borders on oblivion. The state does not preserve life in these cases; it preserves process. And it demands that process be endured publicly so that institutions may feel righteous.

This is why Hochul’s phrase lands so forcefully. Dying is not a moment; it is a condition. One that can last months, sometimes years, stripped of autonomy, dignity, and recognition. To pretend that forcing someone through that ordeal is morally superior to allowing a deliberate, compassionate end is not ethics — it’s theater.

What the Law Actually Does

Contrary to the fever‑dream warnings of its critics, New York’s law is not permissive. It is restrained to the point of severity.

Eligibility is limited to adults with incurable, irreversible illnesses and a prognosis of six months or less to live. Patients must make both written and oral requests. Three medical professionals must sign off — including a psychiatrist or psychologist tasked specifically with assessing coercion or impaired judgment. There is a mandatory waiting period, and the law applies only to New York residents.

This is not a slippery slope. It is a narrow bridge, built slowly, with guardrails on both sides.

And that narrowness exposes the dishonesty of the opposition. If the concern were truly “abuse,” this framework would be celebrated. Instead, it is denounced — because abuse was never the real objection.

The Fear of Transparency

There is a reason this debate feels familiar. Nearly twenty years ago, Boston Legal staged a courtroom argument that now reads less like television drama and more like a legal brief addressed to the future.

In that episode, Alan Shore — played with furious moral precision by James Spader — dismantles the claim that assisted dying is dangerous because it could be misused. His rebuttal is devastatingly simple: we already help people die every day. We do it quietly, through aggressive pain management, terminal sedation, and the strategic withdrawal of care — all under a professional “wink‑wink” designed to maintain plausible deniability.

The danger, Shore argues, is not regulation but secrecy. When a practice exists but is forbidden to speak its name, it loses oversight, consistency, and accountability. The law doesn’t prevent suffering; it merely forces it to happen in the shadows.

New York’s legislation drags that reality into the light.

Sanctity, Selectively Applied

Religious institutions have been among the loudest opponents, framing the law as an attack on the “sanctity of life.” But sanctity, as deployed here, is selective. It sanctifies biological persistence while ignoring lived experience. It demands suffering as proof of virtue — preferably from someone else.

Governor Hochul’s response is quietly radical precisely because it comes from within that moral tradition. A practicing Catholic, she did not abandon faith to support the bill; she interrogated it. Her invocation of mercy is not rhetorical cover. It is a rebuke to a theology that confuses cruelty with righteousness.

If sanctity means anything beyond the mere continuation of cellular activity, it must include agency, dignity, and relief from needless pain. Otherwise, the word is empty.

“Abandonment” and Other Bad‑Faith Arguments

Republican leaders have characterized the law as telling vulnerable people that their lives are “expendable.” This is not just wrong — it is inverted.

The law does not declare lives expendable; it declares choices sacred. It does not force death; it permits autonomy. Every safeguard in the statute exists precisely because the state values life enough to treat the decision to end it with gravity.

If the state truly believed these lives were worthless, it would not require psychiatric evaluations, multiple medical confirmations, or waiting periods. It would not insist on consent rituals designed to be exhaustive and reversible. The accusation of abandonment collapses under the weight of the law’s own procedures.

What opponents actually fear is precedent: the recognition that autonomy does not evaporate at the threshold of death.

Privacy, at the Edge of Life

At its core, this is a privacy issue. Few decisions are more intimate than the conditions under which one leaves the world. Yet the same political movements that insist on government restraint in economic life are eager to insert the state into hospital rooms, bedrooms, and final moments.

Hochul explicitly placed this decision alongside abortion rights and LGBTQ equality for a reason. Once the state claims authority over the most personal aspects of bodily autonomy, suffering becomes policy. The throughline is control, not compassion.

Choosing Humanity Over Dogma

The most haunting moment in that Boston Legal argument is not its legal reasoning but its moral inversion: we routinely euthanize pets to spare them agony, yet deny the same mercy to human beings in the name of abstract principle. The comparison is uncomfortable — which is precisely why it works.

New York’s law does not compel anyone to choose death. It compels the state to acknowledge reality. It recognizes that there is no dignity in forcing a person to endure a prolonged erasure of self for the comfort of observers who will not share that suffering.

Death was never the question. Death has always been certain.

The question was whether the law would continue to sanctify agony — or finally choose honesty, compassion, and restraint.

With this act, New York chose the latter. And in doing so, it joined the growing number of jurisdictions willing to admit what television lawyers, terminal patients, and grieving families have known for decades: there is nothing humane about mandatory suffering.
