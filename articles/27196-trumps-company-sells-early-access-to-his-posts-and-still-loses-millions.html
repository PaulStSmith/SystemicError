Trump’s company sells early access to his posts — and still loses millions

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 11, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Trump Media & Technology Group generated $3 million in third-quarter revenue, triple the prior year’s figure, partly by selling subscribers rapid access to President Donald Trump’s Truth Social posts through the Truth API. The service launched in August and, according to the outlet, has drawn legal scrutiny.

Trump Media reported $409.7 million in net income, but nearly all of that result came from a $496.1 million noncash increase in the value of its cryptocurrency and stock holdings. By the company’s adjusted accounting, its core operations lost $12.3 million.

The company also promoted its $2.5 billion in assets and plans for a $6 billion merger with fusion-energy firm TAE Technologies. Shareholders were scheduled to vote on Dec. 14, with the trust holding Trump’s 41 percent stake already committed to supporting the deal.

The office is the product

The central political fact is not that a media company found another subscription feature. It is that a company substantially owned through Trump’s trust is monetizing faster access to communications from the sitting president.

Presidential posts can move political debate, government expectations and financial markets. Packaging speedier access to them turns proximity to presidential speech into a commercial product. The reporting says the service faces legal scrutiny; it does not establish that the arrangement is unlawful. But the conflict is visible without a legal verdict: public power supplies the attention, while a private company sells the timing advantage.

Paper gains, operating losses

The headline profit disguises the weakness of the actual media business. Trump Media’s reported net income depended overwhelmingly on rising valuations in its investment portfolio, not on people buying its principal products. Its own adjusted figure showed a $12.3 million operating loss.

Interim CEO Kevin McGurn said the underlying business “didn’t flinch” as the stock fell to a record low. That defense shifts attention from performance to solvency: the company has substantial assets, therefore investors should disregard the market’s alarm. Assets may sustain a company, but they do not transform a thin-revenue platform into a healthy operating enterprise.

A network of aligned institutions

The company highlighted a $175,000 advertising campaign for Freedom 250, an entity within the National Park Foundation. Meredith O’Rourke, a major Trump fundraiser, sits on the boards of both the foundation and Trump Media.

The reporting does not establish that this overlapping role produced misconduct. It does reveal the architecture that deserves scrutiny: political fundraising, a presidentially branded business and a foundation-connected entity joined through the same influential figure. Formal separation matters less when the same small network repeatedly occupies the decision-making seats.

Congressional Democrats have vowed to investigate Trump’s businesses over corruption allegations. Those remain allegations. The institutional question is whether Congress will examine specific transactions and conflicts rigorously—or merely convert them into partisan spectacle.

Fusion as an escape route

Trump Media now proposes merging with TAE Technologies, a fusion company, in a transaction valued at $6 billion. Fusion power is not commercially available, yet McGurn’s explanation was strikingly casual: energy could displace the platform, so the company found an energy company.

That is not a coherent account of operational synergy. It is a pivot from a struggling media business toward a capital-intensive industry sustained by vast expectations about future technology. Trump’s trust has already agreed to vote its 41 percent stake for the merger, giving the president’s financial vehicle substantial power over the company’s direction.

Political attention as private capital

This story is larger than one weak quarter. Trump Media converts political loyalty, presidential communication and speculative enthusiasm into corporate value, even while its core operation loses money. Its most valuable resource is not technological innovation or media revenue. It is Trump’s institutional position and the audience attached to it.

The misdirection lies in treating this mainly as an eccentric earnings report. The consequential issue is a political economy in which public authority generates private demand, aligned insiders occupy overlapping institutions, and speculative assets can make an unprofitable operation appear prosperous. The balance sheet may not have flinched. The boundary between governing power and personal commerce has.
