'We drank the Kool-Aid': MAGA voters are sick of 'big liar' Trump

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosSeptember 23, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Exit From MAGA Is Not Yet a Transfer of Power

Disillusionment Without Defection

The reporting describes three former Trump supporters—Ryan Wagner, Kalkaska Mayor Jeff Sieting, and Paul Carroll—who say Trump’s claims and decisions have broken their trust. Wagner cited Trump’s statements about food prices and an unnamed war; Sieting pointed to Trump’s response to the Epstein files and a reported promise of $5,000 if voters kept Republicans in power after the midterms; Carroll blamed the war and rising diesel prices. All three said they did not plan to vote.

Trump Made the Promises

The relevant power does not belong to disappointed voters. It belongs to President Donald Trump and the Republican officials who sustain his governing majority.

The men quoted were consumers of political promises, not authors of policy. Trump chose what to claim about prices, the war, and the strait Wagner referenced. According to the reporting, he also urged the public to “move on” from the Epstein files and tied a promised payment to continued Republican control. The article does not establish the full context or implementation status of that proposal, but Sieting understood it as an attempted political inducement—and called it insulting.

That distinction matters. The story is not fundamentally about voters suddenly discovering dishonesty. It is about a president exercising institutional and rhetorical power while former supporters conclude that what he says no longer matches what they can observe.

The Convenient Redemption Story

Coverage of disillusioned MAGA voters can quietly shift attention from the powerful to the persuaded. “They finally realized” is an emotionally satisfying storyline because it turns politics into a personal awakening: belief, betrayal, remorse.

But remorse is not accountability. Wagner taking down his flag does not constrain presidential power. Sieting turning off Fox News does not reverse a policy. Carroll feeling cheated does not alter the decisions he condemns.

The reporting also offers no basis for treating these three men as representative of MAGA voters nationally. Their testimony establishes their own estrangement, not a measurable collapse of Trump’s coalition. Presenting anecdotal disgust as an electoral wave would merely replace one comforting narrative with another.

Abstention Protects the Machine

Each man says he may answer betrayal by withholding his vote. That could hurt Republican candidates if repeated widely, but it is not the same as transferring power to an opposition capable of checking Trump.

Nonparticipation leaves every other vote carrying more weight. It also allows former supporters to reject the product without choosing who should control the institution that produced it. Sieting predicted a Democratic sweep, but the reporting supplies no polling or electoral evidence supporting that forecast.

The political consequence therefore remains uncertain. Disgust can fracture a coalition. It can also dissipate into private withdrawal while party officials, donors, media allies, and reliable voters keep the governing apparatus intact.

Fox News Was Part of the Bargain

Sieting’s rejection of Fox News exposes another layer of power. He said the network’s portrayal of New York no longer matched what he regarded as reality. His complaint suggests that his political break was not confined to one politician; it extended to the media system through which Republican claims reached him.

Yet the article’s focus on individual believers risks obscuring that system. Political messaging is not simply emitted by Trump and received by isolated voters. It is repeated, normalized, and defended by institutions with far greater reach than any one disillusioned mayor or former supporter.

The evidence here does not establish coordinated deception. It does show a former loyalist concluding that both the politician and a favored media outlet had forfeited credibility. That is a failure of an information structure, not merely a voter’s error in judgment.

A Coalition Can Lose Faith and Keep Power

The larger pattern is brutally simple: recognizing manipulation after power has been delivered does not undo the delivery.

Trump holds the presidency described in the report. Republicans seek to preserve their congressional power. Their former supporters now describe themselves as lied to, insulted, and sold something different from what they received. But unless that recognition changes who governs, it remains politically incomplete.

A governing movement does not need every believer to remain enchanted. It needs enough loyal voters, enough institutional enablers, and enough disillusioned opponents to stay home. The system can survive a crisis of faith. What threatens its control is not private regret, but an organized transfer of power.
