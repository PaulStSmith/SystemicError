Archaeological Bad Faith: JD Vance, Context Collapse, and the Politics of Perpetual Grievance

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 20, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There is something deeply revealing about the way modern reactionary politics operates: it is no longer primarily about defending policies, institutions, or even coherent worldviews. It is about mining the past for fragments, stripping them of context, and redeploying them as weapons in the present. What JD Vance did with the nearly decade‑old quote attributed to someone loosely described as Zohran Mamdani’s former “director of appointments” is a textbook example of this maneuver.

This is not political criticism. It is archaeological bad faith.

The Resurrection of a Line

The quoted sentence—“it’s important that white people feel defeated”—was written in the immediate aftermath of the 2016 election, a moment that felt, to many, like the collapse of an entire post–civil rights consensus. The author of that line was Catherine Almonte Da Costa, who in 2025 was appointed as Director of Appointments in Mayor‑elect Zohran Mamdani’s incoming New York City administration before resigning after the post resurfaced. Trump’s victory was not perceived merely as a partisan loss, but as a signal flare announcing the return of openly reactionary, ethnonationalist politics to the center of American power.

That matters, because language written in shock is rarely careful, polished, or optimized for future dissection. It is emotional, reactive, and situated inside a very specific historical moment. Vance knows this. He is not confused about context. He is counting on its erasure.

Titles Without Substance

Calling someone a candidate’s “director of appointments” would ordinarily be a rhetorical sleight of hand—but in this case, the title was real. Catherine Almonte Da Costa did briefly hold that role in Mamdani’s incoming administration, and the controversy centers on whether decade‑old political speech should disqualify someone from public service today. Campaigns—especially insurgent or local ones—are temporary, fluid organisms. Titles are functional, not ideological. Staffers rotate in and out. They do not define the beliefs of candidates years later, nor do their old writings constitute a governing philosophy.

But outrage politics requires proximity, not relevance. If a line can be made to sound adjacent, it will be treated as if it were foundational.

This is guilt by association, stretched thin and repackaged as moral indictment.

Power vs. Identity: The Deliberate Collapse

The most dishonest move in Vance’s framing is the collapse of political power into racial identity.

The larger argument—clearly visible even in the cropped screenshot—is about power being historically concentrated in the hands of men and/or white people, and the catastrophic outcomes of that concentration. You can disagree with that analysis. You can argue it’s reductive or incomplete. But what it is not is a call for racial humiliation or collective punishment.

Yet Vance presents it precisely that way, because grievance politics depends on this inversion. If losing power is rebranded as being attacked, then democracy itself becomes oppression. Elections become violence. Accountability becomes persecution.

That move is not accidental. It is the emotional engine of modern right‑wing populism.

The Long Memory of Grievance, the Short Memory of Power

Vance’s feigned shock—“Amazing how numb we became to language like this”—is particularly rich coming from someone whose political ecosystem routinely traffics in dehumanization, conspiracy, and outright eliminationist rhetoric. The issue is not harsh language. The issue is who it’s allowed to challenge.

When power is questioned, suddenly tone matters. When hierarchy is defended, cruelty becomes honesty.

This asymmetry tells you everything.

Why This Works on Social Media

Twitter (or X, if one insists) is the perfect delivery system for this kind of manipulation. Screenshots collapse nuance. Dates shrink into footnotes. Context evaporates. A single sentence, surgically extracted, becomes an avatar for an entire ideology.

The audience is not invited to understand; it is invited to react.

And reaction is the point.

Zohran Mamdani and the Politics of Proxy Attacks

Nothing in this episode meaningfully implicates Zohran Mamdani, now the elected mayor of New York City, beyond a legitimate debate about vetting standards and political judgment in senior appointments. The attack is not about his policies, record, or arguments. It is about constructing a symbolic villain through proximity—someone onto whom broader cultural anxieties can be projected without having to engage substantively.

This is proxy warfare: fighting ideas by attacking their supposed associates, long after the moment has passed, long after the context has decayed.

What This Tells Us About the Present

This incident is not really about race, or even about Mamdani. It is about a political movement that cannot defend its grip on power without rewriting the rules of interpretation. Words must be frozen, extracted, and weaponized. History must be flattened into a meme. Intent must be replaced with implication.

Archaeological bad faith thrives in environments where attention is scarce and outrage is profitable. It turns the past into a quarry and politics into a scavenger hunt for sins.

The tragedy is not that people wrote angry, imprecise things in 2016. The tragedy is that nearly ten years later, those fragments are being used to argue that democracy itself is illegitimate unless one group is forever shielded from defeat.

That is not moral concern.

That is grievance, embalmed and redeployed.

And it tells you far more about JD Vance’s politics than it ever could about a stray line written in the wreckage of 2016.
