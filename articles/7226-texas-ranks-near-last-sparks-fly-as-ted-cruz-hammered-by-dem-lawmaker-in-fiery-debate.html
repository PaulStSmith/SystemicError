Rep. Ro Khanna (D-CA) fired off at Sen. Ted Cruz (R-TX) late Saturday night in a heated social-media exchange over taxing billionaires, reminding Cruz of how regressive tax policies in his own state have negatively impacted his own constituents.

The spat was sparked after Khanna publicly championed a proposed ballot measure in California that would, if approved by voters next year, impose a one-time 5% tax on the net worth of Californians worth more than $1 billion, payable over five years. The proposal has already sparked fear among billionaires, including pro-Trump billionaire Peter Thiel, who is reportedly considering cutting ties with the Golden State were it to be adopted.

Late Saturday, Cruz encouraged Khanna to raise the proposed tax from 5% to 50% under the guise that it would drive more billionaires to flee California and potentially move to Texas.

“Please continue driving all the job creators out of California. If anything, 5% is too low. Why not 50%?” Cruz sarcastically quipped. “Texans are enjoying the prosperity!”

Khanna fired back, reminding Cruz of the impact of regressive tax policies on millions of Texans.

“It’s a matter of values. We believe billionaires can pay a modest wealth tax so working class Californians have the Medicaid your party cut,” Khanna wrote in a social media post on X Saturday.

“Texas ranks near last in healthcare, education funding, and worker protections. Meanwhile, we still have an $18 trillion innovation economy because of investment in education & science and attracting hard working immigrants. Happy to show you around the Valley anytime!”

Texas has the 7th most regressive state and local tax system in the nation, meaning low-income Texans pay a disproportionately larger share of their income in taxes than those with high incomes, according to the Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy. Perhaps consequently, Texas also ranks poorly in health care performance, education funding and worker protections.

Regarding health care, the Lone Star State ranks 50th for overall health care performance, has the highest uninsured rate in the nation – by a “wide margin” – and ranked the lowest for health care access and affordability. Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Taxing Tiff: Khanna vs. Cruz

In the latest political theater to grace our social media feeds, Rep. Ro Khanna (D-CA) and Sen. Ted Cruz (R-TX) exchanged fiery tweets over a proposed tax measure in California. The proposal, a one-time 5% tax on Californians worth more than $1 billion, has stirred up a hornet’s nest, drawing comments from both supporters and detractors. Cruz, never one to shy away from controversy, suggested, with a heavy dose of sarcasm, that California should increase the tax to 50% to expedite the billionaire exodus to Texas. Khanna’s retort highlighted the stark differences in values between the two states, particularly around taxation and public services.

Texas: A Cautionary Tale of Regressive Taxation

Khanna’s response to Cruz is a masterclass in how to use facts to dismantle an argument. Texas, as Khanna points out, is “near last in healthcare, education funding, and worker protections.” This isn’t just rhetoric; it’s backed by the distressing rankings from reputable sources. Texas’s tax system is one of the most regressive in the United States, disproportionately burdening low-income families. This approach to taxation correlates with the state’s abysmal rankings in healthcare and education — areas where Texas is at the bottom of the barrel.

Healthcare and Education in Crisis

The Lone Star State has the dubious distinction of ranking 50th for overall health care performance, with the highest uninsured rate in the nation. This isn’t just a number; it’s a reflection of a system that fails its residents, leaving millions without access to basic healthcare services.

In terms of education, Texas is hardly better, ranking 44th in per-pupil funding. This indicates a significant underinvestment in the future of its children and, by extension, the future workforce and economy.

Worker Protections: An Afterthought

Ranking 45th in worker protections, Texas seems to have all but abandoned the rights of workers, favoring a business-friendly environment at the expense of labor rights and safety. This isn’t prosperity; it’s exploitation.

The Bigger Picture: What This Means for California

Khanna’s defense of California’s proposed billionaire tax isn’t just about generating revenue; it’s about underscoring a commitment to a more equitable society. California’s approach, investing in education, healthcare, and worker protections, contrasts sharply with Texas’s ‘prosperity’ built on regressive taxation and underfunded public services.

Cruz’s quip about driving job creators out of California misses the mark. It’s not about driving the wealthy away; it’s about ensuring they contribute their fair share to the society that enabled their success. Khanna’s invitation to Cruz to visit Silicon Valley is more than a polite offer; it’s a challenge to witness firsthand the benefits of a system that values innovation, education, and inclusivity.

Conclusion: A Tale of Two States

This exchange between Khanna and Cruz is more than just a social media spat; it’s a microcosm of the broader national debate on wealth, taxation, and public responsibility. California’s proposed billionaire tax, while controversial, prompts a necessary conversation about how society values wealth and who bears the burden of funding essential public services.

As Khanna aptly points out, the success of Silicon Valley and California’s innovation economy is not in spite of its investment in public services but because of it. Cruz’s response, while glib, inadvertently highlights the consequences of Texas’s regressive tax policies — a cautionary tale that California, and indeed the rest of the nation, would do well to heed.
