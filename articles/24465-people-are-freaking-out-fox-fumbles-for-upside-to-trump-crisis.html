'People are freaking out': Fox fumbles for upside to Trump 'crisis'

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedA crisis for whom?The power is upstreamOptimism as misdirectionA brittle political economyThe system protects the buyer

What happened

AlterNet reports that Fox Business host Stuart Varney and UBS managing director Jason Katz presented a Treasury-bond sell-off as a “generational opportunity” for investors wealthy enough to endure the economic strain. Katz acknowledged that the price was painful but said conditions were “not as bad” for high earners who could buy bonds and wait.

The segment also described corporate growth as heavily dependent on artificial intelligence. Varney said investors needed to see AI spending improve corporate earnings; Katz replied that they “have to” see it, and Varney conceded that otherwise “we’re in trouble.” AlterNet contrasts that confidence with cited polling showing broad dissatisfaction with Trump’s economic management and substantial opposition to AI data centers.

A crisis for whom?

The segment’s definition of opportunity begins where most people’s purchasing power ends. Falling asset prices can reward investors with spare capital, but that does not make the conditions producing those prices broadly beneficial. It means wealth provides access to the recovery while financial pain forces everyone else to absorb the decline.

This is not merely optimistic market commentary. It is a class filter: the same instability becomes hardship for households living on income and a buying opportunity for households living partly on accumulated wealth. Varney and Katz acknowledged that distinction, then treated the privileged side of it as the useful takeaway.

The power is upstream

Ordinary consumers did not set federal economic policy, determine the government’s borrowing needs or make corporations stake so much anticipated growth on AI. Institutional power belongs to the Trump administration, Congress, financial markets and corporate executives allocating enormous sums toward the technology.

Yet the Fox segment centered the investor’s response rather than the decisions creating the exposure. Katz referred vaguely to economic “headwinds” and declined to detail them because, he said, viewers did not need to be bored. That omission matters. Removing policy choices and corporate bets from the discussion makes instability sound like weather: unpleasant, impersonal and nobody’s responsibility.

Optimism as misdirection

The reporting does not establish that Trump alone caused the Treasury sell-off or every measure of economic weakness, and it would be irresponsible to claim that it does. It does show a friendly television platform converting economic alarm into portfolio advice while declining to examine the forces behind it.

That framing redirects attention from governance to positioning. The question becomes whether affluent viewers can profit from fear, not why confidence has deteriorated, who benefits from current policy or who bears the costs. Harm is not denied; it is repackaged as an entry point.

The AI discussion performs a similar maneuver. Public opposition to data centers is treated as an obstacle to growth, while Trump dismisses opponents as people who want the country to become “backwards and poor.” But if the economy is as dependent on AI gains as the cited analysts suggest, skepticism about that concentration is not backwardness. It is a political response to a development model whose rewards and burdens are distributed by people with far more power than nearby residents.

A brittle political economy

According to the figures AlterNet cites, AI accounts for roughly one-third of US economic growth and one-third of stock-market value. Oxford Economics chief US economist Michael Pearce warned that growth increasingly depends on AI gains and the resulting spending by higher-income households. That describes an economy propped up by concentrated assets and concentrated consumption.

Varney’s warning that “we’re in trouble” if AI fails to improve corporate results exposes the weakness beneath the reassurance. The promised upside depends on bond buyers having money to wait, technology companies delivering profits and affluent households continuing to spend. Those are conditions for protecting asset values, not evidence of widely shared security.

The system protects the buyer

The larger pattern is familiar: political and corporate institutions produce or tolerate instability, financial media translates that instability into advice for asset owners, and the public experiencing higher costs is cast as fearful, impatient or hostile to progress.

The crucial divide is not between optimists and pessimists. It is between people empowered to buy during a crisis and people required to live through it. A political economy that calls the first group’s opportunity the country’s good news has already disclosed whose welfare it is organized to protect.
