Rights for Me, Law for Thee

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 16, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Manufactured ConfusionThe Asymmetry DoctrineICE as Cultural SignalThe Free Speech MirageConsequence Is Not CensorshipThe Weaponization of VictimhoodConditional ToleranceBeyond the Individual CaseThe Clear Line

There is a phrase that perfectly distills the modern authoritarian mindset: “Rights for me, the law for thee.”

It is not satire. It is not exaggeration. It is a governing philosophy.

In the aftermath of a teacher resigning after posting “GO ICE” online, a familiar chorus erupted: free speech is under attack; cancel culture strikes again; political correctness has gone mad. But beneath the outrage lies something far more revealing than a social‑media controversy. What we are watching is not confusion about constitutional rights. It is a deliberate attempt to weaponize them.

The First Amendment protects citizens from government punishment for speech. It does not guarantee applause. It does not require employers to ignore conduct that undermines trust. It does not force communities to remain silent when someone publicly aligns themselves with institutions those communities experience as hostile or violent.

Yet a segment of the political right has spent years reframing “freedom of speech” as “freedom from consequences.” The distinction is not subtle. It is intentionally erased.

The Manufactured Confusion

When someone posts “GO ICE,” that is speech. The government cannot imprison them for it. That is constitutional protection. But if that person works in a school serving immigrant families—families who have lived through raids, detentions, and deportations—then the social meaning of that speech matters.

Speech is not emitted into a vacuum. It lands in real communities with real histories.

In immigrant neighborhoods, ICE is not an abstract acronym. It is the knock at 5 a.m. It is the father who does not come home. It is the child who learns to memorize emergency phone numbers in case their parents disappear. When someone entrusted with children publicly cheers that apparatus, families are not misinterpreting the signal. They understand it perfectly.

The backlash is not censorship. It is a community reacting to perceived hostility.

But the outrage machine reframes the story. Suddenly, the teacher is the victim. The families who felt targeted are recast as intolerant. Accountability becomes oppression.

This inversion is not accidental. It is strategic.

The Asymmetry Doctrine

Authoritarian movements operate on asymmetry. Rights are universal in theory but conditional in practice. The in‑group claims expansive liberties; the out‑group faces rigid enforcement.

When right‑wing activists harass school boards, it is framed as civic engagement. When marginalized parents protest discriminatory policies, it becomes disorder. When conservative lawmakers push to ban books, it is “protecting children.” When teachers discuss systemic racism, it is “indoctrination.”

The pattern repeats across issues:

  • Protest is sacred—until it challenges power.

  • Religious liberty is paramount—unless it belongs to a minority faith.

  • Parental rights are inviolable—unless the parent is undocumented, queer, or politically inconvenient.

  • Law and order are non‑negotiable—unless the law constrains the in‑group.

“Rights for me, the law for thee” is not hypocrisy born of confusion. It is hierarchy disguised as principle.

ICE as Cultural Signal

The phrase “GO ICE” is not merely a policy preference. It is a tribal marker. It signals allegiance in a broader cultural conflict.

ICE was created in the post‑9/11 security expansion, framed as a counterterrorism necessity. Over time, it evolved into a central instrument of immigration enforcement, expanding raids, detention capacity, and deportation operations. In many communities, its presence is synonymous with fear.

Cheering ICE in 2026 is not a neutral statement about border management. It is interpreted as endorsement of aggressive enforcement practices that have separated families and destabilized communities.

Supporters insist they are simply backing law enforcement. But law enforcement does not exist outside context. Institutions acquire reputations through their actions. When an agency becomes associated with masked arrests, detention centers, and broad sweeps of civilian populations, public perception follows accordingly.

The question is not whether someone has the right to say “GO ICE.” They do. The question is whether a school community has the right to determine whether that public stance undermines trust. It does.

The Free Speech Mirage

The free speech absolutism suddenly invoked in these moments is highly selective.

The same ideological ecosystem that decries “cancel culture” has spent years advocating for punitive measures against speech it dislikes. Book bans. Curriculum restrictions. Criminal penalties for protest tactics. Legislative attempts to curtail discussions of race, gender, and history.

When power is on their side, speech becomes something to regulate. When power is not, speech becomes sacred.

This selective absolutism reveals the underlying motive: not liberty, but dominance.

True free speech principles require consistency. If you defend the right to praise ICE, you must also defend the right to criticize it. If you demand institutional neutrality toward your speech, you must accept institutional neutrality toward speech that challenges you.

But neutrality is precisely what the authoritarian right rejects. It demands cultural alignment, not pluralism.

Consequence Is Not Censorship

Employment is conditional. Teaching is relational. Public trust is foundational to both.

When a teacher publicly aligns with a controversial enforcement body, particularly one perceived as targeting the families of students, it can erode trust. That erosion has practical consequences. Parents may withdraw cooperation. Students may feel unsafe. Administrators must weigh institutional stability against individual expression.

None of this constitutes government suppression. It is social consequence.

Consequences are part of a functioning society. Every adult understands this intuitively. Speech shapes reputation. Reputation shapes opportunity. That is not tyranny; it is social reality.

The attempt to portray consequence as oppression is itself a form of rhetorical manipulation. It seeks to convert accountability into victimhood.

The Weaponization of Victimhood

Modern right‑wing politics has mastered the art of aggressive grievance. It advances policies that marginalize vulnerable groups, then frames backlash as persecution.

Support mass deportations? You are defending sovereignty.
Oppose mass deportations? You are undermining the nation.

Criticize a teacher’s pro‑ICE post? You are attacking free speech.

This inversion allows cruelty to masquerade as courage. It recasts power as embattled.

The result is a culture in which empathy is dismissed as weakness and cruelty is reframed as strength. In that culture, the demand for consequences feels like oppression because dominance has been normalized as entitlement.

Conditional Tolerance

Tolerance is not a moral absolute. It is a social contract. It requires mutual recognition of dignity.

When a political movement consistently dehumanizes certain populations—immigrants, minorities, LGBTQ individuals—it strains that contract. When it then invokes tolerance to shield itself from criticism, it is exploiting the very norms it undermines.

If you cheer institutions perceived as instruments of harm, you cannot demand insulation from the communities affected by that harm.

The paradox of tolerance teaches that a society committed to pluralism cannot grant unlimited protection to movements that seek to dismantle pluralism. Rights do not obligate communities to accept open hostility within their own institutions.

Beyond the Individual Case

The teacher’s resignation is not the central story. The central story is the ongoing attempt to redefine rights as unilateral entitlements.

“Rights for me, the law for thee” is not merely rhetorical flourish. It is a worldview in which hierarchy replaces equality and grievance replaces principle.

Under this worldview:

  • Immigration enforcement is harsh because harshness is strength.

  • Protest is dangerous because it challenges hierarchy.

  • Diversity is threatening because it dilutes dominance.

  • Accountability is persecution because it constrains power.

This logic corrodes democratic norms. Democracy requires reciprocal obligation. It requires recognizing that the same rights protecting you protect others.

When rights become tribal weapons rather than shared guarantees, democracy weakens.

The Clear Line

The constitutional line is clear: the government cannot punish lawful speech simply because it is unpopular.

The civic line is equally clear: communities are not obligated to reward speech that undermines their safety or dignity.

The teacher had the right to post those words. The community had the right to respond. The institution had the right to evaluate whether trust had been compromised.

None of this is authoritarian. It is civic accountability.

The real authoritarian impulse is the insistence that one’s speech must be insulated from consequence while others’ speech may be regulated, punished, or silenced.

“Rights for me, the law for thee” is not a misunderstanding of freedom. It is a rejection of equality.

And equality, not dominance, is the foundation of any society that intends to call itself free.
