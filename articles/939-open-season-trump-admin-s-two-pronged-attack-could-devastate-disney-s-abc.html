'Open season': Trump admin's two-pronged attack could devastate Disney's ABC

Based on an article by Daniel Hampton

Paulo SantosJune 14, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Brendan Carr’s Bureaucratic Retaliation Is Not Subtle

The Power Is the Point

The source is straightforward about the moving parts: the FCC forced ABC to seek early renewal of its licenses for eight owned stations, then opened a public petition window where anyone can try to block those renewals. The timing matters. This came right after Donald Trump and Melania Trump lobbied ABC over Jimmy Kimmel, while the FCC also launched a separate enforcement action against The View.

This is not a confused regulatory episode. It is power using procedure as a weapon.

Who Actually Decides

The actor with institutional power here is Brendan Carr, not the anonymous public filing process and not the “anyone can petition” window dressed up as civic participation. Carr controls the agency. He can choose the posture, the timing, the scrutiny, and the pressure. That is the decision-making layer that matters.

ABC is the target, not the sovereign. It can hire Paul Clement. It can file memos. It can warn that the DEI inquiry is pretext. But those are defense moves after the fact. The initiating force is regulatory leverage being applied against a broadcaster the administration dislikes.

Retaliation in Legal Clothing

Carr denies the political motive and points to a DEI investigation. That denial is the familiar camouflage of retaliatory governance: say the words “process” and “investigation,” and hope the public mistakes administrative aggression for neutral enforcement.

ABC’s response cuts through that. It said the DEI probe was a pretext for retaliating against a disfavored broadcaster and chilling protected speech. That is the more credible reading on the face of the sequence the article describes. When punishment lands immediately after a presidential grievance, the claim of pure coincidence becomes a cover story, not an explanation.

The Misdirection Is the Mechanism

The article’s surface framing risks obscuring where responsibility sits. The threat is not “the public” exercising democratic oversight. The threat is an agency head handing ordinary citizens a procedural weapon after the White House signals displeasure.

That matters because it relocates blame downward. It turns an act of executive pressure into a story about complaints, petitions, and regulatory routine. It treats deliberate coercion as if it were bureaucratic weather. That is how institutions launder harassment: they distribute the steps so no one step looks fatal on its own.

What ABC Is Up Against

ABC is not facing a one-off rebuke. It is facing a stacked process: early license renewal scrutiny, an open petition period, a parallel equal-time probe over The View, and a timeline that could stretch as long as three years. Even if any license revocation would likely be overturned, as Gigi Sohn notes, the punishment does not need to survive court to inflict damage.

That is the point of bureaucratic intimidation. The cost is not just in final outcomes. It is in legal bills, uncertainty, executive caution, and the pressure to self-censor before the next confrontation arrives.

The Pattern

This is what authoritarian-style pressure looks like in a softened American form: not immediate censorship, but regulatory menace; not a ban, but a warning; not a direct order, but a structure that makes compliance feel cheaper than defiance.

The larger pattern is simple. Power does not always need to win in court when it can raise the price of speech, selectively target opponents, and make punishment feel administratively normal. That is how a government learns to punish without admitting it is punishing.
