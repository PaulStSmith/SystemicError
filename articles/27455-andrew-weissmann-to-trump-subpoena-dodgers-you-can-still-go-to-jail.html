Andrew Weissmann to Trump subpoena dodgers: You can still go to jail

Based on an article by Roxanne Cooper

Paulo SantosNovember 25, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump administration officials who defy congressional subpoenas could still be jailed even if the Justice Department protects them and President Donald Trump promises pardons. Former federal prosecutor Andrew Weissmann says Congress can ask federal judges to enforce its subpoenas through civil contempt—a coercive process beyond the president’s pardon power.

That distinction matters because the conventional enforcement system depends on an executive branch that may have every incentive to shield its own officials. Congress can issue demands, but criminal contempt referrals ordinarily pass through the Justice Department. If department leaders refuse to prosecute administration allies, the referral becomes political theater backed by no immediate consequence.

The protection racket built into criminal contempt

Weissmann’s warning came during an appearance with Bulwark publisher Sarah Longwell, following journalist Catherine Rampell’s interview with Rep. Jamie Raskin about prospective House oversight. Rampell asked how Congress could enforce subpoenas against an administration that repeatedly obstructed investigators during Trump’s first term.

The familiar route is criminal contempt. Congress refers a defiant witness to the Justice Department, which can prosecute—as it did with former Trump adviser Steve Bannon. But Weissmann identified two weaknesses that become decisive when the executive branch is protecting the people Congress seeks to investigate.

First, a loyalist Justice Department can decline to prosecute. Second, even a later prosecution under a different administration could be neutralized by a presidential pardon. In that arrangement, the institution under scrutiny controls the machinery used to punish obstruction by its own allies.

“The criminal side is not going to be a particularly useful sort of cudgel to get people to comply,” Weissmann told Longwell. That is less a defect in Congress’s investigative authority than a consequence of relying on executive officials to enforce it.

A court order Trump cannot pardon away

Civil enforcement offers a different route. Congress can sue to enforce a subpoena, obtain a judicial ruling on the witness’s privilege claims and seek a court order requiring testimony or documents. Continued defiance can then expose the witness to civil contempt and confinement.

Civil contempt is not punishment for a completed federal crime. It is pressure intended to secure compliance with a court order. That means a president cannot erase it with a pardon.

A witness jailed on that basis, Weissmann explained, “holds the keys to their jail cell”: comply and the confinement ends. Under the rules he described, a recalcitrant witness may be confined for as long as 18 months or until the relevant proceeding concludes. If a judge determines that confinement is no longer coercive because the witness will not yield, the witness must be released from civil confinement, although separate criminal exposure may remain.

This is not an automatic congressional power to imprison political opponents. Judges would first decide whether the subpoena is enforceable and whether asserted privileges excuse noncompliance. The crucial point is institutional: once a federal court orders compliance, neither a protective attorney general nor a presidential pardon can dissolve the civil order.

The organized-crime precedent

Weissmann drew on his experience prosecuting organized crime in the Eastern District of New York. He cited Domenico Cefalu, a Gambino crime family member who later became the organization’s boss.

After prosecutors granted Cefalu immunity and secured an order compelling his testimony, he still refused to speak. A judge held him in civil contempt, and Cefalu spent the maximum 18 months confined without testifying. Prosecutors subsequently brought criminal contempt charges as well.

The comparison does not make administration officials mobsters. It demonstrates that civil contempt is a functioning judicial instrument, not a speculative loophole invented for a confrontation with Trump. Federal courts have used confinement to compel testimony from witnesses who believed silence was worth the cost.

Oversight requires choosing an enforceable weapon

Weissmann’s proposed strategy would not make congressional oversight quick. Civil litigation can consume time, and judges—not lawmakers—would control whether and how contempt sanctions are imposed. But it would remove two veto points from Trump’s hands: a Justice Department’s refusal to prosecute and the president’s ability to pardon federal crimes.

That is the political meaning of the warning. An administration can turn criminal contempt into an empty threat by capturing the prosecutor and protecting the offender. It cannot, by those means alone, nullify a federal judge’s civil contempt order.

Officials considering blanket defiance would therefore be betting on more than Trump’s protection. They would be betting that Congress declines to seek judicial enforcement, that a court rejects the subpoena, or that they can endure confinement rather than comply. A pardon would not open that cell.
