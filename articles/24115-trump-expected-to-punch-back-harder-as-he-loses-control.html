Trump expected to 'punch back harder' as he loses control

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Accountability Is Not Power Until Someone Uses It

A House Majority Is Still Hypothetical

The report describes Democratic lawmakers preparing investigations if their party wins the House in the November election. Their proposed targets include alleged profiteering by President Donald Trump and his family, mining and energy deals obtained by Trump allies, and conduct at the Department of Homeland Security. Those inquiries do not yet exist. The election has not occurred, the committee gavels have not changed hands, and the allegations described in the report remain matters for investigation—not established findings.

Trump Holds the Power Now

The article’s speculative focus on how Trump might react risks obscuring the more important fact: as president, he already commands the executive branch. A future Democratic House could issue subpoenas, conduct hearings, expose records and draft legislation. It could not, by itself, administer agencies, prosecute crimes or force the Senate to approve its bills.

That distinction matters. “Heightened accountability” is the article’s premise, but congressional scrutiny is not accountability unless institutions impose consequences. A hearing can reveal abuse. It can also become televised documentation of misconduct that everyone in power has decided to tolerate.

Investigation Is Not a Substitute for Enforcement

Rep. Robert Garcia reportedly wants to examine how Trump and his family may have profited from government and obtained government contracts. Rep. Jared Huffman reportedly wants to scrutinize mining and energy deals involving Trump allies. Those are legitimate subjects for oversight if the committees establish jurisdiction and pursue evidence.

But the promise of many investigations can become its own misdirection. Democrats get to advertise vigilance; Republicans get to denounce the process; officials under scrutiny get months to obstruct and litigate. Meanwhile, the public is encouraged to mistake activity for remedy.

If lawmakers uncover unlawful conduct, the consequential questions will be brutally practical: What statute was violated? Who can enforce it? What penalties apply? Which officials refuse to act? Without those answers, congressional outrage is political theater with a document archive.

Speculation Is Doing Too Much Work

The weakest part of the framing is its leap from anticipated oversight to an imagined presidential escalation. Susan Glasser points to Trump’s conduct after Republicans lost the House in 2018, including his removal of Attorney General Jeff Sessions and the subsequent government shutdown, as evidence that he tends to intensify conflict after political defeat. That history can inform analysis. It cannot establish what Trump will do after an election that has not happened.

The report also floats the prospect of Trump pardoning himself and members of his administration. That is a possibility discussed by the program, not a reported decision. Likewise, the claim that a presidential veto would produce a shutdown for which Trump alone would publicly take blame compresses a complicated appropriations fight—and public opinion—into a convenient forecast.

The danger here is not excessive alarm. It is imprecision. Speculation lets every institution discuss a future confrontation without accounting for what it is doing in the present.

The Enablers Are Not Bystanders

If government contracts, resource deals or departmental corruption warrant investigation, responsibility will not stop with one president’s temperament. Contracts require officials to award them. Agencies require administrators to carry out policy. Congress controls appropriations and oversight. Courts decide whether subpoenas and executive claims have force. Party leaders determine whether misconduct carries a political cost.

Even the report notes that Republicans have investigated the Department of Homeland Security under Secretary Kristi Noem. That detail undercuts any narrative in which accountability begins only after Democrats win an election. Officials already holding investigative authority must answer for how aggressively—or selectively—they use it.

The System Rewards Deferred Accountability

The larger pattern is a government that postpones judgment until after the next election, then calls the delay constitutional restraint. Lawmakers preview hearings. Commentators predict retaliation. Parties rehearse blame over a shutdown that has not occurred. Every actor gets a future role, while present power remains insufficiently examined.

Oversight matters because records, testimony and legislation can constrain officials. But institutional cowardice begins when leaders describe powers they might acquire more confidently than they use the powers they already possess. The central political question is not how Trump may respond to hypothetical accountability. It is whether Congress and the rest of the state are prepared to turn evidence into consequences—or merely another season of hearings.
