'It's not going good': Latino MAGA voters share dismay after 6 months of Trump

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 29, 20254 min read

Latinos for Trump at 2016 RNC. Image: VOA/WikimediaCommons

Nearly half of Latino voters backed Donald Trump for president in November 2024 — and a New York Times report Monday showed not all are delighted with the decision.

In extensive interviews with 11 Latino MAGA voters, the newspaper discovered a lot of dissatisfaction with the direction of the country — particularly with the economy and immigration.

And they described Trump with words such as “arrogant,” “unpredictable” and “uncensored.”

“It’s not going good,” said one of the participants in the interview, Adrianna, 53, from New York.

“Prices are going up. Nobody’s paychecks are really going up. Things that once seemed pretty affordable now seem almost unattainable. A decent vacation costs a great deal.

“But I believe, in time, it will balance itself out. Right now, we’re just on a little shaky ground financially.”

My biggest concern is the cost of just basic needs,” agreed Esteban, 32, from Texas.

“ In the time that I’ve started working, going to school, I’ve seen the prices of everything jump up. I voted for Donald Trump initially because he promised to help the working class.

“From what I’ve been seeing, I don’t think we’re anywhere close to seeing any benefit from the policies.”

And Kevin, 32, from Georgia said, “I voted for him, and I’m kind of disappointed that a lot of the stuff that he said he was going to do, he hasn’t done yet. I just got my energy bill raised out of nowhere, and I haven’t changed how much I consume.”

But it was Trump immigration policies that drew voters’ ire the most.

“I think it’s very aggressive and intimidating,” said Pennsylvania voter Christopher, 28.

“I can just imagine how frustrated and terrified people would be if you have ICE coming into your classroom and, “Oh, you’re being deported.”

And Jose, 45, from Indiana, added, “What’s going on right now, it’s very aggressive. When he ran, the main concern was the bad guys. Now all I’m hearing about is people getting dragged out of work areas, construction sites.

“I don’t really think that that’s where the bad guys are at. These are people that are working, helping the economy.”

However, the report found, only one of the 11 saidthey’d consider votingfor the Democrats, with most saying they were too left-leaning and which promises a lot, but doesn’t deliver.

Our Analysis:

Dissecting Disillusionment: Latino Voters and the Trump Conundrum

The recent New York Times report uncovers a stark reality that nearly half of Latino voters cast their lot with Donald Trump in the November 2024 election—a decision that, for many, has since soured. Through interviews with 11 Latino MAGA voters, a narrative of disappointment and disillusionment emerges, painting a picture of a community grappling with the repercussions of their political choices amidst a landscape of economic instability and harsh immigration policies. Yet, despite their grievances, a surprising reluctance to pivot towards the Democrats underscores a broader political and ideological rift.

Economic Promises: The Mirage in the Desert

The core of the discontent among these voters lies in the unfulfilled economic promises that once lured them to Trump’s camp. Adrianna, Esteban, and Kevin vocalize a shared frustration over the rising cost of living, stagnant wages, and the elusive benefits of Trump’s policies. Their words echo a bitter realization that the prosperity promised is nowhere in sight, leaving them on "shaky ground financially." This disillusionment is not just a reflection of broken promises but a testament to the dangerous allure of populist rhetoric that fails to materialize into tangible improvements for the working class.

Immigration Policies: The Iron Fist

Perhaps more jarring is the voters’ reaction to Trump's immigration policies, described as "aggressive" and "intimidating." Christopher and Jose’s accounts highlight a shift from targeting "the bad guys" to indiscriminate actions that instill fear among working, contributing members of society. This aggressive approach not only fails to distinguish between the criminal elements and hardworking immigrants but also sows division and terror within communities that have historically contributed immensely to the fabric of the American economy and culture.

The Democratic Dilemma

Ironically, despite their disenchantment, these voters express a hesitancy to consider the Democrats, perceived as "too left-leaning" and equally untrustworthy in delivering on their promises. This sentiment underscores a significant challenge for the Democratic Party: how to appeal to disillusioned voters who, despite feeling betrayed by Trump, remain skeptical of the alternative. It illustrates the deep ideological divides and the complexity of voter behavior, which cannot be easily categorized or taken for granted.

Conclusion: A Political Quagmire

The insights from these Latino voters reveal a deep-seated political quagmire. On one hand, there's a clear dissatisfaction with Trump's failure to deliver on economic prosperity and a humane approach to immigration. On the other, there's a palpable reluctance to embrace the Democratic Party, seen as too distant from their concerns or too ideologically radical. This scenario highlights the urgent need for both parties to reassess their strategies and policies if they wish to genuinely serve and represent the interests of diverse voter bases.

The disenchantment of these Latino voters with Trump, juxtaposed with their skepticism towards the Democrats, serves as a poignant reminder of the complexities of American politics. It’s a call to action for more nuanced, inclusive, and genuinely responsive political discourse and policy-making. Ignoring these voices and their concerns only perpetuates the systemic errors plaguing our political system, leaving many Americans feeling alienated and unrepresented.
