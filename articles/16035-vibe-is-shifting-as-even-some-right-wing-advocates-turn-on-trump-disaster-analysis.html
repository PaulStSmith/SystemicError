'Vibe is shifting' as even some right-wing advocates turn on Trump 'disaster': analysis

The cracks within some right-wing subcultures are starting to show as they fray with President Trump’s agenda, according to a new analysis in the New York Times by columnist Michelle Goldberg.

In her column, Goldberg made her case by singling out recent comments by right-wing podcast host Alex Kaschuta, who said that "the vibe is shifting" against conservatives as "the cumulative IQ of the right is looking worse than the market.”

As Goldberg notes, Kaschuta's podcast "Subversive" is known to be a part of the “weird right-wing internet subcultures and mainstream conservatism. She hosted men’s rights activists and purveyors of ‘scientific’ racism, neo-reactionary online personalities with handles like ‘Raw Egg Nationalist.’”

Now, Goldberg observes that “[Kaschuta] abandoned the [MAGA] movement,” and she is not alone.

“Several people who once appeared to find transgressive right-wing ideas scintillating are having second thoughts as they watch Donald Trump’s administration put those ideas into practice,” she penned.

Another notable right-wing name questioning Trump was philosophy Professor Nathan Cofnas, whom Goldberg notes is a “self-described ‘race realist’ fixated on group differences in I.Q." and who recently wrote on X that "All over the world, almost everyone with more than half a brain is looking at the disaster of Trump (along with Putin, Yoon Suk Yeol, et al.) and drawing the very reasonable conclusion that right-wing, anti-woke parties are incapable of effective governance."

Goldberg opined, “It is too early to know what these small cracks in the dissident right mean and whether they presage more substantial defections. They suggest to me, however, that not everyone can sustain the level of cognitive dissonance necessary to rationalize away this administration’s destructiveness.”

She claimed people felt “pressured” into dishonesty because it was a reaction “against wokeness.”

She ended her column saying, "Irving Kristol famously said that neoconservatives were liberals who’d been ‘mugged by reality.’ Maybe soon we’ll need a similar word for the right wingers who can’t stand to live in the world they helped build.”

Sparky's Analysis:

The Crumbling Facade of the Right-Wing Echo Chamber

In a recent column for the New York Times, Michelle Goldberg dives into the emerging fissures within the right-wing subcultures that have, until now, fervently backed President Trump's agenda. Through the lens of Goldberg's analysis, we're witnessing a significant, albeit overdue, reckoning among some of the most vocal proponents of the far-right. The real story here isn't just about a few dissenting voices; it's about the broader implications of a movement grappling with the monster it helped create.

Goldberg's spotlight on Alex Kaschuta, a right-wing podcast host known for her flirtations with the more unsavory corners of conservative thought, is particularly telling. Kaschuta's admission that "the vibe is shifting" against conservatives, and her critique of the collective intelligence on the right, underscores a broader existential crisis within the movement. The use of economic metaphor—comparing the right's intellectual capital to a plummeting market—speaks volumes about the disillusionment setting in.

Kaschuta's podcast, "Subversive," once a haven for those peddling misogyny and racism under the guise of 'men's rights' and 'scientific' inquiry into race, now serves as a case study in the limits of transgressive politics. Her departure from the MAGA movement, as noted by Goldberg, signals a deeper malaise. It's not just about one individual's change of heart; it's about a movement struggling to reconcile its ideals with the tangible outcomes of its ideology in power.

The mention of Nathan Cofnas, a philosophy professor and self-proclaimed 'race realist', adds another layer to this unraveling narrative. Cofnas's critique of right-wing, anti-woke parties as incapable of effective governance is a damning indictment from within. His observations, shared on social media, reflect a growing awareness that the ideological purity tests of the far right are incompatible with the messy realities of governing a diverse and complex society.

Goldberg's analysis raises an uncomfortable question for the right: What happens when the architects of a divisive, exclusionary political culture begin to see the cracks in their own creation? The cognitive dissonance required to maintain unwavering support for Trump's administration, despite its myriad failures, is becoming too great a burden for some.

The columnist's observation that these defections may signal a broader inability to "sustain the level of cognitive dissonance necessary to rationalize away this administration’s destructiveness" is poignant. It speaks to a fundamental human need for consistency between beliefs and reality—a need that the Trump administration's policies have stretched to the breaking point for some of its erstwhile supporters.

Goldberg concludes with a reference to Irving Kristol's description of neoconservatives as "liberals who’d been ‘mugged by reality.’" This framing suggests that we may soon need a new term for right-wingers confronting the dystopian outcomes of their political fantasies. The irony here is as rich as it is tragic; those who once championed the most regressive and divisive policies are now being forced to live in the very world they helped construct—a world that, as it turns out, is not so appealing from the inside.

In sum, Goldberg's column doesn't just point to a few isolated instances of dissent within the right-wing ecosystem. It signals a potential sea change in how conservative ideas are challenged and debated within their own ranks. While it's too early to predict the full impact of these developments, one thing is clear: the right-wing movement is at a crossroads, and its path forward is anything but certain.
