Face of 'evil': Melinda Gates says Jeffrey Epstein gave her nightmares

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosJune 15, 20262 min read

Epstein Was Not a Monster in a Vacuum

The Real Story Is Power

The source material gives us a familiar elite ritual: a wealthy woman, now free to say aloud what she already knew, describes Jeffrey Epstein as repugnant, says Bill Gates kept contact with him despite objections, and points to the Justice Department’s failures and Epstein’s silent male network. That is the surface. The deeper story is not Epstein’s personality. It is the institutional access that protected him, the men who tolerated him, and the systems that let harm persist until after the fact.

Who Actually Had Power

Epstein did not build his immunity out of charisma alone. He operated through proximity to men with money, status, and institutional reach. Bill Gates is named here not because he is the central villain of the Epstein case, but because the article shows how elite men normalize dangerous associations until those associations become embarrassing. The Justice Department also sits in the frame, and for good reason: if a system can be described as having “not done its job,” then the failure is not mystical. It is institutional.

The Convenient Displacement of Blame

French Gates’ anger is understandable, but the article also reveals how elite scandal gets narrated through personal disgust rather than structural accountability. “He was evil” is emotionally legible. It is also politically incomplete. Evil did not act alone. People enabled him, deferred to him, kept the circle closed, and then went quiet when the costs became public. The article points toward that silence, but the framing still risks centering the horror of one man instead of the machinery that kept him useful.

What the Framing Hides

The piece briefly names the women and girls harmed, but the real asymmetry remains: victims are asked to carry the emotional burden of explaining what happened, while powerful men get to hide behind denial, ambiguity, and selective memory. Gates says the “justice system didn’t do its job.” That is true, and it should be said more plainly: when institutions fail upward, the damage falls downward. That is not confusion. It is hierarchy.

Women in Power, But Not as Decoration

French Gates argues for more women in positions of power, and there is a political logic to that claim. But the deeper point is not that women possess some moral essence men lack. It is that concentrated male networks have repeatedly protected abuse, and elite institutions have rewarded discretion over accountability. Adding women to the room matters only if they are there to alter decisions, not to legitimize the same structure with better branding.

The Pattern Beneath the Scandal

This story is not really about one predator, one divorce, or one email cache. It is about the way elite systems convert personal misconduct into background noise until they can no longer contain it. Then the language shifts to regret, disgust, and vague reckoning. That is the recurring pattern: power creates the conditions, institutions absorb the shock, and the public is left with a moral tale instead of a map of responsibility.
