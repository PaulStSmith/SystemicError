'Death knell': Grim warning after Trump White House's 'terrifying attack' on rule of law

Based on an article by Daniel Hampton

Paulo SantosJune 21, 20263 min read

Habeas Corpus Was the Target, and the Lie Was “Efficiency”

The Power Behind the Push

This was not a legal misunderstanding. It was an executive branch attempt to jam through the suspension of habeas corpus so deportations could move faster after judges kept ruling that immigrants had been unlawfully detained. The central actor here was Stephen Miller, a deputy chief of staff and the administration’s ideological engine on immigration, not a lawyer, but still pushing a constitutional rupture from inside the White House.

That matters. Real institutional power sat with Trump’s White House, and the pressure came from people who wanted courts to stop interfering with detention. The aim was not clarity. It was to erase the mechanism that lets detained people challenge the state.

The Brief Summary of the Record

According to reporting cited in Slate, Miller said in May 2025 that suspending habeas in a so-called “time of invasion” was “an option we’re actively looking at.” A confidential April memo from Staff Secretary Will Scharf warned Chief of Staff Susie Wiles that only Congress can suspend habeas, and only in rebellion or invasion. The idea was shelved, but not necessarily abandoned.

That is the basic outline. The rest is politics: a White House testing how far it could push before the legal system, or internal staff warnings, forced it to stop.

The Real Target Was Judicial Control

Habeas corpus is not abstract legal trivia. It is the thing that stops the state from disappearing people into detention without review. Once that right is gutted, deportation becomes less a legal process than a bureaucratic disposal system.

The administration’s own behavior makes the intent plain enough. More than 54,000 habeas petitions have been filed since Trump’s second term began, more than in the previous three administrations combined. That is not evidence of chaos. It is evidence of a government producing unlawful detention at scale, then treating court review as an obstacle to be neutralized.

The Misdirection Is the Point

The favorite trick in stories like this is to frame the issue as a fight over border management, legal complexity, or administrative pressure. That framing hides the actual decision-makers. Judges did not create unlawful detention. Immigrants did not create it either. The administration did, and when the courts interrupted it, the White House reached for constitutional sabotage.

The source also notes the administration admitted to mistakenly deporting people, including Kilmar Abrego Garcia. That detail is the opposite of a justification for suspending habeas. It is the indictment. If the government is already deporting the wrong people, eliminating the right to challenge detention is not a fix. It is an invitation to more error, more coercion, and less accountability.

The Pattern Is Authoritarian Convenience

This is what authoritarian drift looks like in practice: not one dramatic coup, but repeated efforts to make legal restraint disappear whenever it slows the machinery of punishment. Miller’s language about “invasion” is not neutral policy talk. It is a pretext designed to convert executive preference into emergency power.

The conservative lawyers inside the White House appear to have slowed the move, at least for now. That is not institutional health. It is a temporary internal brake on a political project that keeps returning to the same conclusion: if courts protect the targets, then weaken the courts. If law impedes detention, then attack the law. As long as Trump is in office, the pressure to move from governance to raw coercion will remain.
