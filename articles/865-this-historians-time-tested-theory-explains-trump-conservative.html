This historian’s time-tested theory explains Trump: conservative

The Great Man Theory Is a Smokescreen for Institutional Surrender

Power Has Names

The source article tries to use Thomas Carlyle’s “great man” theory to explain Donald Trump’s political effect, with David French treating Trump as a singular force who bent a party and a culture around himself. That is partly true only in the weakest sense: Trump is loud, persistent, and corrosive. But the real story is not that one man mysteriously conquered institutions. It is that institutions with power chose, repeatedly, to accommodate him.

That distinction matters. When a party, a media ecosystem, and religious networks keep rewarding misconduct, the outcome is not caused by charisma alone. It is enabled by decision-makers who find the arrangement useful enough to keep.

Trump Did Not Act Alone

French’s column, as quoted here, frames Trump as someone whose behavior has become so normalized that even “egregious misconduct” barely registers. That is not an accident of psychology. It is the product of organized permission.

Republicans “rallied to his side” when Trump “said something deranged,” and the article presents that as evidence of Trump’s power. But the more important fact is that Republican leaders still defend him. They are not trapped in a fog; they are making a calculation. Trump gives them votes, media attention, grievance fuel, and a vehicle for power. In exchange, they absorb the damage and call it loyalty.

That is not magnetism. It is political collusion.

The Party Chose Corruption

French says Trump’s corruption has “corrupted virtually the entire party,” turning it into a magnet for grifters and conspiracy theorists. That language is useful because it points at the actual mechanism: corruption is not just a personality trait in this story. It is a governing model.

The party did not merely fail to resist Trump. It normalized him, protected him, and reorganized itself around what he could deliver. Once that happens, the line between scandal and strategy disappears. If misconduct no longer costs anything, then misconduct becomes an asset. The party’s leaders are not bystanders in that process. They are the ones who keep it profitable.

The article is strongest when it acknowledges that Trump’s influence is not limited to policy. He has changed what his party will tolerate, what it will excuse, and what it will celebrate. That is the real institutional fact here.

The Evangelical Alibi

French also notes that Trump has influenced the evangelical church more than the church has influenced Trump. That is a damning admission, even if it is presented as a lament. It says something worse than hypocrisy. It says that a moral constituency sold its own standards for access to power.

This is where the article’s frame becomes revealing. It treats the collapse of moral authority as if it were a side effect of Trumpian force. But the church leadership that continued defending him was not conquered. It was complicit. It traded witness for proximity. That is not confusion. It is submission dressed up as discernment.

When institutions built to claim moral authority bend toward a man they know is “out of control,” they are not being influenced in the abstract. They are choosing which sins are acceptable when power is on the table.

The Real Misleading Comfort

The most revealing line in the source is French’s suggestion that “we could be one good leader away from turning the page.” That is the comforting lie at the center of the great man theory. It suggests that institutional degradation can be reversed by finding the right individual.

That is too neat. It turns a structural collapse into a personality problem. It imagines that the same system that rewarded Trump will suddenly recover virtue when a better figure appears. But the machinery that elevated him is still there: opportunistic politicians, indulgent media habits, a party eager for domination, and moral institutions willing to bargain away their credibility.

A good leader can matter. But a good leader does not automatically repair a rotten incentive structure. Without confronting the people and institutions that enabled Trump, “turning the page” is just branding for continued surrender.

The System Behind the Spectacle

The article is right about one thing: leaders can corrode their followers. But that is only half the story. Followers also choose leaders, excuse them, and build systems that reward the worst of them. Trump is not proof that one man can remake history by force of personality alone. He is proof that institutions already weakened by greed, cowardice, and grievance will hand the steering wheel to anyone willing to drive them into the wall.

That is the political pattern here. Not a lone authoritarian miracle. A network of enablers, beneficiaries, and rationalizers pretending that the damage came from elsewhere.
