'Not unifying' and 'wrong': GOP congressman breaks ranks to attack Trump White House

U.S. Rep. Don Bacon (R-NE) blasted President Donald Trump and his administration, decrying a lack of “moral clarity” from the White House while denouncing the “populist” President as “not unifying.”

Congressman Bacon said “absolutely” that President Trump should tone down his rhetoric.

“He’s a populist. He centers on anger and opposition. That’s what he does. But he had a chance to be more Ronald Reagan and try to unify both sides on this,” Bacon said of the Charlie Kirk assassination. “It would be one thing that was old Republicans being murdered, but it’s not.”

He also said he hopes the Trump makeover of the Republican Party is “not a permanent remaking.”

Hate Speech and Threatening the Media

“There have been some wrong statements made, to say the least. When Attorney General [Bondi] said that they were gonna go after hate speech — she backed off of that. But it was wrong,” Congressman Bacon, who is retiring at the end of his term, told CNN’s Manu Raju on Monday. “And I think the president doing threats against media is also wrong. We don’t threaten the media.”

“To threaten media, and so you’re gonna pull their license, that’s not what America’s about. And we do have a freedom of speech, freedom of the press. We should defend that.”

“We have to acknowledge, Republicans and Democrats have been murdered in the last year. And someone tried to burn the house down of the governor of Pennsylvania — wasn’t a guy from the right, it was a antisemitic guy, going after Governor Shapiro, because he’s Jewish.”

“From the White House, we’re hearing, this is just a liberal problem,” Raju said.

“That’s… Not very unifying,” Bacon said. “I don’t think it’s accurate, for one, and it’s not unifying. He had the opportunity here to say, to acknowledge a couple of Democrats were murdered in Minneapolis. Right? By a guy who called himself pro-life. We don’t really know his motive, but still, he surely was more on the conservative side, it seems, from what we know.”

“I do think that we, as officeholders, though, should there should be a line we draw.”

Bacon denounced both Democrats and Republicans using extremist words to characterize each other.

“My point is, we’ve overdone it,” he said, while warning that “there’s a small part of our population that, yeah, they take it serious, and they’re radicalized. So I think we owe ourselves better at how we treat each other.”

Tariffs

“I think he is, by far,” overreaching on tariffs, Bacon continued.

“Article I of the Constitution clearly gives us, the Congress,” he said, power over tariffs.

“Iowa and Nebraska are really struggling right now with our farm economy. We are not growing markets for corn and soybeans. The president’s making trade deals, but not a single country that I can see has bought more corn soybeans. That’s what we really need right now.”

Russia

“Reagan stood up to Gorbachev,” Bacon said of the late GOP and Soviet Union Presidents.

“I don’t I don’t see the moral clarity right now out of the White House. Ronald Reagan had moral clarity.”

“Now, he also was willing to negotiate and try to lower the tensions, but he knew that communism was evil, and he, and he, it was clear that he stood steadfast with our NATO allies. And the president sends out such mixed messages on NATO, and totally moral, ambiguous messages about Ukraine and Russia.”

When asked about the President’s “moral character,” Bacon refused to answer directly.

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Bacon Blast: A Critical Look at GOP Discord and Trump’s Divisive Tenure

In a recent outpour of frustration, U.S. Rep. Don Bacon (R-NE) laid bare the chasm within the Republican Party, attributing it to the divisive and populist antics of President Donald Trump. Bacon’s critique not only underscores a dire need for “moral clarity” from the White House but also echoes a longing for unity that seems to be a relic of the past. Let’s dissect this political disarray with the precision of a scalpel, shall we?

The Trumpian Populism: A Recipe for Division

Bacon’s characterization of Trump as a populist who “centers on anger and opposition” is not just a casual observation; it’s a scathing indictment of a leadership style that thrives on divisiveness. The comparison to Ronald Reagan, a figure often idolized for his unifying rhetoric, places Trump’s shortcomings in stark relief. The Charlie Kirk assassination—a moment ripe for presidential gravitas and unity—was, according to Bacon, squandered by Trump’s divisive rhetoric. This isn’t just a missed opportunity; it’s a deliberate choice that speaks volumes about Trump’s priorities and leadership ethos.

The Media: Between a Rock and a Hard Place

Bacon’s defense of the media against Trump’s threats is particularly noteworthy. Freedom of speech and the press are foundational to American democracy, yet here we find ourselves with a president who seems to believe that these freedoms are negotiable—or worse, revocable—if the press’s narrative doesn’t flatter him. This isn’t just “wrong,” as Bacon politely puts it; it’s a dangerous precedent that undermines the very fabric of our democratic society.

Violence and Blame: The Misguided Attribution of Extremism

The mention of violence against political figures and the attempt on Governor Shapiro’s house illustrates a grim reality of today’s political climate, further exacerbated by Trump’s rhetoric. The attempt to paint political violence as a “liberal problem” is not only inaccurate, as Bacon notes, but also irresponsibly simplistic. It ignores the complexity of political extremism and, in doing so, fails to address the root causes of such violence. By not acknowledging the political affiliations of perpetrators across the spectrum, Trump’s narrative fosters division, not unity.

Tariffs and Trade: The Missteps of Economic Policy

Bacon’s critique extends to Trump’s economic policies, particularly his approach to tariffs and trade. The assertion that Trump has overreached, violating the Constitution’s allocation of power over tariffs to Congress, highlights a fundamental disregard for the checks and balances that are supposed to guide U.S. governance. The economic strain on Iowa and Nebraska’s farm economies is a testament to the real-world consequences of Trump’s trade policies. This isn’t just about corn and soybeans; it’s about livelihoods and the sustainability of rural America.

Russia and the Erosion of Moral Clarity

Perhaps the most damning of Bacon’s criticisms is the comparison between Trump’s and Reagan’s approaches to Russia and NATO. Where Reagan stood firm against the Soviet Union with moral clarity, Trump offers “mixed messages” and “moral ambiguity.” This isn’t just about foreign policy—it’s about the values and principles that the United States represents on the global stage. Trump’s stance, or lack thereof, on Ukraine and Russia not only confuses allies but also emboldens adversaries.

Conclusion: The GOP’s Identity Crisis

Bacon’s reflections, as he nears retirement, reveal a Republican Party at a crossroads. The choice between populist divisiveness and principled unity is not just an ideological debate; it’s a question of the party’s soul. As Bacon hopes for a Trumpian phase not to be a “permanent remaking” of the GOP, one wonders whether the party can navigate back to its foundational values or if it’s destined to wander in the wilderness of Trump’s shadow.

In essence, Don Bacon’s critique is a clarion call for a return to sanity and principles—a call that seems increasingly quixotic in the era of Trumpian politics.
