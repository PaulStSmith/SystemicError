Former “GMA” host Joan Lunden has alleged that her former ABC boss sexually harassed her during the early days of her career, according to a USA Today report Tuesday.

Lunden co-anchored the morning show from 1980 to 1997 and revealed that the harassment happened during her time as host at WABC when she was just 25 years old. She did not identify the former boss connected to the allegations.

The Emmy-nominated broadcaster detailed her experiences as a reporter and anchor at the New York station from 1975 until she joined “GMA” in 1980 in her new book “Joan: Life Beyond the Script.” In the book, she referred to her former boss as “Ted,” who asked her to join a work meet-up on Fire Island, calling the event a “good opportunity for you to socialize with the rest of the team.”

“Not having any close friendships in the newsroom, I was concerned that I would feel awkward and uncomfortable, like a tagalong,” Lunden said. “With some encouragement, I agreed to go. I’d never been to the famed Fire Island before. I’d heard that no cars were allowed on the island, so you had to take a ferry to get there.”

But when Lunden arrived, there was only one other colleague there with his girlfriend — and it appeared to be “an overnight double date,” she explained.

“I was embarrassed that I’d been so naive as to let this situation unfold, and I was offended as a woman that a guy — my superior at work — thought he could get away with this!” Lunden reflected. “He assumed that I would just go along with it.”

She said her boss tried to pressure her to sleep in his bed with him, but instead she slept on a couch.

“’Ted, you know this is not what I signed up for,’ I said, trying to be strong but not too offensive, since he was my boss,” Lunden wrote in her book. “I was praying he would say, ‘Oops, my bad. I’m sorry, let me take you back home.’ Instead, he tried to be charming and make light of it. ‘Look, maybe the original plan fell through, but we’re all here, so let’s just enjoy ourselves.’”

Our Analysis:

The Unveiling: Joan Lunden’s Harrowing Journey

In a revelation that shocks yet unsurprisingly aligns with the grim tapestry of broadcast journalism’s past, former “GMA” host Joan Lunden brings to light allegations of sexual harassment at the hands of her then-boss at ABC. Detailed in a report by USA Today and further elaborated in her book “Joan: Life Beyond the Script,” Lunden’s account sheds light on an all-too-familiar narrative of power abuse within the media industry.

The Alleged Incident: A Cloaked Predation

At the heart of Lunden’s allegations lies an incident cloaked in the guise of professional networking—a work meet-up on Fire Island, pitched as a golden opportunity for Lunden to mingle with the team. Yet, the reality was far from the professional setting she was led to expect. Upon arrival, Lunden found herself in what was effectively an orchestrated double date, with her boss attempting to pressure her into sharing his bed.

Lunden’s account exposes not just an individual’s reprehensible behavior but underscores the systemic issue of power dynamics being exploited for predation. Her narrative, unfortunately, is not unique but part of a broader pattern of behavior that has been pervasive in industries far and wide, where individuals in positions of power leverage their authority to exploit and harass.

The Culture of Silence and Complicity

What is particularly striking about Lunden’s story is not just the harassment itself, but the environment that allowed such behavior to go unchecked. The culture of silence and complicity within the industry that emboldens predatory behavior is as much a culprit as the individuals perpetrating these acts.

Lunden’s reluctance to be “too offensive” in her rebuff of her boss’s advances speaks volumes about the power imbalance at play and the fear of professional retaliation that many victims of harassment experience. This fear perpetuates a cycle of silence, making it all the more difficult for victims to come forward.

Moving Forward: The Need for Systemic Change

Joan Lunden’s courage in sharing her story is commendable and necessary. It adds another voice to the chorus of those calling for change in an industry that has long been plagued by instances of sexual harassment and abuse. However, bringing these stories to light is only the first step in a much-needed overhaul of the systems and cultures that allow such behavior to flourish.

The media industry, and indeed all sectors, must move beyond mere acknowledgment of these issues to implementing concrete changes in policies, practices, and, most importantly, culture. This includes creating environments where victims feel safe to come forward, ensuring thorough investigations of allegations, and holding perpetrators accountable, irrespective of their status or position.

Conclusion: An Ongoing Battle

Lunden’s allegations serve as a stark reminder of the ongoing battle against sexual harassment and power abuse in the workplace. While strides have been made in recent years, particularly with the rise of movements like #MeToo, there is still a long way to go.

The path forward requires not only individual bravery, like that shown by Lunden, but collective action to dismantle the structures that enable such behavior. It is only through systemic change that we can hope to create an environment where professional success is not marred by the specter of harassment and exploitation.
