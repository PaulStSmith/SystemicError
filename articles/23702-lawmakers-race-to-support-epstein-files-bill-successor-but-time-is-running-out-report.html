Lawmakers race to support Epstein files bill successor — but ‘time is running out’: report

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosSeptember 20, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Epstein Files Are Being Buried by Procedure, Not Mystery

The Power Is in Washington

The immediate facts are simple: Representatives Thomas Massie and Ro Khanna are trying to force disclosure of still-withheld federal records concerning Jeffrey Epstein. Their discharge petition has 106 signatures—far short of the 218 required to compel a House vote. Their bill would also require Justice Department cooperation with state investigations and restrict the privileges federal officials may invoke to keep records concealed.

This is not a story about an unknowable secret resisting discovery. It is about institutions choosing whether disclosure will occur.

Johnson Controls the Clock

House Speaker Mike Johnson cancelled the final two legislative weeks of September, leaving supporters only four working days before September 17 to gather the remaining signatures. Whatever justification Johnson might offer, the institutional consequence is unmistakable: House members now have less time to challenge the Trump administration’s withholding of Epstein-related material.

That is power exercised through scheduling. A speaker does not need to defeat a transparency bill on the merits when he can narrow the calendar until the effort suffocates. Procedure provides political cover precisely because it makes an imposed obstacle look like an unfortunate shortage of time.

“Time is running out” is therefore an incomplete diagnosis. Time did not wander off. House leadership removed it.

The Administration Is Withholding the Records

The central actor is the Trump administration, specifically a Justice Department accused of resisting disclosure and obstructing outside scrutiny. New Mexico Attorney General Raúl Torrez has sued the department, alleging that it stonewalled his state’s investigation into Epstein’s activities. Massie and Khanna drafted their legislation to block some of the privilege claims the department has repeatedly used to withhold files.

Describing this merely as a stalled transparency effort obscures agency. The records remain unavailable because federal officials are keeping them unavailable. Congress has the authority to confront that decision, but most House members have not joined the petition that would force them to vote publicly.

Silence Is Also a Decision

The 106 signatories deserve to be distinguished from the lawmakers declining to sign. But the more revealing number is 112: the additional signatures required to bring the bill to the floor.

Members who withhold their names are not standing outside the dispute. They are preserving leadership’s control over whether the dispute receives a vote. That arrangement protects everyone involved: the administration avoids compulsory disclosure, leadership avoids an inconvenient roll call, and reluctant legislators avoid explaining why they opposed one.

The proposed criminal penalties for DOJ and FBI officials who continue withholding covered records sharpen the stakes. If the legislation is as consequential as its supporters argue, then blocking a vote does more than delay publication. It preserves the government’s ability to resist accountability without facing those penalties.

Secrecy Protected by Diffused Responsibility

Epstein’s crimes have produced endless speculation about hidden influence. That atmosphere can itself become a useful distraction. Public attention gravitates toward unnamed people in unreleased documents while the identifiable officials controlling those documents receive less scrutiny.

The larger pattern is institutional cowardice disguised as procedural drift. Executive agencies invoke privilege. Congressional leaders manipulate scarce floor time. Rank-and-file lawmakers avoid a recorded choice. Each actor can claim that no single decision caused the obstruction, even as their decisions combine to produce exactly that result.

The scandal is not only what the files may contain. It is the visible machinery ensuring that the public cannot examine them—and the political class’s reliance on process to make deliberate concealment look like mere delay.
