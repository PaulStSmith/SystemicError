The Trump administration was dealt a rare Supreme Court shadow docket loss on Friday in a case that challenges a Justice Department policy that restricts immigration judges from speaking publicly about immigration issues without prior approval.

Josh Gerstein, senior legal affairs reporter at Politico, shared the shadow docket ruling on X in a case that stems from a lawsuit the National Association of Immigration Judges filed against the Director of the Executive Office for Immigration Review. The immigration judges challenged review policies that limit what immigration judges can publicly say about issues related to their official duties.

NAIJ had said the rule violates the First Amendment. The EOIR chief asked the Supreme Court to halt a lower court’s mandate that sent the case back to the district court, arguing that Supreme Court precedent already makes clear that the Civil Service Reform Act channels these disputes out of the district court.

But on Friday, the Supreme Court denied the Trump administration’s request, so the lower court’s remand order stayed in effect as the underlying jurisdictional and First Amendment issues continue to be litigated.

Gerstein noted it was a “rare” shadow docket loss for the Trump administration, but added on X, “Court tempers blow by inviting DOJ to come back if discovery is approved.”

“At this stage, the Government has not demonstrated that it will suffer irreparable harm without a stay,” the shadow docket ruling said.

As of October, Trump secured victories in 20 shadow docket rulings out of 23 decisions involving his administration this year.

Our Analysis:

The Supreme Court’s Shadow Docket: A Rare Loss for the Trump Administration

In a striking deviation from its usual course, the Trump administration faced a notable setback in the Supreme Court’s shadow docket, a term that might sound like it’s pulled straight from a spy novel but is, in fact, an integral part of our judicial system. This shadow docket, often shrouded in mystery due to its expedited and less transparent decision-making process, handed down a decision that reverberates with the sound of justice, albeit faintly, in a landscape often dominated by political maneuvering.

The case, brought forth by the National Association of Immigration Judges (NAIJ), challenges a Justice Department policy that effectively gags immigration judges, requiring them to seek approval before speaking publicly on immigration issues. This policy, a blatant affront to the First Amendment, represents yet another chapter in the administration’s relentless attempt to control the narrative on immigration, a saga marked by policies that often prioritize political expediency over human rights and legal ethics.

The Supreme Court’s decision to deny the Trump administration’s request to halt a lower court’s mandate underscores a crucial point: the administration’s argument, which hinges on the Civil Service Reform Act, does not, in this instance, trump the First Amendment. The lower court’s remand order, therefore, remains in effect, keeping the door open for further litigation on both jurisdictional and First Amendment grounds.

Josh Gerstein, senior legal affairs reporter at Politico, characterizes this outcome as “rare,” noting the Trump administration’s previous success in securing victories in 20 out of 23 shadow docket rulings this year. This high success rate raises eyebrows, prompting questions about the shadow docket’s transparency and the potential for its use (or misuse) in furthering particular political agendas.

Moreover, the Supreme Court’s invitation for the DOJ to return if discovery is approved adds a layer of complexity to the case. It suggests a conditional openness to revisiting the issue, yet also highlights the ongoing struggle between governmental transparency and secrecy. The ruling’s acknowledgment that the government has not demonstrated it would “suffer irreparable harm” without a stay is particularly telling. It implies that the administration’s often-cited rationale of protecting national interests or maintaining order does not hold water in this context.

In essence, this rare loss for the Trump administration in the Supreme Court’s shadow docket is a small but significant victory for free speech and judicial independence. It serves as a reminder that, even within the often opaque processes of our judicial system, principles like the First Amendment can still emerge victorious. However, the fact that such victories are deemed “rare” should give us pause, prompting us to reflect on the broader implications for justice and transparency in an era increasingly characterized by its lack thereof.

The battle for the soul of America’s immigration policy, and indeed its entire judicial system, is far from over. But for now, the NAIJ’s successful challenge against the Justice Department’s restrictive policy stands as a beacon of hope, a testament to the enduring power of constitutional rights in the face of authoritarian impulses.
