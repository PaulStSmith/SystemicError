The Myth of Meritocracy: The Lie That Holds Inequality Together

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 16, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

For decades, political leaders, business elites, and cultural commentators have repeated the same comforting story: that modern societies operate as meritocracies. According to this narrative, talent rises, effort is rewarded, and success reflects individual virtue rather than inherited advantage.

It is a tidy story. It is also largely fiction.

The conversation between Trevor Noah and Jon Stewart about meritocracy exposes something deeper than economic inequality. It reveals the fragile mythology that powerful societies build around privilege in order to make structural inequality appear fair, natural, and even moral.

Meritocracy as a Moral Cover Story

The myth of meritocracy performs an essential ideological function. It transforms advantage into virtue and disadvantage into personal failure.

If the system rewards merit, then those who succeed must deserve their success. And those who struggle must, by implication, deserve their hardship.

This logic is convenient for the winners.

But history makes the story impossible to sustain. Wealth accumulation has always been deeply intertwined with exclusion, inheritance, and power. From colonial extraction to racial segregation to elite educational pipelines, the ladder of opportunity has rarely been open to everyone at the same time.

The past was not a golden age of merit. It was a system of gates.

Those gates were controlled by race, gender, class, and social networks.

Even in modern economies that claim to reward skill and innovation, structural advantages persist. Access to education, financial capital, professional mentorship, and social connections still plays a decisive role in determining who succeeds.

Merit matters. But merit has never been the only factor, and pretending otherwise obscures the machinery that distributes opportunity.

The Shock of Losing Default Advantage

One of the most striking observations in Noah’s story involves his mother reflecting on poverty during apartheid in South Africa. When she saw homeless white people after the system began to change, she expressed pity for them.

Her reasoning was brutally simple: they had never been taught how to survive hardship.

Black South Africans had been raised with the expectation that the system was stacked against them. Survival required resilience, adaptation, and community support.

White South Africans had been raised with the assumption that society was designed to benefit them.

When that assumption collapses, the psychological shock can be profound.

This dynamic is not unique to South Africa. It appears whenever long-standing hierarchies begin to erode.

Groups that were historically guaranteed advantage often interpret the removal of that guarantee as persecution.

Equality feels like loss.

Loss feels like injustice.

And injustice fuels backlash.

When Equality Is Misinterpreted as Oppression

This phenomenon explains many of the cultural conflicts unfolding across modern democracies.

Consider education. As women gained equal access to universities and professional fields, their academic performance rapidly matched or exceeded that of men in many areas.

The predictable response from some commentators was not celebration of expanding opportunity. Instead, a new grievance narrative emerged claiming that institutions had become biased against men.

This pattern repeats itself across many domains.

When historically excluded groups gain representation in politics, business, media, or academia, their success is often framed not as progress but as unfair favoritism.

The underlying assumption remains unspoken but powerful: that the previous distribution of opportunity was somehow neutral.

It was not.

The Economic Incentives Behind Culture War Politics

The conversation also touches on a critical modern dynamic: the deliberate amplification of grievance for political and economic gain.

In an attention-driven media environment, outrage is profitable.

Public figures who frame social change as an existential threat can mobilize massive audiences. Those audiences generate engagement, influence, and often financial reward.

The culture war is not simply a clash of ideas. It has become a business model.

The incentive structure rewards those who can convince people that equality represents a dangerous social experiment rather than the expansion of basic democratic principles.

Once grievance becomes monetized, there is little incentive to calm the conflict.

Escalation becomes the rational strategy.

The Ownership Question

Noah raises another provocative question: why do societies accept extreme private ownership over resources that originate in the commons?

Consider oil, water, or mineral wealth. These resources exist independently of human effort, yet ownership structures frequently allow a small number of individuals or corporations to control their extraction and profit from them.

Imagine applying the same logic to air.

If someone invented a machine capable of monopolizing breathable oxygen within a city, society would reject the idea of paying that individual simply to breathe.

Yet similar arrangements exist for many natural resources.

The difference lies not in logic but in normalization. Systems that would appear absurd when introduced suddenly become accepted when they emerge gradually.

Over time, the extraordinary becomes routine.

The Privatization of Public Goods

Water offers a telling example.

In many countries, public water infrastructure has deteriorated while private bottled water industries have expanded dramatically. As trust in municipal systems declines, consumers increasingly pay corporations for something that was historically considered a public good.

The shift is subtle but significant.

When essential resources move from public stewardship into private control, the terms of access change. What was once a shared right becomes a commodity.

And once commodification becomes normalized, reversing it becomes politically difficult.

Information Systems and Manufactured Reality

Jon Stewart identifies another critical issue: the fragmentation of the modern information environment.

Democratic societies depend on a shared baseline of facts. Citizens can disagree about policy, values, and priorities, but productive debate requires at least some agreement about reality.

That shared reality is increasingly dissolving.

Algorithm-driven media ecosystems reward sensationalism, emotional outrage, and ideological confirmation. Information circulates within self-reinforcing communities where contradictory evidence is filtered out or dismissed.

The result is not merely polarization.

It is epistemic fragmentation: entire populations operating within different factual universes.

Under those conditions, rational democratic deliberation becomes extremely difficult.

Why the Meritocracy Myth Persists

If the evidence against pure meritocracy is so strong, why does the myth persist?

Because it serves powerful psychological and political functions.

For individuals who have benefited from existing systems, meritocracy offers moral reassurance. It allows them to interpret their success as the result of personal excellence rather than structural advantage.

For political leaders and economic elites, the myth discourages systemic critique. If inequality reflects differences in effort or talent, then structural reform appears unnecessary or even unjust.

The story stabilizes the system.

But stability built on myth has limits.

The Crisis of Legitimacy

Every social system ultimately depends on legitimacy: the belief among citizens that institutions operate according to fair rules.

When large numbers of people conclude that the system is rigged, legitimacy erodes.

Economic inequality alone does not automatically produce instability. Many societies tolerate large wealth gaps as long as upward mobility appears plausible.

But when mobility stalls and opportunity becomes visibly gated, the narrative of merit collapses.

At that point, frustration intensifies.

Some citizens demand structural reform. Others embrace reactionary movements that promise to restore a fictional past where the hierarchy felt more comfortable.

Both responses reflect the same underlying recognition: the system is not functioning as advertised.

Toward an Honest Conversation

Acknowledging the limits of meritocracy does not mean denying the importance of effort, talent, or innovation.

Individual achievement matters.

But honest societies must also recognize the structural forces that shape opportunity.

Education systems, labor markets, housing policy, inheritance law, and access to capital all influence who succeeds and who struggles.

Ignoring those factors does not make them disappear. It merely protects the illusion that the system distributes rewards fairly.

Trevor Noah and Jon Stewart’s discussion highlights a simple but uncomfortable truth.

The myth of meritocracy persists not because it accurately describes reality, but because it makes reality easier for the winners to accept.

And until societies are willing to confront that myth honestly, debates about inequality will continue to orbit the surface of the problem while the deeper structures remain untouched.
