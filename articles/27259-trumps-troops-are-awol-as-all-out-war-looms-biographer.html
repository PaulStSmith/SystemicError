Trump’s troops are AWOL as all-out war looms: biographer

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 14, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

An aggressive letter meets a weakened departmentBlanche’s conflicting obligationsThe professionals leave; the loyalists inherit the machinery

AlterNet’s “AWOL troops” are not soldiers but administration lawyers and senior aides whom Trump biographer Michael Wolff predicts will abandon President Donald Trump as congressional Democrats prepare new oversight efforts. The “all-out war” is also a forecast, not a reported conflict already underway: Wolff argues that Trump wants confrontation but may lack competent officials willing to conduct it.

That distinction matters. The story is not that Trump has lost institutional power. He remains president, controls the executive branch and commands a Justice Department capable of imposing enormous legal and financial costs. The narrower claim is that loyalty-driven government has depleted the professional ranks Trump would need to turn political retaliation into disciplined institutional action.

An aggressive letter meets a weakened department

The immediate dispute involves a letter from Trump’s top lawyer opposing oversight plans by incoming Democratic leaders. AlterNet describes its position as aggressive and highly controversial and reports that Trump may have directed Attorney General Todd Blanche, his former personal lawyer, to threaten Democrats probing corruption allegations.

That account preserves two layers of uncertainty: whether Trump personally ordered the response and what the administration will do beyond sending the letter. Wolff nevertheless expects open conflict after January 3, when Democrats are positioned to exercise congressional authority.

“Nobody [competent] works for the Justice Department anymore,” Wolff said on the Thursday edition of his “Inside Trump’s Head” podcast. Co-host Joanna Cole added that many people had been leaving. Neither comment describes an administration choosing restraint. Wolff’s argument is that Trump’s personnel system has damaged his capacity: officials useful enough to execute a campaign against Congress are also experienced enough to recognize its risks.

The resulting danger is not necessarily an idle presidency. An executive branch stripped of internal competence and resistance can become less predictable, with greater authority concentrated among fewer loyalists.

Blanche’s conflicting obligations

Blanche occupies the central institutional position in this account. He previously defended Trump in the criminal case that produced Trump’s hush-money conviction before Trump installed him as attorney general. Now, according to AlterNet, he is helping lead the response to Democratic oversight.

Wolff believes Blanche is “in deep” and must begin protecting himself from potential legal liability. That is Wolff’s assessment, not a reported finding that Blanche committed a crime. But it identifies the structural conflict created when a president elevates a personal defense lawyer into control of federal law enforcement: the official charged with directing the Justice Department also carries a history of personal service to the president whose administration Congress intends to investigate.

The administration’s letter can therefore be read as more than political messaging. It tests whether executive legal authority will be used to answer legitimate oversight or intimidate those attempting it. Congress has institutional power here as well, but its investigative authority depends on officials complying with demands, courts enforcing boundaries and lawmakers sustaining the confrontation.

The professionals leave; the loyalists inherit the machinery

Wolff compares the present departures to the final weeks after Trump lost the 2020 election. In his telling, “semi-professional” White House personnel fled while Rudy Giuliani and a collection of increasingly marginal loyalists remained. Stephen Miller, Wolff noted, was not part of Giuliani’s post-election circle and returned only after January 6.

This time, Wolff predicts Miller will occupy Giuliani’s role, surrounded by “true believers” with nowhere else to go. He offered former chief of staff Susie Wiles as an example of the broader exodus, though AlterNet’s account provides no further detail about her departure.

That history undercuts the comforting implication of an army going AWOL. Personnel flight may weaken Trump operationally, but it also removes people most likely to understand legal limits, bureaucratic procedure and the consequences of reckless orders. Institutional cowardice and institutional self-preservation can look identical at the exit: experienced officials protect themselves while leaving public power in the hands of those least inclined to restrain it.

Wolff may be right that Trump is “losing his teeth,” but the evidence presented supports a more limited conclusion. Trump’s prospective campaign against Democratic oversight could be understaffed, legally hazardous and chaotically managed. It could still inflict damage. A government does not become harmless when professionals abandon it; sometimes it merely becomes cruder, more concentrated and less accountable.
