Layoffs bite deep into Trump country

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosAugust 22, 20263 min read

Trump’s Economy Is a Management Failure, Not a Mystery

The Story, Briefly

Cornerstone Building Brands is laying off more than 60 workers at its Kearney, Missouri facility, with separations set for around October 19, 2026, just before voters render a verdict on Trump and Republican economic claims. The source ties the layoffs to a broader manufacturing slump under Trump’s tariff regime and quotes economist Robert Shapiro on the damage of policy-driven uncertainty.

Power Has a Name

This is not an abstract “economy” story. Donald Trump and the GOP majorities in Congress hold the levers of federal power, and Trump chose the tariff politics now being sold as industrial renewal. When a president markets self-inflicted volatility as a manufacturing strategy, he is not confused. He is governing through disruption and demanding credit for the wreckage’s opposite.

The layoffs in Kearney are a consequence of that kind of rule. A company can make a reduction in force for its own reasons, but the political environment that shapes investment, production, and demand is not an innocent backdrop. It is policy. It has authors.

The Workers Absorb the Risk

More than 60 workers are being cut loose, permanently, with no bumping rights. Technicians, forklift operators, machine operators, production workers: the people who actually make the plant run are the first to pay when executives decide the numbers no longer work.

The source notes that none of the affected employees are unionized. That matters. It means there is no organized counterweight inside the workplace when the corporation chooses to shrink and the state chooses to destabilize. Workers are left to absorb decisions made far above them, then told to treat the result as an unfortunate market event.

Tariffs as Theater, Uncertainty as Policy

Trump sold tariffs as the path to a manufacturing boom. The reporting points in the opposite direction: lost manufacturing jobs, reduced factory construction, and economic volatility that makes investment harder to justify. That is the real mechanism here. Not a dramatic collapse, but a slow, predictable punishment for businesses and workers alike.

Robert Shapiro’s point is the important one. Investment depends on expectations about future demand and costs. Trump’s trade war politics poison both. The damage does not need to be theatrical to be real. It only needs to make planning impossible.

The Misdirection Machine

The article’s weakest move is how easily it drifts into “the economy” as if it were a force of nature instead of a field shaped by power. That softens responsibility. It makes the layoffs feel like weather hitting the heartland, rather than the result of decisions taken by a president who promised one thing and delivered instability.

That is the familiar trick. The people with authority manufacture the conditions, then let the consequences blur into vague economic disappointment. By the time workers are handed their notices, the political class is already rehearsing excuses.

What This Pattern Reveals

This is the deeper logic of Trump-era economic politics: claim to speak for workers while creating conditions that weaken their leverage, narrow their options, and increase their dependence. Praise manufacturing while undermining the stability manufacturing needs. Celebrate the “heartland” while treating the people who live there as collateral.

The story is not just that layoffs are arriving in a Trump-voting region. It is that Republican power keeps trying to turn organized harm into accidental hardship. The governing style is the message: reward the top, destabilize the bottom, and call the result prosperity.
