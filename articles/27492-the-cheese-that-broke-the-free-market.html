The Cheese That Broke the Free Market

By Paulo Santos

How a humble Brazilian culinary innovation exposes the contradictions of capitalism, protectionism, regulation, and the relentless pursuit of corporate profitability.

There is something almost comical about using a glass of requeijão to challenge the foundations of modern capitalism.

And yet, here we are.

I recently came across a Brazilian video titled “Por Que o Requeijão Só Existe no Brasil?” (“Why Does Requeijão Only Exist in Brazil?”). What initially appeared to be an interesting documentary about Brazilian culinary history turned out to contain the ingredients for a much broader economic discussion.

For those unfamiliar with it, Brazilian requeijão is a smooth, creamy dairy product made by melting fresh curds with dairy fat and emulsifying salts. It is neither American cream cheese nor the Portuguese product that shares its name. It has its own texture, flavor, manufacturing process, and culinary applications.

In Brazil, it is practically a household institution. We spread it on bread, put it on pizzas, stuff pastries with it, incorporate it into sauces, and use it in countless other dishes.

It is relatively inexpensive, versatile, commercially successful, and technologically straightforward to manufacture.

In other words, it is precisely the kind of product that free-market capitalism should enthusiastically embrace.

So why hasn't it conquered the world?

And why, in the country where it succeeded, are manufacturers increasingly interested in selling us something that merely looks like it?

When Necessity Was the Mother of Innovation

The history of Brazilian requeijão begins not in a corporate boardroom but in the practical difficulties of agricultural life.

Before refrigeration became widely available, preserving milk in Brazil's tropical climate was a considerable challenge. Rural communities developed techniques to transform perishable milk into products that could be stored and transported more easily.

Fresh curds were separated from the whey, washed, and cooked with dairy fat, producing what would become traditional Brazilian requeijão, including the firmer varieties known as requeijão de corte.

The objective was straightforward: make better use of an available resource and prevent waste.

There was no marketing department attempting to manufacture demand. There was no venture capital firm demanding exponential growth. There was a problem, and people developed a solution.

In 1911, the Italian immigrant couple Mário and Isaíra Silvestrini created Catupiry in Lambari, Minas Gerais, helping establish another chapter in the history of Brazilian dairy products.

Decades later, in Poços de Caldas, businessman Moacyr de Carvalho Dias, affectionately known as Xixo, and his team developed the creamy requeijão that Brazilians recognize today.

Accounts of its development describe hundreds of experiments culminating in the celebrated formula number 606.

The objective was not to imitate an existing American or European product. It was to create something with its own characteristics, using the resources and knowledge available locally.

And it worked.

Chemistry in the Service of Consumers

The technological challenge was not insignificant.

Milk proteins, particularly casein, form structures influenced by calcium. Heating cheese does not automatically transform it into a stable, homogeneous cream. Under unsuitable conditions, water and fat separate, leaving behind an unpleasant mass.

Emulsifying salts such as sodium citrate and phosphates help modify those protein structures, allowing the proteins to retain water and emulsify fat more effectively.

European cheese manufacturers had already employed this chemistry in processed cheeses. Brazilian manufacturers adapted it to fresh curds, creating a different kind of product.

The result was a mild, creamy, spreadable cheese that did not require months of maturation.

This matters economically.

Traditional aged cheeses require time, controlled storage conditions, substantial quantities of milk, and capital that remains immobilized while the product matures.

Requeijão, by contrast, can be manufactured through a much shorter production cycle. Its higher moisture content also contributes to a greater finished-product yield, although the dairy fat added during manufacturing must be included in any honest comparison of production costs.

None of this means that requeijão is inherently superior to Parmesan, Gruyère, or Cheddar. They are different products serving different culinary purposes.

But where a creamy, spreadable dairy product is desirable, requeijão represents a compelling alternative.

It is an example of chemistry and industrial engineering being used to deliver something genuinely useful to consumers.

Remember that distinction. We will return to it.

The Packaging That Refused to Become Garbage

There was another innovation associated with the commercialization of requeijão: its packaging.

Instead of treating the container as something destined for the garbage, manufacturers sold the product in glass cups that could subsequently be used as ordinary drinking glasses.

Generations of Brazilians grew up drinking water, juice, coffee, and other beverages from former requeijão containers.

Long before corporations discovered that they could put green leaves on their logos and call themselves environmentally responsible, Brazilian households were practicing a perfectly functional form of packaging reuse.

The consumer bought a dairy product and received a reusable glass as part of the transaction.

No sustainability subscription.

No premium environmental surcharge.

No corporate campaign explaining how buying more products would somehow save the planet.

Just a useful container that remained useful after fulfilling its original purpose.

The packaging delivered value beyond the contents.

It was not necessarily the most profitable arrangement imaginable for the manufacturer, but it was certainly a practical one for the consumer.

The Free Market, in Theory

According to the familiar narrative of free-market capitalism, a business identifies an opportunity, develops a product, and introduces it to consumers.

If the product is good, affordable, and useful, consumers buy it. Competitors recognize the opportunity and develop alternatives. Competition encourages improvements, reduces prices, and rewards innovation.

Everybody wins.

Brazilian requeijão appears to satisfy many of these conditions.

It offers potential manufacturing efficiencies, a short production cycle, multiple culinary applications, and demonstrated consumer acceptance in a substantial domestic market.

One would expect international dairy companies to recognize the opportunity, introduce the product into their markets, and let consumers decide whether they like it.

After all, isn't that supposed to be the entire point of a free market?

Instead, outside Brazil, requeijão remains largely a specialty product.

Brazilian expatriates frequently seek it out in stores catering to their communities, sometimes paying considerably more than they would at home.

Having lived in the United States for years, I am familiar with the peculiar experience of discovering that something entirely ordinary in one country can become an expensive specialty product in another.

But requeijão is particularly interesting because there is no obvious technological reason why American or European manufacturers could not produce it themselves.

The chemistry does not cease to function when it crosses the equator.

American cows are not fundamentally incompatible with Brazilian dairy engineering.

The manufacturing equipment is not a closely guarded national secret.

In fact, as far back as June 2000, Pesquisa FAPESP reported that a dairy company in Houston, Texas, had entered into an agreement with Brazil's Federal University of Viçosa to establish a production line for Brazilian dairy products, including requeijão.

The technology was available. Someone had already recognized the commercial opportunity.

And yet, more than two decades later, requeijão has not become an ordinary fixture of American supermarkets.

When Reality Doesn't Fit the Regulatory Schema

Part of the explanation may lie in the regulatory systems governing food production and international trade.

The Brazilian video argues that requeijão falls between established American categories of dairy products.

There is some substance to this argument, although the situation is more nuanced than the video suggests.

American food regulations establish specific standards of identity for numerous products, including cream cheese, processed cheese, and processed cheese spreads.

These standards define permissible ingredients, manufacturing characteristics, moisture content, fat content, and other requirements.

Brazilian requeijão does not necessarily fit neatly into these categories.

The traditional American standard for pasteurized processed cheese spread, for example, establishes a maximum moisture content of 60%, whereas the Brazilian technical regulation allows creamy requeijão to contain up to 65%.

The Brazilian manufacturing process also differs from conventional processed cheese manufacturing.

As a software developer, I recognize this problem immediately.

Imagine designing a database to accommodate every product currently available in a particular market.

You create tables, categories, constraints, validation rules, and relationships.

Then someone arrives with a perfectly legitimate product that your database cannot accommodate.

The product is not defective.

The schema is incomplete.

Unfortunately, institutions sometimes respond to this situation by demanding that reality conform to their schema rather than updating the schema to accommodate reality.

That is not necessarily malicious. Regulatory frameworks are created at particular moments in history to address particular circumstances. They inevitably reflect the products, technologies, and commercial practices familiar to their authors.

But when those frameworks are unnecessarily difficult to navigate or update, they can become obstacles to subsequent innovation.

It is worth emphasizing that American regulations do provide mechanisms for selling foods under appropriate descriptive names when no established standard applies.

Requeijão is not categorically prohibited merely because it cannot be called cream cheese.

The problem, therefore, is not a demonstrated legal impossibility. It is the potential accumulation of classification difficulties, compliance costs, unfamiliar terminology, commercial uncertainty, and barriers to distribution.

Each obstacle might be manageable individually.

Together, they can make introducing a new product considerably less attractive.

Protectionism: The Free Market With an Asterisk

Then there is the less romantic reality of international agricultural trade.

The United States, despite its historical enthusiasm for preaching the virtues of free markets, maintains tariff-rate quotas for several categories of imported dairy products.

These mechanisms allow specified quantities of products to enter under preferential tariff conditions, with different rates applying beyond those quantities.

Other agricultural markets, including the European Union, also maintain their own systems of trade restrictions and agricultural protections.

Some restrictions serve legitimate sanitary purposes. Others serve economic objectives, including protecting domestic producers from foreign competition.

Those are not interchangeable justifications.

Preventing contaminated food from entering a country is consumer protection.

Making a safe, competitive foreign product artificially expensive to protect an established domestic industry is protectionism.

One addresses a public health risk. The other addresses a commercial threat.

And that is where the ideological contradiction becomes apparent.

If international competition is supposedly good for consumers, why should an efficient foreign product face artificially imposed disadvantages merely because domestic manufacturers might find it inconvenient?

If a Brazilian manufacturer can deliver an affordable, desirable dairy product to American consumers, the theoretical free market should welcome the competition.

The actual market, however, is shaped by trade policy, historical relationships, established industries, distribution networks, and political influence.

To be clear, the existence of dairy protectionism does not establish that requeijão specifically has been targeted by domestic manufacturers or their lobbyists. Demonstrating that would require evidence about its precise tariff classification, import conditions, and relevant industry activities.

But the broader mechanisms are real.

And they complicate the comforting fiction that international markets are simply neutral arenas in which the best products compete on equal terms.

A market does not need to formally prohibit competition to make competition economically unattractive.

The Market Cannot Reject What It Has Never Been Offered

Another possible explanation is consumer preference.

Americans are accustomed to cream cheese. Europeans have their own established dairy traditions. Introducing an unfamiliar Brazilian product would require marketing, distribution, refrigerated shelf space, and consumer education.

Those are legitimate commercial considerations.

But there is a circularity here that deserves attention.

Manufacturers may decline to introduce requeijão because consumers are unfamiliar with it. Consumers remain unfamiliar with requeijão because manufacturers decline to introduce it.

Eventually, its absence from the market is interpreted as evidence that there is no demand for it.

But there is an important difference between consumers rejecting a product and consumers never being given a meaningful opportunity to choose it.

The market cannot discover consumer preferences when the available choices are determined in advance by manufacturers and distributors.

Requeijão's success in Brazil does not guarantee that it would succeed elsewhere. Cultural preferences are real, and not every successful domestic product translates into an international success.

Nevertheless, the Brazilian experience demonstrates that the product is capable of achieving widespread consumer appeal.

Its international potential remains a commercial proposition worth testing, not something that can be dismissed merely because it is unfamiliar.

Meanwhile, Back in Brazil…

Here is where the story becomes particularly absurd.

While a genuinely innovative and relatively affordable Brazilian dairy product struggles to achieve mainstream international distribution, manufacturers in Brazil have discovered another opportunity.

They can make something cheaper than requeijão.

Not by improving the manufacturing process.

Not necessarily by reducing energy consumption, improving logistics, or developing a more efficient method of utilizing milk.

But by replacing some of the ingredients that make requeijão what it is.

Dairy fat can be substituted with cheaper vegetable fat. Starches can be employed to thicken mixtures and reproduce the appearance of a creamy dairy product.

The resulting product may resemble requeijão.

It may be packaged like requeijão.

It may occupy the same supermarket shelf as requeijão.

It may even feature photographs of the same appetizing bread, pizza, or pastry.

But it is not necessarily requeijão.

Brazil's Portaria 359 of September 4, 1997, implementing a Mercosur technical standard, defines the identity and composition of requeijão and its varieties.

Products containing ingredients that place them outside the applicable standard cannot legitimately be marketed under the same unqualified product designation.

Consequently, consumers encounter products described as mistura de requeijão e amido, especialidade láctea, or cobertura culinária, among other designations.

There is nothing inherently wrong with manufacturing such alternatives.

A properly identified, affordable substitute might be useful to someone operating on a limited budget. Different formulations may also have legitimate culinary applications.

The problem begins when the marketing depends on the consumer failing to appreciate the distinction.

When the packaging communicates “requeijão” more effectively than the legally required product designation communicates “something else,” the manufacturer may technically comply with labeling requirements while undermining their very purpose.

The label informs.

The packaging misdirects.

And the consumer is expected to reconcile the contradiction.

Two Uses of the Same Science

Consider the difference between the original innovation and the subsequent imitation.

The original manufacturers employed chemistry to transform agricultural resources into a genuinely new product.

A manufacturer producing a cheaper imitation may employ chemistry to reproduce the appearance and certain functional characteristics of that product while reducing its manufacturing cost.

Both require technical knowledge.

Both involve experimentation.

Both may be described in a corporate presentation as innovation.

But they are not necessarily delivering the same kind of value.

In the first case, reducing costs through technological innovation can benefit consumers by making a desirable product more affordable.

In the second, reducing costs by substituting ingredients can benefit consumers if the savings are passed on, the alternatives are clearly identified, and buyers understand the trade-offs.

However, when those substitutions primarily increase margins while marketing preserves the perceived value of the original product, the economic objective is different.

The company is no longer competing primarily by making a better product.

It is competing by making a less expensive product sufficiently resemble an existing one.

There is a fundamental difference between engineering a better product and engineering a cheaper product that consumers will mistake for a better one.

The former creates value.

The latter, when built on misleading consumer expectations, extracts it.

The Supermarket Has Become a Terms of Service Agreement

Requeijão is hardly an isolated example.

Brazilian consumers have become increasingly familiar with products that resemble condensed milk but are actually dairy mixtures.

Elsewhere, consumers encounter chocolate-like confectionery, cheese substitutes, frozen desserts that do not qualify as ice cream, and fruit-flavored beverages containing remarkably little fruit.

Again, the mere existence of these alternatives is not the problem.

The problem is the growing necessity of conducting a miniature forensic investigation every time we go grocery shopping.

What precisely is this product?

What ingredients have been replaced?

Has its composition changed since the last time I purchased it?

Is this the same product in a redesigned package, or a different product in a deliberately familiar package?

What does the small print say?

The supermarket has become a physical manifestation of a software Terms of Service agreement.

The seller presents an attractive proposition, while the actual nature of the transaction is buried in information that the buyer is technically able, but not always practically equipped, to examine.

And when the consumer discovers that the product is not what they expected, the response is predictable:

“You should have read the label.”

Apparently, the burden of maintaining an honest commercial transaction belongs primarily to the party with the least information about the product.

The manufacturer knows exactly what it is selling.

The consumer is expected to figure it out.

Economists call this information asymmetry.

The rest of us might reasonably call it a lousy shopping experience.

The Contradiction at the Heart of the Market

Put these two sides of the requeijão story together.

Outside Brazil, a genuine innovation may encounter regulatory friction, trade barriers, unfamiliar consumer preferences, and distribution systems that discourage its introduction.

Inside Brazil, where the innovation has already earned widespread consumer recognition, manufacturers can exploit that recognition to market cheaper substitutes.

One side of the market can discourage a new product from competing against established ones.

The other can reward manufacturers for exploiting the reputation of an established product.

Neither outcome necessarily maximizes consumer welfare.

Both, however, can be consistent with rational corporate behavior.

And that is the systemic problem.

Corporations are not inherently rewarded for producing the best possible product. They are rewarded for producing commercially successful products.

Sometimes those objectives align.

Sometimes they do not.

A company can create something genuinely useful and make a healthy profit from it. It can also improve its profitability by reducing quality, limiting competition, exploiting consumer inertia, or taking advantage of information asymmetries.

The financial statement does not contain a separate column distinguishing profits earned through genuine innovation from profits earned through the exploitation of consumer confusion.

Revenue is revenue.

Profit is profit.

The balance sheet is remarkably indifferent to how either was obtained.

This does not mean that capitalism cannot produce innovation. Requeijão itself is evidence that commercial enterprise can accomplish exactly that.

It means that innovation is not the only behavior that markets reward.

Nor is consumer welfare the automatic consequence of every commercially rational decision.

Regulation Is Not the Enemy

It would be tempting to conclude that the solution is simply to eliminate regulations and allow the free market to operate without interference.

But the requeijão case demonstrates why that conclusion is inadequate.

The same kind of regulatory mechanism that can become an obstacle to introducing unfamiliar products can also protect consumers against misleading substitutions.

Brazil's standard of identity gives the word “requeijão” a meaning that manufacturers cannot legitimately redefine at their convenience.

Without such a standard, a manufacturer could potentially replace substantial portions of the original ingredients and continue selling the resulting product under the same designation, subject only to general labeling and consumer protection rules.

The consumer would have even less certainty about what they were purchasing.

The appropriate distinction is not between regulation and deregulation.

It is between regulation that serves a legitimate public purpose and regulation that creates unnecessary barriers or primarily serves established commercial interests.

A sensible regulatory framework would accommodate genuine innovation without compromising food safety, facilitate fair competition, require meaningful disclosure, and prevent manufacturers from exploiting consumer confusion.

It would protect the consumer's ability to choose, rather than merely protect the manufacturer's ability to sell.

That distinction should be fundamental to any discussion of economic freedom.

Freedom for the seller is not the entirety of a free market.

The buyer must also have the freedom to make a genuinely informed decision.

A Glass Half Empty

In the middle of the twentieth century, Brazilian dairy manufacturers experimented with hundreds of formulations to create a product that was affordable, versatile, and appealing to consumers.

They packaged it in a container that remained useful after the product was consumed.

They combined agricultural knowledge, industrial chemistry, entrepreneurship, and practical engineering to create something that became part of everyday Brazilian life.

That is what economic innovation can look like.

Today, the same product provides an opportunity to examine a very different set of economic incentives: the protection of established markets, the difficulty of introducing unfamiliar products, the exploitation of consumer recognition, and the pursuit of margins through ingredient substitution.

The irony is almost perfect.

Brazilian manufacturers once used chemistry to transform an agricultural limitation into a valuable product.

Now, chemistry can be used to transform cheaper ingredients into something that resembles that product.

Meanwhile, consumers elsewhere may never encounter the original at a price that would allow it to compete fairly against their established alternatives.

The free market promises that better ideas will compete, consumers will choose, and innovation will prevail.

The actual market offers no such guarantees.

It rewards whatever generates an acceptable financial return, whether that means creating something better, defending an existing market, or making something worse without losing too many customers.

The humble glass of requeijão illustrates the distinction beautifully.

Once, it represented the ingenuity of people trying to make something useful out of what they had.

Today, it also represents the ingenuity of businesses trying to discover how much they can remove from a product before consumers stop buying it.

And we are still expected to call both processes innovation.

That is the systemic error.
