A secret new court with mind-blowing powers has been quietly unleashed by Trump

The Secret Court Is the Policy

Deportation Without Disclosure

Nazira Haji Zada, a 47-year-old lawful permanent resident, was deported to Afghanistan without being charged with a crime or permitted to examine the evidence against her. Her lawyers could not inspect it either. Attorney General Todd Blanche nevertheless declared the proceeding “a win for national security and the rule of law.”

That is enough summary. The political fact is simpler: the government imposed an extraordinary punishment while withholding the case needed to contest it.

Congress Built the Machine

Donald Trump’s administration activated the Alien Terrorist Removal Court, but it did not invent it. Congress authorized the tribunal amid the panic following the Oklahoma City bombing, constructing a mechanism earlier presidents declined to use.

That division of responsibility matters. Congress supplied the machinery; the administration chose to turn it on. The result was not a bureaucratic accident or an unfortunate gap in procedure. It followed the court’s design: a judge reviews classified evidence privately, ordinary federal evidence rules do not apply, and the accused cannot seek suppression of material allegedly obtained through illegal surveillance or searches.

A security-cleared attorney may inspect the secret file but cannot disclose its contents to the person whose future depends on it. The appearance of representation remains. The substance—an informed defense—does not.

“National Security” as an Answer-Proof Claim

The government may possess serious evidence against Zada. The secrecy of the process makes that impossible for the public to assess—and nearly impossible for her to challenge. That is not a minor transparency problem. It transfers decisive power from an adversarial court process to executive assertions that have been insulated from contradiction.

Blanche’s invocation of the “rule of law” reverses the meaning of the phrase. Law here does not restrain state power; it furnishes state power with procedures for avoiding scrutiny. The administration can claim danger, conceal its proof and then cite the resulting deportation as validation of the original claim.

The accusation becomes self-authenticating. The government’s choice to target someone is treated as evidence that the target deserved it.

The Weaker Actor Is Not the Mystery

Coverage of proceedings like this can become preoccupied with whether the accused might secretly be dangerous. That framing places the burden on the person with the least information and the least institutional power. It asks Zada to disprove evidence she cannot see while treating the government’s refusal to disclose that evidence as an unavoidable complication.

The central question is not whether officials know something the public does not. They almost certainly do. The question is why their undisclosed judgment should be sufficient to exile a lawful permanent resident.

National-security cases can require protection of sources and methods. That necessity does not automatically justify a process engineered to prevent meaningful rebuttal. Treating secrecy and due process as opposites is a political choice—one that reliably benefits the institution holding the files, the detention power and the aircraft.

Exceptional Power Does Not Stay Exceptional

The source places Zada’s removal alongside mass immigration detention, the transfer of men to an El Salvador prison, the detention of U.S. citizens and unexplained removals from federal service. Those episodes differ in law and circumstance. The pattern alleged across them is nevertheless recognizable: executive institutions act first, provide vague or inaccessible reasons later, and force individuals to fight from custody, exile or unemployment.

Immigrants are the easiest population on which to normalize that arrangement. They have fewer political defenders, and the word “terrorism” can make procedural abuse sound like vigilance. Once secrecy itself becomes proof of seriousness, officials gain an exceptionally useful power: they can impose consequences without accepting the reputational or legal risks of making a public case.

That is authoritarian practice in administrative form. It does not require theatrical declarations. It requires institutions willing to process people while suppressing the information that could expose error, prejudice or misconduct.

Impunity With a Case Number

The Alien Terrorist Removal Court gives arbitrary power the appearance of judicial regularity. There is a judge, a statute and a lawyer. Yet the accused cannot confront the decisive evidence, the public cannot evaluate it, and the government controls nearly every relevant informational advantage.

That is the larger political lesson. Institutional cruelty rarely arrives labeled as lawlessness. More often, lawmakers authorize an exception during a panic, executives expand it when politically useful, and officials point to the procedure they inherited as proof that the outcome is legitimate.

Zada’s deportation established that this dormant apparatus can be used. The precedent is not merely that one lawful permanent resident was removed. It is that the federal government can conceal an accusation, prevent an effective answer and call the completed punishment the rule of law.
