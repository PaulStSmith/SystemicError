Trump's admin fires a desperate Election Day salvage shot

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 4, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the Trump Justice Department was preparing to send roughly 20 target letters in a criminal investigation involving the campaign of Alaska Senate candidate Dan J. Sullivan. Citing NBC News and unnamed sources, it says the department had already issued 120 subpoenas. A target letter identifies someone as a focus of an investigation; it does not mean that person will be charged.

The action followed a separate Justice Department lawsuit arguing that Sullivan’s presence on the ballot could confuse voters because Republican Sen. Dan S. Sullivan was also running. A court dismissed the administration’s attempt to block certification for lack of standing. The Alaska Supreme Court had already addressed the naming issue and prescribed how the candidates would be distinguished on the ballot.

AlterNet does not establish exactly when the target letters were sent, who received them, what suspected crimes underlie the investigation, or whether charges followed. It reports that the civil litigation continued separately and that the Justice Department filed a notice indicating an appeal, though legal commentator Chris Geidner questioned whether that notice was properly drafted.

The power sits with Justice

Dan J. Sullivan did not control federal prosecutors, subpoena authority, or the machinery that can place a criminal investigation over an election. The Justice Department did. According to the reporting, the U.S. Attorney’s Office for Alaska and officials from the Civil Rights Division participated in the investigation.

That imbalance is the political core of the story. A candidate can defend himself in court and answer subpoenas. The executive branch can compel records, designate investigative targets, litigate over certification, and force everyone else to spend time and money responding. Even without charges, that power can reshape an election’s closing atmosphere.

Confusion was not the unresolved problem

The administration’s stated concern was ballot confusion between two men with similar names. But Alaska’s courts had already considered that problem and imposed rules for distinguishing them. The federal government nevertheless tried to turn the issue into grounds for blocking certification if the Republican lost.

That is not merely concern about clear ballot design. It is an attempt, as described by AlterNet, to make certification conditional on a preferred electoral outcome. The weaker actor—the candidate whose lawful ballot presence allegedly created confusion—became the focus, while the institution escalating a settled naming dispute wielded the real coercive power.

The article outruns some of its evidence

AlterNet calls the Justice Department “politicized” and presents the investigation as an Election Day salvage attempt. The sequence it reports supports serious scrutiny: extensive subpoenas, prospective target letters, certification litigation, and an election-related dispute already adjudicated by Alaska’s highest court.

But the article does not establish the letters’ precise timing or reveal the criminal evidence. Nor does it prove that every investigative step was ordered to rescue the Republican candidate. That motive is the article’s inference, not a demonstrated fact. The strongest indictment rests on documented institutional conduct: federal officials escalated an already-litigated ballot issue and sought leverage over certification.

Process can become punishment

The absence of charges does not make 120 subpoenas and approximately 20 target letters politically weightless. Investigations impose legal costs, generate damaging headlines, and allow insinuation to travel faster than adjudicated facts. When deployed around an election, prosecutorial process can create consequences before any court determines that a crime occurred.

The reported departure from the Justice Department’s traditional caution near elections matters for precisely that reason. Those norms exist because prosecutors possess powers that candidates cannot match and because an investigation’s public shadow can affect voters even when it produces no conviction—or no charge at all.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is executive power converting an electoral loss contingency into a legal controversy: if the favored candidate loses, challenge certification; if courts reject the theory, appeal; alongside that litigation, continue a broad criminal investigation carrying its own pressure and stigma.

Institutional abuse does not require polished lawyering or a successful courtroom result. It can operate through repeated escalation, forcing weaker actors to answer the government while officials present their own intervention as a response to disorder. Here, the reported disorder was two similar names. The consequential decision was the Justice Department’s choice to turn that manageable ballot issue into federal litigation and prosecutorial pressure.
