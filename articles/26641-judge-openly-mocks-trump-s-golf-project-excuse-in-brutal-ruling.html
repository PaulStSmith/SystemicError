Judge openly mocks Trump's golf project excuse in brutal ruling

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that U.S. District Judge Ana Reyes rejected the Trump administration’s claim that a rushed tree-removal operation at Washington’s East Potomac Golf Links was merely routine maintenance based on a 2019 preservation report. On the limited record before her, Reyes concluded that officials accelerated the work because President Donald Trump wanted haste on his proposed redesign of the public course.

The operation began after senior Interior Department official Gregory Wischer instructed the National Park Service on October 5 to act quickly. Approval followed the next day, and crews ultimately removed about 430 healthy, noninvasive trees while marking nearly 300 more. Reyes barred additional cutting through October 26, required advance notice before other irreversible alterations, and said she planned to question senior Interior and Park Service officials.

The administration maintains that the work was separate from Trump’s redevelopment plan and consistent with restoring the course’s historic landscape. Preservationists and two local golfers allege that officials divided the larger project into smaller pieces to avoid required scrutiny. Reyes found the challengers likely to succeed on their claim that the Park Service failed to conduct an adequate environmental review.

The power came from the top

The central fact is not that bureaucrats became unexpectedly passionate about a seven-year-old report. It is that a presidential priority moved through federal agencies with extraordinary speed.

Two Park Service officials reportedly saw no urgent need for the work. That did not prevent an Interior official from demanding action, an acting agency director from approving it the next day, or crews from starting after the government gave the court barely an afternoon’s notice. Institutional power belonged to the president and the political officials directing the agencies, not to the career personnel who questioned the urgency.

The process followed the result

The Park Service granted an initial exemption from deeper environmental review one day before cutting began. It then produced another document on Sunday, after crews had already removed far more trees than originally planned.

That sequence matters. Environmental review is supposed to inform government action before irreversible damage occurs. Here, according to the ruling, the action raced ahead while the analysis lagged behind. Whether that violated federal law remains for the litigation to determine, but the practical consequence is already fixed: hundreds of trees are gone.

Maintenance is the convenient story

The administration’s defense converts an obvious chain of command into a remarkable coincidence: officials suddenly revived a dormant recommendation just after Trump directed the Interior Department to cut trees, yet supposedly acted independently of his golf-course ambitions.

Reyes did not accept that explanation on the limited record, writing that it demanded too much coincidence. The article’s headline presents this chiefly as a judge mocking an excuse, but the sharper issue is institutional misdirection. Focusing on the wit of the ruling risks shrinking an abuse-of-process allegation into courtroom entertainment.

The weaker actors here are not the officials controlling federal land. They are the preservationists and local golfers forced to seek judicial intervention after machinery was already moving. Treating the dispute as a clash between environmental objections and routine upkeep would obscure who possessed the authority to act first and impose consequences before review could catch up.

Delay rewards executive haste

The court can stop future cutting temporarily. It cannot restore the roughly 430 trees already removed. That asymmetry creates a powerful incentive: move quickly, present opponents and judges with a partially completed project, then litigate over what remains.

The reported conduct reveals how procedural safeguards can be defeated without formally abolishing them. An administration can compress timelines, invoke an old document, narrow the stated scope of a project and complete irreversible work before challengers obtain meaningful review. The legal question may concern environmental compliance; the political technique is government by fait accompli.

Public institutions, private priorities

A president’s preference for a “championship-ready” golf course is not self-executing. It becomes public policy only when officials use government authority, agency staff and control over federal land to advance it.

That is the larger pattern exposed here: personal priorities travel downward as institutional commands, while responsibility is dispersed among approvals, exemptions and administrative paperwork. When courts finally identify the chain, the damage may already be part of the landscape. The system’s failure is not confusion about who acted. It is a structure in which power can act immediately and accountability arrives after the trees fall.
