Trump damns NYT 'to hell' in late-night tantrum over 'doctored' photo of his resort

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Paulo SantosOctober 7, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that President Donald Trump spent Monday night accusing The New York Times of doctoring a photograph of Trump National Doral Miami. He said the image made his property look “shoddy” and posted a brighter picture that he claimed showed the real facade.

But the two images were not the same photograph: they showed different landscaping, fountains, framing, and lighting. The comparison therefore did not establish that the Times altered its image. Newsweek had sought comment from the newspaper and photographer Scott McIntyre.

The photograph accompanied reporting on Trump’s plan to hold the G20 summit at his own private club. According to the account cited by RawStory, Trump made at least $2.2 billion last year, accepted a luxury jet from Qatar that the government then spent hundreds of millions of dollars retrofitting, and obtained relief from back taxes that could exceed $100 million.

The president is not the injured party

The institutional power here belongs to Trump. He is the president, commands the federal executive branch, owns the resort under discussion, and possesses a mass political platform from which to attack the journalists covering him.

Yet his posts recast him as the victim of a photograph. The alleged offense was not a government action against him, a legal judgment, or even a demonstrated falsification. It was an unflattering image of a business he owns.

That inversion matters. Trump’s grievance asks the public to treat scrutiny of presidential self-enrichment as persecution of a private businessman. The presidency and the property are kept rhetorically separate when accountability is at issue, then fused when presidential attention can advertise and defend the property.

A brighter picture is not evidence

Trump’s rebuttal did not answer the underlying reporting. It substituted resort marketing imagery for a news photograph and treated visual polish as proof of journalistic misconduct.

Because the pictures were taken from different perspectives under different conditions, placing them side by side establishes almost nothing about whether either was altered. RawStory’s description identifies visible differences; it does not report evidence that the Times doctored its photograph.

The accusation nevertheless serves a political function. It moves the argument from whether a president should direct an international summit toward his own club to whether the club was photographed attractively enough. A conflict involving public authority and private revenue becomes a dispute over curb appeal.

The missing scandal is organized opposition

Trump’s earlier attempt to place the 2019 G7 summit at Doral collapsed after Republican backlash. RawStory says the reported G20 plan has drawn almost no organized protest.

That contrast exposes the enabling structure. Trump can propose using presidential power to route a major diplomatic event through his own property because the institutions capable of imposing political costs are apparently imposing few. The consequential decision is not merely his proposal. It is also the choice by political allies and organized interests to treat it as tolerable.

Paul Rosenzweig’s observation that Trump has “defined deviancy down” describes the result: conduct becomes normalized not because its character changes, but because repetition exhausts resistance. What once produced intraparty backlash returns in an environment trained to regard each new conflict as background noise.

The outrage is the diversion

The central issue is not whether Doral looks beautiful, shabby, dark, or bright. It is whether the presidency is being used in ways that benefit the president’s private interests.

Trump’s attack supplies an easier spectacle: an angry post, an insult, and an unsupported allegation against the press. Coverage can then revolve around the tantrum while the underlying claims—billions in income, tax relief potentially exceeding $100 million, the Qatari jet and its retrofit costs, and the proposed summit venue—receive less sustained attention.

That is not simple confusion. It is a reliable form of misdirection, whatever Trump’s private motive: attack the messenger, narrow the dispute to an emotionally vivid detail, and force critics to litigate the detail while institutional power operates elsewhere.

Power survives through lowered expectations

The larger pattern is normalization through saturation. A president’s private business interests, foreign gifts, tax treatment, and control over government events should invite concentrated scrutiny from Congress, political allies, and the press. Instead, the story can be displaced by an argument over which photograph makes a resort look nicer.

Authoritarian political habits do not require every accusation to succeed. They benefit when power can generate endless false or unsupported controversies, compel institutions to answer them, and make the underlying abuse seem like merely one more item in the churn.

The systemic failure is therefore larger than Trump’s late-night posts. It is an accountability structure so weakened by repetition and political cowardice that a president can redirect attention from the use of public office toward the lighting on his hotel—and reasonably expect much of the political system to follow.
