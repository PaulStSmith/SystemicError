'Cowardly' MAGA candidate derided after 'pathetic' campaign move: 'Embarrassing'

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosOctober 3, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe candidate made the choicePower without accountabilityMockery obscures the substanceA politics of reversible branding

What happened

RawStory reports that Arizona Republican state Rep. Alexander Kolodin removed Donald Trump’s endorsement from the front page of his campaign website while running for secretary of state. A Trump quote displayed there on July 19—calling Kolodin an attorney and “a patriot”—was gone by September 22, replaced by “Protect every vote.”

Democratic state Rep. Oscar De Los Santos called the change cowardly, while journalist Brahm Resnik documented it and other critics mocked Kolodin online. The report does not establish why Kolodin changed the page, and it includes no response from him. It places the move alongside allegations that Florida candidate Byron Donalds and several other Republicans have similarly removed Trump references from campaign materials.

The candidate made the choice

Kolodin holds the relevant agency here. His campaign controls the website, chose to feature Trump’s endorsement and later chose not to feature it. The available evidence establishes a deliberate change in presentation, though not the campaign’s reason for making it.

That distinction matters. Kolodin has not, based on this reporting, renounced Trump or explained a political disagreement. He has merely stopped advertising the endorsement so prominently. The record therefore supports a conclusion about strategic omission, not ideological conversion.

Power without accountability

Trump’s endorsement carried enough value for Kolodin to showcase it when his campaign considered the association useful. Removing it later does not erase that alliance; it changes what voters encounter first.

This is a familiar form of political insulation: accept the benefits of affiliation inside the party, then soften the branding before a broader electorate. The candidate retains the endorsement while attempting to control its visibility. Voters are offered a cleaner slogan—“Protect every vote”—without an explanation of what changed or why.

Mockery obscures the substance

RawStory builds much of its piece around insults from Kolodin’s opponents: “pathetic,” “cowardly,” “embarrassing,” and “Trump stank.” It also revives a 2020 traffic-stop anecdote about an alcoholic beverage found near the scene. That material supplies ridicule, not political understanding.

The consequential question is not whether Kolodin can be humiliated online. It is whether a candidate for statewide office believes voters should evaluate the alliances he cultivated. By centering taunts, the article turns an accountability issue into social-media theater and gives the campaign an easier grievance to answer than the website change itself.

A politics of reversible branding

The broader pattern alleged in the report—Republican candidates benefiting from Trump’s support and later reducing his visibility—shows how party loyalty can become selectively legible. The affiliation remains available for one audience and obscured for another.

That is not confusion. It is message management. The systemic failure comes when coverage fixates on who delivered the sharpest insult instead of documenting how candidates repackage their political commitments without accounting for them. Power survives that bargain; only the branding changes.
