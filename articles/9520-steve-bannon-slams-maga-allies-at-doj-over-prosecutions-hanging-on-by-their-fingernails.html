Steve Bannon slams MAGA allies at DOJ over prosecutions: 'Hanging on by their fingernails'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 17, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Unholy Alliance: Bannon, Trump, and the DOJ

Steve Bannon arrives for a memorial service for Charlie Kirk in Glendale, Arizona. REUTERS/Carlos Barria

One of President Donald Trump’s former advisors slammed some of his allies in the Department of Justice on Wednesday, arguing that they are impeding Trump’s prosecution of his political enemies.

Steve Bannon, who served as a senior adviser during Trump’s first term, joined MAGA commentator Tucker Carlson on “The Tucker Carlson Interview” on Wednesday to discuss Trump’s efforts to “save the Republic.” Bannon said DOJ officials like Attorney General Pam Bondi, FBI Director Kash Patel, and Deputy FBI Director Dan Bongino need to “move faster” to prosecute Trump’s enemies before time runs out.

“I love Kash and I love Dan Bongino. Kash is like a brother to me,” Bannon said. “But they’re two guys hanging on by their fingernails. The same with Pam and [Deputy Attorney General] Todd Blanche.”

Bannon argued that Trump’s Justice Department has not moved fast enough to prosecute the plain-clothes police officers who allegedly agitated the crowd during the January 6 insurrection.

He added that the Department of Justice also has not moved fast enough to remove lawyers who worked on “Crossfire Hurricane,” the FBI’s investigation into Trump’s ties to Russia during the 2016 campaign.

“This is why I say we need 25-30 more prosecutors,” Bannon said. “You gotta get on with it.”

Our Analysis:

The Unholy Alliance: Bannon, Trump, and the DOJ

In a spectacle that can only be described as disturbing yet unsurprising, Steve Bannon, once a linchpin in the Trump White House, has openly criticized figures within the Department of Justice (DOJ) for not acting swiftly enough against “Trump’s enemies.” His comments, made during an interview with MAGA stalwart Tucker Carlson, lay bare the dangerous politicization of the DOJ under the guise of “saving the Republic.”

The Cast of Characters: Starting with the DOJ officials named—Pam Bondi, Kash Patel, and Dan Bongino—Bannon’s lament is not about the failure of justice, but rather about the speed of vengeance. Bondi, a former Attorney General of Florida with a controversial tenure; Patel, a figure deeply involved in attempts to discredit the Russia investigation; and Bongino, a political commentator turned Deputy FBI Director in this alternate reality, are not slow bureaucrats but rather, in Bannon’s view, the last line of defense in a war against Trump’s adversaries.

The Misguided Missive: Bannon’s call for the prosecution of plain-clothes police officers for their alleged role in agitating the crowd during the January 6 insurrection is a masterclass in redirection. The insurrection, a dark day in American history, was fueled by baseless claims of election fraud propagated by Trump and his allies. Yet, Bannon’s narrative shifts blame away from those who incited violence to those who sought to contain it.

Furthermore, his criticism of the DOJ’s pace in removing lawyers from “Crossfire Hurricane,” the investigation into Trump’s ties to Russia, underscores a blatant desire to rewrite the narrative of a thoroughly investigated and substantiated concern about foreign interference in American democracy.

The Dangerous Proposal: Bannon’s assertion that the DOJ needs “25-30 more prosecutors” to expedite these actions is nothing short of alarming. It’s not a call for justice but a demand for a personal army to target political adversaries. This is not how justice systems should operate in democratic societies. The rule of law is grounded in impartiality and due process, not the whims of a president’s advisor.

The Bigger Picture: What Bannon and, by extension, Trump envision is a Department of Justice that acts as an arm of the executive to crush dissent and opposition. This is a stark departure from the DOJ’s mission to enforce the law and defend the interests of the United States according to the law; to ensure fair and impartial administration of justice for all Americans.

In the end, Bannon’s comments to Carlson are not just a lament about slow legal processes; they are a clarion call for the weaponization of the justice system. Such aspirations cannot be taken lightly, for they threaten the very foundations of American democracy: the separation of powers and the rule of law. As observers, critics, and citizens, we must remain vigilant against these insidious attempts to subvert justice for political gain. The Republic, if it is to be saved, will not endure through the prosecution of political enemies but through the steadfast adherence to democratic principles and the unwavering pursuit of true justice.
