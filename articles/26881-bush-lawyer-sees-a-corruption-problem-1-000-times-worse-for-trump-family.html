Bush lawyer sees a corruption problem '1,000 times worse' for Trump family

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 29, 20264 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that Richard Painter, George W. Bush’s former chief White House ethics lawyer, called the Trump family’s business activity “1,000 times worse” than Hunter Biden’s. He was responding to a Financial Times investigation that documented at least 18 meetings between Donald Trump Jr. or Eric Trump and foreign politicians or officials since January 2025, alongside an expansion of Trump family business interests during President Donald Trump’s second term.

Neither Trump son holds a government position. Donald Trump Jr.’s spokesperson said he tells foreign officials that he acts as a private citizen, while the Trump Organization said its business remains separate from the White House and complies with applicable ethics laws. The reporting did not establish that official favors were exchanged for business benefits, and Painter said the contents of the brothers’ conversations are unknown.

The Financial Times reported that Trump’s fortune grew by more than $2 billion last year, including more than $500 million from token sales by World Liberty Financial, the crypto venture his sons co-founded. Since Trump returned to office, his private business has announced 15 overseas projects, with five more listed in presidential ethics disclosures; foreign licensing, development and management revenue rose 40 percent, to $63 million, between 2024 and 2025.

The power is in the presidency

The central issue is not that two presidential relatives have businesses. It is that their father controls the executive branch while those businesses operate in sectors—including cryptocurrency, prediction markets and critical minerals—that can depend on federal regulation or government contracts.

Foreign officials do not need Donald Trump Jr. or Eric Trump to carry diplomatic credentials to understand the family relationship. They can see access to the president’s sons as potentially valuable even if the sons explicitly identify themselves as private citizens. That disclaimer addresses formal status; it does not erase proximity to presidential power.

The Trump Organization’s claim of separation also leaves the underlying conflict intact. A privately owned business can be organizationally distinct from the White House while its owner exercises presidential authority. The relevant question is not whether the entities share an office. It is whether presidential decisions can affect the financial interests of the president and his family.

The comparison conceals the imbalance

Painter’s comparison with Hunter Biden is politically revealing because he supported scrutiny of Hunter Biden’s conduct. But the phrase “1,000 times worse” is his judgment, not a measurable finding established by the reporting.

The more defensible distinction is institutional. Hunter Biden’s business activity drew investigations and denials of wrongdoing from both him and Joe Biden. Here, the person at the center of the family fortune is also the president, and the reported business expansion is occurring during his administration. The presidency is not incidental context; it is the source of the conflict’s scale.

Treating this merely as another story about presidential relatives would misdirect attention from the officeholder with actual authority. Donald Trump Jr. and Eric Trump conduct the meetings and manage business interests. President Trump holds the governmental power that makes access to his family potentially valuable.

Private labels do not neutralize public leverage

The family’s branding makes the relationship difficult to dismiss as accidental. Painter pointed to Donald Trump Jr.’s venture-capital firm, 1789 Capital, and a members’ club called Executive Branch. Those names do not prove an exchange of favors. They do, however, market political association as part of the product.

That is the structural danger documented here: influence can be sold without anyone issuing an explicit promise. A foreign official, investor or applicant for regulatory approval may reasonably believe that cultivating the president’s family could matter. The reporting does not show that such expectations produced official action, but the arrangement invites them while denying the public access to the conversations needed to evaluate them.

This is how opacity serves power. The absence of proof of a bargain becomes a defense, even though the president’s ownership interests, his sons’ foreign meetings and the government’s authority over relevant industries create the conditions under which an undisclosed bargain would be hardest to detect.

Corruption thrives before the receipt appears

The article is strongest when it refuses to claim more than the evidence supports: it does not establish a trade of government favors for business benefits. Its weakness is allowing the unanswered quid-pro-quo question to dominate the frame.

Public corruption is not only a completed transaction that investigators can reconstruct afterward. It also includes institutions designed so that private gain and public authority repeatedly converge without meaningful transparency. By the time a receipt appears—if one ever does—the conflict has already shaped who seeks access, what they believe access can buy and whether the public can trust decisions affecting countries where the presidential family does business.

The larger pattern is not confusion about where business ends and government begins. It is a governing arrangement that preserves formal deniability while leaving the president’s private financial interests exposed to people and governments affected by his administration’s choices. The public is asked to accept separation on assertion alone, while the family retains the money, the access and the presidency.
