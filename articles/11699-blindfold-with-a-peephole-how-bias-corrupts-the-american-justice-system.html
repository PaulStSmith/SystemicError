Blindfold with a Peephole: How Bias Corrupts the American Justice System

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 14, 20257 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The idealized image of justice is an ancient one: a woman, blindfolded, holding scales in one hand and a sword in the other. The blindfold is supposed to symbolize impartiality — the promise that the law will weigh only facts, not status, wealth, or politics. But in practice, America’s justice system has poked a peephole through that blindfold, and who it chooses to look at — and who it chooses to ignore — tells you everything about its bias.

The contrast between two recent cases makes this plain. In one, Luigi Mangione is charged with terrorism for shooting UnitedHealthcare CEO Brian Thompson. One victim, one crime, but because that victim sat atop a corporate giant in the health sector, prosecutors in New York wasted no time applying the weighty “terrorism” label. In another, Vance Boelter stands accused of killing former Minnesota House Speaker Melissa Hortman and her husband, wounding Senator John Hoffman and his wife, and compiling a hit list of seventy other public officials. Yet federal prosecutors have — so far — chosen not to bring terrorism charges.

The crimes are both heinous. The motivations, while not identical, share the essential ingredient of political or ideological targeting. But the legal framing couldn’t be more different. This disparity is not just a curiosity in the annals of criminal law — it’s a flashing warning sign that “justice” in the United States often bends to the status of the victim and the political convenience of the prosecution.

The CEO as Infrastructure

To understand why a single homicide of a CEO might be immediately treated as terrorism, you need to understand the unspoken hierarchy embedded in American law enforcement priorities. In the post-9/11 era, terrorism statutes have expanded in scope, but they’ve also been applied unevenly. When the victim is a corporate executive in a critical industry — especially one tied to national infrastructure like health care, energy, or finance — the act is seen not just as a crime against a person, but as an attack on the system itself.

Prosecutors in New York invoked a state-level terrorism statute that allows any violent crime intended “to intimidate or coerce a civilian population, influence government policy, or affect the conduct of a unit of government” to be labeled terrorism. In Mangione’s case, the theory is that targeting a CEO of a health care giant was intended to send a broader message — whether about corporate policy, healthcare access, or another grievance.

The effect of this approach is twofold: it elevates the crime in the public imagination and it reassures the elite class that the state will treat attacks on them as attacks on the state itself. This is not unique to America — many governments around the world implicitly protect the ruling or corporate elite as if they were national infrastructure. But it’s worth asking whether that protection extends equally to other categories of public servants.

The Political Assassinations Without the “Terror” Label

Contrast that with Boelter’s alleged rampage in Minnesota. The targets were not faceless civilians or random strangers; they were elected officials and their families. The attack was not spontaneous; it was premeditated, complete with a detailed target list spanning dozens of names. If the law is meant to protect democracy itself, the calculated killing of elected representatives should be the textbook definition of terrorism.

And yet, the charges so far have been murder, attempted murder, firearms violations, and stalking — all serious, some carrying potential life sentences or the death penalty, but notably absent is the formal label of “terrorism.”

The reasons given, when they’re given at all, are procedural. Federal statutes around terrorism often require proving a specific ideological motive tied to certain enumerated offenses. State statutes vary wildly. Prosecutors might argue that they’re prioritizing the most straightforward charges to secure a conviction quickly. But that legalistic explanation doesn’t erase the optics: when the political affiliation of the victims doesn’t align with those in power, the rhetorical and symbolic force of the “terrorism” label suddenly seems optional.

The Consequences of Selective Vision

Justice is not just about outcomes — it’s also about legitimacy. Two crimes can both result in convictions, but if the process reveals an underlying bias, the public’s trust erodes. If one victim’s death is treated as a national security emergency while another’s is treated as a standard homicide, the message to the public is clear: some lives are worth more protection, more outrage, more symbolic condemnation.

This disparity doesn’t just insult the dead; it damages the living. It tells political actors that violence against certain groups will not provoke the same unified, national response. It tells would-be offenders that the consequences for targeting political opponents might be less severe if the targets are on the “wrong” team. And it tells the average citizen that the blindfold over Lady Justice is more of a costume prop than a guiding principle.

Humane vs. Biased Justice

It’s important to distinguish between a humane justice system and a biased one. Humane justice means recognizing the humanity of defendants, ensuring fair trials, avoiding excessive punishment, and always keeping rehabilitation in view where possible. A humane system can be strict without being cruel, and merciful without being weak.

A biased justice system, on the other hand, distorts those ideals based on who is involved. It doesn’t just measure the crime — it measures the victim’s social or political status first, and lets that influence the entire prosecutorial approach. That’s what’s happening here: the law is being bent around the identity of the victim rather than applied evenly based on the nature of the act.

When we tolerate bias under the guise of “discretion,” we normalize selective enforcement. We create tiers of justice where CEOs and corporate leaders occupy the top rung, political allies of those in power are somewhere below, and everyone else — including political opponents — are further down the ladder.

The Illusion of Equal Protection

The Fourteenth Amendment promises equal protection under the law. But “equal” has become an aspirational marketing slogan rather than a daily operational reality. The selective use of the terrorism label is just one symptom of a deeper illness: the willingness of prosecutors and politicians to shape legal narratives based on political expedience.

In both cases — Mangione in New York and Boelter in Minnesota — the acts were more than ordinary crimes. They were symbolic, calculated, and intended to send a message beyond the immediate victims. That’s the very essence of terrorism. To call one terrorism and not the other is to admit that the term is not a legal constant but a political choice.

Why This Matters

Some might argue that the label itself doesn’t matter if the sentences are severe. But it does matter. Labels carry weight in public discourse. They influence policy, funding, and public perception. Calling something “terrorism” elevates it in the hierarchy of threats the state takes seriously. It shapes the resources devoted to prevention. It signals to society what kinds of violence are intolerable.

When that label is applied inconsistently, it undermines those very signals. It makes the public cynical about the motives of the justice system. It erodes the moral high ground needed to condemn political violence abroad. And it sets a precedent that can be weaponized in future cases, where the choice of label is determined not by the act, but by the actors.

The Path Back to Real Blind Justice

Restoring credibility to the justice system will require more than convicting high-profile offenders. It will require:

  1. Consistent Application of Definitions: If an act meets the statutory definition of terrorism, charge it as such, regardless of the victim’s social or political status.

  2. Transparency in Charging Decisions: Prosecutors should publicly explain why certain labels are used or not used, especially in politically sensitive cases.

  3. Independent Review of Political Violence Cases: Remove partisan influence from the decision-making process by having an independent body review such cases.

  4. Public Accountability: Legislators and the public must demand consistency not just in sentencing, but in the symbolic framing of crimes.

The law’s legitimacy depends not just on its capacity to punish, but on its ability to convince the public that it is fair. Right now, the selective application of the terrorism label is convincing people of the opposite.

Conclusion

The American justice system does not need to choose between being humane and being impartial — it is supposed to be both. But when it allows bias to creep in, when it treats some victims as more worthy of symbolic protection than others, it ceases to be justice at all. It becomes a political tool, one that reinforces existing power structures rather than defending the principles it was built to uphold.

If Lady Justice is to mean anything, the peephole in her blindfold has to be sewn shut. Until then, every disparity in how the law is applied — like the one between Mangione and Boelter — will serve as a reminder that in America, justice is not blind. It’s looking right at you, deciding whether you’re worth the full measure of its protection.
