Democracy as a Reward Mechanism

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 6, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There is nothing new in the latest polling showing that a majority of Americans do not trust Donald Trump’s commitment to free and fair elections. What is new—and far more revealing—is how long it took for this realization to register as something worth remarking upon.

Trump has been hostile to the very concept of democratic legitimacy since at least 2016. Not since losing. Not since January 6. Since the moment it became clear that elections could produce outcomes he did not personally control. From that point on, the pattern has been relentlessly consistent: elections are legitimate only if they validate him. Loss is fraud. Victory is provisional. Rules exist to be bent, ignored, or retroactively reinterpreted if they fail to deliver the desired result.

This is not a psychological quirk. It is a political worldview.

The Old Lie, Repeated Relentlessly

In 2016, Trump accused the Iowa caucuses of being rigged—despite winning the Republican nomination. He preemptively claimed the general election would be fraudulent. After winning, he created a sham voter-fraud commission to hunt for evidence that did not exist. When it failed, it quietly dissolved, having served its real purpose: normalizing the idea that electoral outcomes are suspect by default.

By 2020, the groundwork was already laid. Any loss could be framed not as defeat, but as theft. Courts were dismissed. Election officials were smeared. Audits were rejected unless they produced the “right” answer. When procedural avenues failed, pressure campaigns followed. When pressure failed, mob violence became the final fallback.

At no point was this subtle. At no point was it ambiguous. The throughline was clear enough to draw with a ruler: democracy is acceptable only as long as it produces the correct winner.

The Poll Isn’t the Story

Recent polling showing that a majority of Americans doubt Trump’s commitment to democracy is being treated as a revelation. It isn’t. It is a lagging indicator.

The story is not that trust is eroding. The story is that trust existed at all after years of explicit anti-democratic behavior. That millions of voters could watch the steady dismantling of electoral norms and still describe Trump as a defender of democracy says less about Trump than it does about the informational ecosystem that sustains him.

This is where analysis must abandon comforting euphemisms. This is not mere polarization. It is not “heated rhetoric.” It is the deliberate construction of an alternate reality in which all institutions that constrain power are framed as illegitimate by definition.

Democracy Reframed as a Prize

For a large segment of Trump’s base, democracy has ceased to be a process. It has become a reward mechanism.

If “our side” wins, the system worked. If “our side” loses, the system was corrupt. There is no neutral standard, no shared rulebook, no external arbiter whose judgment is accepted in advance. Legitimacy is assigned retroactively, based solely on outcome.

This inversion is fatal to democratic systems. Democracies require losers who accept loss. They require rules that bind even those who dislike the result. They require trust not in outcomes, but in procedures. Trumpism rejects all three.

The Brainwashing Question

The word “brainwashed” makes polite analysts uncomfortable. It shouldn’t.

Not because Trump voters are stupid, but because many have been subjected to a closed-loop narrative system designed to preemptively invalidate all counterevidence. Courts are corrupt. Journalists are enemies. Career civil servants are saboteurs. Election workers are criminals. Statistical reality is fake. Any institution capable of disproving the fraud narrative is itself defined as fraudulent.

In such an environment, evidence cannot persuade, because evidence is already classified as hostile propaganda. This is not ignorance. It is inoculation.

Once loyalty replaces law, facts lose their force. Once identity replaces principle, contradictions cease to matter. Trump can openly propose federal takeovers of state elections, float loyalty-tested administrators, and raid election infrastructure in search of debunked fantasies without triggering mass defection—because the movement no longer evaluates actions against democratic norms. It evaluates them against tribal benefit.

Cruelty and Control

There is a deeper element here that polite commentary often avoids: cruelty.

The erosion of democracy is not merely tolerated by Trump’s movement; it is frequently enjoyed. The idea that “the right people” might be disenfranchised, overruled, or silenced is not a bug—it is a feature. Power is valued precisely because it can be exercised without regard for consent.

This is why persuasion fantasies fail. You cannot argue someone back into democratic norms if they have already concluded that norms are obstacles rather than safeguards.

The Real Slap in the Face

The CNN segment framed the polling numbers as a “slap in the face.” It is—but not to Trump. He has been explicit about his intentions for years. The slap lands on institutions, commentators, and voters who insisted—against mounting evidence—that this was all bluster, all theater, all negotiable.

What we are witnessing now is not a sudden authoritarian turn. It is the late-stage recognition of an authoritarian project that has been underway for nearly a decade.

The Consequence of Delay

Democracies do not usually collapse in a single dramatic moment. They erode through normalization. Each transgression becomes the baseline for the next. Each failure to respond lowers the threshold for future abuse.

Polling that shows growing mistrust is better than polling that shows complacency—but it is still downstream of the real damage. The cost of waiting until authoritarian intent becomes undeniable is that, by then, the tools to stop it may already be compromised.

Final Reckoning

The central question is no longer whether Trump respects democracy. That question has been answered repeatedly, loudly, and in public.

The real question is whether enough of the electorate still understands democracy as a shared system of rules rather than a mechanism for enforcing dominance. Whether there remains a critical mass willing to defend procedures even when outcomes disappoint them. Whether legitimacy can be rebuilt once it has been reduced to a partisan weapon.

Trump did not invent the decay of democratic norms in America. But he exposed it, accelerated it, and rewarded it. He showed millions of people that power could be pursued openly, shamelessly, and without regard for consent—and that there would be no immediate consequence for doing so.

That lesson, once learned, is very difficult to unlearn.
