'Screaming' judge rips the DOJ for defying order not to touch golf course

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 13, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe government held the sawDelay became a weaponPublic land, private preferenceAccountability after irreversibility

What happened

AlterNet reports that the Trump administration removed 430 trees from Hains Point in Washington’s East Potomac Park after a federal court had required advance updates before work proceeded. According to testimony described in the report, officials knew about the planned removal days earlier but notified the court and plaintiffs at 4:55 p.m. on the Friday before a holiday weekend.

At the following Monday hearing, Judge Ana Reyes denounced the government’s conduct and halted further work until 7 a.m. the next day, warning that any violation would trigger contempt proceedings. Officials disclosed that another 299 trees were slated for removal, bringing the planned total to 729. Reyes ordered National Park Service deputy director Frank Lands, identified as the official who authorized the work, to appear and said in closing that she had been “lied to.”

The removals are tied to plans for a “world-class,” PGA-level golf course directed through the National Park Service after Trump terminated the National Links Trust’s 50-year lease. Critics cited by AlterNet say the project could displace affordable public recreation and bypass required planning, environmental and historic-preservation reviews. The report does not establish the eventual outcome of the litigation or the proposed additional removals.

The government held the saw

This was not a dispute between equally situated parties. The administration controlled the federal land, the Park Service personnel, the renovation plan and the machinery removing the trees. The court and the plaintiffs had only the legal process meant to constrain that power.

Lands reportedly ordered the removal. Park Service supervisor Kevin Greiss testified that discussions began on Oct. 6, while Lands said he knew by Oct. 5. Yet notice arrived late on Oct. 9, after which hundreds of trees were gone before the court could meaningfully respond. Whatever internal details remained unsettled, the government knew enough to prepare an operation capable of felling hundreds of trees. It knew enough to notify the judge sooner.

Delay became a weapon

The administration lawyer reportedly said officials had been “waiting for details” and had done their best. Reyes rejected that account. Her anger matters less than the timeline that produced it: officials withheld actionable notice until the edge of a long weekend, then created irreversible facts on the ground.

That sequence converts procedure into theater. A requirement to provide updates offers no protection if the powerful party waits until intervention is practically impossible. Formal compliance becomes a tool for defeating the purpose of the order while preserving a thin excuse afterward.

AlterNet’s headline foregrounds a “screaming” judge, but the volume in the courtroom is not the central abuse of power. The consequential act was the executive branch proceeding fast enough to make judicial supervision nearly useless.

Public land, private preference

The administration’s institutional power was deployed behind Trump’s personal vision for a prestigious golf course. The existing facility was described as affordable and accessible, with smaller courses suitable for beginners. The proposed redesign threatened to subordinate those public uses to a more exclusive conception of what federal recreation space should be.

The tree removal makes that hierarchy concrete. Longstanding public amenities, historic landscape features and review requirements became obstacles to an executive preference. Whether every removed tree was historically significant is not established by the report. What is established is that the government planned removals on a vast scale while its authority to proceed was contested.

An Interior Department spokesperson had earlier characterized tree cutting as routine maintenance involving hazardous, invasive or dying trees. But preservation advocate Charles Birnbaum disputed that classification, saying the work required a federal historic-preservation review. That conflict deserved scrutiny before removal, not after hundreds of trees had already fallen.

Accountability after irreversibility

Reyes could pause additional work, summon officials and threaten contempt. She could not restore 430 felled trees. That is the advantage of acting first: even if a court later finds misconduct, the physical result survives the reprimand.

This is a recurring method of executive aggrandizement. Concentrated power moves quickly; oversight is left to investigate dates, reconstruct communications and assign responsibility after the damage is complete. Officials then describe a chain of deliberate administrative choices as delay, confusion or imperfect coordination.

The larger systemic failure is not that a judge lost her temper. It is that legal restraint depended on officials honoring its substance before consequences became irreversible. When an administration treats notice as a box to check at the last possible minute, judicial review stops functioning as a constraint and becomes an autopsy.
