'God no!' Josh Hawley facing friendly fire from GOP over 'insane' stimulus plan

Sen. Josh Hawley (R-MO) wants to use the revenue raised from President Donald Trump's tariffs to cut $600 checks to Americans — but his idea is already being panned by his Republican Senate colleagues, Semafor reported on Tuesday.

“It probably would be the most popular thing that this Congress would do. Which means, of course, Republicans would be against it,” said Hawley, who got the idea from Trump, who proposed it last week.

Multiple Senate Republicans expressed outrage at the idea.

“Oh God, no, insane. Two reasons. Number one, we have a $37 trillion debt ... number two, it’s extraordinarily inflationary. It’s fiscal stimulus,” said Sen. Bernie Moreno (R-OH).

Sen. Ron Johnson (R-WI) agreed, saying, “Once we’re down to a surplus, that’s fine. We’re about $2 trillion away from that. So no, I would oppose it. People love spending money and granting new tax cuts when we can’t afford it. We’re $37 trillion in debt and running $2 trillion a year deficits, some time this madness just has to end.”

Notably, despite their fears over the debt, both Johnson and Moreno voted for Trump's tax cut megabill, which is projected to add $2 trillion to the deficit — although Republican leadership has tried to discredit the nonpartisan analyses showing this.

Hawley, for his part, also voted for the legislation, even while loudly criticizing its steep cuts to Medicaid. He is currently trying to pass legislation that would undo some of those Medicaid cuts, which, like the tariff rebate proposal, has yet to attract any significant support from fellow Republicans.

Trump has repeatedly and falsely claimed that his tariffs are raising revenue from other countries that have supposedly ripped off the U.S. in trade; economists broadly warn that tariffs amount to a hidden sales tax on American consumers that drives up prices, particularly when the tariffs apply to manufacturing inputs and raw materials.

Our Analysis:

The Paradox of Fiscal Responsibility: A Tale of Tariffs, Tax Cuts, and Political Theater

In a move that reads like a script from the political theater of the absurd, Sen. Josh Hawley (R-MO) has proposed a plan to cut $600 checks to Americans, financed by the revenue from President Donald Trump's tariffs. This proposal, as reported by Semafor, not only highlights the inconsistencies within the Republican party's stance on fiscal policy but also underscores the performative nature of political discourse in the modern era.

The Irony of Republican Outrage

The outrage expressed by Hawley's Republican Senate colleagues is a spectacle of selective fiscal conservatism. Sen. Bernie Moreno (R-OH) and Sen. Ron Johnson (R-WI) both condemned Hawley's proposal on the grounds of it being "insane," inflationary, and irresponsible given the nation's $37 trillion debt. Yet, this indignation stands in stark contradiction to their support for Trump’s tax cut megabill, which nonpartisan analyses projected would add $2 trillion to the deficit. This selective amnesia regarding fiscal responsibility reveals a deeper issue within the party — an issue where political convenience trumps principled policy-making.

The Misdirection of Tariff Revenue

Trump's claim, echoed by Hawley, that tariffs are a lucrative revenue source from foreign countries is a misdirection that fails to acknowledge the true cost of tariffs. Economists widely agree that tariffs function as a hidden sales tax paid not by foreign countries but by American consumers and businesses. These taxes can lead to higher prices and can be especially burdensome when they affect manufacturing inputs and raw materials, illustrating a fundamental misunderstanding or misrepresentation of economic principles by those pushing for such policies.

The Medicaid Cuts Contradiction

Hawley’s simultaneous attempt to undo Medicaid cuts — cuts that he previously supported through the tax cut legislation — adds another layer of contradiction to this political saga. It suggests a pattern of policy proposals that are more about optics than outcomes, more about making a statement than making a difference. This approach to governance, where the impacts of policies on real lives are secondary to their political utility, is emblematic of a broader systemic failure.

Conclusion: Theatrics Over Substance

The saga of Hawley's tariff revenue plan is a microcosm of the larger issues plaguing American politics: a preference for theatrics over substance, a disregard for consistency in policymaking, and a failure to address the real economic impacts of proposed policies. As these political games unfold, the American public is left to navigate the consequences of policies shaped more by the desire for political victory than by the commitment to public service and economic reality.

In a political era marked by performative outrage and inconsistent principles, it's crucial to peel back the layers of rhetoric to reveal the substance — or lack thereof — beneath. Only then can the electorate hold their representatives accountable not just for the policies they propose, but for the principles they claim to stand for.
