Trump DOJ team shred 'huge amounts' of files at Epstein lockup after death: Prison guard

By Paulo Santos

An undated photo released by the Justice Department as part of its publication of Epstein-related files. (DOJ)

Just six days after Jeffrey Epstein’s 2019 death at the Metropolitan Correctional Center (MCC) in New York, a Bureau of Prisons “After-Actions team” swept the jail and shredded “huge amounts of paperwork,” recruiting the help of at least two inmates to dispose of the files in a dumpster, a newly unearthed FBI report suggests.

Flagged by famed Epstein reporter Julie K. Brown, the FBI report is a crisis intake form, in which an FBI official documented claims made by a federal corrections officer, who contacted the agency via phone to report misconduct. The correctional officer’s name is redacted in the report, which is dated Aug. 16, 2019, just six days after Epstein was found dead in his cell, with his death controversially ruled a suicide.

“[Redacted] has never seen this amount of bags of shredded documents coming out to be put in the dumpster at the rear gate of the MCC,” the report reads, drafted by an FBI official whose name was also redacted. “Last week Epstein hung himself, and there is an ongoing investigation. There was a [Bureau of Prisons] After-Actions team that come, and they are supposed to review what happened.”

According to the correctional officer, at least one inmate was recruited to help with the disposal of documents, tasked with helping move “bags of shredded documents into the dumpster.” The inmate’s name was also redacted in the report.

“[Redacted] was bringing back bags of shredded papers, around 4 or 5 bags, and caller brought them into the gate to throw into the dumpster. [Redacted] told caller that the after-action team is shredding huge amounts of paperwork,” the report reads.

“Caller found it suspicious that an after-action team charged with investigating would be shredding huge amounts of paperwork with all of the officials from the [assistant inspector general], FBI and [BOP] in the building in the middle of an investigation. Those giving instructions to [redacted] said, ‘Make sure you get that box too.’”

The BOP is a subdivision of the Justice Department, which at the time would have been a part of the first Trump administration.

The Trump DOJ was reported to have directed New York Police Department investigators in 2019 to “stand down” in their criminal probe into Epstein five days after his death. The Trump DOJ also asked the NewMexicoDepartment of Justice tohalt its own investigationinto Epstein’s infamousZorro Ranch property, which is alleged to be the burial site of “two foreign girls,” according to a recently unearthedFBI tip.

Our Analysis:

The Real Story Behind the Epstein Document Shredding

In the wake of Jeffrey Epstein’s death at the Metropolitan Correctional Center (MCC) in New York, a flurry of allegations, suspicions, and controversies emerged, further muddying the waters around an already complex case. Among these, a recent revelation about the Bureau of Prisons (BOP) engaging in what appears to be a hasty and suspicious disposal of documents immediately after Epstein’s death stands out for its implications. To dissect the narrative presented and to pinpoint where accountability truly lies, we must scrutinize the institutional powers at play, the decision-makers, and the framing of the article itself.

Institutional Power and Decision-Making

The Bureau of Prisons, as a subdivision of the Justice Department, holds significant institutional power over federal detention facilities like the MCC. The “After-Actions team” mentioned, presumably tasked with reviewing the circumstances leading to Epstein’s death, would operate under the authority of the BOP. The decision to shred “huge amounts of paperwork” in the midst of an ongoing investigation not only raises eyebrows but also questions about the integrity of the investigation process itself.

The article implicates higher authorities, pointing out that the Justice Department during the first Trump administration reportedly exerted influence to curb investigations related to Epstein in New York and New Mexico. This ties the actions of the BOP directly to the administration’s broader handling of the Epstein case, suggesting that decision-making regarding the shredding incident might not have been confined to the BOP alone but could reflect broader directives or a culture of obfuscation.

Misdirection and Framing

The framing of the article suggests a deliberate attempt to dispose of potentially incriminating or sensitive documents, a serious allegation that implies an orchestrated effort to manipulate or limit the scope of the investigation into Epstein’s death. However, the article stops short of explicitly connecting these actions to specific directives from the Trump DOJ, leaving readers to infer involvement or complicity.

By focusing on the act of shredding documents and the involvement of the BOP’s After-Actions team, the article potentially misdirects responsibility toward actors with limited control over the broader narrative and decision-making apparatus. While the BOP personnel certainly played a role in the physical act of shredding documents, the more critical question of who ordered these actions and why remains inadequately addressed.

Conclusion: Beyond the Surface

The involvement of the Trump DOJ as outlined—directing stand-downs in related investigations—paints a picture of an administration exerting its influence to shape the aftermath of Epstein’s death. This broader context is crucial in understanding the shredding incident not as an isolated act of misconduct by rogue BOP officials but potentially as part of a larger pattern of behavior aimed at controlling the narrative and outcome of the Epstein case.

The real story, then, isn’t just about the shredding of documents; it’s about the systemic obfuscation and the possible obstruction of justice at the highest levels of power. Criticism should not be solely directed at the BOP or its After-Actions team without also considering the environment and directives they operated under. Accountability must be sought not only among those who carried out the shredding but also among those who may have ordered such actions as part of a broader effort to influence the course of justice.
