When “Being Played” Becomes a Cover for Escalation

The Real Center of Power

The source piece is simple enough: a retired intelligence officer and a national security reporter warn that Trump is “being played by Iran,” say he misunderstands asymmetrical warfare, and argue that any real end to the conflict requires not just pressure but regime change. That is the setup. The more important question is who actually has power here.

The answer is not Iran, at least not in the way the column frames it. The institutional power sits with the U.S. presidency, the national security apparatus around it, and the commentators who keep narrowing the menu of acceptable outcomes. They decide whether “peace talks” are diplomacy or cover for coercion, whether sanctions relief is a concession or a tactic, and whether a settlement is treated as an outcome or a failure.

The Article’s Trick

The column presents itself as hard-headed realism, but it smuggles in a political demand: keep the pressure on, do not exit, and do not settle for anything short of regime change. That is not analysis of war. It is advocacy for permanent confrontation dressed up as strategic concern.

Its central move is familiar. It takes a complex conflict and reduces it to one convenient villain with a single explanatory reflex: Iran is “playing” the president. That wording does work. It personalizes a structural policy fight, turns negotiation into gullibility, and treats any compromise as evidence of weakness rather than a deliberate choice.

Blame Without Responsibility

The piece is most revealing in what it avoids. It does not seriously ask what the U.S. campaign has already done, why the war continues, or what escalation serves beyond the logic of containment and dominance. Instead, it treats Trump’s supposed willingness to consider a memorandum of understanding, reopen the Strait of Hormuz, and offer potential sanctions relief as a dangerous lapse.

That framing is misleading. It recasts a possible de-escalation as capitulation, then defines every path except regime change as failure. If the president negotiates, he is being fooled. If he refuses, he is “prematurely” declaring victory. Either way, the column makes sure the only acceptable policy is the one it already wants.

Regime Change as a Euphemism

The authors do not merely criticize the talks; they say Trump must “commit to actual regime change.” That phrase does a great deal of laundering. It turns a demand for external political overthrow into a sober strategic requirement, as if toppling a government were the administrative follow-up to a bad deal.

That is the real ideological core here. The piece treats a foreign state’s internal collapse as a legitimate instrument of U.S. policy and then presents that choice as the responsible alternative to being “played.” The language of security is being used to normalize political violence by another name.

The Pattern Underneath

This is a recurring national-security script: exaggerate the enemy’s agency, shrink everyone else’s responsibility, and present escalation as prudence. The enemy becomes omnipresent, cunning, and forever one move ahead. U.S. leaders become either too soft, too confused, or too eager to leave the field. In that frame, diplomatic compromise is never enough because the real goal is not agreement. It is submission.

That is why pieces like this matter. They do not merely interpret events; they police the boundaries of permissible thought inside a militarized political class. The story is not that Trump is being “played.” The story is that a section of the establishment still wants regime change, and it will describe that appetite as realism until the language stops working.
