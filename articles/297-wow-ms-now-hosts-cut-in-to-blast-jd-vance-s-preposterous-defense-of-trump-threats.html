'Wow': MS NOW hosts cut in to blast JD Vance's 'preposterous' defense of Trump threats

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Paulo SantosJune 23, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Threats Are Not Negotiation

What Happened

Vice President JD Vance, speaking from Switzerland during talks with Iranian officials, defended Donald Trump’s threat to kill those negotiators if the Strait of Hormuz stayed closed. Vance brushed off the threat as ordinary “trash talk,” said the Iranians had only threatened to walk out, and claimed the talks continued through the night with “great progress.” He was still offering cover for the president when MSNBC cut in to call out the performance in real time.

Who Holds Power

The institutional power sits with Trump, not with the Iranian delegates, not with the press, and not with Vance as a spokesperson. Trump is the one making threats. Vance is the one translating them into something supposedly normal, even managerial. That is his job here: not to explain policy, but to launder intimidation into the language of process.

The Real Decision

The decisive act in the source is not the Iranian response. It is the American president’s decision to threaten death at all. That is the fact the administration wants diluted by chatter about “social media threats,” late-night negotiations, and whether anyone “walked out.” Those details do not cancel the threat. They camouflage it.

Misdirection as Method

Vance’s explanation is built to shift attention from deliberate coercion to petty banter. Calling it “trash talk” is not analysis; it is evasion. It recasts a president threatening to kill foreign negotiators as if this were a locker-room spat, not the behavior of a state with overwhelming force. The weaker actor here is asked to absorb the blame for “whining,” while the stronger actor gets to pose as the adult “setting the record straight.”

The Pattern

This is a familiar piece of political machinery: projection, then confession, then denial. The administration makes the aggressive move, then insists the other side “threw a wrench in the system,” then pretends the whole thing was just rough talk among serious people. That is how institutional cruelty protects itself. It does not always hide what it is doing. Sometimes it simply renames it until the press, and too many officials, start repeating the lie in procedural language.

Systemic Error

The deeper story is not that diplomacy got heated. It is that American power can threaten lethal force in public, and its vice president can still frame that threat as ordinary bargaining. That is the sickness: violence presented as candor, intimidation presented as professionalism, and responsibility pushed downward onto the target. In that system, the strongest actor is never the problem. He is just “responding.”
