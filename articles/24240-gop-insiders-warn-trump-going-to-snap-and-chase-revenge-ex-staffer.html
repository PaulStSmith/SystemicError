GOP insiders warn Trump going to snap and chase revenge: ex-staffer

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosSeptember 30, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that former Trump administration official Miles Taylor predicts President Donald Trump will respond to expected Republican losses in the November midterms by pursuing revenge and creating political turmoil. The election has not happened, and the article provides no result; “Trump going to snap” is Taylor’s forecast, not an established event.

Taylor cited former White House press secretary Stephanie Grisham, who said Trump does not care whether Republicans retain Congress and could use defeat to portray himself as a victim. Reporters Brian Karem and Jim Acosta likewise predicted increasingly erratic conduct, attributing the danger partly to aides unwilling to restrain him.

AlterNet also relayed Grisham’s allegation that taxpayer-funded advertisements promoted Trump rather than Republican candidates and did so in defiance of federal law. The article does not identify the statute, document an official finding, or report Trump’s response, so the claimed illegality remains unsubstantiated within the supplied reporting.

The forecast is not the fact

The story’s central weakness is grammatical as much as evidentiary: speculation repeatedly hardens into apparent certainty. Republican defeat is presumed. Trump’s reaction is foretold. Motive is assigned through former officials and journalists who know his conduct but cannot know an election result or presidential response that has not occurred.

Their warnings may be informed. They are still warnings. AlterNet should distinguish documented preparation from predictions based on temperament. The article identifies no revenge order, operational plan, target list, or specific post-election action. “Havoc” and “pure hell” convey alarm without defining what institutions or people would face what conduct.

Power sits in the presidency

The meaningful issue is not whether Trump might become emotionally unstable. He holds presidential authority. A hostile Congress could constrain legislation and conduct oversight, but it would not erase executive control over agencies, appointments, enforcement priorities, public communications, or the machinery available to punish perceived enemies.

That is why staff resistance matters. Acosta’s allegation that aides placate Trump instead of challenging him describes institutional surrender, not merely a difficult workplace. Presidential power becomes more dangerous when advisers treat obedience as their job and restraint as somebody else’s responsibility.

Republicans enabled this dependency

Grisham’s account portrays Republican candidates as weaker actors tethered to a leader who may value personal grievance above their electoral survival. But the party is not an innocent bystander. It invested authority in Trump, organized itself around his image, and left its candidates dependent on his attention.

If he promotes himself with resources that could assist them, the problem is larger than personal vanity. It exposes a party structure in which collective goals remain subordinate to one officeholder’s political brand. Trump has agency over that choice; Republican officials have agency over the arrangement that makes it decisive.

Psychology obscures accountability

Words such as “snap,” “unhinged,” and “spoiled brat” personalize what is fundamentally an institutional question. They invite readers to watch Trump’s mood when they should watch decisions: who receives orders, who executes them, who supplies legal rationales, and who refuses oversight.

This framing also gives future enablers an alibi. If harmful conduct is presented as an eruption of temperament, officials can later describe themselves as witnesses to chaos rather than participants who carried out identifiable acts. Volatility may explain behavior. It does not dissolve responsibility.

The system rewards anticipatory obedience

The larger pattern is a presidency organized around personal loyalty, surrounded—according to the people quoted—by officials unlikely to impose meaningful resistance. The decisive danger in that structure is not one predicted outburst after one hypothetical defeat. It is the number of empowered people prepared to convert grievance into government action.

AlterNet’s reporting points toward that structure but retreats into dramatic prophecy. The sharper story is already visible: concentrated executive power, a party dependent on one leader, and aides accused of choosing accommodation over restraint. Elections may change the pressure on that system. They do not determine whether its officials possess agency.
