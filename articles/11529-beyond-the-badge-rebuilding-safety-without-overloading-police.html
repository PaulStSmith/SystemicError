Beyond the Badge: Rebuilding Safety Without Overloading Police

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 17, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Take a look at the cartoon above.

It lays bare one of the most glaring contradictions in the American conversation around policing: the fantasy that armed officers are society’s universal problem-solvers. They are tasked with everything from homelessness to drug addiction, domestic violence, school safety, and even mental health crises, while simultaneously being resourced and trained almost exclusively in the tools of violence and coercion. The result is predictable—failure, brutality, and a system that creaks under its own contradictions. The cartoon doesn’t just lampoon the absurdity; it offers the corrective vision at the heart of the “Defund the Police” movement.

Let’s be absolutely clear: “Defund the Police” is not about abandoning public safety, nor is it some anarchist fever dream of letting society descend into chaos. It is about precisely what the cartoon depicts—redistributing responsibility for the enormous range of social problems away from a militarized force of armed enforcers and toward institutions designed to actually address those problems at their roots.

The Burden Dumped on Policing

At the top of the cartoon, the police officer is crushed under boulders that symbolizes society’s refusal to invest in the social safety net. Each label on those rocks—gang violence, drug possession, homelessness, domestic violence, school security—is a shorthand for decades of political cowardice. Rather than confronting inequality, disinvestment, or the collapse of affordable housing, lawmakers shove these problems into the arms of law enforcement. Instead of creating comprehensive mental health services, they criminalize erratic behavior and throw people into jails. Instead of addressing the root causes of drug addiction, they declare a “war on drugs” and unleash paramilitary squads into poor neighborhoods.

And then, with straight faces, defenders of the status quo ask: “If not the police, then who?”

The cartoon answers: the institutions that should have been built in the first place.

The Redirected Resources Vision

In the bottom panel, the absurdity is stripped away. Now, instead of being buried by an impossible mandate, the police officer holds a single stone labeled “Keep the Peace.” Everything else is carried off by programs designed for those exact issues: affordable housing programs, universal health care, drug treatment centers, mental health clinics, education and job placement programs, regulation of sex work, and social workers to handle cases that require care rather than coercion.

This redistribution isn’t utopian dreaming—it’s evidence-based policy. Countries with stronger social safety nets experience lower crime rates. When housing is affordable and healthcare is accessible, desperation doesn’t drive theft. When treatment and harm reduction replace criminalization, drug overdoses and violence plummet. When jobs are available and schools are resourced, communities stabilize. In short, the best crime prevention strategy is not an endless cycle of arrests and incarceration—it’s the creation of conditions where crime is less likely to occur in the first place.

The Police State as Social Band-Aid

What we have in the United States is the opposite. Decades of neoliberal austerity and bipartisan cowardice gutted public institutions while bloating police budgets. In city after city, the police department is the single largest line item in the municipal budget, often dwarfing spending on housing, health, or education. Mayors will tell you there’s no money for schools, but they’ll find millions for new riot gear, surveillance toys, and military-grade vehicles.

This is not just bad policy—it’s actively destructive. The police are asked to respond to crises they cannot solve, with tools that only make them worse. A mental health crisis becomes an arrest or a shooting. Homelessness becomes trespassing citations and sweeps that scatter people like refuse, destroying their belongings. Domestic violence calls end with survivors retraumatized and sometimes even jailed themselves. School safety is militarized until students—especially students of color—find themselves criminalized for teenage misbehavior.

Policing has become the last refuge of a political system too cowardly to address root causes. Instead of universal healthcare, we get cops with tasers. Instead of public housing, we get handcuffs. Instead of living wages, we get surveillance cameras on every corner.

The Political Cowardice Behind the Myth

Defenders of the system rely on a manufactured fear: without police, chaos will reign. But the cartoon reminds us that chaos already reigns precisely because of this arrangement. The cop buckling under the weight of the boulder is not a symbol of stability—it is a symbol of dysfunction. He is drowning because the state refused to build the raft of social supports that would have prevented this flood in the first place.

When conservatives and centrists wring their hands about “defunding,” what they are really defending is a political arrangement that allows them to avoid responsibility. As long as we keep everything under the heading of “crime,” the real failures—housing, healthcare, education—don’t have to be addressed. Just throw another billion at the police and call it safety.

Evidence of Alternatives

But here’s the kicker: we already know alternatives work. Programs like Housing First have slashed chronic homelessness in places willing to invest. Portugal’s drug decriminalization program has drastically reduced overdoses and drug-related crime. In communities that replaced police with trained crisis response teams for mental health calls, outcomes are safer both for residents and for officers. Social workers addressing domestic violence and child welfare cases can intervene without the intimidation and escalation that armed police often bring.

These aren’t fringe experiments—they’re proven policies ignored only because they don’t fit the tough-on-crime theater that wins elections.

Reclaiming the Meaning of Public Safety

At its heart, the cartoon—and the movement it reflects—is about redefining what we mean by public safety. True safety does not come from an occupying force of armed men prowling neighborhoods. True safety comes from knowing you will not lose your home if you lose your job. It comes from being able to afford medical care when you are sick. It comes from knowing your child’s school is resourced and safe. It comes from living in a community where addiction is treated as a health issue, not a crime.

Police abolitionists aren’t naïve. They aren’t pretending violence doesn’t exist. What they argue—what the cartoon illustrates—is that pouring billions into policing hasn’t stopped that violence. It has only brutalized communities already suffering under inequality. Safety is not the presence of more cops; safety is the absence of the conditions that breed harm.

Conclusion: Redistribute, Don’t Abdicate

The question is not whether police should exist in some form, but whether they should continue to serve as society’s dumping ground for every problem politicians don’t want to solve. The answer should be obvious: no. We should shrink their scope, defund their bloated budgets, and reinvest those resources where they will actually produce safety and stability.

The cartoon cuts through the noise. In one panel, the absurdity: cops buried under a mountain of tasks they cannot possibly perform. In the next, the common sense: invest in the systems that address the roots, and leave the police only what they are minimally equipped to handle—keeping the peace in the rare instances when violence actually erupts.

That is not utopia. That is policy grounded in reality, history, and evidence. The tragedy is that we have to draw cartoons to explain what should be obvious: policing as it exists today is a monument to political failure. And the only way forward is to stop pretending that the police can, or should, carry the weight of society’s sins.
