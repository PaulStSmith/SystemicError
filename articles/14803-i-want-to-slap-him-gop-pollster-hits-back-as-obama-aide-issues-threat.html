'I want to slap him': GOP pollster hits back as Obama aide issues threat

Republican pollster Frank Luntz tried to turn down the temperature on Friday in a heated online feud being waged by former Barack Obama campaign strategist and press official Johanna Maska.

Maska took particular umbrage over Luntz's appearance on a podcast with Gov. Gavin Newsom (D-CA), and said she "wanted to slap" him over it in a video posted to X.

"Frank Luntz, you may know, is a Republican pollster, he does these focus groups," Maska said. "He was also involved in Newt Gingrich's rise. He was involved in reforming welfare. He actually bragged on the podcast about reforming welfare. And if you remember in the '90s, that meant that moms, who have a job, by the way, job is being a mom, had to go find a job outside of the home.

"And on this same podcast today that I'm listening to, Frank Luntz had the audacity to say that it is moms who are causing the current political division because we're not turning off screens. I wanted to slap him!"

"He was involved in setting the stage for this division!" she continued. "He is part of the problem. And I am sorry, but moms not being able to control what is addictive, that we have allowed unregulated to take over our children's brains, not because of moms, but because of underregulation. You're going to say it's our fault? Frank Luntz, you caused this division!"

Luntz, a Trump-skeptical pollster who had been attempting to make a point that social media is doing deep social harm to the next generation, responded to the post.

"Johanna, you say you want to hurt me physically over a podcast comment. You even plan to track me down so you can hit me," wrote Luntz. "I assume you don't really mean it — but maybe you do? That's the problem with social media; it's often used by people like you to threaten people like me. And when children threaten each other the same way, moms and dads need to take the phone away and kick their kids off the computer."

"I agree with you: We need some regulations to keep children away from online toxicity. But parents who teach respect will be far more effective than any regulation," he added. "I'm sorry I made you so angry. But nothing I said deserves the response you gave — you actually made my point perfectly."

Our Analysis:

The Clash of Perspectives: Luntz vs. Maska

In the latest political theater, we witness the unfolding of a rather juvenile spat between Republican pollster Frank Luntz and former Barack Obama campaign strategist Johanna Maska. The core of their dispute? A podcast appearance by Luntz with California Governor Gavin Newsom, which lit Maska’s fuse to the point of metaphorical violence. But let’s dissect the layers of irony, hypocrisy, and sheer absurdity that encapsulate this feud, shall we?

First, the audacity of Frank Luntz to pin the blame of current political divisions on mothers for not policing screen time is as laughable as it is tragic. Luntz, a figure with a history steeped in the kind of political engineering that has arguably fueled said divisions, seems to have forgotten the mirror in his own house. Maska’s retort, though exaggerated in its aggression, touches on a nerve that is all too raw in the American psyche: the scapegoating of individuals for systemic failures.

"He was involved in setting the stage for this division!" Maska exclaims. And she is not wrong. Luntz played a pivotal role in the political landscape of the '90s, aiding in the rise of Newt Gingrich and the reform of welfare—a reform that, as Maska points out, placed undue burdens on mothers. The irony of Luntz lamenting the divisiveness in today’s political climate, after having been an architect of division himself, is rich, to say the least.

Then there’s Maska’s claim that “moms not being able to control what is addictive, that we have allowed unregulated to take over our children's brains, not because of moms, but because of underregulation.” Here, Maska hits a critical point about the systemic nature of the issue. It’s not merely a matter of parental control but a broader failure of regulation and the unchecked power of tech companies. Yet, her delivery, marred by a violent metaphor, unfortunately, undermines the validity of her argument.

Luntz’s response is a masterclass in deflecting criticism. "You say you want to hurt me physically over a podcast comment... That's the problem with social media; it's often used by people like you to threaten people like me." Here, Luntz plays the victim, sidestepping the substance of Maska’s critique to focus on her admittedly ill-advised expression of anger. His call for respect and parental responsibility, while not without merit, rings hollow coming from someone whose career has contributed to the very environment he now decries.

The Heart of the Matter

At its core, this exchange between Luntz and Maska is emblematic of a larger, systemic failure to address the root causes of societal issues. Blame is shifted onto individuals—mothers, in this case—while those in power, who have the means and responsibility to enact meaningful change, engage in performative hand-wringing.

Luntz’s assertion that "parents who teach respect will be far more effective than any regulation" is a simplification of a complex issue. While parental guidance is undoubtedly important, it is not a panacea for the societal and technological challenges we face. To suggest so is to absolve the architects of our current predicament of their responsibility.

In the end, this feud, with all its sound and fury, signifies nothing if it does not lead to a deeper conversation about accountability, regulation, and the collective responsibility we bear for the society we create. Maska and Luntz, in their heated exchange, offer a mirror to the broader political discourse—one that is often more concerned with scoring points than with addressing the systemic errors at its foundation.
