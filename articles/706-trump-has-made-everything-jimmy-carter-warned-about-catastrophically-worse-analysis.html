Trump has made everything Jimmy Carter warned about catastrophically worse: analysis

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

The Carter Speech Trump Proved Right

The Real Story

The immediate subject here is Jimmy Carter’s 1979 warning about national decay, but the political fact pattern is contemporary: Trump’s administration not only revived the conditions Carter described, it managed the symbolism around Carter’s death with petty executive control. Carter did not receive the usual flag-at-half-staff treatment because Trump did not want it interfering with his inauguration. That is the governing style in plain view: institutional ceremony subordinated to one man’s schedule, and national memory bent around his ego.

Who Actually Holds Power

Carter’s old complaint about a Congress “twisted and pulled” by powerful interests still matters, but the source article is clearer than it may realize about where responsibility sits now. Power does not live in the people being blamed for social exhaustion, loneliness, or distrust. It lives in the hands of executives, party leaders, donors, media gatekeepers, and the political operators who decide what gets enforced, ignored, or spun.

Trump is the obvious figure at the center here, but the deeper power structure is broader than one presidency. Schmidt’s point is that the country has had decades to absorb Carter’s warning and instead let institutional decay deepen. That is not an accident of mood. It reflects a system that rewards short-term leverage, permanent campaigning, and spectacle over repair.

Misdirection as Method

The article’s strongest line is also its indictment: Carter was talking about a government “incapable of action” because it had been captured by “well-financed and powerful special interests.” That remains the cleanest explanation. But modern political coverage often drifts away from this and toward safer abstractions. It blames public confusion, cultural decline, social media, or some general national sadness as if harm simply happened to society.

That framing is convenient for the people with the most control. It turns deliberate outcomes into ambient crisis. It treats governance failure as a mood disorder. It makes structural abandonment sound like everyone’s fault and no one’s decision.

Schmidt himself moves partly in that direction when he lists mental health, loneliness, and mass shootings as evidence of rot. Those are real consequences. But consequence is not the same thing as cause. If the country is isolated, inflamed, and narrowed, the question is who built the incentives, platforms, and political economy that make those conditions profitable.

Carter’s Warning Was Not Sentimental

Carter’s speech is being treated here as a kind of lost moral document, but its political force was more concrete than nostalgia. He was naming a government that had become reactive, captured, and self-protective. He was also describing a public losing confidence not because it had suddenly become weak, but because the governing class had become visibly unable or unwilling to answer basic problems.

That distinction matters. Carter did not say Americans were failing because they lacked virtue. He said institutions were failing because they had stopped earning trust. That is a very different diagnosis from the usual elite complaint that the public has become too cynical, too distracted, or too unruly. The source article is right to recover that speech because it exposes the fraud of replacing institutional failure with personal lecturing.

The Republican Confession

Schmidt’s role matters precisely because he is not an outsider tossing stones. He is a Republican adviser talking like a witness for the prosecution. That gives the piece unusual force. It is not partisan melodrama to say the system got worse. It is an insider conceding that the governing order he served has helped produce the country Carter warned about.

That concession also reveals something else: even elite critics now speak as if the damage is diffuse and cultural, when much of it is plainly political. Trump’s treatment of Carter’s death was not a misunderstanding or a lapse in tone. It was an assertion of control over public ritual. Likewise, the broader drift from civic confidence to fragmentation has been enabled by leaders who benefit from exhaustion, polarization, and permanent grievance.

The ruling tactic is not competence. It is managed breakdown.

The Pattern Beneath the Panic

This story is not really about whether Carter was prophetic. It is about the recurring habit of American power to notice collapse only after it has already been monetized and normalized. First the institutions hollow out. Then the commentary class mourns the hollowing. Then the same people who profited from neglect present themselves as sober observers of the wreckage.

That is the system. It absorbs warnings, flatters itself for remembering them, and keeps the structure that made the warnings necessary in the first place. Carter named special interests. Schmidt describes social damage. The missing link is accountability: who made the choices, who kept benefiting, and who still gets to pretend the crisis arrived on its own.
