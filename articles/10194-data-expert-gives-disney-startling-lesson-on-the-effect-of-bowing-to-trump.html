Data expert gives Disney startling lesson on the effect of bowing to Trump

A veteran data journalist is offering a lesson to those in positions of power about why they keep losing when they bow to President Donald Trump.

Writing on Monday, G. Elliott Morris explained on his "Strength in Numbers" Substack that powerful people mistakenly assume Trump holds more control over the country than he actually does. When Disney/ABC capitulated to Federal Communications Commission Chair Brendan Carr and conservative affiliate owners Sinclair and Nexstar, they effectively shot themselves in the foot, he said.

Even as of Monday, people were still searching for instructions on how to cancel their Disney+ accounts after the issue exploded on Sept. 18.

"This is not limited to internet posters and Google searchers; investors are worried too. Disney’s stock is down 2% over the last week, while the overall market is up nearly 1%," Morris wrote.

According to Morris, this fallout stems from people underestimating how unpopular Trump is, and how those who capitulate to him also become unpopular by association.

Morris examined Trump’s approval ratings in his first year, comparing them to those of previous presidents since George W. Bush. The only president ranking lower than Trump today is Trump in 2017.

Recent polls have shown Trump performing poorly on specific issues such as trade, the economy, inflation, and immigration.

"But the problem for Trump (and those who ignore the data) runs deeper than the topline indicates," Morris wrote. "It’s not just that more people disapprove than approve of Trump, but that the disapprovers feel their emotions much, much more intensely. Depending on the polls you pick for your average, between 46 and 50 percent of U.S. adults tell pollsters they “strongly disapprove” of the job Trump is doing as president. That is double the percent that strongly approve (24%)."

Ultimately, this translates into people being angry enough to take action—such as canceling subscriptions or choosing different family vacations instead of Disney World.

"Other companies have behaved similarly: Paramount did what it apparently needed to do to get a merger with Skydance through Carr’s FCC, too. And Target very publicly rolled back lots of corporate DEI policies this spring," Morris noted.

He pointed out that the backlash against Target was so strong that it saw a notable decline in sales in the second quarter. The company's stock price fell by 24% between January and March compared to the previous year. By August, the CEO and COO announced their resignations, Forbes reported.

Morris urged corporations not to assume that 2025 is the same as 2024.

" Elections reward coalitions of voters, while markets respond to consumers — voters and non-voters — whose preferences show up not just on Election Day, but in audience ratings, subscription revenue, purchases (like trips to Disney World), etc," said Morris.

"If a president is unpopular with the broader public (and not just the out-party), you should expect friction for brands, platforms, and legacy media that appear to bend toward him."

Our Analysis:

Analysis of "A Veteran Data Journalist's Lesson on Trump's Influence"

The article in question dives into a compelling narrative, spearheaded by G. Elliott Morris, about the misconceived perception of power wielded by former President Donald Trump and the consequent pitfalls for those who align with him. The analysis provided by Morris is a sharp reminder of the complex interplay between politics, public sentiment, and corporate decisions. Let's dissect the key points, shall we?

The Misjudgment of Trump's Influence

Morris's primary thesis centers on the overestimation of Trump's control over the national discourse and the underestimation of the public's discontent with him. This is a critical error, showcasing a blatant disregard for empirical data. The case of Disney/ABC bowing to pressure from FCC Chair Brendan Carr, alongside conservative affiliates Sinclair and Nexstar, serves as a textbook example of this miscalculation. The backlash was swift and severe, with tangible repercussions such as a measurable dip in Disney's stock by 2%. This financial hit is emblematic of a broader trend of reactivity to Trump-associated decisions, yet it seems the lesson remains unlearned.

The Intensity of Disapproval

Morris adeptly highlights a crucial nuance often glossed over in political discourse: the intensity of disapproval. It's not merely that Trump garners disapproval, but the depth of this sentiment is profound. With between 46 and 50 percent of U.S. adults expressing "strong disapproval" of Trump's presidency, the emotional charge behind these numbers suggests a populace not just passively discontent but actively antagonistic. This fervor translates into concrete actions—canceling subscriptions, boycotting products—which directly impact companies' bottom lines.

Corporate Consequences

The analysis further delves into the repercussions faced by companies like Paramount and Target, which have also succumbed to political pressures, presumably to curry favor or avoid confrontation with Trump-aligned forces. The fallout from these decisions has been stark, with Target's sales plummeting and its stock price taking a nosedive. The departure of Target's CEO and COO could be seen as a direct outcome of their misjudged political calculus.

The Misalignment with Public Sentiment

Morris's warning to corporations is searing and grounded in reality: the political landscape of 2025 will not mirror that of 2024, and the whims of a president unpopular with the broader public will not align with market dynamics. This misalignment is where many fall short, failing to recognize that voter coalitions and consumer preferences are not one and the same. The assumption that bending towards Trump—or any polarizing figure, for that matter—will be beneficial in the long run is myopic. The data suggests otherwise, with a clear indication that such strategies often lead to brand damage, financial loss, and a tarnished reputation.

Conclusion

In essence, Morris's analysis offers a stern rebuke to the short-sightedness of those in positions of power who fail to grasp the real dynamics at play. The underestimation of Trump's unpopularity and the overestimation of his influence have led to costly missteps for companies. This piece serves as a stark reminder that in the realm of politics and public opinion, ignorance is not bliss—it's a strategic blunder with real-world consequences. The lesson here is clear: aligning with divisive figures without heeding the intense disapproval they inspire is not just bad politics; it's bad business.
