Music legend's estate refuses to let Trump play beloved hit at Freedom 250 rally: report

Based on an article by Bennito L. Kelty

Trump Wanted Leonard Cohen’s Halo. Cohen’s Estate Refused the Propaganda Transfer.

The Only Real Power Here

Trump’s rally was not just a campaign stop; it was an attempt to borrow legitimacy from a dead artist. He wanted “Hallelujah” at his Freedom 250 event, with tenor Christopher Macchio apparently set to perform it. Leonard Cohen’s estate stopped that use cold and said it was unauthorized. That is the core fact. Everything else is decoration around a simple power struggle: one side tried to conscript a song into political theater, and the other side withheld permission.

What the Source Actually Shows

The reporting says Trump had been talking for weeks about using the song at the June 24 rally. It also notes that several artists withdrew from the event, while a smaller set stayed on the bill, including Flo Rida, Vanilla Ice, Alexis Wilkins, and Lee Greenwood. The White House did not immediately respond to the estate’s statement. That silence matters. It is what institutional embarrassment looks like when a forced spectacle runs into a legal and reputational wall.

The Theft Was the Point

This was not an innocent booking dispute. Trump’s operation wanted the emotional authority of “Hallelujah” without the consent of the people who control its use. That is a familiar political move: take a cultural object with public affection attached, strip it of context, and redeploy it as an applause machine. When it fails, the failure is often described as a mix-up or a logistics issue. It was neither. It was an attempted appropriation.

Who Took Responsibility, and Who Didn’t

The estate acted directly. Trump’s orbit tried to proceed until blocked. The White House, predictably, ducked the issue. That distribution of action is the story. The people with formal power rarely frame these episodes as choices; they outsource them to “events,” “performers,” or “planning.” But the decision to stage the rally with this song in mind came from the top. So did the decision to convert a political rally into a pageant of borrowed sentiment.

The Useful Idiom of “Entertainment”

The source details a parade of performers and dropouts, which can make the episode look like backstage drama. That framing is convenient because it shrinks the political meaning. It turns a coercive symbolic grab into an awkward booking story. In reality, the episode shows how modern political power operates through culture: not just by speaking, but by commandeering symbols that already carry emotional weight. When the symbol resists, the campaign is exposed as dependent on imitation and theft.

A Familiar Pattern

This is the same pattern that runs through a lot of contemporary right-wing spectacle: take what you did not make, strip away consent, wrap it in national language, and hope the audience confuses volume for legitimacy. Cohen’s estate denied the transfer. That refusal is more than a copyright defense. It is a rare reminder that not every symbol is available to power, and not every public performance can be laundered through a dead artist’s reputation.
