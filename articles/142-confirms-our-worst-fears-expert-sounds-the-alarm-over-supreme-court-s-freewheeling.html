'Confirms our worst fears': Expert sounds the alarm over Supreme Court's 'freewheeling'

The Court That Wants the Last Word

Power, Not Principle

The Supreme Court’s 6-3 ruling in Wolford v. Lopez is not a neutral act of constitutional interpretation. It is an exercise of institutional power by Republican-appointed justices who are using judicial review to erase gun regulations they dislike. The issue is not ambiguity. The issue is that the Court has given itself permission to decide policy while pretending to discover law.

Hawaii Did What States Are Supposed to Do

Hawaii passed a law restricting guns on private property. The state, according to the source material, showed that the statute was rooted in history and tradition. That should have ended the inquiry if the Court were actually applying its own stated test. Instead, the majority threw out the law anyway. When a state tries to reduce gun violence and the Court intervenes to stop it, the decisionmaker is not confused. The decisionmaker is choosing violence-friendly rules and dressing them up as constitutional inevitability.

The Blame Is Being Misdirected

The familiar trick here is to treat regulation as the problem and gun violence as background noise. The Court’s defenders will call this legal reasoning, but the practical effect is unmistakable: a law meant to mitigate violence gets killed, and the harm gets displaced onto lawmakers, states, and anyone trying to respond to the damage. That is not judicial restraint. It is a hostile veto over democratic attempts to manage a known danger.

The Court as Super-Legislature

Stern’s sharper charge lands because it describes the real mechanism: a supermajority imposing its preferred gun policy under the thin cover of doctrine. The ruling does not read like a court discovering limits. It reads like a court manufacturing them. The justices are not simply interpreting history; they are selecting outcomes and then raiding history for justification after the fact.

A Wider Pattern of Cruelty

The same day, the Court sided with the Trump administration to strip Temporary Protected Status from more than 350,000 Haitian immigrants, a move described by analysts as inhumane. That matters because it shows a broader pattern: the Court does not merely resolve disputes. It helps enforce a political order in which vulnerability is treated as disposable and state power is deployed upward, against those with the least protection.

The System on Display

The deeper lesson is that this is what captured institutions look like in practice: not overtly lawless, but selectively legal; not openly ideological, but aggressively so; not accidental, but systematic. In Wolford, the Court did not just strike down a Hawaii law. It revealed a jurisprudence built to privilege gun absolutism, insulate itself from accountability, and call the result constitutional.
