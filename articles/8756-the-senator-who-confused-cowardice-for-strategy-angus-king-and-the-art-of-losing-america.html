The Senator Who Confused Cowardice for Strategy: Angus King and the Art of Losing America

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 13, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Anatomy of a CaveKing’s Post-Defeat SermonThe Logic of LosersA Profile in Anti-CourageA Catastrophic Message to the CountryThe Price of CowardiceThe Deepest Circle (Metaphorically)The Final Word

Senator Angus King has now said the quiet part out loud: “Standing up to Donald Trump didn’t work.” And with that one sentence, he didn’t merely misdiagnose a political crisis — he confessed to his own role in manufacturing it. King’s post-hoc absolution tour is the purest distillation of the Democratic Senate’s fatal flaw: a pathological preference for surrender dressed up as sober governance.

Let’s be brutally clear. Standing up to Trump didn’t work because Angus King and seven other Democrats collapsed at the crucial moment. They rolled over when history demanded steel. They mistook cowering for compromise and appeasement for pragmatism. And now King wants to lecture the country about how resistance itself is futile. No, Senator — the only thing that proved futile was trusting you to hold the line.

The Anatomy of a Cave

During the shutdown fight, Democrats held public opinion. Polls showed voters blaming Trump and the GOP. The party had fresh electoral victories powered by anti-MAGA energy. The ACA subsidy protections they demanded were popular. For once, Democrats controlled the narrative.

Yet days into the standoff, a bloc of skittish Senate Democrats — with Angus King among them — defected. They accepted a “deal” that amounted to absolutely nothing: a vague promise of a future vote, unenforceable, unenacted, and ultimately meaningless. They handed Trump a victory and received a fortune cookie in return.

It was political malpractice. It was strategic incompetence. And it was entirely predictable from a caucus that treats confrontation as a communicable disease.

King’s Post-Defeat Sermon

Now, from his cable news pulpit, King claims the effort to “stand up to Trump” failed. What he really means is that he failed, and he cannot bear to admit it. So he reframes his own capitulation as a grand cosmic inevitability.

He points to Trump weaponizing federal agencies during the shutdown — like refusing SNAP funds and keeping ICE operational — as if this proves resistance is pointless. But those abuses weren’t arguments for surrender. They were arguments to go harder. If a president uses hunger and deportation as bargaining chips, you don’t fold; you escalate. You seize the funding levers. You drag the fight into the daylight. You demand accountability with fire, not sighs.

Instead, King saw cruelty and responded with a shrug.

The Logic of Losers

King’s worldview is emblematic of the Democratic Senate’s most corrosive instinct: the belief that power is somehow unseemly when Democrats wield it. Mitch McConnell weaponizes procedure like a blunt instrument. Trump turns institutions into personal playthings. But Democrats? They deliver a sternly worded press release and call it courage.

When King says “standing up to Trump didn’t work,” he’s laundering surrender as wisdom. He’s telling his party — and the country — that authoritarianism can’t be fought, only bargained with. He’s telling millions that the struggle is unwinnable, that the bully in the room has already won.

But here’s the truth he cannot face: it did work. Public support was on Democrats’ side. The pressure was working. Trump was flailing. The shutdown was hurting the GOP. Victory was within reach — right up until Angus King handed it away.

A Profile in Anti-Courage

If this was an isolated incident, it might be forgivable. But King’s entire posture has long been one of caution bordering on paralysis. He markets himself as a pragmatic independent, but in practice he behaves like the Senate’s Chief Clerk of Consensus, forever smoothing the edges of Republican aggression while filing down the Democratic spine.

His instinct for conciliation would be admirable in a time of normal politics. But these are not normal times. You cannot appease authoritarianism into moderation. You cannot compromise with a movement intent on dismantling democracy. And you cannot negotiate your way out of a constitutional crisis with the political equivalent of a Hallmark card.

Yet King persists in believing that if Democrats just “show good faith,” the other side will reciprocate. It’s magical thinking dressed up as mature governance.

A Catastrophic Message to the Country

By declaring that “standing up to Trump didn’t work,” King has done something even more damaging than his shutdown betrayal. He has validated the Republican narrative that resistance is futile. He has broadcast weakness where strength was needed. He has told the public that the problem wasn’t Donald Trump, or MAGA extremism, or Republican obstruction — it was the Democrats who dared to resist.

This isn’t just wrong. It’s dangerous.

Authoritarians thrive when their opponents convince themselves that fighting back is pointless. Every time a Democrat publicly frames resistance as failure, Trump grows stronger. Every time the Senate signals fear, the GOP pushes further. Every time a supposed “independent” claims standing up to a demagogue is impossible, it becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy.

The Price of Cowardice

It is no exaggeration to say that King’s rhetoric will be cited for years as Exhibit A in the case against Democratic timidity. When future historians write about the slow erosion of American democratic norms, they won’t just discuss the villains. They’ll discuss the enablers — the ones too timid to fight, too cautious to act, too scared to seize the tools the Constitution handed them.

Angus King has now placed himself firmly in that ledger.

This is not a matter of personal animus. It’s a matter of accountability. Because every surrender sets a precedent. Every cave teaches the GOP that Democrats can be broken with a little bluster. Every failure to confront Trump strengthens the machinery of cruelty — especially ICE, a rogue agency that must be dismantled, defunded, and ultimately abolished.

And when the Senate cedes ground, it’s not just political theater. Real people suffer.

The Deepest Circle (Metaphorically)

In Dante’s Inferno, the deepest circle of Hell is reserved for traitors — those who betray their obligations when the stakes are highest. Angus King isn’t a traitor in the literal sense, but he betrayed something essential: the expectation that elected officials will defend the republic when it’s under threat.

When he chose to collapse instead of confront, he consigned the country to unnecessary suffering. When he now claims resistance itself is pointless, he paves the road for future capitulations.

If there is a metaphorical political Hell, it is reserved for leaders who treat cowardice as strategy and defeat as wisdom.

Angus King carved himself a place there the moment he mistook his own weakness for inevitability.

The Final Word

Standing up to Trump didn’t fail — Angus King did. And until Democrats stop treating men like him as moral authorities and start recognizing them as cautionary examples, the cycle will repeat. Democracy doesn’t die when the strong are defeated. It dies when the timid convince themselves that strength is impossible.

Our readers deserve to know the truth: the test of our time isn’t whether Trump can be beaten. It’s whether Democrats can muster the courage to fight him. Angus King answered that question with a whimper.

The rest of us don’t have that luxury.
