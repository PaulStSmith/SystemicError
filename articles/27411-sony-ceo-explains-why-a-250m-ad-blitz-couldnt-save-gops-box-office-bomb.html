Sony CEO explains why a $250M ad blitz couldn’t save GOP’s box office bomb

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 23, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Republicans could buy saturation, but they could not buy an audience. After Elon Musk reportedly spent roughly half a billion dollars backing Republican candidates—and after the party outspent Democrats on Senate advertising by more than $250 million by late October—Republicans still suffered major defeats, including Texas Senate nominee Ken Paxton’s resounding loss. Sony chief executive Rothman’s explanation is blunt: money cannot rescue a political product voters do not want, especially when the campaign has no persuasive message and delivers its attacks through obsolete channels.

A financial advantage mistaken for political power

Writing in The New York Times, Rothman compared the Republican campaign to a failed movie launch. “Spending money, even tens or hundreds of millions of dollars, without a message that spurs people to action is like lighting the cash on fire,” he wrote.

The distinction matters. Wealth can purchase airtime, consultants and repetition. It cannot guarantee that voters will accept what they are being sold. Republicans treated their overwhelming financial advantage as if exposure itself were persuasion. According to Rothman, Democrats instead prevailed through stronger messages and campaigns rooted in recognizable communities.

He pointed to Democratic victories by Annie Andrews in South Carolina, Abdul El-Sayed in Michigan and Gina Hinojosa in Texas, along with Mary Peltola’s win in Alaska. Peltola’s “Fish, Family, Freedom” slogan compressed local identity and political appeal into three memorable words. Julia Letlow, meanwhile, narrowly became the first Republican woman elected to represent Louisiana in the Senate, evidence that the lesson is not simply that one party possesses an automatic communications advantage. Candidates still have to give particular voters a reason to choose them.

That is what the spending numbers obscure. The institutional power belonged to the billionaires, party committees and campaign professionals deciding where the money went and what it said. Voters were not confused by failing to obey the advertising imbalance. The people controlling Republican resources made an expensive strategic choice, and the candidates bore the consequence.

Republicans flooded a shrinking channel

Rothman also identified a basic failure of distribution. Republicans concentrated heavily on conventional television advertising in Texas and Ohio, even as audiences have fragmented across digital and social platforms. At Sony, he wrote, five employees now work in digital and social marketing worldwide for every one assigned to legacy media.

Viewers of the Ohio State and University of Texas football games on the Saturday before the election were bombarded with negative advertisements targeting Sherrod Brown and James Talarico. Both men nevertheless became senators-elect by comfortable margins.

The failure was not a shortage of attacks. It was the assumption that repeatedly placing those attacks in front of a large television audience would produce the intended political response. Republican strategists used the mechanics of mass broadcasting long after mass attention had splintered—and relied on negativity without establishing a compelling affirmative case.

That makes the “box office bomb” comparison more than a Hollywood flourish. A studio can saturate television with trailers, but it cannot compel people to buy tickets. A political donor can dominate the airwaves, but not dictate how citizens interpret the pitch. Musk’s reported spending bought extraordinary access to voters’ screens. It did not buy their allegiance.

The post-defeat scramble for an explanation

Donald Trump reportedly responded to the midterm setbacks by moving between denial and anger. That reaction shifts attention away from the decisions that produced the losses: candidates selected, messages approved, media purchased and a governing brand that money alone could not make attractive.

An emerging argument that the United States is returning to “four-party politics” offers another way to interpret the result. Catholic University professor emeritus John Kenneth White described Democrats as divided between moderates—whom he credited as the 2026 majority-makers—and socialists seeking a larger government. Republicans, in his account, are splitting between Trump loyalists and Reagan-era conservatives.

That framework may describe genuine ideological tensions, but it does not erase the immediate verdict delivered by the campaigns themselves. Republicans possessed the larger advertising arsenal and deployed it according to a dated theory of voter behavior. Democrats, Rothman argued, more effectively matched message, candidate and audience.

The revealing fact is not that money suddenly stopped mattering. It plainly determined who could blanket major broadcasts and who could afford a quarter-billion-dollar Senate advertising advantage. The limit appeared elsewhere: concentrated wealth could amplify the Republican message, but it could not make that message persuasive. Republicans did not lose because voters somehow failed to notice them. They spent lavishly to ensure voters did—and many voters rejected what they saw.
