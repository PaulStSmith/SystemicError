Right-wing Republicans fear antisemitism bill stops spreading of anti-Jewish myth: report

A House is struggling to pass a bipartisan antisemitism bill because some right-wing lawmakers are concerned that it would stop them from stating the foundational myth of antisemitism.

The Antisemitism Awareness Act passed the House easily in the last congressional session despite bipartisan concerns about how the bill defined antisemitism, but Democrats are wary of supporting the measure as the Trump administration attacks universities and visa holders over alleged antisemitism on campus.

Now some Republicans are expressing other concerns, reported Punchbowl News.

"During a contentious Senate HELP Committee markup on Thursday, Sen. Rand Paul (R-KY) joined with the panel’s Democrats to adopt a number of amendments that imperiled the bill’s status in the GOP-controlled Senate," the outlet reported. "The most prominent amendment stated that criticism of Israel isn’t antisemitic."

"The issues don’t stop there. Senate HELP Committee Chair Bill Cassidy (R-LA) inserted a manager’s amendment intended to address right-wing Christian concerns that the bill impeded on their free speech to allege Jews killed Jesus," the report added. "But since the markup adjourned last week without a final vote, that amendment hasn’t been adopted yet."

The deicide myth can be traced to the origins of Christianity and has been used to justify the Holocaust and other miseries inflicted upon Jewish people, although Jewish individuals played no role in Christ's crucifixion.

Cassidy pledged last week to vote again to advance the bill out of committee, but there would not be enough GOP support if Paul continues to side with Democrats.

Rep. Jamie Raskin (D-MD), who voted for the previous antisemitism bill, urged Democrats to oppose this legislation, saying it would help President Donald Trump "undermine American democracy" with his attacks on higher education.

Reps. Thomas Massie (R-KY) and Marjorie Taylor Greene (R-GA) said Sunday they would oppose a separate bill introduced by Rep. Mike Lawler (R-NY) that would penalize international governmental organizations from boycotting Israel, and House GOP leadership removed that bill from this week’s floor schedule.

Our Analysis:

Political Maneuvering at the Expense of Combating Antisemitism

In a display of political irony that could only be conjured up in the convoluted corridors of Capitol Hill, a bipartisan effort to combat antisemitism has devolved into a quagmire of partisan bickering and ideological posturing. The Antisemitism Awareness Act, a piece of legislation that should ostensibly unify lawmakers across the aisle in opposition to a virulent strain of hatred, is instead being hamstrung by a combination of right-wing obstinance and strategic concerns about the broader political implications of the bill.

The Paradox of Partisan Antisemitism Legislation

At the heart of the controversy are amendments and concerns that would be comedic if they weren't so deeply troubling. Sen. Rand Paul (R-KY), often a libertarian maverick, has found common ground with Democrats—not in a noble bipartisan effort, but in a bid to ensure that criticism of Israel is not conflated with antisemitism. While this distinction is crucial for maintaining the integrity of legitimate political discourse, it's telling that this is where the lines are drawn, highlighting the political tightrope walked between combating hatred and preserving free speech.

Even more bewildering is the amendment proposed by Senate HELP Committee Chair Bill Cassidy (R-LA), aimed at appeasing right-wing Christian groups concerned that the bill might infringe upon their "right" to perpetuate the deicide myth—an age-old antisemitic trope that has fueled centuries of Jewish persecution. That this is a sticking point in the legislation is a stark reminder of the deeply entrenched bigotry that still permeates certain segments of American politics.

The Shadow of Trump's America

The manipulation of antisemitism legislation for political ends is a dangerous game. Rep. Jamie Raskin (D-MD) has articulated a growing concern among Democrats that supporting this bill, in its current contentious state, could inadvertently bolster President Trump's broader attacks on American democracy—particularly his administration's targeting of universities and visa holders under the guise of combating antisemitism. This is a classic Trumpian maneuver: weaponizing legitimate concerns for ulterior motives, in this case, undermining the autonomy of higher education and restricting immigration.

Furthermore, Reps. Thomas Massie (R-KY) and Marjorie Taylor Greene (R-GA)'s opposition to a separate bill penalizing international governmental organizations from boycotting Israel underscores the intricate dance of allegiance and opposition within the GOP. The removal of this bill from the week’s floor schedule by House GOP leadership signals a strategic retreat, likely born from the realization that the optics of intra-party conflict over such a sensitive issue could be politically damaging.

Conclusion

What we are witnessing is not a solemn legislative effort to address the scourge of antisemitism but a political circus where the fight against hatred is secondary to partisan maneuvering and ideological purity tests. The Antisemitism Awareness Act, in theory, should be a rallying cry for all lawmakers to stand united against bigotry. Instead, it has become a battleground for political one-upmanship, with the very real consequences of antisemitism left languishing in the wings.

The tragedy here is not just in the legislative impasse but in the message it sends. At a time when antisemitic incidents are on the rise, the inability of Congress to pass clear, unambiguous legislation condemning and combating antisemitism is a damning indictment of the current state of American politics. It's a stark reminder that, in the eyes of some lawmakers, political gain trumps the fight against hate.
