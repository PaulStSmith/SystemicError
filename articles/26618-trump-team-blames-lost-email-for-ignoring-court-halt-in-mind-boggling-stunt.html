Trump team blames lost email for ignoring court halt in mind-boggling stunt

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 14, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power was at the topAn email is not an explanationPublic money, personal propagandaThe cost of engineered dysfunctionA system built to evade ownership

What happened

AlterNet reports that U.S. District Judge Jennifer Rochon ordered the Trump administration to stop a $20 million Department of Homeland Security advertising campaign praising President Donald Trump, including an ad titled “God Made Trump.” On Sunday, October 11, 2026, she halted the vendor purchase order, stayed the Office of Management and Budget’s apportionment, and barred DHS from spending congressionally appropriated border-security funds on the campaign.

The order took effect at 6:45 p.m., but the ads continued Sunday night and into early Monday. In a status report, Justice Department lawyers said government counsel emailed the order to agency counsel at 7:58 p.m., but agency counsel did not see that email until Monday morning. DOJ apologized; the reporting does not establish whether the continued broadcasts were intentional or merely the product of a failed response.

AlterNet also cites reports that Trump personally directed his budget director to fund the ads and that Customs and Border Protection leaders did not approve using their budget for them. Those claims were attributed to unnamed people familiar with the matter and federal sources.

The power was at the top

The operative decision was not made by a distracted DHS employee. According to the reporting cited by AlterNet, Trump instructed his budget director to use public money for advertisements glorifying Trump. OMB then apportioned the money, DHS placed the order, and a private vendor distributed the product.

That chain matters. Congress appropriated the funds for border security. Executive officials allegedly redirected $20 million toward presidential self-promotion, while CBP leaders were reportedly left outside the decision. The people controlling the money exercised the power; the administrators whose budgets supplied it absorbed the consequence.

An email is not an explanation

DOJ’s account may explain how agency counsel failed to learn of the order promptly. It does not explain why the administration lacked a working procedure for stopping a nationwide advertising campaign when a federal judge issued an immediate restraining order.

Reducing the episode to an unread email misdirects attention from institutional responsibility to clerical mishap. Government counsel received the order at 6:46 p.m. and did not forward it to agency counsel until 7:58 p.m. The administration then depended on somebody noticing an email to execute a time-sensitive judicial command. Even if no one deliberately defied the court, that is a breakdown created by the government’s own chain of command.

Public money, personal propaganda

The central abuse alleged here is not merely waste. It is the conversion of appropriated public funds into political tribute. An advertisement called “God Made Trump” does not resemble a neutral border-security message; as described, it elevates the officeholder whose administration controlled the spending.

That collapses a distinction essential to public administration: federal agencies serve statutory purposes, not the personal image of the president. When presidential aides can commandeer an agency budget without its operational leadership signing off, appropriation becomes suggestion and administration becomes patronage.

The cost of engineered dysfunction

The continued broadcasts created a second injury after the disputed spending itself. A court issued a specific order, yet the executive branch could not promptly translate that order into action. Whether caused by indifference, incompetence, or inadequate procedure, the foreseeable result was the same: taxpayer-funded praise continued after a judge said it must stop.

Calling this “mind-boggling” risks making it sound mysterious. Nothing about the structure is mysterious. Authority was concentrated above the officials responsible for the affected budget, while accountability dissolved across lawyers, agency counsel, DHS, OMB, and a contractor. Power traveled downward quickly; responsibility traveled nowhere.

A system built to evade ownership

This episode reveals a durable governing pattern: senior officials make the consequential choice, subordinate institutions execute it, and failure is later narrated as an administrative misunderstanding. The unread email becomes the story because it is easier to ridicule than the underlying allocation of power.

The sharper question is why public institutions were organized to obey a reported presidential demand for self-promotional spending more effectively than they obeyed a federal court’s order to stop it. That imbalance is not comic bureaucratic chaos. It is what government looks like when loyalty to the person at the top outruns loyalty to appropriations, procedure, and institutional responsibility.
