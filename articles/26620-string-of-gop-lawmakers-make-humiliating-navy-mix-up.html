String of GOP lawmakers make humiliating Navy mix-up

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 14, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that several Republican officials marked the U.S. Navy’s 251st birthday on Tuesday with images of foreign warships. Georgia Gov. Brian Kemp and Rep. Tim Moore used pictures of Indian vessels; Ohio state Rep. Mike Dovilla, a longtime naval intelligence officer, used Russian ships; and Border Patrol official Jeffrey J. Dinise featured a Russian Kirov-class battlecruiser. Wall Street Journal editor Dave Brown identified the errors.

The posts paired patriotic tributes with basic failures of recognition. Kemp and Dovilla were campaigning in reportedly close races, while Moore held a substantial polling lead. The article does not establish who selected the images or whether the officials reviewed them before publication.

AlterNet places the blunders against a more consequential record: unusually long deployments and reported hardship during President Donald Trump’s war with Iran, the transfer of naval assets away from the Pacific, and Trump’s proposals for “Trump-class” battleships and a return to steam-powered systems—ideas the article says experts and critics have called obsolete, inefficient and wasteful.

Patriotism as a content template

The humiliation is not merely that politicians mistook Indian and Russian ships for American ones. It is that the imagery was apparently interchangeable because the Navy itself was incidental. The posts needed gray hulls, flags and ceremonial gratitude. Accuracy was optional.

That is how institutional reverence becomes political branding. The officials claimed identification with military service while publishing material that undercut the competence their messages were designed to project. Dovilla’s mistake is especially damaging because he invoked more than 20 years in naval intelligence as a credential. The reporting does not show that he personally chose the image, but attaching his name to it was still his political decision.

Who actually holds power

These officials are not powerless social-media users. Kemp governs a state, Moore serves in Congress, Dovilla holds state office, and Dinise is a federal official. Their communications carry public authority even when staff members may assemble them.

Trump holds vastly greater power over the Navy than any of them. According to AlterNet, his administration has directed deployments, moved major assets between theaters and promoted costly changes in fleet design and propulsion. Confusing one warship for another is embarrassing. Deploying ships in ways that strain crews or leave a strategic region without an aircraft carrier has material consequences.

The joke obscures the governing record

AlterNet’s framing foregrounds a comic procession of image errors. That is easy political content: ridicule requires little explanation, and the screenshots supply their own punch line. But the article’s later details describe a far more serious failure of stewardship.

The USS Lincoln reportedly remained at sea for a record 265 days amid poor conditions, low morale and attempts by crew members to go overboard. Other vessels reportedly faced food shortages. Moving the USS George Washington from the Pacific to relieve the Lincoln, AlterNet says, left the region without an aircraft carrier as China pressed its interests.

Those outcomes cannot be blamed on careless graphics. They flow from decisions made by civilian leaders with command authority. Fixating on foreign ships in birthday posts risks converting a dispute over military management into a harmless story about Republican incompetence online.

Spectacle over capacity

Trump’s proposed “golden fleet” fits the same pattern at a larger scale: martial aesthetics substituted for operational judgment. The article reports that he promoted “Trump-class” battleships as exceptionally powerful even though experts described battleships as obsolete. His proposed return to steam power likewise drew criticism as inefficient and wasteful.

The evidence presented does not establish why Trump favored those ideas. It does show a governing preference for grandiose symbols while sailors and existing ships reportedly bore the costs of extended deployments. The contrast is between advertised strength and maintained capacity—and only the latter keeps a navy functional.

The systemic error

Political militarism often asks the public to admire armed force without examining how political leaders use it. Ceremonial posts reward the performance of loyalty; deployment choices, procurement schemes and crew conditions demand scrutiny of power.

The foreign-warship blunders expose the emptiness of the performance, but embarrassment is the smallest consequence. The larger pattern is a political class using the Navy as patriotic scenery while the administration controlling it makes decisions that, according to the reporting, exhaust personnel, disrupt strategic coverage and prioritize presidential spectacle over military judgment.
