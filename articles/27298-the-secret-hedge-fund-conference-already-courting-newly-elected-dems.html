The secret hedge-fund conference already courting newly elected Dems

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosNovember 16, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Influence starts before the oathThe convenient fiction of the corrupted individualCelebration without leverage

Newly elected Democrats were already being courted by wealthy interests before taking office. According to AlterNet, Majority Democrats—an organization financed primarily by hedge-fund billionaire Steve Mandel—held an invitation-only “What’s Next” conference in Denver last week, bringing Democratic newcomers together with political consultants while excluding labor, civil-rights, environmental, Black and Hispanic leaders.

That guest list is the story. Access was extended to professionals paid to shape political decisions, but not to major constituencies whose votes and organizing helped produce Democratic victories. The imbalance does not prove that any attendee surrendered a principle. It does show who possessed the money and infrastructure to begin influencing new officeholders immediately.

Influence starts before the oath

Democrats celebrating Senator-elect Scott Colom’s victory in Mississippi and Governor-elect Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins’s win in Alaska face a familiar problem: winning an election changes who occupies office, but it does not dismantle the influence industry surrounding that office.

The newly elected officials were seven weeks away from the reported January 3 congressional swearing-in when AlterNet described the Denver gathering. Yet the contest over their priorities had already begun. Corporations, Wall Street lobbyists, cryptocurrency and artificial-intelligence interests, corporate law and public-relations firms, CEOs and billionaire technology executives do not wait for a new Congress to settle in. Their advantage lies partly in arriving early, offering relationships and policy frameworks before competing constituencies can establish comparable access.

Organizations presenting themselves as representatives of “moderate Democrats,” including Third Way, operate within that struggle over the party’s direction. AlterNet argues that such groups, supported by corporate and Wall Street money, seek to preserve the existing distribution of wealth and power rather than pursue progressive change. Majority Democrats’ conference supplied a concrete example of the machinery involved: concentrated financing, private access and professional political intermediaries.

Calling the conference “secret” risks making discretion itself sound like the central offense. The more consequential fact is not merely that the meeting was invitation-only. It is that money purchased an organized channel to incoming public officials while workers and civil-rights and environmental constituencies reportedly had no seat in the room.

The convenient fiction of the corrupted individual

The usual Democratic postmortem blames particular lawmakers for becoming timid, divided or beholden to donors. Joe Manchin and Kyrsten Sinema serve as ready shorthand for that history. But reducing political failure to defective personalities lets the influence system disappear behind its most visible participants.

New lawmakers enter an institution surrounded by donors, lobbyists, consultants and advocacy organizations with the resources to define what counts as realistic. Those actors do not need to issue crude orders. They can narrow the available choices, supply research and language, confer legitimacy and make policies threatening concentrated wealth appear reckless or electorally impossible.

That is why personal integrity, while relevant, is an inadequate safeguard. Newly elected Democrats may believe they can accept access to wealthy networks without becoming accountable to them. Some may succeed. But the political problem is structural: one side can finance private conferences and sustained professional persuasion, while less wealthy constituencies must fight simply to be heard.

The conference therefore should not be treated as evidence that every participating Democrat has been “bought.” The supplied reporting does not establish that. It establishes an attempted cultivation—and an unequal field on which that cultivation occurs.

Celebration without leverage

AlterNet’s argument places responsibility on the voters and organizers who produced the Democratic victories: electoral support must become continuing pressure after the campaign ends. Franklin D. Roosevelt’s reported response to a voter demanding that he deliver on his program—“Ma’am, I want to do those things, but you must make me”—captures the distinction between holding office and possessing enough political force to govern against entrenched interests.

That principle should not become an excuse for elected officials. Voters are not responsible for rescuing politicians from every lobbyist they voluntarily entertain. Officeholders retain agency over whose counsel they seek, whose money they accept and which promises they abandon. But organized public pressure can alter the costs of those decisions, giving lawmakers a reason to resist influence that otherwise arrives with few penalties.

The same conflict appears in the reported debate over Senate Democratic leadership. AlterNet praises Chuck Schumer’s performance but argues that his longevity and proximity to Wall Street and corporate Democrats make Senator Chris Van Hollen the preferable leader for a more progressive caucus. That is not simply a disagreement over generation or style. It is a dispute over which relationships will shape the party when donor access collides with campaign commitments.

Democratic victories created an opportunity for policy change. They did not settle who will control the winners. In Denver, wealthy interests reportedly began making their case behind closed doors while organized labor and several core Democratic constituencies remained outside. The unanswered question is whether the officials invited into that room will treat such access as one perspective among many—or allow it to define the limits of what their victories are permitted to accomplish.
