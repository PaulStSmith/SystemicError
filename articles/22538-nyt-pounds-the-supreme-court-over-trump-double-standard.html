NYT pounds the Supreme Court over Trump double standard

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Trump’s Tariffs Are Not a Policy Dispute. They Are a Test of Who Gets to Ignore the Law.

The Real Power Is in the Oval Office

The core fact in this story is not Supreme Court inconsistency. It is that Donald Trump is imposing tariffs the Court has already rejected, and he is doing it anyway. The executive branch is not confused here. It is acting with open contempt for legal limits and treating damage to workers and consumers as an acceptable cost of unilateral power.

The economic record in the source is blunt enough: the tariff rate fell to 7 percent after the Court threw out Trump’s original policy, then climbed again to 11 percent as he kept pushing. That is not a legal abstraction. It is a president using state power to raise prices and manufacture chaos because he can.

The Court’s Delay Is Not Neutral

The Times is right to note the double standard: Biden’s student-loan efforts were met with quick judicial intervention, while Trump’s defiance sometimes gets time and hesitation from the Republican-appointed majority. That difference matters because the Court is not merely interpreting law in the abstract. It is deciding, through speed and posture, whose violations are stopped immediately and whose are allowed to keep doing harm.

Delay is not a procedural footnote. In Trump’s hands, delay is enforcement failure. If the Court already knows the tariffs are illegal and still “sometimes takes its time or demurs,” then it is not simply being cautious. It is tolerating executive lawlessness long enough for the policy to do its work.

The Article Names the Problem, Then Softens It

The source gets close to the truth, then steps back from it. It says Trump is breaking the law. Good. It says Congress and the judiciary need to respond. Also true. But the framing still invites readers to see this as a dispute among institutions, rather than what it is: an executive branch testing how much punishment it can inflict before anyone stops it.

That matters because the harm is already visible. The tariffs “hurt the American economy,” raise prices, and have not delivered the manufacturing revival Trump promised. Yet the language of “trade war” and “policy in flux” can sanitize what is really happening: deliberate economic coercion dressed up as national strength.

Canada Is Not the Story’s Villain

The source notes that Canada is “standing up to his bullying,” which is the right frame. Trump is the aggressor. The foreign government refusing to kneel is not the provocation; it is the response to provocation. The usual habit in political coverage is to blur that line until force looks like negotiation and resistance looks like escalation.

That blur serves Trump. It turns a president imposing illegal tariffs into just another participant in a market drama. He is not. He is using federal power to impose costs on people who did not authorize him to do it.

Big Government for Him, Not for the Public

Ed Gresser’s point cuts through another layer of propaganda: the same administration that whips up panic about socialism is practicing aggressive state management of the economy. This is not market conservatism. It is selective authoritarian economics, where the government reaches hard into commerce when it benefits the ruler and pretends restraint is a virtue when ordinary people need protection.

That is the pattern. Trump’s politics are not about shrinking the state. They are about weaponizing it. Tariffs, company stakes, legal defiance, price pressure: these are not contradictions. They are the operating system.

What This Reveals

The larger lesson is simple: American institutions fail when they treat obvious abuse as a matter of tone, timing, or partisan symmetry. Trump is not being misunderstood. He is being permitted. The Court’s hesitation, Congress’s passivity, and elite commentary’s fixation on process all help normalize the same basic fact: power can ignore law until someone with authority actually stops it.
