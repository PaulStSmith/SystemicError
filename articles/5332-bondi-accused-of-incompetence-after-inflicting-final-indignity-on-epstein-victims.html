Bondi accused of 'incompetence' after inflicting 'final indignity' on Epstein victims

U.S. Attorney General Pam Bondi attends a House Judiciary Committee hearing on oversight of the Justice Department to testify, on Capitol Hill in Washington, D.C., U.S., February 11, 2026. REUTERS/Kent Nishimura

Pam Bondi’s “sneering” performance before the House Judiciary Committee on Tuesday prompted the editors of the New York Times editorial board to lambast the attorney general on Saturday morning.

In a brutal editorial, the editors went far beyond Bondi’s abrasive refusal to answer questions, choosing instead to respond to legitimate questions with personal attacks and complaints, to how the Department of Justice under her watch has done nothing but botch— perhaps intentionally — the handling of the Jeffrey Epstein files.

Worse still, they noted, was her treatment of Epstein’s victims, some of whom sat in the gallery, where they watched her ignore their plight during a nationally televised hearing.

“Ms. Bondi’s performance was more than just political theater. It was a final indignity in a process that has victimized Mr. Epstein’s victims all over again,” they wrote. “Under the guise of transparency, the Justice Department has managed to expose the victims to further humiliation while shielding the powerful behind a wall of redactions.”

Making their case, the editors noted, “The department’s release of these files has been dominated by incompetence. Ms. Bondi has long had the authority to make them public, but she spent months refusing and yielded only after Congress forced her hand,” adding, “Instead, in a grotesque failure, the D.O.J. uploaded dozens of unredacted images to its website, including nude photographs of young women and possibly teenagers.”

That led Epstein survivor Annie Farmer to point out it is “hard to imagine a more egregious way of not protecting victims,” and the editors to add, “Ms. Bondi’s department shattered the trust of women who had already been betrayed by the legal system once before.”

“Ms. Bondi’s refusal to look the survivors in the eye was symbolic of a broader failure,” the editors pointed out before dropping the hammer with: “The Department of Justice had an opportunity to finally prioritize the women who were preyed upon by Mr. Epstein and his circle. Instead, through a combination of malice and incompetence, it has done the opposite. It has stripped the victims of their privacy while wrapping perpetrators in a cloak of state secrecy.”

Our Analysis:

The Disgrace of Pam Bondi and the DOJ’s Handling of the Epstein Case

The editorial by the New York Times on Pam Bondi’s conduct before the House Judiciary Committee not only exposes the inherent flaws within the Department of Justice under her tenure but also highlights a grotesque mockery of justice, especially concerning the Jeffrey Epstein scandal. Let’s dissect the layers of this appalling narrative, where incompetence intertwines with callous indifference, rendering the victims of Epstein’s heinous acts secondary to political maneuvering and bureaucratic shielding.

The Mockery of Transparency and Justice

Bondi’s performance—and let’s use that term loosely here—was a stark display of the systemic failure that plagues our justice system. It’s one thing to be non-cooperative in a legal setting, but to elevate such behavior to an art form of sneering disdain for legitimate inquiry is both repugnant and telling. The New York Times editorial board rightly lambasted her for not only her abrasive refusal to answer questions but for her outright attacks and complaints in response to legitimate concerns. This isn’t just poor form; it’s a deliberate affront to the concept of accountability.

A Betrayal of the Victims

The mistreatment of Jeffrey Epstein’s victims is a blaring siren calling attention to the Department of Justice’s dismal failure under Bondi’s watch. These individuals, already subjected to unimaginable trauma, were forced to witness a national spectacle where their plights were ignored, and their abuser’s accomplices shielded. The editorial points out that through a so-called guise of transparency, the DOJ managed to inflict additional humiliation on the victims, all the while protecting the powerful with a “wall of redactions.”

Incompetence or Malice? The Unforgivable Exposure

The revelation that the Department of Justice, under Bondi’s command, released unredacted images, including nude photographs of young women and possibly teenagers, is appalling. This isn’t just incompetence; it’s a flagrant violation of privacy and a potential act of re-victimization. The fact that these documents were released publicly, after months of Bondi’s refusal and only under congressional pressure, speaks volumes about the priorities of her office. It’s a stark reminder that when forced to choose between transparency and the protection of victims, her department chose neither.

The Final Indignity

The editorial’s conclusion that Bondi’s refusal to even acknowledge the survivors, some of whom were present during the hearing, symbolizes a broader failure is a damning indictment of her tenure. The Department of Justice had a prime opportunity to prioritize the victims, to offer them some measure of justice, and to hold the powerful accountable. Instead, they were met with malice, incompetence, or perhaps a toxic mix of both, that served to strip them of their dignity while cloaking perpetrators in secrecy.

Conclusion: A Call for Accountability

The handling of the Jeffrey Epstein files under Pam Bondi’s watch is a clear example of how not to administer justice. It reflects a system that prioritizes the protection of the powerful over the rights and dignities of victims. The New York Times editorial does not mince words in its critique, and neither should we. This isn’t just a failure of one individual or office; it’s indicative of a systemic rot that requires immediate and decisive action to rectify.

The victims of Jeffrey Epstein deserved better. They deserved a Department of Justice that prioritized their healing and justice over political gamesmanship and bureaucratic cover-ups. Pam Bondi’s tenure and actions, as laid bare by this editorial, should not only be a cause for concern but a rallying cry for systemic reform. Let’s not let this be the end of the story but rather the beginning of a much-needed overhaul of a broken system.
