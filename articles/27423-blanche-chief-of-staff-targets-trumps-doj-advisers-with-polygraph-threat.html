Blanche chief of staff targets Trump’s DOJ advisers with polygraph threat

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 24, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Loyalty enforcement reaches the loyalistsDOJ calls it investigative integrityAn ouster over stalled leak investigationsPolygraphs for critics, exceptions for allies

President Donald Trump’s Justice Department has turned its suspicion inward. Todd Blanche’s chief of staff warned senior DOJ officials that polygraph tests could be used to identify people leaking information to journalists, Bloomberg reported Monday. The prospective targets are not career employees at the department’s margins, but advisers central to the administration’s effort to bring federal law enforcement into closer alignment with the White House.

That distinction matters. This is not merely an argument over handling sensitive information. It is an administration that has already carried out personnel purges and pursued politically charged prosecutions threatening its own appointees with a notoriously coercive screening tool as distrust spreads through the institution they helped remake.

Loyalty enforcement reaches the loyalists

According to Bloomberg reporter Ben Penn, Blanche’s chief of staff, Chad Mizelle, announced the possibility of polygraphs during a meeting with top officials. The warning reached the “core group of advisers” involved in Trump’s overhaul of the department.

The reported threat exposes the logic of governing through personal loyalty: compliance does not produce trust. It produces escalating demands for proof of compliance. Once DOJ’s institutional independence is treated as an obstacle to presidential control, even officials selected to carry out that project can become suspected liabilities.

The timing sharpened the message. Bloomberg reported that the discussion came as Trump was scheduled to visit the Justice Department later Monday to deliver a speech. The president’s appearance and the internal leak crackdown place the department’s public alignment with Trump beside its private anxiety over who is speaking outside approved channels.

DOJ calls it investigative integrity

The department presented the meeting as a defense of legitimate law-enforcement work.

“Jordan convened members of leadership offices to remind them that leaking information related to ongoing investigations undermines the integrity of this Department,” DOJ spokesperson Emily Covington told Bloomberg. She said disclosures could endanger cases involving national security and public safety, impede efforts to hold criminals accountable, and distract law-enforcement personnel from their work.

Protecting active investigations is a real institutional responsibility. But the reported campaign extends beyond a neutral reminder about confidentiality. The administration has been pursuing federal employees over disclosures used in reporting critical of Trump, according to Bloomberg. That context makes the central power question unavoidable: who decides whether a disclosure threatens an investigation, embarrasses the president, or does both?

The administration controls that classification and the machinery used to investigate suspected leakers. Employees facing polygraphs do not possess comparable leverage. Describing the crackdown solely as protection for law enforcement obscures the coercive structure: political leadership can subject subordinates to interrogation while defining unauthorized scrutiny of itself as institutional harm.

An ouster over stalled leak investigations

The National Security Division has already paid a personnel price. Bloomberg reported that Blanche removed division chief John Eisenberg earlier this month after progress stalled on leak investigations, including efforts to subpoena journalists.

That episode indicates the pressure is not confined to rhetoric. A senior official was ousted amid dissatisfaction with the pace of investigations aimed at tracing information delivered to the press. Subpoenaing journalists would move the campaign beyond disciplining government employees and toward using federal power to identify their contacts.

The administration may characterize these measures as ordinary protection of sensitive material. But the reported pattern is narrower and more politically revealing: stories critical of the president prompted investigations, insufficient progress prompted an ouster, and mistrust within leadership prompted a polygraph warning.

Polygraphs for critics, exceptions for allies

The Justice Department is also not acting alone. The Pentagon under Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth subjected members of the military’s Joint Staff to polygraph examinations over leaks to journalists, according to a September report by The New York Times. Those disclosures reportedly included information about shortages of crucial munitions created by the Iran war.

At the FBI, Director Kash Patel reportedly allowed pro-Trump appointees—including Deputy Director Dan Bongino—to assume office without completing mandatory polygraph tests. Yet Patel required FBI employees to undergo lie-detector examinations after criticism of his own conduct began reaching the press.

Taken together, those accounts show polygraphs functioning less as a consistently applied security safeguard than as an instrument imposed downward. Favored appointees reportedly received exemptions; employees connected to embarrassing disclosures received tests. The discrepancy does not establish what any individual intended, but it does reveal whose credibility the administration presumes and whose it makes conditional.

Now even the officials enlisted to align DOJ with the White House are being told they may have to prove their fidelity. An institution reorganized around loyalty has arrived at its predictable destination: political leaders policing the people they chose, because obedience secured through fear never produces confidence—only another list of suspects.
