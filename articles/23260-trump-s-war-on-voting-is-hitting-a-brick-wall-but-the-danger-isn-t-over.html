Trump's war on voting is hitting a brick wall —but the danger isn't over

Based on an article by Brad Reed, Common Dreams

Paulo SantosSeptember 9, 20263 min read

Trump’s Mail-Ballot Sabotage Runs Into Time, Not Principle

The reporting describes a familiar Trump operation: attack the mechanics of voting, inject confusion, then hope institutional delay does the rest. This time, states have already started mailing ballots, courts are blocking parts of the scheme, and election officials are openly saying the president’s order is not practically workable. That does not make the danger smaller. It just means the disruption is now being forced through other channels.

The Power Is Still With the States

The first fact worth naming is where the actual authority sits. States run elections, and many of them have already begun sending ballots. Wisconsin and North Carolina are moving. Nine more states are set to mail ballots within two weeks. Trump can issue orders, posture for the cameras, and pressure the Supreme Court, but he does not command the election machinery the way he wants to.

That is why the Washington Post report matters: not because Trump has suddenly become harmless, but because his power is running into deadlines, procedures, and the stubborn fact that ballots are already in motion.

The Decision Was His

This is not a mystery or a bureaucratic misunderstanding. Trump signed an executive order in March instructing the Postal Service not to deliver ballots in states that have not given the federal government access to voter lists. That is a deliberate act aimed at making voting harder under the banner of compliance and order.

The idea is simple: create a federal choke point, let states scramble, and pretend the resulting chaos is an accident. It is not. It is political interference dressed up as administrative concern.

Confusion Is the Point

The reporting makes clear that this effort does more than risk a court fight. It creates anxiety before a single vote is counted. Pima County’s recorder says her office is flooded with worried calls and cannot give reassuring answers. An elections manager in Clark County says voters want to know whether the rules will change, and officials have to tell them they do not know yet.

That is the damage. Trump does not need to successfully stop every ballot to succeed at poisoning the process. He only needs enough uncertainty to reduce participation and enough noise to give bad-faith actors an excuse later. Ben Hovland’s warning is the right one: this can dissuade voters and manufacture pretexts for attacking the result.

Even Republicans Are Admitting the Obvious

One of the more revealing details comes from Don Millis, the Republican chair of the Wisconsin Elections Commission. He says there is “no practical way” for clerks to comply, and he adds that the scheme is likely to hurt Republican turnout more than Democratic turnout.

That matters because it punctures the usual fake frame that this is some neutral election-integrity crusade. When a Republican election official says the plan is unworkable and self-defeating, the story is no longer about wisdom or fairness. It is about ideological vandalism that even its own side recognizes as operationally stupid.

The Courts Are a Brake, Not a Solution

Trump is pushing the Supreme Court to let the order take effect. The courts may stop the most immediate version of the attack, but that does not erase the underlying tactic. Delay itself becomes a weapon when ballots are about to go out. So does the mere possibility of intervention. The goal is not only to win a ruling. It is to keep everyone guessing long enough to degrade trust.

That is why the language of “running out of options” should not reassure anyone. It suggests the machinery of sabotage is being forced to adapt, not abandon its purpose.

The Larger Pattern

This is what authoritarian politics looks like in a procedural democracy: not just outright theft, but manufactured instability, intentional confusion, and pressure on institutions to normalize the abnormal. Trump is not merely criticizing mail voting. He is using the office to contaminate confidence in the vote itself.

The deeper story is not whether one executive order gets blocked. It is that a president can weaponize uncertainty against participation and then count on the political system to describe it as a dispute over rules. That framing is too polite. This is deliberate obstruction aimed at the electorate, and the cost is paid by voters who are told to keep participating while the system is being set on fire around them.
