New Trump-era movie is mind-bending, pretentious and a total box office bomb

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 7, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Digger, a $180 million climate-disaster film starring Tom Cruise, failed at the box office. The film depicts an oilman whose Greenland drilling triggers ecological catastrophe, then reveals that this narrative is a play performed in a future already devastated by those decisions.

The review praises the film’s climate politics, elaborate structure and visual ambition but argues that its slow pace, theatrical excess and fixation on elite depravity blunt its impact. It concludes that The Day After Tomorrow, a commercially successful 2004 disaster film, remains the more effective climate parable.

AlterNet places that comparison in the context of President Donald Trump’s dismissal of human-caused climate change and removal of government scientists, while reporting that his administration is pursuing more extraction in Greenland.

Power is not a personality flaw

The fictional Digger is not dangerous merely because he is arrogant. He owns an oil enterprise, has access to the president and can shape the public account of a worsening disaster. The scientists around him possess expertise but not command. Their warnings become optional because political and economic authority belongs to the men creating the danger.

That is the relevant structure: industry makes the profitable decision, executive power protects it, and experts are permitted to speak only until their knowledge threatens the arrangement. Calling the resulting conduct “ignorance” risks understating what the review itself describes. Once warnings have been delivered repeatedly, continued drilling is a choice made under conditions of knowledge.

The review finds the film’s central evasion

AlterNet’s strongest criticism is not that Digger is pretentious. It is that the film spends more attention examining powerful perpetrators than depicting those forced to absorb the consequences. That imbalance grants elites psychological complexity while the public becomes scenery in its own catastrophe.

The reported scene blaming ordinary people for needing oil exposes the deeper failure. Consumers did not design the energy system, control extraction capital or gain presidential access. Treating dependence as consent transfers responsibility from institutions with decision-making power to people whose daily survival has been organized around decisions they did not make.

Spectacle cannot substitute for political clarity

The film’s nested-play device apparently asks how future societies should remember those who ruined their world. But formal complexity can obscure a simple chain of agency: executives pursued extraction, political leaders enabled them, scientists warned them, and everyone else inherited the damage.

That does not make artistic experimentation illegitimate. It makes clarity politically consequential. A climate film that turns accountable decisions into a maze of perspectives can reproduce the same fog that protects power outside the theater. The issue becomes humanity’s tragedy rather than the foreseeable result of choices made by identifiable institutions.

A box-office bomb has limited reach

AlterNet contrasts Digger with The Day After Tomorrow, citing research that found the older film significantly affected climate-risk perceptions, behavioral intentions and policy priorities among roughly 21 million Americans who saw it theatrically. The review does not establish that simpler storytelling always produces political change, but it does establish the practical problem facing Digger: a message cannot shape a mass audience that largely does not encounter it.

Commercial failure does not disprove the film’s argument. It does, however, expose the weakness of treating a lavish Hollywood production as a substitute for politics. A $180 million warning that cannot hold public attention remains an expensive warning, not an intervention in the institutions authorizing extraction.

The system survives by spreading responsibility downward

The larger pattern is familiar: concentrated power makes the consequential decisions, then responsibility is diluted across “humanity,” consumer appetite and personal hypocrisy. That framing is useful to officeholders and industries because it converts governance into guilt and policy into lifestyle.

AlterNet identifies that evasion inside Digger, even as its review sometimes reduces the problem to whether one climate movie is more entertaining than another. The decisive question is not which film frightens audiences most effectively. It is whether the story names who can authorize harm, who can stop it and who is expected to live with the result.
