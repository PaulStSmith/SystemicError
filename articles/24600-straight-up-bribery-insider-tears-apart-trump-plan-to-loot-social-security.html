'Straight-up bribery': Insider tears apart Trump 'plan' to loot Social Security

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 10, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power is in CongressManufactured scarcity protects wealthThe framing outruns the evidenceThe system rewards spectacle

What happened

AlterNet reports that former Social Security Commissioner Martin O’Malley called Donald Trump’s stated offer of $5,000 to every American adult if Republicans retain Congress in the midterm elections “straight-up bribery.” O’Malley argued that conditioning a public payout on an electoral result amounts to paying people to vote a certain way.

The interview also addressed Social Security’s projected funding shortfall after 2032. O’Malley backed lifting the payroll-tax cap—reported as $184,500—so higher earners contribute on more of their income. He blamed income inequality for accelerating depletion of the program’s reserves and accused Trump, Elon Musk and several Republican politicians of hostility toward Social Security.

But AlterNet does not substantiate its headline’s larger premise: it identifies no specific Trump plan to remove money from Social Security, and it provides too little detail about either the reported $90 checks for seniors or the proposed $5,000 payments to establish their authority, funding or status. The White House disputed the article’s broader portrayal of Trump’s record but did not directly answer O’Malley’s criticism of the conditional payment proposal.

The power is in Congress

O’Malley is an advocate here, not the decision-maker. Congress controls Social Security’s tax structure and would have to legislate any change to the earnings cap, benefits or privatization. The president can propose, pressure and administer within statutory limits, but cannot personally dispense federal money merely by announcing a figure.

That distinction makes the alleged electoral condition more serious, not less. A promise to deliver public money if voters preserve one party’s congressional control treats the Treasury as a campaign inducement. O’Malley’s “bribery” description is political rhetoric, not a legal finding, but it identifies the underlying abuse clearly: collapsing the boundary between governing and rewarding electoral obedience.

Manufactured scarcity protects wealth

The policy dispute is not mysterious. The article presents one concrete revenue option: tax more earnings above the existing cap. That would place more of the financing burden on people whose wages currently exceed the taxable ceiling.

Calling Social Security’s finances an abstract “solvency” crisis can conceal this distributive choice. Lawmakers may collect more from high earners, reduce benefits, privatize parts of the program or combine measures. Each option assigns costs to different people. “Reform” is therefore not a neutral technical exercise; it is a decision about whether affluent earners or beneficiaries absorb the adjustment.

The framing outruns the evidence

AlterNet weakens its case by declaring that Trump and his allies are devising excuses to “loot” Social Security without documenting such a plan in the supplied reporting. The interview establishes O’Malley’s accusations and policy argument. It does not establish theft, a mechanism for accessing the trust funds or even the operational details of the proposed payments.

That exaggeration supplies an easy escape route for officials who should instead be pressed on answerable questions: Who authorized these payments? What law permits them? Where would the money come from? Is eligibility actually tied to an election result? What specific benefit cuts or privatization measures do named politicians support? Scathing language cannot substitute for the missing institutional facts.

The system rewards spectacle

The larger pattern is fiscal politics conducted through spectacle. A vast social-insurance program funded by workers becomes the backdrop for unexplained checks, campaign-season payout promises and moral claims about “waste.” Meanwhile, the consequential decision—who must contribute and who may lose benefits—receives less attention than the provocation.

The danger is not mere inconsistency over what counts as “socialism.” It is the conversion of public administration into personal political branding while structural choices are obscured. Social Security’s future will be determined through legislation, taxation and benefit rules. Any coverage that substitutes ominous labels for those mechanisms helps powerful actors avoid accountability for the decisions only they can make.
