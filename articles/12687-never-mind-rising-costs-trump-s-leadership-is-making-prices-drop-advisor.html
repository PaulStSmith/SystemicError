Never mind rising costs — Trump's 'leadership’ is making prices drop: Advisor

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 17, 20255 min read

White House economic adviser Kevin Hassett walks back after a TV interview outside the White House in Washington, U.S., June 27, 2025. REUTERS/Ken Cedeno

Prices across a wide range of sectors have been rising, and economists expect that once President Donald Trump’s tariffs fully go into effect, the increase may be substantial. But according to one top Trump official, prices are dropping. He credits “patriotism” and the President’s “leadership.”

“Economists, researchers and analysts have warned that President Donald Trump’s sweeping trade policy of tacking steep tariffs on most goods that come into America will deliver a taxing blow to consumers via higher prices,” CNN reported last week, while noting that “recent months’ economic data has shown that overall inflation has remained fairly tame.”

The price of gas, for example, may be down by 11.9% (yet nowhere near as low as what President Trump claims), but electricity and natural gas costs are up.

Through the end of May, food prices are up 2.9%, meats, poultry, fish, and eggs are up overall 6.1%, and electricity is up 4.5%, according to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics. Also, rents are up 3.8%, and auto insurance is up 7%.

Bottom line, inflation is currently at 2.4%. The new report is due to be out on Tuesday and is expected to show significant price increases. One prediction shows inflation is likely to jump to 2.7% in the upcoming report.

White House Director of the National Economic Council (NEC), Kevin Hassett, offered a different take.

On CNBC on Monday (video below), Hassett pointed to what he says is a report from the Council of Economic Advisers “that showed that import prices into the U.S are dropping, actually dropping during all this.”

He points to what he calls President Donald Trump’s “leadership” for the decrease in at least some prices.

Hassett says his “theory” as “an economist of why that is, is that Americans, because of President Trump’s leadership, have recognized that when they buy an American product, they not only get perhaps a better product, certainly a better product, most of the time, but they’re also making their community stronger.”

“And so there’s, I think a lot of patriotism in the data,” Hassett insisted.

“The bottom line is people prefer American products, and so therefore, the demand for imports has gone way down, down so much that even with what tariffs have been there, where people would say, ‘oh, they might increase prices at least a little bit,’ we’ve seen prices going down,” he claimed.

Our Analysis:

Navigating the Mirage of Economic Patriotism

In a recent display of economic analysis that might seem more at home in the realm of wishful thinking than in the rigorous world of economic policy, the Trump administration has put forth a narrative that is as confounding as it is misleading. Kevin Hassett, the White House Director of the National Economic Council, credits a supposed surge in "patriotism" and President Trump's "leadership" for a claimed decrease in certain prices, despite a wide array of data suggesting otherwise. This claim not only defies the consensus of economists but also flies in the face of reality for the average American consumer.

The Facts on the Ground

Let's dissect the numbers that present a starkly different narrative from Hassett's optimistic proclamation. According to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics:

  • Food prices are up 2.9%

  • Meats, poultry, fish, and eggs have increased by 6.1%

  • Electricity costs have surged by 4.5%

  • Rent prices are 3.8% higher

  • Auto insurance premiums have risen by 7%

These figures hardly paint a picture of a nation reveling in economic prosperity due to a newfound wave of patriotism. Instead, they depict an economy where the cost of living is on a steady rise, squeezing the budgets of American families across the board.

The Illusion of Lower Import Prices

Hassett's assertion hinges on a report from the Council of Economic Advisers, claiming that import prices into the U.S. are "actually dropping." This statement is a masterclass in selective interpretation, conveniently ignoring the broader context of inflation currently sitting at 2.4%, with predictions of a rise to 2.7%. To attribute a supposed decrease in import prices solely to consumer patriotism and preference for American-made products is not only simplistic but dangerously misleading.

The reality is that tariffs and trade policies have complex effects on the economy. They may protect certain industries but also lead to increased costs for consumers and retaliatory measures from other countries. To suggest that a surge in nationalistic purchasing behavior is powerful enough to counteract these forces is to misunderstand the very nature of global trade dynamics.

The Patriotism Narrative

The notion that American consumers are turning en masse to domestic products out of a sense of patriotism, thus driving down import prices, is a narrative devoid of empirical support. While it's true that there may be a segment of the population more inclined to "Buy American" as a political statement, the idea that this behavior has significantly impacted import prices strains credulity. The global economy is not a simple machine that can be redirected by sheer will or patriotic sentiment.

Moreover, Hassett's claim that American products are "certainly a better product, most of the time," is a sweeping generalization that fails to account for the vast diversity of goods in the global market. Quality is not a monopoly held by any single country, and consumers make purchasing decisions based on a variety of factors, including price, quality, and preference, not merely national origin.

Conclusion: Beyond the Rhetoric

The Trump administration's attempt to frame economic challenges through a lens of patriotism and leadership is a disservice to the American people. It's crucial to look beyond the rhetoric and understand the economic realities facing consumers. Prices are rising in several key sectors, and simplistic explanations do not suffice in addressing the complexities of inflation and trade policies.

Economic policy should be grounded in reality, not patriotism. As consumers feel the pinch of higher prices, it's clear that leadership requires more than optimistic narratives—it demands concrete actions that genuinely address the economic concerns of the day.
