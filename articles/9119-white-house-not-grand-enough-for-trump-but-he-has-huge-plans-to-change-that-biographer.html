White House not grand enough for Trump — but he has huge plans to change that: biographer

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 1, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Grandiose Delusions of Trump’s White House Renovation Plans

Donald Trump delivers remarks at the U.S. Ambassador’s Residence in Tokyo. REUTERS/Evelyn Hockstein

President Donald Trump has even larger ambitions for his White House renovation project than he has revealed publicly, according to his biographer.

Journalist Michael Wolff, who has written four books about Trump, discussed the president’s ambitions for the ballroom renovation project, that involves demolishing the East Wing, on a new episode of the “Inside Trump’s Head“ podcast on The Daily Beast.

Wolff argued that Trump may also be seeking to demolish and rebuild the West Wing because it doesn’t comport with Trump’s vision of himself.

“And so for this figure, Trump, a figure of nothing so much as pure grandiosity, it must feel dispiriting to show up there every day,” Wolff said. “Even the Oval Office is small.”

“The West Wing is dumpy; many of the offices are a pure functional government feel,” Wolff said. “Nothing to suggest, as Trump obviously wants it to be suggested, that he is the grandest, most powerful, historical being of all time.”

“So why not? Why not just say, ‘Well, we’re going to tear this down. We have corporate contributors. We’re going to build the West Wing as it should look.’ Or as it no doubt looks in his head.”

“Perhaps he can have a throne behind the Resolute Desk,” the Daily Beast’s chief content officer Joanna Coles said.

“It’s one of those things that we would think that he could never do. He would never do that,” Wolff continued. “There are some things that are certainly a step too far. But we would have said that last week about the East Wing itself.”

Our Analysis:

The Grandiose Delusions of Trump’s White House Renovation Plans

In a revelation that seems ripped from the pages of a dystopian satire, yet sadly roots itself in our all-too-real political landscape, President Donald Trump’s ambitions for the White House renovations have taken a turn for the grandiose. Journalist Michael Wolff, a figure who has chronicled Trump with a lens that vacillates between fascination and horror, brings to light plans that extend far beyond mere aesthetic upgrades. These plans, as discussed on the “Inside Trump’s Head” podcast, suggest a demolition and reconstruction not just of the East Wing, but potentially the West Wing as well.

Trump’s Vision: A Monument to Himself

The notion that the current architectural state of the White House fails to live up to Trump’s grand vision of himself speaks volumes about the man. According to Wolff, “The West Wing is dumpy; many of the offices are a pure functional government feel,” a sentiment apparently unbefitting of Trump’s self-perceived grandeur. This perspective is a stark reminder of Trump’s relentless pursuit of self-aggrandizement, often at the expense of tradition, practicality, and perhaps, decency.

Wolff’s insights suggest that Trump sees the White House not as the nation’s home, but as a stage for his own historical significance—a place that should scream of power and grandiosity. The idea of tearing down and rebuilding parts of this iconic structure to suit one man’s ego is not just an affront to its historical significance; it’s a metaphor for Trump’s approach to the presidency: reshape it until it reflects him, no matter the cost.

Corporate Contributors: The Price of Grandiosity

Perhaps the most chilling aspect of this plan is the mention of “corporate contributors” bankrolling the renovation. The implications of such arrangements are profound, raising questions about the influence such contributors might wield in the redesigned halls of power. The commodification of the White House, turning it into a project funded by the highest bidder, underscores a disturbing trend towards the erosion of democratic principles in favor of corporatocracy.

A Throne Behind the Resolute Desk?

Joanna Coles’s quip about a throne behind the Resolute Desk, while humorous, underscores a terrifying possibility: that the lines between governance and monarchy, democracy and autocracy, are blurring under Trump’s watch. Wolff rightly points out that what once seemed too far-fetched now teeters on the brink of reality. If the East Wing’s renovation was unthinkable last week, who’s to say a throne is out of the question?

Conclusion: The Edifice Complex of a Presidency

Trump’s desire to remodel the White House in his image is more than an expensive renovation project; it’s a physical manifestation of his presidency’s ethos. It’s about legacy, power, and a disturbing inclination towards autocracy disguised as renovation. As citizens, we must question the implications of such grandiosity—not just for the aesthetics of a building, but for the fabric of our democracy.

In a world where the lines of satire and reality are increasingly blurred, Trump’s renovation plans serve as a sobering reminder of the importance of guarding against the encroachment of individual egos on our collective institutions. The White House, after all, is not Trump’s house to remodel on a whim—it belongs to the nation, its history, and its people.
