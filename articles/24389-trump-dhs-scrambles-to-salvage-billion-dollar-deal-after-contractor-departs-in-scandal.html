Trump DHS scrambles to salvage billion-dollar deal after contractor departs in scandal

Based on an article by Matthew Chapman

Paulo SantosOctober 3, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory, citing The PunchUp and federal procurement records, reports that the Department of Homeland Security replaced Salus Worldwide Solutions as the contractor running Project Homecoming. DHS awarded Virginia-based Response AI Solutions the program’s next phase—worth up to $2.062 billion over three years—after Secretary Markwayne Mullin reopened the contract to competition. DHS has not publicly explained why Salus lost the work.

Salus had received an initial $915 million contract in May 2025 and another $200 million in June for six additional months. A rival bidder’s lawsuit disclosed that DHS contracting personnel and attorneys had warned of an “appearance of impropriety,” while Salus founder William Walters III has maintained that the competition was fair and his company submitted the best technical proposal at the lowest price.

The company later faced scrutiny over an alleged kickback effort involving former DHS adviser Corey Lewandowski, who is reportedly under federal investigation and whose spokesperson denies wrongdoing. Salus denies illegality. Democratic Rep. Bennie Thompson sought a formal investigation after leaked data reportedly indicated that about half the “voluntary” departures credited to Salus involved migrants already in ICE custody.

The buyer held the power

This was never merely a contractor problem. DHS controlled the specifications, the competition, the money, the oversight and the decision to keep paying. Salus could pursue public funds, but federal officials decided whether it received them.

That distinction matters because the department’s own personnel reportedly raised concerns before or during the arrangement. The DHS division overseeing the contract was led by a former Walters colleague who held off-site meetings at Salus offices before the award and congratulated him afterward. Those facts do not by themselves prove corruption, but they make scrutiny predictable—and make DHS responsible for explaining how its safeguards functioned.

The scandal was enabled, not discovered late

Salus lacked prior experience as a lead federal contractor, according to the reporting. A competitor challenged the rapid award. Internal officials flagged appearances. Yet DHS still granted the company $915 million, then supplied another $200 million despite documents suggesting most of the original budget had already been spent with limited results.

Calling the replacement a scramble risks treating the department as a victim of circumstances. The reported record instead shows an institution repeatedly exercising discretion in Salus’s favor before reversing course. Reopening the competition may have limited further exposure, but it does not erase the decisions that created it.

Accountability cannot end with a new vendor

Handing Project Homecoming to Response AI Solutions changes the recipient of the money. It does not establish whether the original procurement was fair, whether performance claims were accurate, or whether political access influenced other DHS awards.

The alleged effort by a Salus representative to arrange kickbacks for Lewandowski remains an allegation. His spokesperson denies wrongdoing, and the reported federal investigation is not a finding of guilt. But DHS’s silence about why Salus lost the contract leaves the public with a multibillion-dollar substitution where an institutional accounting should be.

“Voluntary” is doing political work

The dispute over departure statistics exposes more than questionable contractor performance. If roughly half the departures credited to Salus involved people already in ICE custody, as the leaked data reportedly suggested, then the program’s public description may obscure the coercive power behind its results.

Migrants were the objects of the policy, not the authors of the procurement failure. Focusing primarily on whether they departed “voluntarily” can misdirect attention from officials who designed the metric, approved the claims and paid the contractor. The weaker actors become the spectacle while the powerful actors remain an administrative blur.

A system that converts failure into another contract

This episode reflects a durable governing pattern: officials outsource politically charged state action, contractors collect enormous sums, and responsibility fragments when the arrangement draws scrutiny. The company points to the agency’s selection process. The agency points to procurement machinery. A replacement arrives, and the underlying decisions disappear behind a new corporate name.

The central issue is not that government contracting became messy. It is that DHS reportedly continued committing public money after serious warnings and now offers no public explanation for the contractor’s removal. Without that explanation—and without findings on the allegations—the new award looks less like accountability than institutional damage control.
