Trump gets stark history lesson over monument obsession from expert

Donald Trump (Reuters)

President Donald Trump has spent much of his second term so far scrambling to add his name, or some other mark, onto America’s national monuments, from the Kennedy Center to the White House, to even replacing Martin Luther King Jr. Day with his own birthday for free admission into national parks.

It’s an impulse shared by many dictators and autocrats around the world, wrote Keith Richburg for The Washington Post, as they all seek to leave a permanent manifestation of their own will and their impact on the nation.

“Kenya, where I lived from 1991 to 1994, was ruled for 24 years by ‘Big Man’ authoritarian president Daniel arap Moi,“ wrote Richburg. “A major downtown Nairobi street was named Moi Avenue. A sculpture in Nairobi’s Central Park displayed what appeared to be a replica of his fist, clutching his characteristic ‘rungu’ baton, bursting through the top of Mount Kenya. His face graced every banknote and coin. One day in October, I was planning to run errands but found all the banks and offices closed; I didn’t realize it was ‘Moi Day,’ a national holiday.”

But “nothing lasts forever,” wrote Richburg. These monuments are rarely as permanent as autocrats imagine they will be — and Trump is likely no exception.

“When the regimes finally crumble — and they eventually do — those statues and portraits become the first, most visible targets of suppressed rage,” he wrote. “Think of the statue of Saddam being pulled down in Baghdad. In Basra, southern Iraq, after the regime fell, I saw one giant poster of Saddam dressed in a traditional kaffiyeh, with his mouth shot out. Across Syria after the December 2024 revolution, statues of Bashar al-Assad and his father, Hafez al-Assad, were reduced to rubble. In the Philippines, a giant likeness of the late dictator Ferdinand E. Marcos carved out of a mountainside was later blown up with dynamite. When Mobutu fell in Zaire, the locals carved his face out of the banknotes.”

All of Trump’s changes, from his name and banner on federal buildings to his dictate for Cabinet members to wear shoes he gives them, are “mostly corruption and vanity at work, rather than coercion and terror,” concluded Richburg. “Leaders come and eventually go, and many of their garish tributes to themselves end up on history’s junk pile — or become lasting monuments to their ridiculousness. Trump will be no different.”

Our Analysis:

The Spectacle of Vanity: Decoding Trump’s Monumental Ego

In an era already saturated with political theatre, President Donald Trump’s attempts to imprint his legacy onto America’s national monuments and significant public days take the drama to new heights—or perhaps more aptly, new lows. Keith Richburg’s commentary for The Washington Post draws a parallel between Trump’s actions and those of autocrats across history, suggesting a shared impulse to immortalize their rule through physical and symbolic manifestations. However, to dissect the implications of Trump’s moves, we need to unravel the threads of power, decision-making, and responsibility.

Who Holds the Power?

At the core of this spectacle lies the question of institutional power. In the United States, the presidency is a position of significant power, both symbolically and institutionally. However, this power is not absolute and is bound by constitutional checks and balances. The ability to rename national holidays or alter national monument designations typically involves a complex interplay of federal powers, including Congress and, in some cases, the judiciary. Therefore, while Trump may propose or push for these changes, their realization often requires acquiescence or active support from other branches of government or federal bodies.

Decision-Making at Play

The decision to pursue the renaming of landmarks and the alteration of national symbols is attributed directly to Trump. This initiative, as reported, reflects a broader pattern of behavior aimed at cementing his personal brand into the fabric of the American identity. Yet, it’s crucial to differentiate between what a president proposes and what he can unilaterally enforce. Many of the actions described, such as changing the date of a national holiday or modifying federal buildings, would necessitate a broader approval process. Therefore, while Trump is the architect of these proposals, their fruition depends on a wider circle of decision-makers.

Misdirection of Responsibility?

Richburg’s article frames these actions squarely as a manifestation of Trump’s vanity and desire for a lasting legacy, likened to the efforts of dictators to immortalize their rule. This comparison, while striking, may inadvertently misdirect responsibility by equating Trump’s desires with the actual institutional ability to enact them without opposition. It’s critical to acknowledge the role of the legislative and judicial branches in either facilitating or obstructing these endeavors. The framing risks oversimplifying the dynamics at play, suggesting a level of unilateral power that the presidency does not hold—even under a figure as controversial as Trump.

False Equivalences and Scapegoats

Drawing parallels between Trump and autocrats like Saddam Hussein or Ferdinand E. Marcos is compelling for narrative purposes but risks creating a false equivalence. Unlike the dictators mentioned, Trump operates within a system designed to limit the concentration of power. This distinction is crucial for understanding the nature of his proposals and their potential impact. The comparison, while highlighting the absurdity of Trump’s actions, may inadvertently elevate them to a more sinister level than warranted by the reality of American governance structures.

Conclusion

Trump’s attempts to leave a personal mark on America’s national monuments and traditions are emblematic of his presidency’s broader themes of ego and excess. However, it’s essential to ground our critique in the reality of institutional checks and balances that frame his ability to effect such changes. While the desire to immortalize his presidency is clear, the capacity to do so unilaterally is not. The real story here is not just about one man’s vanity but about the resilience (or lack thereof) of American institutions in the face of such challenges. As history has shown, monuments to ego are often the most fleeting. The critical question moving forward is not just about how Trump will be remembered, but how America’s democratic institutions respond and adapt to these unprecedented challenges.
