Trump may set GOP back decades with his reaction to racism scandal: GOP strategist

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 9, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:Analyzing the Fallout: Trump’s Racist Video and the GOP’s Fragile Gains

U.S. President Donald Trump speaks with members of the media on board Air Force One en route to Palm Beach, Florida, U.S., February 6, 2026. REUTERS/Ken Cedeno

The Republican Party has made steady gains with nonwhite voters since the late 1990s, but President Donald Trump’s chaotic response to a racist video shared on his social media account may very well wipe out those gains before he leaves office, one GOP strategist warned on Sunday.

Early Friday morning, a video was shared on Trump’s social media account that included a short clip depicting Barack and Michelle Obama as primates in the jungle, sparking immediate outrage from Republican and Democratic lawmakers alike. In a move that surprised some analysts, Trump would later remove the video amid backlash and blame its publication on a White House staffer.

However, the damage to the Republican Party may already be done, warned Arizona GOP strategist Barrett Marson, speaking with The New York Times in its report Sunday.

“He’s losing that,” Marson told the Times, referring to the Republican Party’s decades’ worth of gains with nonwhite voters. “He’s now going to burn those gains to the ground.”

Trump’s overall chaotic response to the fallout from the video, coupled with his lack of an apology, also set the stage for a similar incident to occur before he leaves office, Marson warned.

“He can’t admit a mistake, and therefore he cannot learn from the mistake,” Marson said. “So do I think this could happen again? 100%. Is this the last time that he posts or reposts something that is offensive and racially charged? I’m sure that it is not.”

Trump made historic gains in 2024 with nonwhite voters, securing 45% of the Hispanic vote, the highest Hispanic vote share for a Republican presidential candidate on record and surpassing the previous record of 44% set by President George Bush in 2004. Trump’s gains with Hispanic voters was an increase of 13 percentage points when compared with 2020.

Those gains, however, could soon be lost, Marson feared, and in spite of the president pulling the racist video down, a move that surprised even President Bill Clinton’s former speechwriter, Jeff Shesol.

“It is surprising, in itself, to ever see him take a step back, to do anything other than, in the moment, double down and triple down, so in that sense it is surprising,” Shesol told the Times. “It feels significant.”

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Fallout: Trump’s Racist Video and the GOP’s Fragile Gains

In a recent spectacle that underscores the chaotic nature of President Donald Trump’s tenure and its implications for the Republican Party’s relationship with nonwhite voters, we observe a political drama unfold that is as disturbing as it is predictably tragic. The core of this issue—a racist video shared on Trump’s social media—acts as both a mirror and a maelstrom, reflecting and amplifying the systemic flaws within the GOP’s strategy towards minority voters.

A Step Backward in Racial Politics

First, let’s dissect the immediate cause of the uproar: a video depicting Barack and Michelle Obama as primates. This act, fundamentally repugnant and blatantly racist, not only sparked “immediate outrage” from across the political spectrum but also served as a grim reminder of the undercurrents of racism that still plague American society and, by extension, its political entities. The fact that this video was shared by the President of the United States adds a layer of institutional endorsement to such bigotry, making it all the more reprehensible.

The GOP’s Fragile Gains with Nonwhite Voters

The Republican Party, as noted by Arizona GOP strategist Barrett Marson, has made “steady gains with nonwhite voters since the late 1990s.” These gains, however, are now threatened to be “burned to the ground” by Trump’s actions and the broader implications of his leadership style. It’s crucial to understand that these gains were never robust or deeply rooted in a shared vision of inclusive progress. Instead, they were always precarious, contingent on the GOP’s ability to navigate the complex landscape of America’s racial politics with a modicum of grace and empathy—qualities conspicuously absent in Trump’s response to the backlash.

Trump’s Inability to Apologize: A Symptom of a Larger Malaise

Marson’s commentary on Trump’s incapacity to “admit a mistake” and hence “learn from the mistake” is particularly telling. It’s not just about the inability of one man to confront his errors; it’s reflective of a party-wide crisis of accountability. This incident isn’t merely a blemish on Trump’s record; it’s emblematic of the GOP’s systemic failure to genuinely engage with nonwhite communities beyond electoral maneuvering.

The Surprising Removal of the Video

Trump’s decision to remove the video, described as “surprising” by Jeff Shesol, a former speechwriter for President Bill Clinton, signals a rare acknowledgment of a misstep. Yet, without a sincere apology or a commitment to address the underlying issues, this action is little more than a superficial gesture, a feeble attempt to mitigate damage rather than an earnest effort to rectify the wrong.

The Future of the GOP’s Relationship with Nonwhite Voters

The article highlights Trump’s “historic gains in 2024 with nonwhite voters,” particularly among Hispanic voters. This statistic, while noteworthy, now stands on precarious ground. The real question is not about the temporary electoral successes but rather about the long-term impacts of incidents like these on the GOP’s relationship with nonwhite communities. Can a party that oscillates between courting and alienating nonwhite voters through such incidents hope to build a lasting, trust-based relationship with these crucial demographic segments?

In conclusion, this episode is not an aberration but a symptom of a deeper malaise within the GOP—a malaise characterized by a transactional approach to minority engagement, a reluctance to confront internal prejudices, and a leadership style that oscillates between indifference and outright hostility towards the principles of diversity and inclusivity. The path forward requires more than the removal of a video; it demands a systemic reevaluation of the party’s values, strategies, and leadership.
