'You're not answering the question:' CNN host holds conservative's feet to fire on Colbert

Audie Cornish and Terry Schilling

CNN’s Audie Cornish challenged a conservative political operative to address whether President Donald Trump’s administration was imposing right-wing ideology on the media.

Panelists on “CNN This Morning” discussed late night-talk show host Stephen Colbert’s feud with his own network CBS, which he claimed had barred him from airing an interview with Democratic Texas senate hopeful James Talarico because network lawyers had warned him it went against federal “equal time” rules, and conservative activist Terry Schilling downplayed the move.

“Well, look, I think that this is a story much ado about nothing,” said Schilling, president of the American Principles Project. “I think that what’s happening is James Talarico and Stephen Colbert are taking advantage, getting as much spotlight as they can on this. Colbert does not have a broad viewership. He’s been falling in the numbers.”

Colbert led off Monday’s show by announcing Talarico’s absence and directing viewers to his YouTube page, where the scrapped interview was posted and has garnered more than 5 million views so far, and Schilling suggested that the TV host’s influence was largely social media-driven as his broadcast ratings decline.

“So why fight it?” Cornish asked. “This is one of my questions. When I think of someone like [FCC chair] Brendan Carr, who I’m going to play a clip for you in a moment, the Republican line is usually he doesn’t matter, it’s too small, but then they weigh in, right? So here’s Brendan Carr at the end of January, talking about how he thinks about these rules. “

Carr warned last month that the Federal Communications Commission would re-examine the long-standing exception to “equal time” rules that applied to “bona fide news,” such as candidate interviews, but he questioned whether so-called “fake news” should enjoy the same protections – although talk radio won’t face the same scrutiny.

“What’s clear to everyone, they mean, the folks who are in charge, clearly are aiming towards state-run TV,” argued Democratic strategist Antjuan Seawright, as Schilling scoffed. “If you are anti this administration, if you’re anti their policy, their agenda, they want to silence and suffocate your voices and the rule, the same rules do not apply to Fox News. They do not apply to networks and coverage. That praises the uplift the right-wing extreme agenda.”

Cornish pointed out that Carr had argued during the controversy around ABC suspending late-night talk show host Jimmy Kimmel that the media had been culturally captured by the left, and the host asked wither the administration was pushing to swap out left-leaning ideology for their own right-wing ideology.

“Well, look, to be fair,” Schilling jumped in, “This Texas primary election is in less than a month, right? It’s in just a couple of weeks. I think that this is a fair thing and, frankly, I don’t think that Colbert really wanted to deal with having [Rep.] Jasmine Crockett on the show. We’ll see.”

“You’re not answering the question about is it a swap?” Cornish interrupted. “Is it basically, are you now watching an ideology in place that you’re like, look, the left, your time is over. It is now the time to balance it more and therefore Carr and others are going to be more aggressive about inserting and forcing these conversations about point of view?”

Schilling disagreed, saying the administration hasn’t done anything like Cornish had suggested.

“No, I think we have a long way to go before anyone should be worried about the right wing taking over America’s media apparatus,” Schilling insisted. “You’ve got the New York Times, you got the Washington Post, you got MSNBC, you know, I think you have a lot of news outlets that are very left-leaning. The right is more decentralized. It’s more blogs and podcasters.”

“But we’re at the table with someone who’s been banned from the White House,” Seawright interjected, gesturing toward an Associated Press correspondent, Michelle Price.

“Is that true?” Cornish asked.

“The Associated Press is banned from the White House [press pool],” she said.

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Ideological Tug-of-War in Media: A Critical Look at the CNN Segment

In a recent segment on CNN This Morning, Audie Cornish delved into the complex interplay of media, politics, and regulatory frameworks, using Stephen Colbert’s tussle with CBS over an unaired interview as the springboard. This incident, seemingly minor on the surface, unravels to reveal deeper currents of ideological contention and regulatory maneuvering within the American media landscape. Let’s break down the key elements of this discourse, highlighting the undercurrents of power, control, and ideological battles that define today’s media ecosystem.

Colbert vs. CBS: A Microcosm of a Larger Battle

At face value, CBS citing federal “equal time” rules to bar Stephen Colbert from airing an interview with Democratic Texas senate hopeful James Talarico seems like a straightforward regulatory compliance issue. However, conservative activist Terry Schilling’s dismissal of the situation as “much ado about nothing” and his suggestion that Colbert and Talarico were merely seeking the spotlight misses the forest for the trees. This incident is emblematic of the ongoing struggle over who gets to speak in the media and under what conditions.

Schilling’s attempt to downplay Colbert’s influence by pointing to his declining broadcast ratings, while simultaneously neglecting the interview’s viral success on YouTube, is a clear oversimplification of media dynamics in the digital age. Influence cannot be solely measured by traditional TV ratings, especially when digital platforms can amplify a message to millions.

The “Equal Time” Rules and the Specter of “State-Run TV”

The discussion quickly pivots to a broader, more ominous concern: the potential for government entities to leverage regulatory frameworks like the “equal time” rules to impose ideological conformity on the media. FCC Chair Brendan Carr’s comments about re-examining exceptions for “bona fide news” and his questioning of protections for “so-called ‘fake news’” reveal a dangerous flirtation with content control. Democratic strategist Antjuan Seawright’s assertion that attempts to silence oppositional voices echo efforts towards establishing a state-run media, particularly when such rules are applied unevenly, resonates in a media environment already rife with accusations of bias and censorship.

The Right-Wing Takeover Narrative: A Red Herring?

When Cornish presses the issue of whether there’s an effort to replace left-leaning ideology with right-wing ideology in the media, Schilling’s response is telling. He asserts that the right is far from dominating America’s media apparatus, pointing to major outlets like the New York Times and MSNBC as bastions of left-leaning journalism. This, however, is a straw man argument that sidesteps the crux of Cornish’s inquiry. The concern isn’t about which side has more megaphones but about the integrity of the media’s role in a democracy and the potential for regulatory overreach to skew public discourse.

Moreover, Schilling’s depiction of the right as “more decentralized” and embodied by “blogs and podcasters” minimizes the significant influence of right-wing media giants, not to mention the role of algorithm-driven amplification of content across digital platforms that often skews towards sensationalism and polarization.

The Exclusion of the Associated Press from the White House Press Pool

The segment takes an even more disconcerting turn with the revelation that the Associated Press has been banned from the White House press pool. This piece of information, dropped almost casually into the conversation, is perhaps the most alarming indication of the state of press freedom in the current administration. It’s a stark reminder that the battles over media access and representation are not just theoretical debates but have real-world implications for the flow of information to the public.

Conclusion: The Struggle for the Soul of American Media

This CNN segment, while framed around a specific incident involving Stephen Colbert and CBS, opens up a Pandora’s box of issues related to media freedom, regulatory overreach, and the ideological battleground that the American media landscape has become. The conversation goes beyond mere partisan squabbling to touch on fundamental questions about who controls the media narrative in the United States and how that control affects the democratic process.

As observers, participants, and, most importantly, as citizens, it’s crucial that we remain vigilant about these dynamics, question the motives behind regulatory changes, and defend a free and independent press. Only through sustained scrutiny and public discourse can we hope to maintain the equilibrium necessary for a healthy democracy.
