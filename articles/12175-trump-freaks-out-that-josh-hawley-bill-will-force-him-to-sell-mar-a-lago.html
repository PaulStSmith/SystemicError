Trump freaks out that Josh Hawley bill will force him to sell Mar-a-Lago

U.S. President-elect Donald Trump reacts on the day he delivers remarks, at Mar-a-Lago in Palm Beach, Florida, U.S., December 16, 2024. REUTERS/Brian Snyder

Sen. Josh Hawley (R-MO) proposed legislation that would restrict U.S. lawmakers from stock trading — and President Donald Trump was not happy about it.

In a committee vote Wednesday, Hawley and Democrats passed the bill out of committee, where it will hit the full Senate for a vote.

Business Insider reporter Bryan Metzger spoke with Hawley on Thursday, and he said Trump had called him the night before, telling him other Republicans had told him he'd be forced to sell Mar-a-Lago under Hawley's law.

However, Trump still supports a stock trading ban for Congress members, Hawley said.

"Hawley says that Trump didn’t bring up the fact that the bill *would* bar him from trading stocks, that he was just concerned with divestiture, which does not apply to him under the bill," wrote Metzger on X. "Hawley claims Trump told him he was 'exonerated' after he explained it."

Republicans ganged up on Hawley in the committee hearing at which the bill was passed on Wednesday.

"Let's be honest, this is a publicity show," claimed Sen. Bernie Moreno (R-OH).

After Hawley voted with Democrats, Trump lashed out on Truth Social, calling him a "second-tier senator."

Our Analysis:

The Art of Distraction and Diversion in Political Theater

In a recent episode of political theater that could rival any prime-time drama, Senator Josh Hawley (R-MO) finds himself at the center of controversy, not just with the opposition but within his own party—and notably with former President Donald Trump. Hawley's proposal to restrict U.S. lawmakers from stock trading has stirred the pot in ways that highlight the deep-seated issues within American politics. Let's dissect this narrative with the precision of a scalpel, shall we?

Firstly, the irony is rich with Trump's reaction to Hawley's proposed legislation. Trump, reportedly concerned that he'd have to sell Mar-a-Lago under the new law, was reassured that the legislation wouldn't affect him in the way he feared. This interaction is emblematic of the self-centered perspective that often pervades the political elite, where the primary concern is how legislation affects them personally, rather than its impact on the integrity of the governmental process or, heaven forbid, the public good.

Moreover, Trump's support for a stock trading ban, in principle, juxtaposed with his knee-jerk reaction to Hawley's proposal, underscores a prevalent issue in politics: supporting reforms in theory but recoiling when they come too close to home. It's a classic case of "do as I say, not as I do," where the rules are for thee, but not for me.

Hawley's claim that Trump was "exonerated" after being reassured that his personal empire wouldn't be affected is a fascinating insight into how politics is often more about personal assurances than about the broader legislative impact. It's a microcosm of a larger problem where legislation is navigated through the lens of personal gain or loss rather than public benefit.

The backlash Hawley faced from his own party, including being dubbed a "second-tier senator" by Trump, reveals the cutthroat nature of politics where allegiance often lies with personal or party interests above principles or policy. The critique by Sen. Bernie Moreno (R-OH), dismissing the endeavor as a "publicity show," further illustrates the cynicism within the halls of power, where genuine attempts at reform are often scorned and belittled.

This entire saga is a stark reminder of the systemic flaws within U.S. politics, where personal interests, party loyalty, and public posturing often eclipse the pursuit of meaningful reform. Hawley's initiative, whether one agrees with it or not, was an attempt to address a legitimate concern regarding conflicts of interest in Congress. Yet, the reaction it elicited speaks volumes about the priorities and values of those in the political arena.

In conclusion, as we peel back the layers of this political drama, what's revealed is a troubling picture of a system where legislative efforts are hampered by egotism, party politics, and the shadow of personal interests. It's a system in dire need of reform, where the principles of transparency, accountability, and public service are reinstated as guiding stars. But as this episode demonstrates, the road to such reform is fraught with obstacles, not least of which is the resistance from within.
