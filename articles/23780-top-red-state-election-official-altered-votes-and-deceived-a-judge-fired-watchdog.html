Top red state election official altered votes and deceived a judge: fired watchdog

Missouri’s Election Watchdog Says the Secretary Wanted No Watchdog

The Accusation Is Abuse of Power, Not Office Drama

A lawsuit by former Missouri election-integrity director Nick La Strada accuses Secretary of State Denny Hoskins’s administration of attempting to manufacture evidence for a federal inquiry, intervening unlawfully in a certified local election, and spending public money to influence another. These remain allegations, but their political meaning is already clear: the official entrusted with policing Missouri elections is accused of treating election law as an obstacle to his own power.

The Power Sits With Hoskins

Hoskins was elected secretary of state in 2024. He hired La Strada in January 2025, publicly praising his election-administration experience, then fired him after La Strada objected to conduct he believed violated election and campaign laws and testified at a federal hearing.

That chain of authority matters. La Strada did not control the office, its money, its personnel, or its access to Missouri’s voter-registration machinery. Hoskins did. When La Strada reported an alleged discussion among senior officials about fabricating an anonymous whistleblower submission using an “IP scrambler” and burner phone, his Capitol badge was disabled, he was told his presence at the hearing was unnecessary, and he was dismissed shortly afterward.

Hoskins has declined to comment. Silence is not proof of the allegations, but it does not redistribute responsibility. These accusations concern actions by his senior staff, inside his department, followed by a firing he personally carried out.

“Election Integrity” Becomes a Branding Exercise

The phrase “election integrity” is often deployed as partisan advertising: it implies vigilance while allowing officials to decide which rules deserve enforcement and which can be bent for political convenience.

La Strada’s account describes that inversion in unusually concrete terms. The office allegedly wanted evidence whose origins could not be traced while responding to a federal inquiry. It allegedly displaced the declared winner of a certified Marshall election through a retabulation La Strada says only a judge could authorize. It allegedly used state resources for a mailer opposing St. Louis County’s Proposition B despite his warning that doing so appeared unlawful.

If substantiated, this was not confusion about complicated procedure. It was the deliberate use of state authority to shape evidence and outcomes, paired with retaliation against the official who objected.

The Scapegoat Is the Man Who Followed the Process

The familiar misdirection would be to present this as a personnel dispute, an internal Republican feud, or a clash of professional opinions. That framing would protect the institution by shrinking alleged misconduct into workplace conflict.

La Strada’s party loyalty is politically relevant only because it removes an easy partisan alibi. According to his attorney, he remained a loyal Republican but would not place the party above lawful elections. Yet even that formulation understates the issue. Public officials are not entitled to obedience when their instructions conflict with the law. Refusing to assist alleged misconduct is not disloyalty; demanding assistance would be the abuse.

The weaker actor lost his job. The official with removal power kept his office. Any account that centers La Strada’s motives while treating Hoskins’s authority as background has the power structure backward.

An Empty Position Sends Its Own Message

Hoskins reportedly has not appointed another director of election integrity. That vacancy is not merely an administrative loose end. In the context of the lawsuit, it suggests an office more comfortable without an internal official empowered to say no.

Institutions rarely announce that oversight has become inconvenient. They disable access, exclude dissenters from hearings, remove employees, and leave posts vacant. The result is compliance without the embarrassment of issuing a written order for it.

Missouri’s elections bureaucracy controls voter data and administers rules with consequences far beyond a single local contest. Removing internal resistance from such an office concentrates discretion precisely where scrutiny should be strongest.

The System Protects Power by Calling Its Constraints Optional

The larger pattern is not election denial in its loudest form. It is election manipulation through administrative control: exploiting obscure procedures, public funds, personnel authority, and informational asymmetry while maintaining the official vocabulary of integrity.

Authoritarian practice does not always begin with discarded ballots or canceled elections. It can begin with officials deciding that certified results are negotiable, evidence is manufacturable, public resources are political assets, and employees who invoke the law are disposable.

The lawsuit still must be tested in court. But the political question exposed by its allegations is immediate: whether “election integrity” describes a binding obligation or merely a label worn by those powerful enough to punish anyone who takes it seriously.
