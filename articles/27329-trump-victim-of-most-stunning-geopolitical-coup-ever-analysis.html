Trump victim of ‘most stunning geopolitical coup’ ever: analysis

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosNovember 18, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

From a loose slogan to an authoritarian partnershipThe charge against TrumpA headline that reverses the allegation

AlterNet’s headline casts Donald Trump as the victim of a geopolitical coup. The analysis it describes says almost the opposite: Jonathan V. Last, writing in The Bulwark, argues that Trump has made the United States a willing partner of authoritarian powers, allowing Vladimir Putin’s Russia and Xi Jinping’s China to gain American alignment without surrendering their relationship with Iran.

That is not a story about something done to Trump. It is an accusation about what Trump, as president, is doing with American power.

From a loose slogan to an authoritarian partnership

Last begins with George W. Bush’s “Axis of Evil,” the label his administration applied in 2002 to Iraq, Iran and North Korea. Even on its own terms, Last writes, that grouping was incoherent. Iran and Iraq were enemies, while North Korea sold assistance to governments willing to pay. They were repressive states, but not a functioning alliance.

The more consequential alignment, in Last’s account, emerged later among Russia, China and Iran. By 2012, he argues, the three governments had spent years developing relationships intended to constrain liberal powers.

That distinction matters. Bush’s phrase bundled adversaries together for the political purposes of the War on Terror. Last is describing something more concrete: governments coordinating where their interests overlap, despite differences among them.

But he also revives Bush’s morally loaded terminology, which risks flattening distinct relationships into another sweeping civilizational camp. The useful question is not whether every government fits neatly inside an “evil” axis. It is how state power is being exercised, which partnerships it strengthens and whose interests those choices serve.

The charge against Trump

Last’s central claim is that Russia did not need to defeat the United States because it managed to “flip it.” He characterizes the Trump administration as a Putin-controlled regime and presents Trump’s reported meeting with Xi in Shenzhen that Thursday as the moment the new alignment would become visible.

“Putin and Xi” had effectively exchanged Iran for “Donald Trump’s America,” Last writes, calling the development “the most stunning geopolitical coup” ever. He further argues that Russia and China achieved this without abandoning Iran, even as, in his description, America was at war with it.

The supplied reporting does not detail the policies, agreements or concessions underlying that assessment. It does establish that Last’s argument concerns Trump’s use of the presidency, not Trump being personally duped or overpowered. His language is polemical, but the institutional structure is plain: the president controls the foreign-policy machinery of the United States. Putin and Xi may benefit from Trump’s decisions; they do not issue those decisions from the Oval Office.

Calling Trump the “victim” therefore redirects attention from the officeholder exercising American power to the foreign leaders allegedly profiting from it. That framing grants Trump passivity precisely where Last assigns him agency.

A headline that reverses the allegation

There is a legitimate argument to examine in Last’s commentary: whether Trump’s foreign policy weakens traditional liberal alliances while improving the strategic position of authoritarian governments. AlterNet’s account, however, supplies more invective than policy evidence. “Puppet regime” and “idiot president” communicate Last’s judgment; they do not, by themselves, demonstrate the mechanism of the alleged geopolitical realignment.

The headline makes the distortion worse. A president who chooses accommodation, alignment or deference is not necessarily the casualty of those choices. The country whose power he directs may bear the consequences. Allies may face them. People subjected to the strengthened reach of repressive governments may face them. But the reporting presented here does not establish Trump as the coup’s victim.

Last’s actual accusation is harsher: Trump is the means by which the supposed coup succeeds. If that accusation is to carry more than rhetorical force, it must rest on the president’s concrete decisions and their beneficiaries. The “victim” framing obscures exactly that chain of responsibility.
