'Never thought I'd see the day!' CNN analyst floored by GOP's eye-popping reversal

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 18, 20255 min read

(Screengrab via CNN)

The Republican Party under President Donald Trump has gone from a largely expansionist, neoconservative foreign policy to old-school isolationism, to the point that they repudiate their own party's previous military leadership, CNN data analyst Harry Enten told host Kate Bolduan with a degree of astonishment.

A key demonstration of this is Vice President JD Vance proclaiming it is not in anyone's interest "for Europe to be a permanent security vassal of the United States," as an argument against taking leadership in NATO affairs.

" JD Vance has even since before they got in office, very clearly has jumped on board with Donald Trump's isolationist, America First kind of agenda when it comes to foreign policy," said Bolduan. "But do Republican voters agree at this point?"

"Increasingly so," confirmed Enten, a frequent number-cruncher on Trump's sinking political fortunes. "I mean, look, the percentage of Republicans who believe that the U.S. should have ... an active role in world affairs. I mean, look at this trendline dating back over the last few decades. I mean, this is very clear. It was 74 percent in 2004 ... look at it now. It's just 54 percent. So it's a bare majority that is within the margin of error. In fact, if you look at 2023, it was slightly under 50 percent. Kind of vacillates back and forth on the 50 percent line. But republicans are increasingly of the view that the U.S. should not, in fact, play an active role in world affairs."

"And JD Vance was also commenting in this interview on the Iraq War, suggesting that it could have been avoided if the Europeans had been a little more independent," said Bolduan. "What did you find about sentiment on that war?"

"Yeah, this — you know, I remember we were talking in the break," said Enten. "I remember when the Iraq War started, there were so few Republicans who were opposed to us going into the Iraq War. It was just 5 percent who opposed the U.S. going into Iraq. And now you look and you say, what percentage of Republicans, in fact, say that it was not worth it? It's 53 percent. I never thought, Kate Bolduan, that I would see the day in which a majority of Republicans said it wasn't worth it to go to Iraq, especially considering where Republicans were at the start of the Iraq War."

Our Analysis:

The Republican Foreign Policy Shift: A Dive into Isolationism

In a recent conversation that would have seemed almost unfathomable a decade ago, CNN data analyst Harry Enten laid out a stark transformation within the Republican Party regarding its stance on foreign policy. Once champions of a muscular, interventionist approach under the banner of neoconservatism, the party under the influence of figures like President Donald Trump and Vice President JD Vance has veered dramatically towards isolationism. This pivot isn't just a minor adjustment but a repudiation of the party's own recent history.

Vance's NATO Comments: A Symbol of the Shift

The words of JD Vance, stating that it is not in anyone's interest "for Europe to be a permanent security vassal of the United States," sharply encapsulate this transformation. Vance, aligning with Trump's "America First" agenda, questions the value of leadership in NATO, a cornerstone of post-World War II American foreign policy. This stance, once perhaps a fringe position within the party, signals a profound realignment with historical isolationist tendencies.

The Republican Base: Following Along or Leading the Charge?

But where does the Republican base stand in this shift? According to Enten, the tide among Republican voters is unmistakable. A majority, albeit a slim one, now questions the merit of an active U.S. role in world affairs. The numbers are telling: from a robust 74% in 2004 advocating for engagement to barely half that now, with trends in 2023 showing even less enthusiasm. This is not just a shift; it's a seismic realignment in Republican foreign policy orthodoxy.

It's crucial to note, however, that this trend does not emerge in a vacuum. It reflects broader skepticism about military interventions and nation-building efforts, especially after the long, costly wars in Iraq and Afghanistan. The mention of the Iraq War is particularly poignant. Once virtually a litmus test of Republican orthodoxy, with only 5% opposition at its commencement, now over half of Republicans deem it a mistake.

A Critical Perspective

While some might view this shift as a healthy correction, a move towards restraint and prudence, it's imperative to scrutinize the potential ramifications. The pendulum swinging towards isolationism raises critical questions about global stability, the future of international alliances, and America's role on the world stage. Vance's critique of European dependence on U.S. security, while not without merit, oversimplifies complex geopolitical realities. It ignores the strategic benefits the U.S. gains through its leadership and the stabilizing effect it has had in post-World War II Europe.

Moreover, the embrace of isolationism by the Republican Party, if it continues, risks abdicating the United States' position of global leadership. This is not merely about military intervention but about diplomatic influence, economic strategies, and the global values the U.S. has traditionally championed.

In Conclusion

The Republican Party's foreign policy transformation reflects a broader reevaluation of America's role in the world. However, the swing towards isolationism, as highlighted by Enten's analysis and Vance's comments, warrants a critical examination. The implications of this shift are vast, touching on national security, global stability, and the moral leadership of the United States. As this trend unfolds, the fundamental question remains: what will the cost of this isolationism be, both for the United States and for the world at large?
