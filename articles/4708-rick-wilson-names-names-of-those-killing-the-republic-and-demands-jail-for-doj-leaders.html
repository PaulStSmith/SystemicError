Rick Wilson names names of those killing the republic — and demands jail for DOJ leaders

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 2, 20265 min read

U.S. Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche speaks to the media as U.S. President Donald Trump listens in the Press Briefing Room at the White House in Washington D.C., June 27, 2025. REUTERS/Ken Cedeno

Lincoln Project co-founder and former Republican strategist Rick Wilson penned a Thursday column alleging that the downfall of the American republic will come at the hands of damned souls he described as a cancer. Then he named names of those propping up President Donald Trump.

Wilson began by saying that if someone truly wants to understand how the country is destroyed, they must look beyond the “fringes of MAGA screeching on Twitter” and instead look directly into the bottomless black wells where conscience was long ago replaced with corruption and greed.

He began with the “criminals at the Department of Justice.”

“The sneering Todd Blanche, Ghislaine Maxwell’s legal sugar daddy. The grinning, soulless she-demon Pamela Jo Bondi, reveling in the agony of the Epstein victims. The drunken dwarf Kash Patel, using the big boy helper steps to board his taxpayer-funded jet to party with hockey players or to visit his girlfriend,” Wilson said.

Patel, he argued, is nothing more than a “’Deep State’ pod-bro conspiracist now sitting on a booster cushion in J. Edgar Hoover’s chair.” Former Fox News host and close Trump ally Jeanine Pirro, who took over the Washington, D.C. district attorney’s office is actually “the Franzia-soaked harpy, shrieking performative vengeance.” Meanwhile, Susie Wiles, Trump’s chief of staff is “the silent enabler” of “criminal mastermind Donald Trump.”

These are the names he wants said loudly and often, for they are the bright white spot shining “on the MRI of Washington power where the cancer grows.” The cancer doesn’t merely metastasize, he said, but consumes and expands everything it touches, corrupting it. He also acknowledged that they come along with “a collection of lesser enablers,” all of whom make up the “cancer at the heart of our democracy.”

One example Wilson gave is Trump’s “cash-and-carry pardon sales, his rampant theft and his commitment to protecting himself and his allies.” Were we living in a normal life, they’d be marched off to prison, or as Wilson preferred, “the gibbet.”

He wants them to spend the rest of their lives fighting legal battles and investigations while being “treated as the criminals and conspirators they most certainly are. No law firm should hire them, lest they be excluded from any and all government contracts (I was told that’s perfectly normal!).”

He also wants prosecutions that involve jail time, explaining that the only way to stop the level of corruption is to stop letting people get away with it.

“We must send people to jail. Not out of spite, but out of necessity.,” Wilson said.

Our Analysis:

The Dire Diagnosis of Democracy: Rick Wilson’s Scathing Critique

Rick Wilson, a former Republican strategist and co-founder of the Lincoln Project, has penned a column that reads less like a political analysis and more like a necropsy report on the American republic. His diagnosis? Terminal, with the cause of death being a malignant tumor composed of those he deems the damned souls propping up President Donald Trump. Let’s dissect this grim prognosis with the precision it demands, shining a spotlight on the sharp, scathing, and undeniably grounded accusations leveled by Wilson.

The Architects of America’s Downfall

Wilson’s approach is anything but subtle. He bypasses the usual suspects—the “fringes of MAGA screeching on Twitter”—and aims his scalpel straight at the core, the “bottomless black wells where conscience was long ago replaced with corruption and greed.” His gaze is unforgiving, his language vividly venomous.

He labels certain individuals within the Department of Justice not merely as enablers but as “criminals”, a bold assertion that underscores the severity of his accusations. Todd Blanche is dubbed a “legal sugar daddy” to Ghislaine Maxwell, Pamela Jo Bondi a “soulless she-demon,” and Kash Patel reduced to a “drunken dwarf” and a “’Deep State’ pod-bro conspiracist.” These are not mere critiques; they are character assassinations based on their alleged actions and affiliations.

Jeanine Pirro and Susie Wiles do not escape Wilson’s wrath. Pirro is characterized as a “Franzia-soaked harpy,” and Wiles as the “silent enabler” of Trump, the “criminal mastermind.” The language here is deliberately inflammatory, designed to provoke outrage and highlight the perceived moral decay at the heart of Trump’s circle.

The Cancer at the Heart of Democracy

Wilson’s metaphor of a cancer growing within the power structures of Washington is powerful and evocative. It suggests not just a problem but a terminal disease, one that “consumes and expands everything it touches, corrupting it.” This is a potent visualization of his belief that the corruption surrounding Trump is not static but actively spreading, posing an existential threat to American democracy.

He rails against what he perceives as the most egregious sins: “cash-and-carry pardon sales,” “rampant theft,” and a systematic effort to protect allies from accountability. These are heavy charges, ones that Wilson argues should see these individuals not just ousted from power but marched “off to prison,” or even “the gibbet,” a hyperbolic yet impactful call for retribution.

A Prescription for Justice

Wilson’s prescription for this malignancy is as radical as his diagnosis. He calls for nothing short of legal warfare: prosecutions, jail time, and a societal ostracization of those involved. His argument is that mere exposure or public shaming is insufficient; the only cure for this level of corruption is incarceration. “We must send people to jail. Not out of spite, but out of necessity,” he asserts, framing his call to action as a moral imperative rather than a vindictive wish.

Analysis

Rick Wilson’s column serves as a fiery polemic against what he views as the architects of America’s downfall. His language is unapologetically aggressive, his accusations severe, and his solutions drastic. While some might argue that his rhetoric is excessively harsh or even counterproductive, it’s clear that Wilson believes the situation demands nothing less than total war against corruption.

However, in this scorching critique, one must not overlook the importance of due process and the presumption of innocence until proven guilty. While Wilson’s frustration and anger are palpable and, in many ways, understandable given his perspective, the path forward must also involve a commitment to the principles of justice and fairness, even for those we vehemently oppose.

In essence, Rick Wilson’s column is a clarion call to action, a demand for accountability, and a stark warning of what he sees as the potential end of the American republic if these “cancers” are not excised. Whether one agrees with his methods or his message, the urgency and intensity of his concern for the state of American democracy are undeniable.
