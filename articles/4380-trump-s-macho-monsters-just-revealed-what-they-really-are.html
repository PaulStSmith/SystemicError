“No stupid rules of engagement, no nation-building quagmire, no democracy-building exercise, no politically-correct wars.”

These are the ghouls who were delighted — thrilled — when masked ICE thugs in Minneapolis shot Renee Good in the face and Alex Pretti in the back. They then went on TV, giddy, and smeared them to the world. And killed dozens of people so far this year in their concentration camps while delighting in tearing children from their parents.

“We want the bureaucrats to be traumatically affected. When they wake up in the morning, we want them to not want to go to work, because they are increasingly viewed as the villains. We want their funding to be shut down … We want to put them in trauma.”

Yeah, trauma. It’s what today’s Republicans love, so long as it happens to other people. It’s their drug of choice.

Of course they weren’t billionaires, so their lives don’t much matter, right? Like the millions who lost their health insurance when the Big Beautiful Bill redirected ACA subsidies and Medicaid revenue to tax cuts for the morbidly rich. Or the pregnant women across red states who are dying at more than twice the rate of women in blue states because of misogynistic GOP anti-abortion laws.

Trump, Hegseth, Vance, Miller, Leavitt, et al think this sort of thing makes them seem “macho” and “tough.” Nearly 90 percent of Republican voters agree with them.

Those soldiers are just suckers and losers, after all; they should have had the good sense of the Trump men to complain about bone spurs or simply flee the country to avoid the draft, like Grandpa Drumpf did when Germany kicked him out for refusing to serve.

After all, they’re not white people and they’re not rich.

This isn’t the language of leaders reluctantly using force as a last resort; it’s the rhetoric of psychopaths who see the rest of humanity as disposable, as dots in a video game, as objects whose death is entertainment, so long as their own luxury and power are secure.

When a college Republican chat room devolved into a Nazi-loving, Black- and Hispanic-loathing festival of hate, conspiracy theories, and Hitler adoration last week it was just another Thursday. Like Musk giving the Nazi salute — twice — at a Trump rally.

Our Analysis:

Analysis of “Across Iran and the Caribbean” by Thom Hartmann

Thom Hartmann’s article is a scorching critique of the Trump administration and its allies, painting them as not just political figures with controversial policies, but as malevolent actors delighting in death and destruction. Hartmann’s language is hyperbolic, yet it underscores a deep concern about the ethical and moral direction of contemporary American politics. Let’s dissect the key points and the underlying issues with factual precision and critical examination.

The Allegations of Indiscriminate Killing

Hartmann accuses Donald Trump and associates (referred to derisively throughout) of a series of violent actions, including the bombing of an Iranian girls’ school and the killing of sailors and fishermen. These claims are presented without specific dates or verifiable details, making them difficult to substantiate. However, the broader accusation of a cavalier or even enthusiastic approach to military engagements and collateral damage is worth examining against the backdrop of Trump’s military policies and actions, such as the increased use of drone strikes—which indeed have resulted in civilian casualties, as reported by various human rights organizations.

Disdain for the Vulnerable

The article highlights a purported disregard for human life, especially of those in precarious positions, be it immigrants, federal workers, or the uninsured. Here, Hartmann touches on real and significant policy changes, such as the Trump administration’s aggressive immigration policies, layoffs of federal employees, and attempts to dismantle the Affordable Care Act. These actions have had profound effects on the lives of millions, amplifying feelings of insecurity and abandonment among vulnerable populations.

The Politicization of Human Suffering

A major theme in Hartmann’s critique is the alleged politicization of human suffering by Trump and his allies, who are depicted as using tragedy and violence as tools for political gain or to solidify their base’s support. This accusation, while stark, speaks to the broader concern about the erosion of empathy and the increasing polarization in American politics, where loyalty to party or leader can sometimes overshadow basic human compassion.

The Rhetoric of Dehumanization

The language attributed to Trump’s supporters and officials, as described by Hartmann, is dehumanizing and celebrates violence. This rhetoric, if accurately represented, is deeply troubling and reflects a dangerous normalization of cruelty and a disregard for life. The real-life implications of such rhetoric, from emboldening extremist groups to influencing policy decisions, are well-documented and merit serious concern.

Conclusion: Beyond Hyperbole

While Thom Hartmann’s article is heavily laden with hyperbole and unverified claims, the core issues it raises—about the value of human life in policy making, the consequences of militaristic and aggressive foreign policy, and the dehumanizing rhetoric in political discourse—are critically important. These issues demand a reasoned and facts-based discussion, rather than the sensationalism that can sometimes dominate public debate.

In a world teeming with misinformation and polarized opinions, it is essential to sift through the noise and focus on the substance of the critiques being leveled against those in power. The moral and ethical dimensions of governance and policy cannot be overstated, and it is the responsibility of both the media and the public to hold leaders accountable with a keen eye on the facts and a commitment to humanitarian principles.
