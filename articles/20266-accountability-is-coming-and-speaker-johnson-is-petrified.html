Accountability is coming — and Speaker Johnson is petrified

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Mike Johnson Is Not Afraid of Chaos. He Is Afraid of Records.

The Tell

The source story is simple enough: Mike Johnson, speaking before a live audience, described a future Democratic House as a machine for investigations, subpoenas, and scrutiny of Trump allies, while former Republican Steve Schmidt read that as panic. Johnson then cast himself as the keeper of a “protection program,” and Schmidt argued that what he really fears is not partisan overreach but accountability.

Power Knows What Audit Looks Like

Johnson’s remark matters because it reveals where the real power sits. He is not a nervous backbencher guessing at consequences. He is the Speaker of the House, a man close enough to the machinery of power to understand exactly what a serious congressional majority can do. When he says Democrats will “go after” the president’s family, cabinet, donors, and friends, he is not describing abstract partisan warfare. He is describing the basic mechanics of oversight, the same machinery his side has spent years weakening, delaying, and discrediting whenever it pointed upward.

The Protection Racket Language Was the Point

“I run the protection program” is the part that gives the game away. That is not the language of constitutional stewardship. It is the language of a political protection racket, where loyalty buys insulation and power promises immunity. Schmidt is right to treat the line as astonishing, but the deeper scandal is that it sounds believable. Johnson did not accidentally expose a moral lapse; he described a governing culture in which the House majority behaves less like a legislature than a shield.

Accountability Gets Framed As Abuse

The misdirection in this kind of rhetoric is familiar. Scrutiny of power gets recast as persecution of the innocent. Investigations become threats. Subpoenas become vengeance. Oversight becomes mob action. That framing is useful because it asks the public to sympathize with people who held office while punishing the people who were harmed by what that office enabled. Schmidt’s sharper point is the one Johnson cannot say out loud: if the evidence is clean, there is nothing to fear. Panic is what corruption looks like when it hears footsteps in the hallway.

Trump Did Not Operate Alone

The article’s most important claim is also the least sentimental: Trump did not corrupt institutions in isolation. According to Schmidt, he did it with the help of a MAGA Congress that abandoned its duty, tolerated the politicization of the Justice Department and other agencies, and protected power instead of checking it. That is the pattern. Corruption at the top depends on lawmakers who turn constitutional oversight into a loyalty test and then call it prudence. Silence is not neutrality. In a system like this, silence is infrastructure.

The Real Fear

Johnson’s anxiety is not that Americans will see unfair treatment. It is that they may finally see a record. Documents. Witnesses. Hearings. The plain, procedural stuff that corrupt systems hate most because it does not need drama to be devastating. That is why his language sounds defensive rather than confident. He is not preparing for an era of vengeance. He is bracing for the return of questions that should have been asked years ago.

The Systemic Pattern

This story is about more than one Speaker’s nervous phrasing. It exposes a durable political arrangement: power commits the damage, allies cushion the fallout, and then everyone pretends oversight is the real danger. That inversion is how authoritarian habits survive inside formal democracy. Not by announcing themselves, but by teaching their defenders to call accountability a threat and impunity a virtue.
