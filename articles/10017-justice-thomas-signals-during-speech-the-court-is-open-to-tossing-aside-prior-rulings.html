Justice Thomas signals during speech the court is open to tossing aside prior rulings

In a rare public appearance, Supreme Court Associate Justice Clarence Thomas hinted that the nation’s highest court may very well be considering tearing up legal precedents that have been in effect for decades, and some nearly a century old.

Speaking Thursday at the Catholic University Columbus School of Law in Washington, D.C., Thomas argued that the Supreme Court should rethink the way law is interpreted as it relates to stare decisis, the legal doctrine that suggests courts should follow legal precedent, or prior decisions – when making rulings on similar legal matters.

“At some point we need to think about what we’re doing with stare decisis,” Thomas said, according to a Saturday report from ABC News. “And it’s not some sort of talismanic deal where you can just say ‘stare decisis’ and not think, turn off the brain, right?”

The Supreme Court is poised to reconvene at the beginning of October for its next term next, and may have several cases reach its desk that challenge longstanding legal precedents.

The Court is considering reviewing a lower court’s decision related to Obergefell v. Hodges, the landmark 2015 ruling that permitted same-sex marriages. It’s also set to revisit a case challenging the 90-year precedent that restricts the power of the president to indiscriminately terminate federal agency staff, as well as a case that established rules around ethnicity as it relates to redistricting.

“We never go to the front [to] see who’s driving the train, where it [is] going. And you could go up there in the engine room, find it’s an orangutan driving the train, but you want to follow that just because it’s a train,” Thomas said.

“I don’t think that I have the gospel, that any of these cases that have been decided are the gospel, and I do give perspective to the precedent. But it should; the precedent should be respectful of our legal tradition, and our country, and our laws, and be based on something, not just something somebody dreamt up and others went along with.”

Thomas was instrumental in the Court’s overturning ofRoe v. Wadein 2022, a decision that kicked off a wave of trigger laws already adopted by many red states that outright bannedabortion, and in many cases, with no exceptions for rape or incest.

Our Analysis:

Justice Clarence Thomas Signals Potential Upheaval in Legal Precedents

In what can only be described as a chilling prelude to the Supreme Court’s next term, Associate Justice Clarence Thomas has thrown a gauntlet that could very well dismantle decades, if not a century, of legal precedents. Speaking at the Catholic University Columbus School of Law, Thomas didn’t just hint at a reconsideration of stare decisis; he practically shouted it from the rooftops. This doctrine, which emphasizes the importance of adhering to precedent in legal decisions, appears to be on precarious ground, if Thomas’ words are anything to go by.

“At some point we need to think about what we’re doing with stare decisis,” Thomas proclaimed, according to ABC News. This statement is not merely an invitation for legal contemplation but a stark warning that the foundational principles which have guided American jurisprudence for ages might be up for grabs.

The Assault on Stare Decisis

Thomas’ critique of stare decisis is not just an academic exercise; it’s a frontal attack on the very notion of legal stability and predictability. By questioning the sacredness of legal precedent, Thomas is signaling the Court’s willingness to overturn decisions that have long been considered settled law. The potential cases on the Court’s docket—ranging from the landmark 2015 ruling on same-sex marriages (Obergefell v. Hodges) to challenges against the 90-year precedent limiting presidential power over federal agency staff—suggest a Court that is not just open to revisiting established laws but possibly overturning them.

Thomas’ metaphor of an orangutan driving the train is as bewildering as it is concerning. It trivializes the thought, care, and legal reasoning that have underpinned decades of jurisprudential decisions. To suggest that legal precedents might be no more than whimsical fancies “dreamt up” is not just an oversimplification; it’s a dangerous misrepresentation of legal history and the role of the Supreme Court in American society.

The Roe v. Wade Precursor

One cannot ignore Thomas’ pivotal role in the overturning of Roe v. Wade in 2022. This decision not only reversed nearly half a century of reproductive rights but also activated a slew of trigger laws across red states, imposing draconian bans on abortion, often without exceptions for rape or incest. This move laid bare the tangible consequences of abandoning stare decisis—a reality that could extend into other areas of law and life in the United States.

The Implications

What does this mean for the American legal landscape? If Thomas’ rhetoric is a harbinger of things to come, we might be witnessing the beginning of a seismic shift in how the Supreme Court views its role and the laws it is sworn to interpret. The implications are profound and far-reaching. Overturning long-established precedents could not only undo decades of social progress but also erode public trust in the judiciary. It sends a message that legal decisions are not built on the bedrock of judicial principle but are subject to the whims of the sitting justices.

Thomas’ argument that precedents should be “respectful of our legal tradition, and our country, and our laws” is ironic, given that the overturning of such precedents would, in fact, represent a radical departure from those very traditions. It suggests a selective reverence for the past, one that picks and chooses which “traditions” to uphold based on current political and ideological leanings.

Conclusion

Clarence Thomas’ recent statements should not be viewed lightly. They represent a potential unraveling of the legal fabric of the United States, with the Supreme Court at the helm. The principle of stare decisis has long ensured a measure of continuity and predictability in American law. To discard it now, in pursuit of a regressive and ideologically driven agenda, is not just an affront to the doctrine itself but to the very notion of justice.
