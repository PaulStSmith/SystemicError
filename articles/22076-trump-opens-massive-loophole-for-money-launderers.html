Trump opens massive loophole for money launderers

Trump’s War on Transparency Is Not a Mistake

The Registry They Didn’t Want

The Treasury Department just erased a rule that forced U.S.-formed companies to reveal their real owners, then decided most of the existing data should be destroyed. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent wrapped that in the usual fraud of “common sense” and “small business” rhetoric. Former prosecutors see what it is: a deliberate demolition of a basic anti-corruption tool.

This is not bureaucratic drift. It is an executive branch choosing opacity for the people with the most to hide.

Who Gains From Darkness

Beneficial ownership disclosure exists for one reason: to make shell companies harder to use for bribery, money laundering, and tax dodging. Removing that requirement does not help honest local businesses that already operate in the open. It helps the people who need layers of corporate concealment to move dirty money without scrutiny.

That is the central political fact here. The administration is not loosening a harmless rule. It is reopening a hiding place.

The Target Is Not Fraud

Former prosecutor Michael Feinberg described the current posture as a fixation on low-level targets like daycares and food trucks while giving major financial actors a pass. That matters because it reveals the operating principle: punish the weak, spare the connected.

The same dynamic runs through Liz Oyer’s warning that Trump has walked away from major cases against wealthy people, surrendered investigations, pardoned them, and erased restitution owed to victims. That is not inconsistency. It is selective enforcement. The state remains muscular, but only where the targets lack power.

“Common Sense” as Cover

The phrase “small business” is doing propaganda work here. It tries to turn an anti-corruption rollback into a populist gesture, as if transparency were some elite burden imposed on honest Americans. It is the opposite. Transparency is what prevents the wealthy, the criminal, and the politically protected from using ordinary business forms as laundering equipment.

The administration’s rhetoric misdirects blame toward regulation itself, instead of the people exploiting the system. That is the familiar authoritarian move: break the safeguards, then call the wreckage freedom.

Enforcing Inequality

Feinberg’s warning about losing years of investigative expertise is the deeper injury. Once the institutional memory is gutted, future cases against complex fraud become harder to build and easier to bury. That is how impunity becomes durable: not by announcing it, but by making enforcement structurally incapable.

Oyer’s point about disproportionate targeting is equally important. A justice system that comes down hardest on low-income people, especially people of color, while loosening scrutiny on wealthy networks is not malfunctioning. It is reproducing hierarchy through prosecutorial discretion.

The Pattern Is the Point

This story is not about a single rule change. It is about what kind of state Trump wants: one that can still police the vulnerable, but cannot reliably see or punish the powerful. Destroy the records, hollow out the staff, pardon the connected, and then pretend the result is prudence.

That is the political meaning of the Treasury move. It is anti-corruption policy in reverse: an institutional gift to secrecy, delivered in the language of restraint.
