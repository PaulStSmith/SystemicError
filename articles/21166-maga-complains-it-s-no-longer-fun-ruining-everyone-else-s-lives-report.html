MAGA complains it's no longer 'fun' ruining everyone else's lives: report

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Trump’s Funhouse Was Built on Other People’s Damage

President Donald Trump and his MAGA movement retook the government in early 2025, then treated layoffs, spectacle, and Project 2025 as a prank on the republic. By July 2026, the same machine looked less euphoric: the wars, the economic strain, the failed stunts, and the evaporating loyalty from Republican allies had drained the mood. That is the surface story. The deeper story is who got power, who used it, and who is now pretending the damage was just a phase.

The Damage Was the Governing Style

The article’s central fact is not that Trump’s circle stopped having fun. It is that they enjoyed themselves while “sacking and pillaging” the government. That matters. The layoffs, the Project 2025 agenda, the war with Iran, and the election fraud lies were not random misfires or chaotic accidents. They were acts of power carried out by people who knew exactly what they wanted: domination, grievance politics, and institutional vandalism dressed up as restoration.

This is what authoritarian politics looks like when it is not pretending to be managerial. The cruelty was not a bug in the system the way defenders like to imply. It was part of the entertainment.

Trump Held the Steering Wheel

The article is clear about where institutional power sat: Donald Trump was president, and his movement had “regained control of America’s government.” That is the fact that should govern the reading. Trump was not some noisy outsider orbiting a hostile state. He was the state’s executive center, using it to reward loyalists, punish enemies, and stage political theater on federal ground.

The point is not merely that he acted badly. It is that the office of the presidency, the machinery of the government, and the silence or compliance of party actors gave him the room to do it. Power did not drift into chaos. It was exercised.

Republicans Did Not Resist

The article notes that Republican lawmakers are “quiet-quitting,” while the Wall Street Journal called Congress a “zombie Congress.” That is not resistance. That is political self-preservation after the arson has started. These lawmakers helped create the conditions for Trump’s return, then discovered that loyalty to him is expensive when his approval sinks and his stunts stop working.

The same applies to the former allies now keeping their distance: Carlson, Kelly, Owens, Jones, Coulter, Greene, Rogan. Their discomfort does not cleanse them. It proves the coalition was never grounded in principle. It was a media-and-grievance ecosystem held together by shared appetite for destruction, not by any serious program for governing.

Washington Is Not the Victim Here

Sally Quinn’s framing centers the fear and passivity of longtime Washington insiders. That is emotionally legible, but politically incomplete. The capital’s elite discomfort is not the main moral event. The main event is that powerful institutions abided, normalized, and then adapted to a movement that was openly hostile to democratic norms.

The article is most useful when it accidentally reveals this truth: the people who “abided by norms” were not shielded by them. Norms did not protect anyone from a president who came to break them. They only made the establishment slower to recognize the breakage as deliberate. The misdirection is in treating elite helplessness as the core drama when the core drama is elite complicity and delay.

The Collapse of the Act

Trump’s failed attempts to revive his image through a UFC fight on the White House lawn, the Great American State Fair, and a speech falsely claiming the 2020 election was stolen all point to the same problem: spectacle cannot indefinitely substitute for legitimacy. Once the performance gets stale, the coalition starts to fray. The fighters, the influencers, the senators, and the donors all begin checking the exits.

That does not make the danger smaller. It makes it more unstable. A movement that governs through grievance and theatrical dominance becomes more erratic when applause fades. The question is never whether the act is embarrassing. It is what they are still doing with the power they already seized.

The System Behind the Circus

The larger pattern is simple: American institutions keep mistaking authoritarian performance for political noise until it hardens into state power. Then, when the damage becomes impossible to ignore, the same institutions congratulate themselves for “seeing the moment clearly” while the people who enabled it search for a dignified retreat.

This is how democratic decay advances in plain sight. The damage is celebrated as fun, the enablers call it strategy, the late critics call it exhaustion, and the public is left to absorb the consequences. The lesson is not that Trump’s movement has lost its sparkle. It is that the republic was treated as a toy long before the toy started breaking.
