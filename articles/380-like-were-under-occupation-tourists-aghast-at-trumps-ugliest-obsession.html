'Like we’re under occupation': Tourists aghast at Trump’s ugliest obsession

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosJune 21, 20263 min read

Trump’s Capital Project Is Not Renovation. It Is Occupation.

A City Turned Into a Stage Set

The source’s basic fact pattern is simple: Washington, D.C., is being chopped up by Trump-branded construction, and visitors are being shut out of public space while the administration sells it as beautification. That is the surface. The political reality is uglier. The city is being treated less as a capital than as a backdrop for one man’s vanity, with access, preservation, and public use all subordinated to his taste and calendar.

The complaint from tourists and preservationists matters because it exposes the actual power structure. Ordinary visitors do not decide which landmarks get fenced off. Trump does.

The Decision Is the Point

This is not accidental disruption or bureaucratic drift. Trump ordered the East Wing of the White House demolished for a massive ballroom. He pushed a no-bid renovation on the Lincoln Memorial reflecting pool. He is micromanaging city construction tied to his birthday and the nation’s 250th anniversary. The pattern is consistent: public space becomes a personal canvas, and public money becomes an instrument of personal display.

The source notes a $600 million bill for the ballroom and more than $14 million for the reflecting pool project, at roughly seven times a competitor’s estimate. That is not efficiency. It is the familiar authoritarian luxury model: inflate the cost, erase the objection, wrap it in patriotic language, and force everyone else to live with the debris.

The Damage Is Not Collateral

Preservationists talk about disappointment and access problems. That is polite language for a government that has made the city harder to use and uglier to inhabit. Chain-link fences, cranes, dust, steel beams, overturned dirt, blue sealant peeling off the memorial pool, and portable toilets scattered around the area are not signs of renewal. They are the visual grammar of power asserting itself without restraint.

What gets lost in the soft framing is who absorbs the cost. Visitors lose access. Preservation advocates lose heritage. Taxpayers fund the spectacle. Trump gets the symbolism. That distribution is the story.

The Scapegoat Is Always the Public

The administration’s language, according to the source, tries to sell this as making D.C. “safe and beautiful.” That phrase is doing ideological work. It implies that what existed before was unsafe, deficient, or in need of correction, while hiding the fact that the disruption comes from the very authority claiming to fix it. This is a standard move: declare a public place broken, then justify its seizure as improvement.

The article’s framing is weaker when it leans on irony and tourist disappointment alone, because the core issue is not taste. It is control. The city is being locked out so the president can brand it.

Monarchic Instincts in a Constitutional Capital

Robert, the retired history professor quoted in the source, gets closest to the truth: this is a man with the instincts of an absolute monarch presiding over a commemoration of separation from monarchy. That is not a poetic flourish. It is a structural comparison. Trump’s treatment of Washington reflects a political style that prefers spectacle over stewardship, personal imprint over civic function, and command over consent.

Even the language of “restoration” becomes a joke under this regime. Restoring a memorial by flooding it with taxpayer-funded blue plastic residue is not preservation. It is performative governance, where the image of action matters more than the result.

The Real Pattern

This story is not just about one ruined cityscape. It is about how power behaves when it stops answering to the public and starts answering to itself. Public land becomes private theater. National symbols become props. Heritage becomes a nuisance. And the damage is sold as renewal by the same people causing it.

That is the broader pattern: authoritarian politics rarely begins with a coup. It often begins with a fence, a contract, a construction permit, and the smug insistence that what you are watching is improvement.
