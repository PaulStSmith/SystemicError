MAGA Senate hopeful’s ‘desperate’ makeup stunt torched amid blistering ad war

The Mascara Attack Is a Cover Story for Political Failure

The Powerful Chose the Distraction

The Senate Leadership Fund, backing Republican nominee Mike Collins, produced an ad mocking Democratic Sen. Jon Ossoff as a “pretty boy” because his campaign paid an Atlanta-area makeup artist $2,887. That is the relevant context. The political substance is the decision by a powerful super PAC to turn an ordinary television-production expense into a test of masculinity.

Collins Benefits, So Collins Owns It

The ad belongs to the super PAC, but its intended beneficiary is Collins. His campaign does not become a bystander merely because an allied organization delivered the insult.

According to Atlanta Journal-Constitution columnist Bill Torpy, Collins is contending with ethics allegations and accusations of antisemitism and racism involving a family member and former aides. The cosmetics attack offers an escape hatch: replace scrutiny of conduct and judgment with a sneer about Ossoff’s face.

That is not confusion. It is misdirection.

Masculinity as Political Camouflage

“Put down the mascara and join the real world” is not an argument about public money, public policy, or official misconduct. Campaign funds paid a makeup professional for work commonly required under high-definition lighting. The ad instead relies on the premise that grooming makes a male politician unserious or effeminate.

The point is humiliation, not accountability. Collins’ allies are trying to define political legitimacy through gender performance because gender performance is easier to manipulate than a candidate’s record.

The Hypocrisy Is Structural

Torpy identifies Donald Trump and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth as prominent Republicans who also use makeup for television. Hegseth reportedly had a Pentagon green room modified for that purpose, while Trump reportedly keeps face makeup nearby.

The contradiction will not trouble the people who made the ad because consistency was never the standard. Makeup is acceptable when powerful Republican men use it and degrading when it can be weaponized against an opponent. The rule is not “real men do not wear makeup.” The rule is “our men define masculinity; yours can be punished with it.”

The Coverage Should Name the Tactic

Calling the spot desperate is fair but incomplete. “Desperate” describes the campaign’s condition. It does not describe the method.

The method is scapegoating through gender policing: select a trivial expense, strip away its professional context, attach a feminizing insult, and invite voters to substitute contempt for evaluation. Reporting that treats this chiefly as a funny case of Republican hypocrisy risks preserving the diversion. The significant fact is not that Trump and Hegseth also use makeup. It is that an institution with money and electoral influence deliberately converted routine image production into a weapon of social discipline.

The Politics of Manufactured Contempt

This episode reveals a durable political pattern. When scrutiny becomes dangerous, powerful actors manufacture a weaker target: a makeup artist’s invoice, a candidate’s appearance, an insinuation about manhood. The smaller the object, the easier it is to flood the contest with ridicule while leaving consequential questions unanswered.

The mascara is incidental. The real product is contempt—financed, scripted, and deployed to keep voters looking anywhere except at the people seeking power and the record they would rather not defend.
