The problem with 'Daddy Trump'

By Paulo Santos

Ronald Reagan’s policies were terrible, but he tried to present himself as a decent man.

Trump is a despicable human being. His odiousness affects many of us because presidents are parent figures to the nation as a whole.

According to psychological research, we respond to presidents much as we did to parents when we were kids.

George Lakoff, professor of cognitive linguistics at Berkeley, has found that two competing models of parenting shape political preferences: either the “strict parent” or “nurturant parent.”

The strict parent views the world as a dangerous place that needs to be controlled. The nurturant parent emphasizes empathy and mutual responsibility.

Lakoff has found that presidents are elected either because a large portion of the public wants a tough, judgmental parent — or a caring, nurturing one.

Reagan fit into the strict parent model; Barack Obama, the nurturant parent one.

But I think Trump represents a third model — the cruel and abusive parent. A parent so malignantly narcissistic that he wields punishment for his own satisfaction, often in unpredictable ways that make him even more terrifying.

In other words, Trump is not just abusing presidential power by violating laws and the Constitution. His behavior is also abusive.

His malignant narcissism is viciously vindictive. His cruelty borders on sadism; he seems to take pleasure in causing others pain. And he often changes his mind or alters the punishment, creating even more confusion and fear.

I don’t want to oversimplify the very complex relationships between the parenting we had (or subconsciously want) and how we respond to Trump. But I believe these emotional connections are real and important. Critiques of Trump’s policies alone don’t get at them.

Many of us who had nurturing parents are depressed and disoriented by Trump. We find it hard to comprehend how such a detestable person can wield so much power over us.

Even many voters who hold to the strict parent view — who may have had a strict parent and perhaps voted for Trump because they felt the nation and the world were getting out of control — reject his abusiveness. About 6 in 10 U.S. adults now say that Trump has “gone too far” in using presidential power to achieve his goals.

A third group is profoundly shaken by Trump. Of the people I know who are most emotionally devastated by him, many had at least one abusive parent.

I suspect some are overwhelmingly drawn to him for the same reason, but instead of being shattered by him they are fanatically loyal. This would include the sycophants now surrounding him in the White House and Cabinet who appear to share his cruelty and sadism.

Research shows that abusive parents often become more abusive over time — more enraged, more paranoid, and less predictable.

Hence, the children of cruel and abusive parents tend to abandon them as soon as they are able. Or cling to them ever more desperately.

Let’s hope all of America does the former with Trump, and the sooner the better.

Our Analysis:

Trump: The Abusive Patriarch of American Politics?

In an article that is both a psychological exploration and a political critique, Robert Reich delves into the deeply unsettling parallel between the familial archetype and the presidency of Donald Trump. Reich, a professor of public policy at Berkeley and former secretary of labor, does not mince words when he describes Trump not just as a problematic leader but as a despicable human being. This isn't just a political analysis; it's a scathing moral indictment.

Trump's Uniquely Troubling Presidency

Reich contrasts Trump with past presidents, positing that while figures like Ronald Reagan embraced the "strict parent" model and Barack Obama the "nurturant parent" one, Trump embodies something far darker: the cruel and abusive parent. This isn't a mere difference in policy or approach; it's a fundamental deviation in character and intent. The assertion here is bold and profoundly disturbing: Trump, according to Reich, takes pleasure in cruelty, engages in malignant narcissism, and wields punishment with a sadistic joy.

This parallel between domestic abusers and Trump's presidency is not drawn lightly. It's based on the psychological frameworks proposed by George Lakoff, suggesting that our political preferences might indeed stem from deep-seated familial dynamics. But Reich goes further, suggesting that Trump's behavior triggers a visceral response in many Americans, particularly those with nurturing parents or, tragically, those who have suffered at the hands of abusive ones.

The Emotional Toll of Trump's Presidency

Reich's analysis takes us into the psychology of the American electorate, suggesting that Trump's presidency has not just political ramifications but profound emotional and psychological ones. He posits that Trump's unpredictable and vindictive nature doesn't just create policy uncertainty; it instills fear and confusion, reminiscent of the terror an abusive parent might wield over their children.

The suggestion that a significant portion of Trump's support base might be composed of individuals with a history of abusive parenting, either identifying with Trump's authoritarianism or seeking a perverse form of validation from it, is a disturbing indictment of the cyclical nature of abuse. Equally, Reich points out the broad swath of Americans repulsed by Trump's abusiveness, highlighting the national yearning for a return to a more empathetic, nurturing form of leadership.

A Call to Political and Emotional Liberation

Reich's conclusion is both a warning and a plea: that America, like the children of abusive parents, must either abandon Trump or "cling to him ever more desperately." This framing not only emphasizes the urgency of moving away from Trump's leadership style but also the potential difficulty in doing so, given the complex psychological dynamics at play.

Conclusion: A Razor-Sharp Indictment with Broad Implications

Reich's article is a fierce critique of Donald Trump, not just as a president, but as a person. It's a reminder that the implications of leadership extend far beyond policy into the very psyche of a nation. While some might argue that drawing parallels between family dynamics and political leadership oversimplifies the complexities at play, Reich's argument is compelling in its clarity and its urgency.

In essence, this article isn't just a political critique; it's a call to examine the deeper emotional and psychological impacts of leadership. It challenges us to consider not just what policies are enacted, but the character of those who lead us and the emotional toll their leadership takes on the nation.
