Republican senator admits the quiet part out loud when it comes to Epstein files

U.S. President Donald Trump walks on the tarmac at Morristown Airport, as he departs for Washington, in Morristown, New Jersey, U.S., July 6, 2025. REUTERS/Nathan Howard

Sen. Markwayne Mullin (R-OK) took to the Senate floor on Thursday, where he confessed that he wants to give the president "cover" on Jeffrey Epstein.

Sen. Ruben Gallego (D-AZ) submitted a list of demands that Democrats want for the release of files related to the Epstein investigation.

"The Committee demands that the Department of Justice produce the full and complete files related to Jeffrey Epstein and his associates within 30 calendar days," the demands list says, according to an MSNBC report.

Mullin, however, opposes the release of all documents as previously promised by several officials in President Donald Trump's administration.

Citing "boundaries between the separation of power" between the executive and legislative branches, Mullin said that his resolution would call on the other government branch, the judiciary, to release the grand jury documents. It does not mandate the release.

"What my colleague's resolution does is truly trying to tell the FBI and the DOJ how they can proceed in doing their job and how they can actually present the information," said Mullin. Instead, his resolution would do that with the judges who presided over the grand jury.

Mullin believes that Democrats want to run the release of the documents through Congress and that it would end in partisan politics.

"Well, I'm sure Congress has never once played politics with any information. I'm sure that this investigation will be handled like many of the other investigations have been handled in this administration, like Democrats have done over the last eight years. I'm sure it will be handled like every other thing, like going after the baseless impeachments or the baseless special counsels, or the unbelievable amount of charges they tried to file against the president. I'm sure this will be handled the exact same way," argued Mullin.

"What we're simply wanting to do here is give them cover," Mullin said of those in the Trump administration. "So, if my colleague from Arizona actually wants transparency, then he would drop his objection to my resolution and just simply have mine go through and we can move forward with the transparency that maybe both of us actually want. Therefore, I object to combining them."

FBI Director Kash Patel and Deputy Director Dan Bongino both pledged to release the files upon taking office. Attorney General Pam Bondi said that she had the documents "on my desk," and was reviewing them. She has already released volume one of the documents to far-right influencers.

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Shadows of Power: The Epstein Files Controversy

In a recent spectacle on the Senate floor, Sen. Markwayne Mullin (R-OK) emerged as the latest character in the ongoing drama that orbits the infamous Jeffrey Epstein case. Mullin, in a move that could be seen as both a protective gesture towards the executive branch and a strategic political maneuver, voiced his opposition to the full release of documents related to the Epstein investigation, documents that were once promised by officials in President Donald Trump's administration.

The Veil of Separation of Powers

Mullin's argument hinges on the "boundaries between the separation of power" between the executive and legislative branches. Yet, this reasoning feels conveniently timed and strategically deployed. It's curious how these concerns about separation of powers become particularly pronounced when the information at stake could potentially embarrass or implicate members of the executive branch or its allies. Mullin proposes that the judiciary, rather than Congress, should handle the release of grand jury documents, a move that conveniently sidesteps direct accountability and potentially muddies the waters of transparency.

The Irony of Seeking Transparency

The senator's claim that Democrats aim to politicize the investigation's findings is rich with irony. Mullin's own resolution, under the guise of seeking transparency, appears to be a thinly veiled attempt to control the narrative by dictating how and through whom these documents should be released. His assertion that he is simply giving the Trump administration "cover" is a candid admission that his motives may be more about protecting political allies than about ensuring the public's right to know.

The Partisan Ping-Pong

What Mullin decries as potential partisan misuse of the Epstein documents by Democrats can easily be flipped on its head. His resolution and the resistance to combining it with the demands from Sen. Ruben Gallego (D-AZ) could be seen as preemptive partisan defense. It's a classic case of the pot calling the kettle black. The senator’s sarcastic remarks about Congress never playing politics with information underscore a deeper truth about the pervasive nature of partisanship in handling sensitive or potentially damaging information.

The Promises Unkept

The pledges made by FBI Director Kash Patel, Deputy Director Dan Bongino, and Attorney General Pam Bondi to release the Epstein files now seem like empty promises designed to appease public demand for transparency without any real intention of following through. The selective release of documents to far-right influencers by Bondi does not inspire confidence in an unbiased or comprehensive disclosure.

Conclusion: The Mirage of Transparency

The handling of the Epstein investigation documents is emblematic of a broader issue within American politics: the manipulation of transparency for political gain. Mullin’s maneuvers on the Senate floor, while framed as a defense of procedural integrity and transparency, are instead a stark illustration of the lengths to which political figures will go to protect their allies and control the narrative. In the shadowy corridors of power, the truth about Epstein and his associates remains obscured, a victim of the very political machinations that officials like Mullin claim to abhor. The real casualty in this saga is the public's trust in their government to act in the interest of justice, rather than in the service of partisan or personal protection.
