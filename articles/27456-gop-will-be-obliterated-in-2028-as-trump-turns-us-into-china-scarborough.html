GOP 'will be obliterated in 2028' as Trump turns US into China: Scarborough

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 25, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Government power as a bargaining chipSecurity scrutiny reduced to a transactionRepublican cowardice is an institutional choice

Joe Scarborough argued Tuesday that Republicans face electoral ruin in 2028 if they continue surrendering economic and national-security policy to President Donald Trump. On MSNBC’s “Morning Joe,” Scarborough accused Trump of using federal power to secure corporate concessions, override security concerns and enrich himself and his associates. AlterNet’s account supports the charge that Scarborough condemned Republican submission; it does not, however, include the quoted prediction that the GOP “will be obliterated.”

Government power as a bargaining chip

Scarborough’s criticism centered on Trump’s interventions in major corporations. He said the federal government had acquired partial control of 40 companies, citing the libertarian Cato Institute, and argued that Trump was replacing market competition with political command.

Co-host Willie Geist called the emerging system “state capitalism,” comparing Trump’s approach to the Chinese model under President Xi Jinping. Scarborough used the more ideologically loaded phrase “state socialism.” The label matters less than the conduct he described: the president deciding which executives may remain, what companies must surrender and which security objections can be discarded.

Scarborough pointed to Intel as an example. Sen. Tom Cotton, an Arkansas Republican, had alleged that the company’s chairman possessed connections to communist China. According to Scarborough’s account, Trump responded by demanding the chairman’s removal unless the company gave the federal government a 10 percent stake.

That is not conventional Republican deregulation. It is executive coercion presented as dealmaking: presidential authority becomes leverage, and corporate governance becomes subject to terms dictated by one officeholder.

Security scrutiny reduced to a transaction

AlterNet also reported Scarborough’s attack on Trump’s demand involving Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang. The supplied account describes Trump as seeking Huang’s reinstatement and Scarborough as saying that Treasury, intelligence and national-security agencies had been investigating possible transfers of information or technology to China. The excerpt does not clearly identify the position to which Huang was supposedly being restored, so the precise action remains unclear.

Scarborough’s stated objection was nevertheless specific. He accused Trump of brushing aside those investigations and ordering that Huang be put “back in.” He further claimed that Huang’s case involved a $25 million donation to Trump’s presidential library.

Those allegations, as presented, came from Scarborough. But the political structure he described is plain: agencies tasked with assessing security risks conduct investigations, while the president allegedly treats their work as negotiable when money and corporate control enter the discussion. The public rationale is national strength; the operative mechanism is personal discretion.

Scarborough also alleged that Trump made tens of millions of dollars during the episode and that Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick’s former firm gained hundreds of millions. AlterNet’s excerpt does not detail the transactions, so those figures should remain attributed claims rather than established findings.

Republican cowardice is an institutional choice

Scarborough framed congressional Republicans as enablers, not helpless spectators. He said they could resist Trump or “sit back like cowards for another two years” while he endangered national security. Mika Brzezinski summarized their prevailing posture in two words: “Roll over.”

That is the sharper political point beneath the argument over whether Trump’s model qualifies as socialism or capitalism. The president holds executive power, but Republican lawmakers possess legislative authority, investigative machinery and control over their own political conduct. Refusing to use those powers is itself a decision.

Scarborough tied that submission to what he characterized as the party’s worst defeat since 1966 in terms of reshaping the political map. His warning was therefore less a forecast produced from electoral data than an indictment of incentives: Republicans may continue protecting Trump because confronting him carries an immediate political cost, even if obedience leaves them defending government takeovers, discarded security reviews and presidential profiteering allegations later.

The “socialism” language is designed to expose Republican hypocrisy, but it can also obscure the more precise abuse Scarborough alleged. The issue is not simply that Washington owns corporate stakes. It is that Trump allegedly uses state power selectively—rewarding compliance, demanding concessions and subordinating institutional judgments to transactions he controls. Republicans cannot credibly blame an ideology for conduct their own surrender makes possible.
