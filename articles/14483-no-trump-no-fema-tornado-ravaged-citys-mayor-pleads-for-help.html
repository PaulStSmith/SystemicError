No Trump, no FEMA? Tornado-ravaged city’s mayor pleads for help

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 24, 20256 min read

A woman sits on top of the wreckage of her home caused by the tornado in St. Louis, Missouri, U.S. May 18, 2025, REUTERS/Lawrence Bryant

At least 28 people across three states were killed when tornadoes struck Kentucky, Missouri, and Virginia on Friday, with a governor and a mayor calling them among the worst they’ve ever seen. Unseen, however, has been any acknowledgment or support from President Donald Trump or, according to some reports, the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA).

“What we need right now is federal assistance,” declared St. Louis, Missouri Mayor Cara Spencer on MSNBC (video below) on Monday, “we need federal assistance.”

“This is where FEMA and the federal government has got to come in and help communities,” Mayor Spencer urged. “Our city cannot shoulder this alone. The State of Missouri cannot shoulder this alone. We need partners at the national level, at the federal level to step up and help.”

Spencer said, “This is what the federal government is for. We need your help, we need the help of the broader community.”

“FEMA has not been on the ground—we do not have confirmed assistance from FEMA at this point,” Spencer said. “I do want to say, however, every other level of government has been on the ground with us, helping in every capacity possible. But when you have a disaster of this scale, eight miles of just pure destruction, this tornado didn’t just touch down and leave, this tornado ripped through our community for a full eight miles in the city of St. Louis, and this is an area that has needed help, that we need investment, you know, our North St. Louis has been neglected for a long time, and we need the help of our partners here.”

At a news conference, Spencer had called it “one of the worst storms,” ABC News reported. She said that “the devastation is truly heartbreaking—and let’s not forget people have lost their lives. We are continuing to make sure that we are identifying all those that are injured, in addition to the massive amount of property damage that has taken a huge toll.”

Tornadoes were reported in three more states, bringing the total to six states and 26 tornadoes.

“Over 462,000 customers were without power across multiple states, stretching from Michigan to Tennessee.”

As of publication time, NCRM was unable to find anything from President Donald Trump on his Truth Social page about the tornadoes’ death and destruction. It does not appear he has offered support or guidance, nor has he suggested he will visit the areas.

“You can not only see the destruction, you can feel it,” Kentucky Democratic Governor Andy Beshear told reporters on Saturday as he toured his state, according to ABC. “Beshear, who declared a state of emergency ahead of the storms, said he’s been governor for at least 13 federally declared disasters related to weather and this storm was one of the worst in terms of loss of life and damage.”

Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem in a social media post made no mention of FEMA, but said that she had spoken to Missouri Governor Mike Kehoe, Kentucky Governor Andy Beshear, and Illinois Governor JB Pritzker, “to offer federal resources and action for the deadly tornadoes and storms impacting Missouri, Kentucky, and Illinois.”

She suggested that the federal government would take a back seat to local efforts.

“We discussed how while emergency management is best led by local authorities, we reinforced that DHS stands ready to take immediate action to offer resources and support,” Noem wrote on Saturday. “Local emergency managers should swiftly notify people in the affected areas to take action to protect themselves and their belongings. DHS stands ready to help when a state needs, requests, and declares an emergency.”

Fred Wellman, an Army combat veteran and host of the “On Democracy” podcast from the St. Louis area, on Saturday wrote: “Yesterday my hometown was hit with massive tornadoes. We weren’t expecting them in places that have never been hit before and have no idea who will help us. FEMA is all but dead and our state is run by Republicans that hate the city. This is the canary in the coal mine.”

On his Substack he noted: “A tornado went through my town yesterday, and no one in this entire country is going to help us….and the ones that should are fine with that.”

Monday morning headded, “Here we are two days later and not one peep from Trump or even a response from FEMA at all. 5 dead, 5,000 homes damaged, $1.6B in damage and not even a s—– Truth social post or email from FEMA.”

Our Analysis:

Analysis: Federal Response to Tornado Disaster - A Case of Negligence?

In a time of crisis, the citizens of Kentucky, Missouri, and Virginia found themselves grappling with the aftermath of devastating tornadoes that claimed at least 28 lives across three states. The destruction, as reported, was unprecedented, leaving communities in dire need of assistance. Yet, amid this chaos, there appears to be a stark absence of federal leadership and support, particularly from President Donald Trump and the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA).

Presidential Silence: A Deafening Void

The lack of public acknowledgment or support from President Trump is troubling, to say the least. In an era where social media platforms like Truth Social provide instant communication channels, the absence of even a token gesture of solidarity or sympathy from the President is a glaring omission. This silence speaks volumes, reflecting a disturbing indifference to the suffering of American citizens facing life-altering devastation.

FEMA's MIA Status: A Failure to Serve

Equally concerning is the reported absence of FEMA on the ground. As St. Louis Mayor Cara Spencer poignantly highlighted, the scale of destruction demands federal intervention—a fundamental expectation of the federal government in times of disaster. FEMA's role is precisely to aid communities during such crises, yet if reports are accurate, its inaction here is a gross abdication of its responsibilities. This is not just an operational failure; it's a betrayal of the trust communities place in their government.

Local Desperation Meets Federal Inaction

Mayor Spencer's pleas for federal assistance underscore a desperate need for support that has, so far, been met with silence. The detailed account of the tornado's eight-mile swath of destruction through St. Louis highlights the severity of the situation. When local and state resources are overwhelmed, federal assistance is not just helpful; it is essential. The failure to provide timely support not only hampers recovery efforts but also signals a worrying disregard for the welfare of affected communities.

The Response from Homeland Security

The remarks from Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem, while acknowledging communication with state governors, suggest a passive stance that favors local management of the crisis over direct federal intervention. While local leadership in emergency situations is crucial, the federal government’s support role is not just supplementary; it is critical to the recovery process. The suggestion that DHS "stands ready" feels insufficient in the face of actual, immediate need.

The Bigger Picture: Indicative of a Larger Issue?

Fred Wellman’s comments paint a grim picture of a community left to fend for itself, a sentiment that appears to be echoed across the affected areas. This situation raises broader questions about the federal government's commitment to disaster response and its willingness to support its most vulnerable citizens.

Conclusion: A Call for Accountability

The federal government's response—or lack thereof—to the tornado disaster is not just a failure of policy or procedure; it is a failure of empathy, leadership, and responsibility. This crisis should serve as a wake-up call for a comprehensive evaluation of disaster response protocols and the importance of timely, efficient, and empathetic action from all levels of government. The citizens of Kentucky, Missouri, and Virginia deserved better. The question now is whether this will be a learning moment or a forgotten footnote in the ongoing saga of disaster management failures. In either case, the need for accountability is undeniable.
