'Devastating': Glee erupts as Susan Collins' 'road salt' boast blows up in her face

Based on an article by Bennito Kelty

Paulo SantosOctober 8, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that Sen. Susan Collins, during a Tuesday debate in Maine against Democratic challenger Troy Jackson, answered a question about President Donald Trump’s tariffs on Canada by touting her success in getting road salt removed from the tariff list. Jackson replied, “What about everything else? We can’t eat road salt.”

Jackson also linked household affordability to what he called Trump’s “Iran war,” arguing that fewer people would be able to afford to drive. Clips circulated online, where Democratic officials, strategists and liberal commentators praised his retort and mocked Collins’ answer. The reporting does not establish whether the exchange changed any votes.

Power was already in the room

Collins is not an observer pleading for relief. She is a sitting United States senator defending her record. Trump imposed the tariffs at issue. Collins presented one exemption as proof that she had exercised influence successfully.

That makes the unanswered question more important than the celebrated comeback: What did Collins do about the broader policy? Her road-salt example shows access and some leverage. It does not demonstrate that she meaningfully restrained the administration’s wider tariff agenda or protected Mainers from its other costs.

A narrow exception is not a governing record

Politicians routinely market carve-outs as victories because carve-outs let them claim effectiveness without confronting the policy producing the damage. The governing failure becomes background scenery; the exception becomes the headline achievement.

Jackson’s line worked because it exposed that substitution in five words. Road salt matters in Maine, but Collins offered it in response to a question about tariffs on Canada, not a question about winter road maintenance. The mismatch made her answer sound less like evidence of control than an admission of how little control she could claim.

The weaker actor did not create the problem

The viral framing naturally centers Jackson because he delivered the sharp line. But Jackson, as the challenger described by RawStory, did not impose the tariffs. Collins did not originate them either, based on this reporting. Trump holds the executive power behind the tariff policy, while Collins holds legislative power and political influence as an incumbent senator.

Reducing the episode to Jackson “destroying” Collins risks turning a dispute over material policy into campaign-spectacle scoring. The retort identified the inadequacy of Collins’ defense; it did not cause that inadequacy. The governing choices came first.

Virality is not accountability

RawStory’s evidence for “glee” is a collection of approving social-media reactions, largely from Democratic and liberal figures. Those reactions establish that the clip traveled within a partisan media ecosystem. They do not establish broad public opinion or electoral movement.

Claims that the line could sway the election were predictions, not results. Treating applause as consequence flatters the political internet’s preferred fiction: that a devastating post is equivalent to a change in power. It is not. Collins’ record, Trump’s policies and voters’ material conditions will persist after the clip disappears.

The politics of the token concession

This exchange reveals a familiar governing pattern. An administration adopts a broad policy; an allied officeholder secures a limited exemption; the exemption is then advertised as protection from the very policy the officeholder failed to stop.

Jackson’s comeback punctured that sales pitch because it restored scale. A senator’s power should be judged by the harms she prevents, the policies she enables and the interests she chooses to confront—not by one item successfully removed from a list. When government delivers broad costs and officials campaign on narrow escapes, the exemption is not the whole achievement. It is evidence of the larger failure surrounding it.
