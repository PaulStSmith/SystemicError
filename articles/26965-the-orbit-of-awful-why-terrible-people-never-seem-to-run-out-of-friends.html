The Orbit of Awful: Why Terrible People Never Seem to Run Out of Friends

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 15, 202610 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There is something about the Trump universe that has bothered me for years, and it isn’t the usual question.

I understand why millions of strangers vote for Donald Trump.

I may despise their choice. I may reject the increasingly threadbare excuse that they simply don’t know what he is. But at least the mechanism is comprehensible. Most voters have never met Trump. They encounter a manufactured version of him through television, social media, rallies, partisan media and political advertising.

What is harder to understand is everyone standing closer to the fire.

The people who actually know these people.

The businessmen who socialize with them. The politicians who dine with them. The donors who vacation with them. The lawyers who represent them. The aides who spend twelve hours a day around them. The wealthy friends attending weddings and parties. The people who have presumably watched the Trump family behave when there are no television cameras pointed at them.

They cannot plead ignorance.

And yet the orbit remains crowded.

That question became impossible to ignore after ProPublica reported an extraordinary detail about Donald Trump Jr.’s wedding celebrations with Bettina Anderson in the Bahamas.

According to the reporting, Umar Kremlev — a Russian oligarch close to Vladimir Putin and president of the International Boxing Association — paid hundreds of thousands of dollars toward the festivities. The spending reportedly included renting a private island, accommodations and fireworks, with payments flowing through an IBA-affiliated entity in Dubai.

This wasn’t a bottle of champagne.

This was the kind of “wedding gift” that requires accounting departments.

Kremlev was reportedly there, too, alongside members of the Trump family and a contingent of Russian-speaking guests whose presence apparently puzzled some attendees.

Bettina Anderson’s defense was wonderfully revealing.

Kremlev, she explained, was simply a dear friend who had generously hosted celebrations for them. There was “NO CONSPIRACY, NO MYSTERY.” Sometimes, she argued, generosity is just generosity.

Perhaps.

But that explanation leads directly to the more interesting question.

Why do people like this keep finding one another?

And why, after everything we have learned about Donald Trump and the ecosystem surrounding him, are there still so many people eager to enter that ecosystem?

The answer is uncomfortable because there isn’t one answer.

There is an entire ecology.

Some of Them Actually Like This

We need to dispense with one of political journalism’s most persistent comforting assumptions: that everyone surrounding an awful person must secretly recognize the awfulness.

Some people don’t.

Or, more precisely, they recognize exactly the same behavior we recognize and assign it the opposite value.

Trump humiliates people publicly.

He demands loyalty while offering little in return.

He boasts incessantly.

He treats wealth as proof of personal superiority.

He treats rules as obstacles for lesser people.

He attacks institutions that constrain him.

He praises those who submit and retaliates against those who refuse.

Many of us look at this and see narcissism, corruption and authoritarianism.

Someone with a dominance-oriented conception of the world can look at exactly the same behavior and see strength.

That distinction matters.

If you believe society naturally consists of winners and losers, masters and servants, predators and prey, then cruelty toward people below you isn’t necessarily a character defect.

It can become evidence that you belong above them.

Trump’s great political innovation was not convincing everyone that he was kind.

It was convincing millions of people that kindness was weakness.

Once that transformation occurs, behavior that should disqualify someone instead becomes part of the attraction.

The bully isn’t embarrassing.

He’s the guy you want sitting at your table.

Provided, of course, that you’re not the person he’s bullying.

The Transactional Orbit

Then there are people who don’t particularly care whether Trump is admirable.

They care whether Trump is useful.

This may be the largest category in the professional political ecosystem.

They want something.

A cabinet position.

A judgeship.

A contract.

A regulatory exemption.

A tariff carve-out.

A pardon.

An ambassadorship.

Access to federal officials.

An introduction to someone wealthy.

A government policy that makes their company considerably more valuable.

Or simply a photograph demonstrating that they can get into rooms other people cannot.

None of these people needs to love Donald Trump.

They merely need to calculate that whatever they gain from proximity to him is worth whatever they must tolerate to obtain it.

Morality becomes another column in the spreadsheet.

And once you understand the relationship that way, something else becomes apparent.

Trump’s orbit contains a powerful selection mechanism.

Imagine beginning with one thousand people.

Trump does something outrageous.

One hundred decide they cannot tolerate it and leave.

Nine hundred remain.

Then he does something worse.

Another hundred leave.

Then something worse.

Another hundred.

Keep repeating the experiment.

Who eventually remains?

Not a random sample of the original population.

You have progressively removed everyone whose ethical boundaries were triggered by the behavior they witnessed.

What remains is a population increasingly selected for its ability to rationalize, tolerate, participate in or enjoy that behavior.

It is political natural selection.

Except the environmental pressure favors sycophancy.

Every scandal asks another question:

“Is this enough for you to leave?”

And everyone who remains answers:

“No.”

Ask that question enough times and eventually the answer itself becomes the principal qualification for membership.

The People Who Think They Can Use Him

There is another species in the ecosystem: the sophisticated operator who believes he has figured everything out.

Yes, Trump is crude.

Yes, Trump is ignorant.

Yes, Trump is unstable.

But we understand how to manage him.

We will flatter him.

We will give him applause.

We will let him perform for the cameras.

And while he’s distracted, we’ll get the policies we actually want.

This is one of history’s recurring jokes, and somehow every generation believes it has invented a better punchline.

The American religious right made perhaps the clearest version of this bargain.

Nobody needed Donald Trump to become a model Christian.

The transaction was simpler.

They had voters.

He wanted voters.

He had judicial appointments.

They wanted judicial appointments.

Deal.

They could overlook the adultery, the sexual misconduct allegations, the vulgarity, the dishonesty and practically every traditional moral standard they had spent decades demanding politicians publicly perform.

They would give him legitimacy.

He would give them power.

And for a while, everyone could pretend they were the ones using him.

But there is a problem with repeatedly surrendering your principles in exchange for access to power.

Eventually you no longer possess principles with which to resist that power.

The people who believed they could control the strongman gradually discovered that their careers depended upon pleasing him.

They thought they were holding the leash.

Then one day they discovered they were wearing the collar.

Power Is a Drug

There is another explanation that requires no grand ideology whatsoever.

Proximity to power feels good.

Imagine being moderately wealthy and successful but otherwise anonymous.

Then suddenly the president knows your name.

Your calls get returned.

Cabinet officials recognize you.

Billionaires invite you to dinner.

Reporters ask for your opinion.

Foreign dignitaries shake your hand.

Secret Service agents stand outside rooms you’re allowed to enter.

You board aircraft other people cannot board and enter buildings other people cannot enter.

You know things before everyone else knows them.

You become important.

Or at least you feel important.

Human beings are extraordinarily susceptible to status, and political power provides one of the purest concentrations of status civilization has invented.

Once someone becomes accustomed to that environment, leaving doesn’t merely mean abandoning a politician.

It means becoming ordinary again.

Yesterday you were sitting twenty feet from the president.

Tomorrow nobody cares what you think.

People have rationalized considerably worse things than Donald Trump to avoid that demotion.

The Most Effective Influence Operation Is Friendship

Which brings us back to Umar Kremlev.

Bettina Anderson’s defense of his extraordinary generosity misunderstands — perhaps deliberately, perhaps not — the nature of the concern.

Suppose she’s completely right.

Suppose Kremlev genuinely likes Donald Trump Jr.

Suppose they really are friends.

Suppose nobody has ever discussed sanctions, Russian policy, business favors, diplomatic access or anything remotely improper.

That does not eliminate the counterintelligence problem.

It makes the mechanism better.

People imagine foreign influence as espionage theater.

A mysterious man in a trench coat.

A clandestine meeting.

An envelope filled with cash.

A whispered demand.

Real human influence can be far subtler because human beings are reciprocal creatures.

You invite someone to dinner.

Then to your yacht.

Then to your villa.

You introduce him to useful people.

You solve a problem for him.

You pay for something.

You become friends.

You help each other.

Eventually, there doesn’t need to be a transaction.

Your friend calls.

“Don, there’s this sanctions issue that’s causing us enormous trouble…”

Now Donald Trump Jr. isn’t helping a Russian oligarch.

He’s helping Umar.

His friend.

That is infinitely more psychologically powerful than a briefcase full of money.

The best influence operation is the one the target doesn’t experience as influence at all.

It feels like friendship.

And Sometimes Awful People Simply Like Other Awful People

There is one final explanation political analysis frequently avoids because it feels insufficiently sophisticated.

Maybe some of these people simply aren’t very good people either.

We constantly search for mechanisms that preserve an assumption of underlying decency.

Money corrupted them.

Propaganda fooled them.

Power seduced them.

Fear intimidated them.

Career incentives trapped them.

Sometimes those explanations are correct.

But they aren’t universal.

Sometimes cruel people enjoy the company of cruel people.

Narcissists can enjoy other narcissists when the relationship is mutually advantageous.

Grifters recognize other grifters.

Authoritarians admire successful authoritarians.

Extremely wealthy people who resent society telling them what they may do with their money can find common cause with politicians who resent society telling them what they may do with their power.

People who consider empathy an impediment don’t necessarily recoil when they encounter someone similarly unconstrained by it.

They may experience recognition.

Finally. Someone who gets it.

This possibility deserves considerably more attention because it changes how we understand the entire Trump ecosystem.

Perhaps we shouldn’t picture a fundamentally decent political establishment being slowly contaminated by one uniquely malignant personality.

Perhaps Trump became a focal point around which people with compatible appetites could organize.

Opportunists found opportunities.

Authoritarians found authority.

Grifters found customers.

Billionaires found deregulation.

Religious reactionaries found judges.

Foreign interests found access.

Careerists found careers.

Cruel people found permission.

And everyone found everyone else.

That isn’t merely a political coalition.

It is a social network.

The Orbit Is the Point

Which finally answers the question that started all of this.

How can Donald Trump — and people around him who have repeatedly demonstrated corruption, cruelty, narcissism and contempt for ordinary restraints — continue attracting people into their orbit?

Because we’re assuming the orbit exists despite those characteristics.

For many participants, it doesn’t.

Some tolerate them because they want something.

Some rationalize them because their careers depend upon it.

Some believe they can manipulate the system.

Some become addicted to proximity to power.

Some are foreign actors cultivating useful relationships.

Some admire dominance.

And some simply feel at home.

The orbit then perpetuates itself.

Each person who joins provides legitimacy to everyone already there. Each billionaire makes the politicians look respectable. Each politician makes the businessmen look connected. Each celebrity makes the parties look glamorous. Each foreign dignitary makes the family look internationally important.

And each participant can look around the room and reassure himself:

Surely this can’t be that bad. Look at all these respectable people.

Except everyone else in the room may be performing exactly the same calculation.

That is how corruption becomes culture.

That is how abnormal behavior becomes ordinary.

That is how moral boundaries disappear without anyone formally announcing that they have been abolished.

And that may be the most dangerous thing about the Trump orbit.

Donald Trump will eventually leave politics.

The network of people who discovered that shamelessness works will remain.

They have met one another now.

They have exchanged telephone numbers.

They have done business together.

They have learned which politicians can be bought with access, which billionaires will finance ideological projects, which media figures will manufacture narratives, which lawyers will construct convenient theories, which activists will mobilize the base and which voters will tolerate almost anything provided the cruelty lands on someone they dislike.

Trump didn’t create every one of these people.

He introduced them.

So perhaps the question we’ve spent the last decade asking was wrong.

We keep asking:

“How can all these people tolerate being around Donald Trump?”

Perhaps we should ask something considerably darker.

What if tolerating Donald Trump was never the unfortunate cost of admission?

What if it was the qualification?

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
