Elite Proximity Is Not Innocence

A Narrow Interview, A Wider Crime Scene

House Oversight was looking into Jeffrey Epstein’s ties to a high-powered corporate lawyer, Kathy Ruemmler. In a closed-door July 15 interview, Ruemmler insisted her advice to Epstein was ordinary professional work and said she had dealt with him because he expressed remorse after his 2008 guilty plea for soliciting a minor. But the transcript, as reported by ABC News, also shows something else: Epstein was not just a client in the abstract. He helped her secure work with French banker Ariane de Rothschild, and he connected another lawyer at her firm with a criminal-case client.

That is the real context. This was not a story about an unfortunate misunderstanding. It was a story about elite networks doing what they always do: laundering proximity into respectability.

Who Holds Power Here

Ruemmler is not a powerless bystander. She is a former Goldman Sachs general counsel, which means she comes from the same institutional class that shapes money, law, and access behind closed doors. Epstein’s social and professional utility depended on that class as much as it depended on his personal rot. The lawmakers, for their part, do hold formal oversight power. But in this exchange, the witness was not submitting to scrutiny. She was trying to turn the hearing into a lecture on standards.

That matters because power in Washington is often exercised less through direct command than through the ability to redefine the terms of accountability. Ruemmler’s posture was not defensive. It was disciplinary.

The Association Defense Is Convenient

Her central claim was that people should be judged for conduct, not associations. That sounds principled until you notice who gets to use it. In practice, the association defense is a privilege of the well-connected. Ordinary people are crushed by proximity all the time. The powerful, by contrast, are allowed to present their social and professional ties as incidental, even when those ties helped build careers, broker access, and preserve status.

Ruemmler’s own account undercuts her line. She says she was just giving legal advice “as many people do,” while also acknowledging Epstein helped her professional life. So the association was either meaningful enough to benefit from or meaningless enough to dismiss. She does not get both versions at once.

The Misdirection Is the Point

Her remark about lawmakers who had “publicly endorsed a candidate” accused of sexual assault is not a rebuttal. It is a dodge. It shifts the frame from Epstein’s network and her own ties to a partisan side swipe at the investigators. That tactic is familiar: when elites are asked to explain their proximity to predation, they try to dissolve the specific case into generalized hypocrisy somewhere else.

This is how accountability gets blurred. Not by denying facts outright, but by flooding the room with false equivalence. The issue is not whether a congressmember has ever made a bad endorsement. The issue is whether a high-status lawyer was willing to normalize closeness to Epstein, then scold the people examining that closeness as though the real scandal were their nerve.

What Epstein’s Circle Actually Shows

This story is not mainly about one lawyer’s discomfort. It is about the social architecture that made Epstein useful for so long: wealth, prestige, legal insulation, and people with serious credentials willing to treat him as a professional contact instead of a contaminant. The chain is familiar. The predatory man is enabled by institutions that prize access over judgment, and then those same institutions complain when anyone asks who knew what, and when.

The larger pattern is simple. The powerful do not merely avoid scrutiny. They recruit it into etiquette. They turn investigation into impropriety, association into abstraction, and complicity into a rhetorical debate about standards. That is not confusion. It is the system protecting itself.
