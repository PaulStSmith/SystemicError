'Kitchen-table economics' just upended a safe Senate seat

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosOctober 5, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet argues that economic pain from Republican policy has made Kansas’s traditionally safe Senate seat competitive. Democratic candidate Adam Hamilton received endorsements from two former Republican governors, including Mike Hayden, while a leading Republican Senate super PAC reportedly redirected money from North Carolina to defend incumbent Senator Roger Marshall.

The outlet attributes the shift to farm losses and rising costs linked to Trump administration decisions. It reports that retaliatory trade measures reduced demand for Kansas crops, tariffs raised machinery costs, relaxed beef-import restrictions hurt ranchers, and the war in Iran disrupted fertilizer and diesel supplies. AlterNet says roughly 700 Kansas farms closed over the past year, though it provides no sourcing for that estimate in the supplied text.

An AP-NORC poll cited by AlterNet put approval of Trump’s handling of the economy at 26 percent and his handling of living costs at 17 percent. The article presents those numbers, the Republican endorsements, and the super PAC’s spending decision as evidence that economic discontent has destabilized the race—not proof that Hamilton will win.

Power has an address

Farmers did not impose the tariffs, alter import restrictions, or wage the war described in the reporting. The Trump administration made those decisions. Republican senators possessed the institutional power to oppose them, constrain them, or withdraw political support. According to Hayden, Marshall instead repeatedly backed the tariffs and the war despite their effects in Kansas.

That distinction matters. “Kitchen-table economics” can sound like weather: prices rise, income falls, voters become restless. But the account describes government choices moving through supply chains and landing on particular people. Washington exercised power; Kansas farmers absorbed the consequences.

Marshall owns his choice

Marshall is not merely sharing a party label with an unpopular president. The reported indictment is more specific: he supported policies that Hayden says were harming the state Marshall represents.

That is agency, not association. Calling Trump an electoral “albatross” risks making Republican candidates sound like passive victims of someone else’s toxicity. Marshall’s vulnerability, as presented here, follows from his own alignment with decisions that raised costs and weakened markets for his constituents.

The super PAC’s intervention reinforces the point. National Republican operatives are moving resources because they perceive danger in Kansas. Money may protect an incumbent from accountability, but it cannot convert his record into an accident.

The euphemism hides the machinery

“Kitchen-table economics” describes where people feel the damage, not where it originates. It compresses tariffs, war, import policy, congressional obedience, lost markets, and farm closures into a pleasant domestic metaphor.

That framing can also obscure who pays first. A strong stock market may reward people who own substantial financial assets while doing nothing for a farmer facing more expensive equipment, fertilizer, and diesel alongside weaker crop demand. The contradiction is not voter confusion. It is a distribution of gains and losses produced by policy.

AlterNet is strongest when it connects those policies to identifiable actors. It is weaker when it leaps from polling and campaign spending to sweeping claims about what Americans collectively know or why they will vote. Economic approval data show deep dissatisfaction; they do not establish a single cause or forecast an election result.

Pain is not the same as power

The article suggests voters may punish Republicans once material harm becomes personal. Perhaps. But electoral pain does not automatically undo the policies that created it, compensate closed farms, or give producers control over trade and war decisions.

Nor should cross-party endorsements be romanticized. Former Republican governors can confer legitimacy on Hamilton, but they do not hold the seat. Marshall does. The administration directs executive policy. National groups command campaign money. Farmers possess the consequences and, eventually, a ballot.

That imbalance is the political core of the story: those with the least control over federal policy are forced to bear its most immediate costs, while those who enabled it mobilize money to preserve their offices.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is familiar. Leaders impose policies from above, loyal legislators protect them, and public discussion later treats the resulting hardship as an impersonal problem of “the economy.” Responsibility dissolves precisely when consequences become undeniable.

Kansas disrupts that evasion because the chain is unusually concrete: decisions in Washington, retaliation and disruption abroad, higher costs and lost sales at home, then political danger for an incumbent who supported the decisions. The seat has not been won, and the supplied reporting does not establish that it will be. But it does expose the structure clearly: institutional power made the choices, political obedience enabled them, and ordinary people received the bill.
