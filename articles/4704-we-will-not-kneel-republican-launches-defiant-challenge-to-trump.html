'We will not kneel': Republican launches defiant challenge to Trump

By Paulo Santos

Steve Schmidt, a Republican strategist who advised President George W. Bush and the 2008 presidential campaign of Arizona Sen. John McCain, accused America’s rich and powerful of “kneeling” to President Donald Trump — and urged defiance.

“We recognize that the world’s richest people of the most powerful corporations, the big media companies, the big tech companies, the big banks have made a decision for the privilege of resting their head on a Mar-a-Lago pillow,” Schmidt said in a Substack posted on Wednesday. “They are satisfied to live on their knees, but we are not.”

Criticizing the US hockey team that accepted a Presidential Medal of Freedom as having “desecrated its gold medal” for allowing “the equivalent of the Fuhrer Medal” to be hung around their necks, he contrasted their complacence with the attitude of anti-Trumpers like himself.

“We will defy this, we will oppose it, and we will not kneel to Donald Trump,” Schmidt said.

He went on to quote thinkers whose ideas influenced American politics, from Thomas Paine during the American Revolution to Dr. Martin Luther King, Jr. from the civil rights movement.

“Tyranny like hell is not easily conquered,” Schmidt said. “Yet we have this consolation with us that the harder the conflict, the more glorious the triumph. What we obtain too cheap, we esteem too lightly. It is dearness only that gives everything its value.”

He concluded, “Heaven knows how to put a proper price upon its goods. And it would be strange indeed if so celestial an article as freedom was not highly rated.”

Schmidt is an outspoken critic of Trump. Speaking to CNN’s Jim Acosta earlier this month, Schmidt slammed the Supreme Court for repeatedly ruling in Trump’s favor.

“The Roberts court has destabilized our American society through partisan rulings,” Schmidt argued. “We have a corrupt Supreme Court with [Judge Samuel] Alito’s misconduct, Clarence Thomas’s misconduct, flying around with all these billionaire extremist doners from here to there and everywhere.”

He continued, “The court has lost its reputation for a reason, and now we have Trump’s visage looming down at the American people from the Department of Justice, which is a corrupt institution that can’t be trusted, filled with corrupt prosecutor who abuse their oath, abuse the Constitution, and abuse the American people in the name of power.”

In November, Schmidt expanded his criticism to the Democratic Party, saying that “this moment requires a philosophical understanding of something deeper than Democrats have been able to articulate, which is this: the maximum abuse of power — which is what Trump is doing — demands, on a constant basis, the maximum use of power by every Democratic leader, wherever they may be.”

When Disney CEO Bob Iger suspended talk show host Jimmy Kimmel after Kimmel criticized Trump supporters, Schmidt attacked Iger’s personal character.

“We will put billboards up in L.A. with Bob Iger’s picture, a yellow stripe under it and the word ‘COWARD,’” Schmidt said. “It’s a billboard town and everyone can reflect on it as they drive by ... the cowardice is appalling.”

Our Analysis:

Steve Schmidt’s Bold Accusation: A Deep Dive

Steve Schmidt, a Republican strategist with a storied history in American politics, has unloaded a barrage of criticism aimed not just at President Donald Trump, but at a cross-section of America’s elite. Schmidt’s rhetoric, as fiery as it is, paints a picture of a nation at a moral and ethical crossroads. Let’s dissect his claims with the precision of a scalpel, shall we?

The Alleged Kneeling of America’s Elite

“Kneeling” to President Donald Trump, Schmidt asserts, is the chosen posture of “the world’s richest people, the most powerful corporations, big media companies, big tech companies, big banks.” This evocative imagery is intended to shock and provoke. But is it hyperbolic, or a stark depiction of reality? Schmidt’s analogy to the privilege of resting one’s head on a Mar-a-Lago pillow is clever. It suggests a transactional relationship between Trump and various sectors of American power — one that prioritizes comfort and access over principles. But while his critique might resonate with those already disillusioned, it risks oversimplification. The dynamics of power and influence are complex, often involving more than just a binary choice between defiance and subservience.

The Presidential Medal of Freedom Controversy

Schmidt’s comparison of the Presidential Medal of Freedom to “the equivalent of the Fuhrer Medal” is, to put it mildly, provocative. This analogy is a double-edged sword. On one hand, it underscores the gravity with which Schmidt views the perceived moral decay under Trump’s administration. On the other, such comparisons can be alienating and might detract from the credibility of his argument. The question arises: does this rhetorical flourish illuminate or merely inflame?

Schmidt’s Call to Arms

Invoking the spirits of Thomas Paine and Dr. Martin Luther King, Jr., Schmidt positions himself as a modern-day harbinger of resistance against tyranny. His language is lofty, his references grandiose. Yet, one cannot help but wonder if the grandeur of his prose serves to inspire or merely to decorate. “Tyranny like hell is not easily conquered,” he quotes. True, but is his framing of the situation as a clear-cut battle between good and evil too simplistic for the nuanced realities of political governance and societal division?

Critique of the Supreme Court

Schmidt’s attack on the Supreme Court, citing “partisan rulings” and individual justices’ misconduct, ventures into territory that demands serious scrutiny. The accusation of the court’s destabilization of American society is a hefty one. While there’s merit to the concern over the politicization of the judiciary, Schmidt’s portrayal verges on depicting the court as a monolithic entity bending to Trump’s will. This is a critical conversation to be had, yet it requires a balanced approach that acknowledges the complexity of legal philosophies and the autonomy of judicial thought.

The Democratic Party’s Philosophical Shortcomings

Schmidt’s critique of the Democratic Party is perhaps where his analysis shines brightest. His call for a “philosophical understanding” that transcends current Democratic strategies is a compelling one. His argument that “the maximum abuse of power...demands...the maximum use of power by every Democratic leader” is a clarion call for strategic reevaluation. In this, Schmidt touches on a genuine strategic conundrum facing the Democratic Party: how to effectively counter what he sees as an existential threat without compromising their own ethical standards.

The Attack on Bob Iger

Here, Schmidt’s strategy becomes somewhat muddied. While his passion is evident, the personal attack on Bob Iger for suspending Jimmy Kimmel appears petty in the context of his broader narrative. The use of billboards to label Iger a “COWARD” might satisfy an immediate desire for action, but does it contribute to the broader discourse in a meaningful way? Or does it risk descending into the very tactics Schmidt criticizes?

Conclusion

Steve Schmidt’s broadside against Trump, the Supreme Court, America’s elite, and even the Democratic Party is a fiery testament to his deeply held beliefs. His analysis, though, is a mixed bag. While he rightly calls attention to critical issues, his rhetoric sometimes veers into territory that could undermine his arguments. In the end, Schmidt’s commentary is a reminder of the power of language in political discourse — for better or for worse.
