The Age of Abundance: Why AI Exposes Capitalism’s Cracks

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 14, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

We’re living through a technological revolution that could free humanity from repetitive, meaningless labor — and at the same time, collapse the very economic order built on it. Artificial Intelligence isn’t just another tool; it’s a mirror held up to the contradictions of modern capitalism.

During a conversation with one of my professors, he said something familiar: “This revolution will create fewer jobs than it destroys.” He was right. But what often goes unsaid is that this could be a good thing. Because, let’s face it — how many jobs today truly create value? How many are what anthropologist David Graeber called bullshit jobs — positions that exist only to give the illusion of productivity and justify a paycheck?

In Bullshit Jobs: A Theory, Graeber described five types of these roles: corporate flunkies, damage controllers, paper pushers, symbolic managers, and creators of bureaucracy. It’s an uncomfortable but accurate portrait of our age: millions of people spend their days simulating productivity, maintaining the fiction of a functioning system. When AI reveals that much of this work can be automated, the problem isn’t the technology — it’s the system that never knew what to do with human potential once freed from drudgery.

As I said passionately during that exchange: why does Shell receive billions in subsidies when it earns profits in the trillions? This distortion lays bare that modern capitalism doesn’t reward work or risk — it rewards ownership and proximity to power. Those who proclaim they “don’t want hand-outs” live off institutionalized ones: tax breaks, incentives, bailouts, and public subsidies masquerading as “economic policy.”

This is just the visible face of something deeper — institutionalized corruption. Not bribery, but structure: when the system itself is designed to funnel public wealth into private hands and call it policy. Permanent subsidies, selective tax forgiveness, privatizations at fire-sale prices, and public contracts without genuine competition — all are legal forms of corruption, tolerated because they serve the elite who wrote the rules. And that’s why any direct redistribution proposal, such as a Universal Basic Income, is attacked as “populism,” while billion-dollar handouts to the powerful are celebrated as “growth.”

When AI tears down these façades, it’s not destroying something essential — it’s exposing the fiction we’ve kept alive. The real challenge isn’t preserving useless jobs but reimagining an economy where people can live well without begging for permission to survive.

That’s where Universal Basic Income (UBI) enters the picture. UBI isn’t a hand-out — it’s a social dividend, the fair return on centuries of collective knowledge and now, automation. AI wasn’t built in a vacuum: it stands on public data, taxpayer-funded research, and infrastructure maintained by society. If machines are replacing part of human labor, then the fruits of that replacement should rightfully return to the people.

But basic income alone isn’t enough. It must stand alongside universal healthcare and universal housing. A person with access to food, shelter, and health can finally work on self-betterment — study, create, care for others, or simply breathe without the terror of destitution. These aren’t luxuries; they’re the foundations of civilization. True freedom begins when survival is no longer a daily negotiation.

And let’s be honest: everyone already lives off collective support. Roads, hospitals, electricity, public education, legal systems, agricultural subsidies, corporate bailouts — all are redistributions of wealth. The issue isn’t whether redistribution exists; it’s who benefits from it. We already have a universal basic income — it just flows upward.

UBI doesn’t eliminate ambition or responsibility; it liberates them. Studies from Finland, Canada, and Kenya show it doesn’t make people lazy — it makes them human. It replaces desperation with dignity.

This isn’t a naïve utopia. It’s the logical next step in a world where technology can provide abundance but our economic religion demands scarcity. AI forces us to confront a question far older than capitalism itself: do we build systems that serve humanity, or do we keep sacrificing humanity to preserve systems?

The answer will define whether the age of AI becomes an era of emancipation — or the final chapter of a civilization too afraid to evolve.
