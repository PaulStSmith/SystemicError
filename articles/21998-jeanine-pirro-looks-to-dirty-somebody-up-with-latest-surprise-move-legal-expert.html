A Special Grand Jury Is a Political Weapon When Power Wants It To Be

Power, Not Procedure

The fact worth starting with is simple: Jeanine Pirro, now U.S. attorney in Washington, has reportedly moved to convene a special grand jury, and she put Steven Vandervelden in charge. That is the institutional fact pattern. Everything else is about motive. When the Justice Department starts choosing unusual process before it has a clean criminal case, the question is not whether the machinery is legal in the abstract. The question is who it is meant to pressure, humiliate, or discipline.

The Likely Target Is the Point

Lisa Rubin’s reading is the right one to take seriously: a special grand jury can be used to generate a report in narrow circumstances, including alleged misconduct by a public official, even where a normal indictment path may not be viable. That matters because it suggests the purpose may not be conviction. It may be reputational damage. In other words, the state does not need to prove a crime if it can still force a public cloud over someone it wants to mark.

That is not law enforcement in the ordinary sense. It is institutionalized insinuation.

Reputation as the Endgame

The source points to a darker logic: if prosecutors know they cannot meet the threshold to indict, they can still try to make the target carry the stain of investigation. That is a misuse of public power, but it is not random. It is a deliberate way to convert prosecutorial authority into political narrative. You do not need to win in court if your real aim is to tell the public that your enemy is probably guilty of something.

That tactic depends on asymmetry. It works when the office is stronger than the target and when procedural jargon is enough to launder a political attack into something that sounds sober and legal.

Why Vandervelden Matters

Rubin’s other point is just as revealing: Vandervelden is described as lightly experienced and not a former federal prosecutor with the kind of DOJ background one would expect in this role. That is not a minor personnel detail. It is part of the signal. If the operation were being run with a conventional prosecutorial purpose, experience would matter. If the operation is being run for control, optics, or loyalty, then selecting a less seasoned lieutenant starts to make sense.

That is how institutions decay: not always through open abuse, but through appointments that tell you the process has been subordinated to the objective.

The Justice Department as Instrument

The larger pattern is not mysterious. When political actors treat the Justice Department as a vehicle for signaling, pressure, and public contamination, the department stops functioning as a neutral legal institution and becomes an arm of factional power. Rubin is careful to frame this as a possibility, not a confirmed charge. But the possibility itself is the indictment of the system: the structure now invites exactly this kind of misuse.

If the purpose of a grand jury is to create a public record of wrongdoing without the discipline of a real criminal case, then the machinery is being bent toward state-sponsored character assassination. That is not a side effect. That is the point.

Systemic Error

The deeper problem is not one official’s style or one unusual grand jury. It is the normalization of legal process as political theater, where public power is used to imply guilt without proving it and to punish without owning the punishment. That is how institutional cowardice and authoritarian habit meet in the same room: through procedure that looks orderly while serving a dirty purpose.
