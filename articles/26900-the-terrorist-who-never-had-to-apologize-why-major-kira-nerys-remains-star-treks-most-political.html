SYSTEMIC ERROR / COMMENTARY

The Terrorist Who Never Had to Apologize: Why Major Kira Nerys Remains Star Trek’s Most Politically Courageous Character

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 29, 202637 min read

Deep Space Nine dared to make a former terrorist a hero without sanitizing her past. But its greatest achievement was something even more uncomfortable: showing that a just cause does not automatically justify every action taken in its name.

There is a peculiar tendency in modern political discourse to divide the world into two categories: heroes and villains.

Heroes are supposed to be good people who do good things for good reasons. Villains are bad people who do bad things for bad reasons. When a hero does something questionable, the audience expects an explanation, a redemption arc, or an apology. When a villain does something admirable, we expect to discover an ulterior motive.

It is a comforting arrangement. It allows us to evaluate people without confronting the messy, frequently contradictory nature of morality.

And then there was Major Kira Nerys.

In 1993, Star Trek: Deep Space Nine introduced a character who had spent most of her life fighting a brutal military occupation through sabotage, assassination, and guerrilla warfare.

By virtually any conventional definition, she had been a terrorist.

She did not deny it. She did not pretend that her resistance movement had always distinguished perfectly between combatants and civilians. She did not spend seven seasons begging forgiveness from the people who had occupied her planet.

And she was one of the heroes.

Not a villain undergoing rehabilitation. Not a misguided extremist whom an enlightened Starfleet captain would eventually teach the error of her ways.

A hero.

A woman capable of extraordinary compassion, tremendous courage, profound loyalty, and moral judgments that should make any reasonable person deeply uncomfortable.

Nearly three decades after Deep Space Nine ended, Kira remains one of the most remarkable characters in televised science fiction.

And the reason is not simply that she was a terrorist who became a hero.

It is that the series understood something modern political discourse frequently forgets:

The legitimacy of a cause and the legitimacy of the methods used to pursue it are two entirely separate questions.

The Federation Did Not Liberate Bajor

To understand Kira, we must first understand the society that produced her.

For approximately fifty years, Bajor existed under Cardassian occupation.

Its resources were exploited. Its population was subjected to forced labor, collective punishment, arbitrary detention, torture, and mass murder. Its cultural and religious institutions were suppressed. Entire communities suffered under an imperial administration that regarded the inhabitants as obstacles to Cardassian prosperity.

The Cardassians did not consider themselves villains, of course.

Empires rarely do.

They had strategic interests, economic requirements, security concerns, and an elaborate political vocabulary through which they could explain why occupying somebody else’s planet was entirely reasonable.

For the Bajorans, the experience was considerably less abstract.

And this is an important detail: the Federation did not liberate Bajor.

There was no dramatic arrival of the Enterprise, no spectacular Starfleet intervention in which superior technology and enlightened diplomacy brought the occupation to an end.

The Bajoran resistance fought for decades, imposing an increasingly intolerable cost on the occupation until Cardassia withdrew.

The Federation arrived afterward, when the Bajoran provisional government requested assistance.

Kira therefore had no reason to regard Federation civilization as the source of her freedom.

She had earned that freedom herself, alongside people who had sacrificed their lives fighting an enemy infinitely better equipped than they were.

When she first meets Benjamin Sisko, she is not a grateful native awaiting instruction from a more advanced society.

She is a veteran of an independence struggle, suspicious of outside powers and determined that Bajor will never surrender control of its destiny again.

That distinction made her unusual from the beginning.

Where Captain Picard might approach a political problem through diplomacy, ethical principles, and carefully constructed arguments, Kira approached it through the practical experience of someone who had watched diplomacy fail while people were being murdered.

She knew something that the Federation’s philosophical elegance could sometimes obscure.

There are situations in which asking nicely does not work.

There are situations in which negotiation is impossible because one party has no interest in negotiating.

And there are situations in which the continued existence of an oppressive system depends precisely on the expectation that its victims will remain peaceful.

Kira did not have to learn those lessons in a classroom.

She had lived them.

The Comfortable Myth of Peaceful Liberation

There is a particularly convenient fantasy frequently promoted by comfortable societies: that oppressed populations should achieve freedom without making anyone uncomfortable.

They should protest peacefully, petition respectfully, and demonstrate impeccable moral discipline while waiting for their oppressors to recognize the injustice of their behavior.

If violence erupts, attention immediately shifts from the original oppression to the behavior of those resisting it.

The question stops being “Why are these people being occupied?” and becomes “Why can’t these people resist more politely?”

This is an extraordinarily useful arrangement for an occupying power.

It allows the institution responsible for systematic violence to portray itself as the defender of civilization whenever its victims resort to violence in return.

That does not mean all violent resistance is justified.

It means the demand for nonviolence cannot become an unconditional obligation imposed exclusively on the oppressed.

An occupying army does not acquire moral legitimacy merely because it wears uniforms, follows orders, and conducts its brutality through an organized chain of command.

Nor does a resistance movement automatically lose legitimacy because its members lack uniforms, attack military infrastructure clandestinely, or refuse to fight a conventional war they have no possibility of winning.

This distinction is fundamental to Kira’s worldview.

She understood that Cardassian occupation was not some unfortunate misunderstanding between two equally legitimate political interests.

It was an exercise of domination.

And domination does not become morally neutral because the people administering it describe themselves as maintaining order.

But What About Gandhi?

At this point, somebody will inevitably raise the most famous counterexample in modern history.

“What about Mahatma Gandhi? Didn’t India achieve independence through peaceful resistance?”

Yes. Gandhi demonstrated the extraordinary power of nonviolent resistance.

But the popular version of that history frequently confuses nonviolence with passivity.

Gandhi did not simply write polite letters to British officials asking them to reconsider colonialism.

He organized mass boycotts, strikes, civil disobedience, and systematic noncooperation with the colonial government. He encouraged Indians to refuse participation in institutions through which British authority was exercised.

His famous Salt March was not a friendly demonstration expressing mild dissatisfaction with taxation.

It was a deliberate violation of imperial law, designed to undermine the legitimacy of British rule and mobilize a population against it.

Gandhi did not seek to persuade the British Empire merely through the moral elegance of his arguments.

He sought to make its continued occupation of India politically and practically untenable.

He was nonviolent.

He was not harmless.

And there is another detail that popular retellings tend to overlook.

Gandhi did not liberate India single-handedly.

By the time India achieved independence in 1947, Britain had endured two devastating world wars. Its economic and geopolitical position had changed substantially. Indian nationalism encompassed numerous movements, including those that rejected Gandhi’s commitment to nonviolence. The Indian National Army, military unrest, and the Royal Indian Navy mutiny of 1946 all formed part of the political environment in which British authorities confronted the future of colonial rule.

Historians debate precisely how much weight to assign each factor.

But treating Indian independence as the exclusive product of one man’s peaceful philosophy reduces a profoundly complicated historical struggle to a comforting bedtime story.

None of this diminishes Gandhi’s achievements.

Quite the opposite.

His genius was recognizing that an empire’s apparent military superiority did not mean it could govern indefinitely without the cooperation of the people it ruled.

He transformed noncooperation into an instrument of political power.

And that brings us to the real question.

Would Gandhi’s methods have liberated Bajor?

Perhaps.

But how would the Cardassian occupation authorities have responded to millions of Bajorans refusing to work, boycotting colonial institutions, and deliberately defying military orders?

Would they have negotiated?

Would they have arrested the movement’s leaders?

Would they have executed them?

Star Trek gives us ample reason to consider the latter possibilities, but not enough information to declare nonviolent resistance inherently impossible.

And that uncertainty is precisely the point.

Gandhi proved that armed struggle is not the only available form of resistance.

He did not prove that nonviolent resistance succeeds under every conceivable form of oppression.

Nor did he establish that a population subjected to violent occupation automatically forfeits its right to armed resistance because another population, under different historical circumstances, pursued an alternative strategy.

Nonviolence is a method of resistance. It is not a moral obligation that automatically renders every other method illegitimate.

Gandhi and Kira Nerys represent profoundly different approaches to confronting imperial power.

But they share a fundamental conviction.

The legitimacy of an empire’s rule does not derive from its ability to impose that rule by force.

And the people living under occupation are not obligated to accept their subjugation simply because their occupiers possess the stronger army.

The disagreement concerns how that resistance should be conducted.

And that is a disagreement worth having without pretending the answer is either simple or universal.

Ireland: When Yesterday’s Terrorists Become Tomorrow’s Statesmen

If Gandhi represents the power of nonviolent resistance, Ireland illustrates a different and considerably messier path toward national self-determination.

For centuries, Ireland lived under English and later British domination. Land ownership, political representation, religious identity, and economic power were shaped by a colonial relationship whose consequences survived long after the formal structures changed.

In 1801, Ireland became part of the United Kingdom.

That incorporation did not settle the question of Irish national identity, nor did it eliminate demands for independence.

By the early twentieth century, those demands had produced an increasingly confrontational political movement.

The Easter Rising of 1916 was suppressed by British forces, and its leaders were executed. Rather than extinguishing republican nationalism, the aftermath contributed to a transformation in public attitudes.

In 1919, the Irish War of Independence began.

The Irish Republican Army employed guerrilla tactics against British security forces and institutions. Its fighters conducted ambushes, attacked police barracks, disrupted communications, and targeted individuals associated with the British administration.

To British authorities, the IRA was an unlawful armed organization committing acts of terrorism.

To its supporters, its members were soldiers fighting for Irish independence.

Sound familiar?

The resemblance to Bajor is difficult to miss.

A population seeking self-determination confronts a vastly more powerful state. Conventional military victory is unrealistic. Resistance fighters resort to irregular warfare, attempting to make continued political control increasingly expensive and difficult.

And the occupying or governing power responds with military force, arrests, reprisals, and an insistence that legitimate political authority belongs exclusively to established institutions.

Neither the Irish nor the Bajoran struggle can be understood merely by examining individual acts of violence without considering the political conditions in which those acts occurred.

But there is a complication.

Not every IRA operation was morally defensible.

Civilians died. Sectarian violence occurred. People were targeted for suspected collaboration, and innocent individuals suffered because armed organizations decided they represented the enemy.

The political objective of independence did not automatically legitimize those actions.

And the history becomes even more complicated after the Anglo-Irish Treaty of 1921.

The treaty led to the creation of the Irish Free State in 1922, while Northern Ireland remained within the United Kingdom.

Irish republicans themselves divided over the settlement, and the disagreement escalated into civil war.

People who had fought together against British rule now fought one another over what independence should mean.

Defeating an external authority did not resolve the fundamental question of political legitimacy. It merely changed the circumstances in which that question had to be answered.

And this is exactly the sort of problem that Deep Space Nine explored in Bajor’s post-occupation politics.

Liberation was not the end of history.

It was the beginning of another struggle over who should govern, by what authority, and according to which principles.

The Troubles: A Different IRA, a Different Conflict

Decades later, Northern Ireland became the center of another violent struggle.

The Troubles, conventionally dated from the late 1960s to the 1998 Good Friday Agreement, involved Irish republican paramilitaries, loyalist paramilitaries, British security forces, and communities divided over constitutional identity.

Catholic and nationalist communities had experienced longstanding discrimination in housing, employment, and political representation.

Civil rights campaigns challenged those arrangements.

Violence escalated.

British troops were deployed in 1969, initially welcomed by some Catholic communities as protection from sectarian attacks. Relations subsequently deteriorated dramatically.

The Provisional IRA emerged during this period, pursuing the end of British rule in Northern Ireland and Irish reunification.

It conducted attacks against military and security targets.

It also carried out bombings that killed civilians.

Loyalist paramilitaries committed their own atrocities, frequently attacking Catholic civilians. British security forces were implicated in unlawful killings and other abuses, while allegations and evidence of collusion became part of the conflict’s enduring legacy.

The violence cannot be adequately understood as a simple confrontation between good people and bad people.

But that complexity must not become an excuse for moral evasion.

The deliberate killing of civilians was wrong regardless of which organization carried it out.

And the political grievances underlying the conflict did not make every violent response legitimate.

Here we return directly to Kira.

A resistance fighter may regard an occupying soldier as an enemy.

But does that make the soldier’s wife an enemy?

Does it make his children enemies?

Does it make a civilian working in an administrative office a legitimate target for assassination?

Kira’s answer in “The Darkness and the Light” is horrifyingly broad.

And Irish history gives us ample reason to recognize the danger of that reasoning.

The Agreement That Changed the Question

In 1998, the Good Friday Agreement established a framework for political power-sharing, institutional reform, and the peaceful determination of Northern Ireland’s constitutional future.

It did not reunify Ireland.

It did not eliminate every historical grievance.

It did not magically reconcile everyone who had spent decades regarding their neighbors as enemies.

But it established an essential principle: Northern Ireland’s constitutional status would depend on democratic consent.

The people living there would have a recognized political mechanism through which that future could be determined.

The agreement also recognized that people could legitimately identify as British, Irish, or both.

In other words, it created space for competing national identities without requiring one population to eliminate or permanently dominate the other.

And perhaps that offers a lesson more important than the familiar debate over whether the IRA were terrorists or freedom fighters.

A political struggle can have legitimate objectives while employing indefensible methods.

An armed campaign can become less defensible as realistic alternatives for political change emerge.

And a settlement does not require everyone to agree about the past before they can begin constructing a different future.

By the end of Deep Space Nine, Kira has undergone a comparable transformation.

She remains proud of having fought to liberate Bajor.

But she is no longer fighting to destroy the institutions of an occupying power.

She is helping build institutions capable of sustaining peace.

She has not renounced resistance.

She has recognized that the purpose of resistance is not to make war permanent.

A successful liberation movement must eventually answer a question that guerrilla warfare cannot resolve: what happens when the fighting stops?

And that question leads us to perhaps the most politically uncomfortable comparison of all.

Palestine: The Allegory Star Trek Could Explore at a Safe Distance

There is another reason the Bajoran occupation feels so recognizable.

The parallels with the Palestinian experience were not accidental.

Michael Piller, one of Deep Space Nine‘s creators, discussed multiple historical inspirations for the Bajorans, including the Palestinians, Kurds, Jews, and Native Americans.

The Bajorans were never intended as a perfect reproduction of one particular people.

Instead, their history combined recognizable elements of colonial domination, displacement, religious identity, armed resistance, and national liberation.

But the Palestinian comparison deserves particular attention.

Because it takes the moral questions embodied by Kira Nerys and places them in a conflict where people are far less willing to entertain ambiguity.

Occupation Is Not an Abstract Political Disagreement

In 1948, following the end of British administration in Palestine, the establishment of Israel, and the ensuing war, hundreds of thousands of Palestinians fled or were expelled from their homes.

Palestinians remember this displacement as the Nakba, or catastrophe.

For Jewish Israelis, the establishment of Israel is associated with national independence and the realization of Jewish self-determination, profoundly shaped by centuries of persecution and the Holocaust.

These histories are not interchangeable, but neither can simply be erased from an honest account of the conflict.

In 1967, Israel occupied the West Bank, including East Jerusalem, and the Gaza Strip.

The International Committee of the Red Cross regards these territories as occupied Palestinian territory and applies the law of occupation to them.

Palestinians have lived with the consequences of military control, restrictions on movement, settlement expansion in the West Bank, displacement, and recurring cycles of violence.

Israelis, meanwhile, have experienced attacks against civilians, hostage-taking, rocket attacks, and other serious threats to their security.

Those realities are not excuses for one another.

They are part of the political circumstances that any serious analysis must confront.

And here is where the fictional distance of Deep Space Nine becomes so revealing.

When Cardassian troops occupy Bajor, the audience immediately understands why Bajorans resent them.

When Bajoran resistance fighters sabotage Cardassian military installations, viewers generally understand why those fighters regard their actions as justified.

When Kira describes the occupation’s brutality, we do not demand that she first demonstrate sufficient sympathy for the political interests of Cardassia.

We recognize domination for what it is.

But when similar questions arise in real-world conflicts, audiences bring decades of national loyalties, historical grievances, religious identities, and political narratives into the discussion.

The analytical distance disappears.

And people who readily recognize the legitimacy of resistance in fiction may become considerably more selective about recognizing it in reality.

Resistance Is Not a Blank Check

This is where we must resist the temptation to turn a legitimate political grievance into unlimited moral permission.

Palestinians possess the right to self-determination.

That right does not grant an armed organization permission to deliberately massacre civilians, take hostages, or treat every Israeli as a legitimate military target.

The attacks led by Hamas on October 7, 2023, which deliberately targeted civilians and involved hostage-taking, cannot be justified by invoking the occupation.

But neither can those attacks erase the rights of millions of Palestinian civilians.

Israel’s responsibility to protect its population does not give its armed forces unlimited freedom in conducting military operations.

International humanitarian law requires the distinction between combatants and civilians, prohibits indiscriminate attacks, and imposes obligations concerning proportionality, precautions, and the protection of people under occupation.

Palestinian civilians do not lose those protections because Hamas operates among them.

Israeli civilians do not lose their protections because they live in a state whose government maintains an occupation.

And children do not inherit collective guilt from the political decisions or violent actions of adults.

This is the precise moral boundary that Kira crosses when she declares every Cardassian on Bajor a legitimate target.

Her anger is understandable.

Her experience of occupation is real.

Her cause is legitimate.

But none of those facts makes every civilian associated with the opposing population personally responsible for the occupation.

A Cardassian civilian is not automatically a combatant.

An Israeli civilian is not automatically a combatant.

A Palestinian civilian is not automatically a combatant.

That principle cannot change depending on whose suffering we happen to find more politically convenient.

The Danger of Seeing Entire Populations as the Enemy

One of the most destructive aspects of prolonged conflict is the tendency to transform political identities into moral verdicts.

A member of the opposing population ceases to be an individual.

Instead, that person becomes a representative of everything their government, military, political movement, or ancestors have ever done.

Responsibility becomes hereditary.

Guilt becomes collective.

Innocence becomes conditional.

This is how a child can be described as a future enemy.

It is how civilian deaths become acceptable collateral damage without meaningful scrutiny.

And it is how atrocities can be rationalized as necessary steps toward peace.

The logic is seductively simple.

If the other population is the problem, then removing that population appears to solve the problem.

But the people who suffer under that reasoning are overwhelmingly individuals who did not create the political circumstances in which they were born.

And extermination is not peace.

It is the destruction of the people who would otherwise have experienced either war or peace.

A political settlement cannot be measured solely by whether the shooting has stopped.

It must also confront whether the people affected can live with security, dignity, political rights, and some meaningful control over their future.

Otherwise, what is being described as peace may be nothing more than domination temporarily meeting less resistance.

The Limits of the Bajoran Allegory

We should acknowledge where the analogy breaks down.

Bajor was a sovereign civilization conquered by an external imperial power. Its occupying forces ultimately withdrew, leaving Bajorans to reconstruct their political institutions.

The Israeli-Palestinian conflict involves overlapping national histories, two peoples with claims to self-determination, contested territory, questions of statehood, refugees, settlements, and profoundly different visions of a political settlement.

Israeli Jews are not simply Cardassians.

Palestinians are not simply Bajorans.

Hamas is not the Shakaar resistance.

And a science-fiction narrative cannot substitute for detailed historical analysis.

But allegories do not need to reproduce every historical detail to reveal something important.

Their purpose is to isolate questions that become difficult to examine when surrounded by familiar political loyalties.

Who has the right to resist?

What makes an occupation legitimate or illegitimate?

When does a civilian become a military target?

Can an individual bear responsibility for a political system without being personally guilty of its crimes?

And perhaps most importantly, can we recognize the suffering of one population without treating the suffering of another as acceptable?

Deep Space Nine never offers a complete theory of international relations.

It does something more modest and, in many ways, more useful.

It makes the audience confront a sympathetic protagonist who has arrived at an indefensible conclusion.

Kira believes that every Cardassian on Bajor was collectively responsible for the occupation.

And yet, elsewhere in the series, she demonstrates that individual Cardassians can be innocent, honorable, courageous, and deserving of protection.

The contradiction is not an accident.

It is the human tendency to understand moral principles clearly in the abstract and struggle to apply them when confronted with the people we regard as enemies.

The Question the Allegory Leaves Us With

The Bajoran story ends with Cardassian withdrawal.

The Palestinian story has no equivalent conclusion.

That difference matters.

It means the real-world questions of sovereignty, security, accountability, and political rights cannot be resolved simply by borrowing the ending of a television series.

But the underlying moral challenge remains.

If we believe an occupied population has the right to self-determination, that principle must not depend on whether we approve of its religion, culture, or political leadership.

If we believe civilians must be protected from deliberate attacks, that principle must not depend on whether their government is behaving justly.

And if we believe collective punishment is wrong, we cannot make exceptions whenever the people being punished belong to a population we have learned to distrust.

Human rights that disappear when applied to our enemies are not universal human rights.

They are privileges reserved for people we like.

And that is precisely the moral failure Kira Nerys forces us to confront.

She was justified in resisting the occupation of Bajor.

She was not justified in declaring every Cardassian civilian a legitimate target.

Those statements can both be true.

They must both be true.

Because if we abandon the distinction between resisting oppression and deliberately harming innocents, we have surrendered the very moral framework that allows us to condemn oppression in the first place.

The Scene That Changes Everything

In the fifth-season episode “The Darkness and the Light,” Kira confronts Silaran Prin, a Cardassian civilian who was horribly disfigured in an attack carried out by her resistance cell.

Years earlier, the Shakaar resistance had targeted the residence of Gul Pirak, a Cardassian officer who had ordered the deaths of fifteen Bajoran farmers for refusing to display a Cardassian banner.

The bombing destroyed part of the residence, killing twelve Cardassians, including members of Pirak’s family, and seriously injuring twenty-three others.

Prin, who worked cleaning uniforms, survived.

Years later, he systematically murders members of Kira’s former resistance cell in revenge.

When he finally confronts her, he demands to know whether she feels ashamed of what she did.

Kira refuses to apologize.

Her response culminates in the extraordinary declaration:

“You were all guilty and you were all legitimate targets!”

Consider the implications.

Here is a protagonist, a senior officer aboard a Federation-administered space station, explicitly defending the proposition that civilians associated with an occupying population were legitimate targets for lethal violence.

Not just soldiers.

Not just political officials.

Civilians.

Including someone whose principal contribution to the occupation was ironing shirts.

From the perspective of contemporary international humanitarian law, that sweeping justification is indefensible.

And that’s precisely what makes the scene so remarkable.

The writers do not interrupt to have Sisko explain that Kira is wrong.

They do not manufacture a convenient revelation that Prin was secretly committing atrocities.

They do not retroactively transform every victim of the bombing into an enemy combatant.

Prin really was a civilian harmed by the Resistance.

His suffering was real.

And Kira’s justification was morally dangerous.

Yet the episode does not suddenly transform Kira into its villain.

Instead, it forces the audience to confront two truths simultaneously.

The Bajorans were justified in fighting Cardassian occupation.

And the Bajoran resistance could still commit acts that were morally wrong.

Neither truth cancels the other.

Collective Responsibility Is Not Collective Guilt

This is where the moral argument becomes particularly important.

Cardassian occupation was not maintained by soldiers alone.

It required administrators, engineers, bureaucrats, transport workers, industrialists, intelligence officers, and countless civilians whose professional activities helped sustain the colonial enterprise.

Occupation is a system, not merely a collection of armed men.

Kira understands that.

And she is correct.

But understanding the systemic nature of oppression does not mean every participant in that system becomes an equally legitimate target for violence.

There is a crucial difference between collective responsibility and collective guilt.

A civilian administrator who knowingly organizes the confiscation of Bajoran agricultural production may bear substantial moral responsibility for the occupation.

A military intelligence officer identifying resistance members for execution bears even more.

A child born into a Cardassian family living on Bajor has no comparable responsibility.

Neither does a civilian automatically become a legitimate target simply by paying taxes, manufacturing ordinary goods, or existing within the occupying society.

Modern international humanitarian law makes a similar distinction. Civilians generally retain protection against direct attack unless, and for such time as, they directly participate in hostilities. Merely contributing indirectly to a society’s war effort does not automatically remove that protection.

And this presents a devastating problem for Kira’s argument.

If every Cardassian civilian was a legitimate target merely because Cardassia occupied Bajor, then what principle would prevent an opposing force from declaring every Bajoran civilian a legitimate target because they supported the Resistance?

The moral logic would become symmetrical.

Everyone is guilty because everyone belongs to the society on the opposing side.

And once that principle is accepted, almost any atrocity can be justified.

History is overflowing with examples of exactly that reasoning.

The fact that one side has a just cause does not eliminate the moral distinction between those fighting and those who are not.

This is not moral equivalence.

The Cardassian occupation and the Bajoran struggle for liberation were not interchangeable political projects.

One was an exercise of imperial domination. The other was resistance to that domination.

But the difference between their causes cannot provide unlimited permission to kill.

A just cause does not make every method just.

That is a moral boundary worth defending precisely because history provides so many opportunities to abandon it.

Kira Was More Complicated Than Her Defenders Sometimes Remember

There is an additional wrinkle in the popular description of Kira as someone who never reconsidered her past.

She did.

In fact, Deep Space Nine began exploring her capacity to distinguish individual Cardassians from the occupation long before “The Darkness and the Light.”

In the first-season episode “Duet,” Kira encounters Aamin Marritza, a Cardassian who served as a filing clerk at Gallitep, a notorious labor camp where Bajorans were tortured and murdered.

At first, she is determined to see him punished.

To Kira, service at Gallitep is sufficient evidence of guilt.

But as the story unfolds, she discovers something remarkable.

Marritza is not the sadistic camp commander he has pretended to be.

He is a deeply traumatized former clerk who feels overwhelming shame about the atrocities he witnessed and his inability to stop them.

He has deliberately assumed the identity of the notorious Gul Darhe’el, hoping that his prosecution will force Cardassia to confront its crimes.

Kira eventually recognizes his humanity.

When a Bajoran extremist murders Marritza simply because he is Cardassian, Kira is horrified.

The episode ends with her rejecting precisely the kind of ethnic collective guilt she later appears to defend in her confrontation with Prin.

This is not a minor detail.

It demonstrates that Kira’s moral development was neither linear nor complete.

She could recognize the innocence of one Cardassian while still defending an earlier attack that harmed other Cardassian civilians.

And, contrary to the popular claim that she never expressed regret, Kira explicitly acknowledges in “Duet” that she regrets much of what she had to do during the occupation.

She does not renounce the Resistance.

But she does recognize that some of her actions carry a moral burden.

That distinction matters.

Because regret is not the same as surrender.

A person can believe that fighting was necessary while regretting the suffering inflicted in the process.

A resistance fighter can recognize that civilians were harmed without accepting the occupying power’s claim that all resistance was criminal.

And a liberated population can acknowledge wrongdoing without retroactively legitimizing its own subjugation.

Indeed, that may be the most mature position available.

The Word “Terrorist” Is Doing Too Much Work

Part of the problem arises from the word itself.

“Terrorist” has become one of the most politically powerful labels in modern language.

Once applied, it can function as a substitute for analysis.

A government identifies an organization as terrorist, and the conversation frequently stops.

Its political objectives become irrelevant. Its historical grievances become excuses. Its members become monsters whose actions require no further context.

But violence is not morally evaluated solely by the identity of the person committing it.

The act matters.

The target matters.

The intention matters.

The circumstances matter.

Deliberately attacking civilians to intimidate a population is not morally transformed into legitimate warfare because the perpetrators wear military uniforms.

Conversely, sabotaging a military installation does not automatically become terrorism because the perpetrators belong to a non-state resistance movement.

A state may commit acts of terror.

A resistance movement may conduct legitimate military operations.

And the same resistance movement may commit atrocities.

These possibilities are not mutually exclusive.

This does not mean that every movement claiming liberation deserves sympathy.

Almost any violent organization can manufacture a narrative in which its actions are necessary, its enemies are evil, and its cause is uniquely righteous.

Which is why the justification must be examined rather than accepted at face value.

But the same scrutiny must apply to states.

A flag, an official uniform, and a government press secretary do not magically convert every violent act into a morally legitimate exercise of authority.

This was one of the most intellectually ambitious ideas underlying Deep Space Nine.

The series refused to assume that institutional authority and moral authority were the same thing.

And nowhere was that refusal more apparent than in Kira.

September 11 Changed the Conversation

When Deep Space Nine premiered in 1993, American audiences inhabited a different political environment.

Terrorism was certainly not a new phenomenon. Neither were politically motivated attacks on civilians, domestic extremist violence, or state-sponsored repression.

But September 11, 2001, transformed the political meaning of terrorism in American public life.

The word became inseparable from national trauma, security policy, military intervention, surveillance, and an increasingly expansive political vocabulary of existential threat.

The distinction between understanding the motives of violent extremists and excusing their actions became harder to maintain in public debate.

To explain why someone might resort to terrorism could itself be interpreted as expressing sympathy.

And sympathy could be interpreted as endorsement.

Nana Visitor, the actress who portrayed Kira, later recognized how unusual the character had been.

In an interview with Women at Warp, she discussed the difference between creating Kira before September 11 and imagining a comparable character afterward.

Visitor emphasized that the series had allowed audiences to accept a protagonist who had committed acts of terrorism without immediately requiring them to condemn her.

She also recalled criticism of Kira’s abrasive, uncompromising personality, including from Star Trek fans who found her insufficiently agreeable.

That last point deserves attention.

Kira was not simply politically confrontational.

She was a woman who refused to make herself more palatable for the comfort of others.

She was frequently angry.

She was argumentative.

She challenged authority.

She did not regard diplomacy as an obligation to remain silent when confronted with injustice.

And she had very little patience for people who expected the victims of occupation to express gratitude for being permitted to speak.

Those qualities would have been unusual in a male protagonist.

In a female protagonist of 1990s television, they were particularly striking.

Yet I would be cautious about claiming that a character like Kira could never be written today.

Contemporary television remains capable of depicting sympathetic revolutionaries, morally compromised protagonists, and violent antiheroes.

The more interesting question is whether a major franchise would allow someone like Kira to remain fundamentally convinced of the legitimacy of armed resistance without constructing the entire narrative around her need for redemption.

That is a much more demanding proposition.

The Federation’s Own Moral Compromises

Kira is not an isolated example of moral ambiguity in Deep Space Nine.

She exists in a series that repeatedly interrogated the distance between the Federation’s professed ideals and the actions its representatives were prepared to take.

Consider Captain Benjamin Sisko in “In the Pale Moonlight.”

Facing the possibility of losing the Dominion War, Sisko participates in a conspiracy involving deception, forged evidence, and ultimately murder.

His actions help bring the Romulan Empire into the war.

And the episode ends without giving viewers a comforting resolution in which his behavior is declared entirely justified.

Sisko’s own conscience becomes the battlefield.

Or consider Section 31.

Here we encounter an organization that claims to protect the Federation through methods the Federation publicly condemns.

Its defenders can always invoke the same argument.

The circumstances are exceptional.

The threat is existential.

Ordinary morality is a luxury.

The ends justify the means.

What makes this particularly interesting is that Kira and Section 31 represent almost opposite political experiences.

Kira fought from a position of weakness against an overwhelming occupying power.

Section 31 operates from within one of the galaxy’s most powerful political institutions.

Both can invoke necessity.

But necessity alone cannot establish the morality of their actions.

A society claiming to defend civilization does not become exempt from civilization’s principles when those principles become inconvenient.

And a resistance movement claiming to fight for freedom does not become exempt from moral scrutiny merely because its enemy is oppressive.

The uncomfortable lesson is that the temptation to justify atrocities through necessity exists on every side of a conflict.

DS9 was willing to explore that temptation without pretending that every actor was equally responsible for the circumstances that produced it.

That is a far more sophisticated position than simplistic moral equivalence.

The Empire Becomes the Occupied

Then, in its final season, Deep Space Nine produces one of the most brilliant political reversals in the history of the franchise.

Cardassia falls under the increasingly brutal domination of the Dominion.

The Cardassian government, having allied itself with a vastly more powerful imperial force, discovers that it is no longer an equal partner.

Its sovereignty becomes conditional.

Its population becomes expendable.

Its leaders learn that the Dominion regards Cardassian interests as secondary to its own strategic objectives.

Sound familiar?

Eventually, Damar, a senior Cardassian military figure, leads a rebellion.

And the Federation needs someone to teach his movement how to fight an occupying power.

Someone experienced in sabotage, clandestine organization, intelligence gathering, and guerrilla warfare.

Someone who understands how to resist a technologically superior enemy.

Someone who has spent years doing exactly that.

Major Kira Nerys.

The former Bajoran terrorist is sent to train Cardassian resistance fighters.

To make her presence more acceptable to the Cardassians, she receives a temporary Starfleet commission and the rank of commander.

Think about the extraordinary political implications.

The empire that spent decades describing Bajoran resistance as terrorism now requires the expertise of a Bajoran resistance fighter to regain its own freedom.

Yesterday’s terrorist becomes today’s military adviser.

Yesterday’s imperial officers become today’s insurgents.

And the techniques once condemned as criminal become essential instruments of liberation.

Nothing about the techniques has fundamentally changed.

What has changed is who needs them.

If that does not expose the political convenience with which the word “terrorism” is sometimes applied, very little will.

Damar Learns What Occupation Actually Means

But the greatest achievement of this storyline is not the irony of Kira training Cardassians.

It is what happens to Damar.

Initially, Damar remains deeply invested in the mythology of Cardassian greatness.

His rebellion is motivated primarily by opposition to Dominion domination, not by some sudden conversion to liberal democracy or universal human rights.

He wants Cardassia to regain its independence.

He has not necessarily concluded that Cardassia was wrong to deprive Bajor of its own.

That distinction is important.

People frequently recognize oppression when they experience it personally while remaining remarkably comfortable with the oppression they previously inflicted on others.

Damar is no exception.

Then the Dominion murders his family in retaliation for his rebellion.

He is devastated.

His wife and son were not combatants. They had no involvement in his military operations. They were innocent civilians murdered because of their association with a resistance leader.

Damar is appalled by the cruelty.

He cannot understand what kind of government would order such a thing.

And Kira delivers one of the most devastating responses in the entire series:

“Yeah, Damar, what kind of people give those orders?”

That’s it.

No lengthy lecture.

No self-righteous sermon.

Just a question.

A question that forces Damar to confront the fact that the atrocities he now finds incomprehensible were once routine instruments of Cardassian rule over Bajor.

What he experiences as an unforgivable crime was something his own society had normalized.

His family matters to him.

Bajoran families once mattered just as much to the people who lost them.

For the first time, Damar is being asked to understand the Cardassian occupation from the perspective of its victims.

And notice something especially important.

Kira realizes almost immediately that she has spoken at a terrible moment.

Her remark is morally justified, but its timing is cruel.

Garak, however, recognizes that Damar needs to confront the past if he is to help create a different Cardassia.

This is the difference between humiliation and moral reckoning.

Kira is not demanding that Damar hate his people.

She is challenging him to recognize that love for one’s people does not require defending everything one’s government has done.

Indeed, sometimes genuine patriotism requires precisely the opposite.

The Moment Damar Chooses a Different Cardassia

The transformation continues.

As the resistance develops, old imperial loyalties become increasingly incompatible with the practical requirements of liberation.

Some Cardassians remain unwilling to accept that opposing the Dominion may require fighting other Cardassians who collaborate with it.

For them, national identity is still more important than the political reality of occupation.

Kira understands the problem immediately.

Resistance cannot function if the enemy can neutralize it simply by placing members of the occupied population between its military forces and the insurgents.

But that creates another profoundly uncomfortable question.

When does a collaborator become a legitimate military target?

Not every civilian who accommodates an occupying power is a combatant.

Not every person trying to survive under occupation is a traitor.

And not every Cardassian loyal to the old order bears equal responsibility for Dominion atrocities.

Yet armed collaboration and active participation in military operations can make an individual a legitimate target.

These distinctions are difficult.

They are also essential.

Ultimately, Damar faces a choice between the old imperial vision of Cardassia and the possibility of a different future.

He chooses the latter.

Not because Kira has persuaded him to stop loving Cardassia.

But because he begins to understand that the Cardassia he once served cannot simply be restored without restoring the very attitudes that helped produce its catastrophe.

The empire has to die for the nation to have a future.

And the teacher who helps him understand this is a woman his empire once considered an enemy of civilization.

That is extraordinary writing.

Why “Both Sides” Is Not an Answer

There is a fashionable response to morally complicated conflicts that sounds sophisticated but frequently amounts to intellectual laziness.

“Both sides did terrible things.”

Often, this is factually true.

It is also frequently inadequate.

Two parties can commit comparable acts of violence while occupying radically different political and moral positions.

An empire invading another society and a population resisting that invasion are not necessarily morally equivalent simply because both resort to violence.

The origins of the conflict matter.

The objectives matter.

The distribution of power matters.

The available alternatives matter.

And the methods matter.

A serious moral analysis requires all of those considerations.

It cannot simply count atrocities, declare the totals comparable, and congratulate itself on being impartial.

But neither can it reverse the logic and declare every action by the oppressed morally acceptable.

That merely replaces one form of selective morality with another.

The difficult position is to insist on both principles.

The Bajorans had a legitimate cause for resistance.

The Cardassian occupation was an oppressive political project.

And Bajoran resistance fighters remained morally accountable for their conduct.

Those statements are compatible.

Indeed, insisting on their compatibility is what prevents moral analysis from collapsing into tribal loyalty.

If our principles apply only when our preferred side benefits from them, they are not principles.

They are preferences.

The Difference Between Regret and Repudiation

This brings us back to the central claim that makes Kira such an extraordinary character.

She never repudiates the Bajoran struggle for independence.

She never concludes that armed resistance was inherently wrong.

She never accepts the Cardassian interpretation of her actions as nothing more than criminal terrorism.

And she never agrees that political maturity requires adopting the worldview of the people who once ruled her planet.

But that does not mean she remains morally unchanged.

She learns to recognize individual Cardassians as human beings rather than interchangeable representatives of an enemy population.

She develops personal relationships with people whose backgrounds would once have made them objects of suspicion.

She recognizes that revenge can destroy innocent lives.

She learns that even someone who served an oppressive institution may possess courage, conscience, and the capacity to change.

And through her relationship with Damar, she demonstrates that reconciliation does not require forgetting history.

This is the crucial distinction.

Kira can regret an action without regretting the struggle.

She can acknowledge an innocent victim without declaring the occupation legitimate.

She can forgive an individual Cardassian without forgiving Cardassian imperialism.

And she can help liberate Cardassia without pretending that the occupation of Bajor never happened.

That is not hypocrisy.

That is moral complexity.

It is also a far more realistic picture of historical reconciliation than the comforting fantasy in which everyone apologizes, embraces, and agrees to stop discussing the past.

Sometimes reconciliation requires an honest acknowledgment that one party inflicted a terrible injustice on another.

Sometimes justice requires recognizing that the victims of that injustice also committed wrongs.

And sometimes a society cannot move forward until it stops treating those two facts as mutually exclusive.

Could Star Trek Still Create Kira Today?

I suspect it could.

But I am less certain that contemporary audiences would allow her to remain as uncomfortable as she was.

The problem is not necessarily that modern television lacks morally ambiguous characters.

We have plenty.

The problem is that audiences increasingly approach fictional characters as if they were candidates for public office, requiring ideological consistency, moral purity, and a position on every contemporary controversy.

A character’s objectionable statement can become evidence that the entire production endorses it.

An exploration of violent resistance can be mistaken for an endorsement of terrorism.

An attempt to explain the historical origins of extremism can be condemned as justification.

And a refusal to provide a neat moral resolution can be interpreted as a failure to take a position.

But fiction is not obligated to provide moral instruction manuals.

Sometimes its purpose is to place us in situations where our principles collide and force us to decide which ones actually matter.

This was one of Deep Space Nine‘s greatest strengths.

It trusted viewers to recognize the difference between depicting a character’s beliefs and endorsing those beliefs.

It trusted them to sympathize with someone while disagreeing with her.

And it trusted them to understand that moral growth does not necessarily produce ideological conformity.

Kira did not become a better person because she abandoned everything she believed.

She became a better person because her understanding of those beliefs grew more sophisticated.

She learned to recognize the humanity of individuals she had once regarded as enemies.

But she never forgot why those individuals had been enemies in the first place.

And that is a distinction worth preserving.

The Most Radical Thing Deep Space Nine Ever Did

The genius of Kira Nerys is not that Deep Space Nine portrayed a terrorist as a hero.

Literature has been doing that for centuries.

One nation’s terrorist has frequently been another nation’s revolutionary, freedom fighter, or founding father.

The interesting question has never been whether that political labeling exists.

The interesting question is whether the people carrying those labels remain accountable to principles beyond their own cause.

And Kira’s story refuses to provide an easy answer.

She was right to fight the Cardassian occupation.

She was wrong to suggest that every Cardassian civilian was automatically a legitimate target.

She could be courageous without being innocent.

She could be compassionate without being forgiving.

She could be morally admirable without being morally infallible.

And she could evolve without repudiating the cause that defined her life.

That is an extraordinarily difficult character to write.

It is even more difficult to sustain across seven seasons.

Yet Deep Space Nine managed it.

The series also delivered one final, devastating political irony.

When Cardassia became the occupied society, it discovered that resistance was not quite as objectionable as its leaders had always claimed.

Suddenly sabotage was necessary.

Guerrilla warfare was patriotic.

Armed insurgency was heroic.

And the Bajoran woman whom Cardassia had once regarded as a terrorist became the person qualified to teach its people how to fight for freedom.

The methods had not magically changed their moral character because Cardassians were now employing them.

The cause had changed.

The power relationship had changed.

And the former occupiers were finally learning what occupation meant.

The lesson was never that terrorism becomes acceptable when our side practices it.

It was that resistance to oppression and the deliberate targeting of innocents must be evaluated as separate moral questions, regardless of who is doing the resisting.

And perhaps that is the most enduring achievement of Major Kira Nerys.

She reminds us that we do not have to choose between recognizing the justice of a struggle and acknowledging the wrongdoing committed in its name.

We can do both.

We must do both.

Because the moment we exempt our heroes from moral scrutiny, we surrender the very principles that made them heroic.

And the moment we demand that victims apologize for resisting oppression before we acknowledge their humanity, we have already accepted the oppressor’s version of history.

Deep Space Nine understood that distinction.

Thirty years later, the rest of us are still struggling with it.
