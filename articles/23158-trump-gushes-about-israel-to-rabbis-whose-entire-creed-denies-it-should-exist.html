Trump gushes about Israel to rabbis whose entire creed denies it should exist

Trump Mistook a Feud for a Fellowship

The Oval Office Owned the Frame

Donald Trump invited six Orthodox rabbis to the White House, praised his support for Israel, and ended up putting two estranged Satmar brothers in the same room for the first time in years. The reporting treats the oddity like a feel-good Oval Office anecdote. It is better understood as a display of executive theater built on selective attention and shallow political use of religious symbolism.

Power Sat in the Invitation

The people with actual institutional power were Trump and the White House team. They controlled the setting, the guest list, the cameras, and the message. The rabbis were not staging a summit; they were brought into someone else’s political stagecraft. The fact that the administration apparently did not even realize it had invited opposing factions inside the same Hasidic community says a lot about how casually power treats the people it tries to display.

Misdirection Is the Point

Trump’s declaration of support for Israel sharpened the irony, because the Satmar movement is explicitly anti-Zionist on theological grounds. That contradiction is not a side note. It is the core political trick: use Jewish religious leaders as props for an Israel message, even when their actual religious politics cut against the administration’s script. The story turns on image management, not understanding. The White House wanted validation; it got complication.

The Brothers Were Not the Story

Aaron and Zalman Teitelbaum’s long succession fight, the harassment claims, the forgery accusations, and the social rupture inside their community are all real. But they are not evidence of presidential statesmanship. They are evidence of how deep the conflict already was before Trump stumbled into it. Their brief reconciliation happened because the room was forced open, not because the administration had any coherent strategy for peacemaking. If anything, the accidental thaw exposes how little seriousness the White House brought to the encounter.

Performative Access

The guest list also tells the political story. JD Vance, Marco Rubio, Jared Kushner, and Mike Lawler were part of the meeting. That is not religious outreach in any neutral sense. It is coalition signaling, prestige laundering, and a careful photograph of proximity to power. The first presidential hosting of Orthodox rabbis at the White House since Carter becomes less a historical milestone than a reminder that modern politics often confuses access with substance.

What This Really Reveals

This is a small story with a familiar architecture: authority hosts, symbols are arranged, contradictions are ignored, and the resulting image is sold as significance. That is how political power works when it values optics over comprehension. The deeper pattern is not interfaith harmony or diplomatic finesse. It is a presidency that treats communities as backdrops, then mistakes the resulting confusion for leadership.
