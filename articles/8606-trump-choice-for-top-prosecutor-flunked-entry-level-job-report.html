Trump choice for top prosecutor 'flunked' entry-level job: report

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 18, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Troubling Ascension of Jason Reding Quiñones: A Critical Examination

Jason Reding Quiñones/Department of Justice

President Donald Trump’s choice to lead federal prosecutions in the Southern District of Florida actually flunked out of an entry-level job in that same office several years ago.

Jason Reding Quiñones was hired as a prosecutor in 2018 and placed on drug and gun cases to gain trial experience, and while most of the office’s new hires eventually get promoted to more prestigious divisions, Reding Quiñones was flushed out, sources told the Washington Post.

“Most of those prosecutors win promotion,” the Post wrote. “Reding Quiñones flunked, failing to impress his supervisors with his work ethic and legal acumen and earning poor marks on his performance evaluation, according to multiple people familiar with the matter.

“He left the office in 2024,” the Post reported. “A year later, President Donald Trump tapped Reding Quiñones, a lieutenant colonel in the Air Force Reserve, to return to the Miami-based U.S. attorney’s office as its boss.”

Reding Quiñones’s sudden rise was boosted by conservative lawyer and online activist Mike Davis, who recommended him to the Trump administration to lead investigations into the president’s political enemies after the two appeared together on a panel titled “Modern Lawfare and the American Democracy” at a conservative legal conference.

“What’s good about Jason is he is not worried about his next job,” Davis told the Post. “He’s going to be there and be a good patriot. He doesn’t care what people think about him. He’s going to follow the law and the constitution.”

The “Lawfare” panelists had talked about special counsel Jack Smith’s investigations of Trump during Joe Biden’s presidency, and Reding Quiñones asked what steps should be taken to now that Trump was back in the White House, and Davis proposed a response that’s remarkably similar to what his recommended hire is taking as U.S. attorney.

“There must be severe consequences,” Davis told the panel. “We should not take the high ground. … There has to be severe legal, political and financial consequences for this unprecedented republic-ending lawfare.”

Davis proposed charging Trump foes with conspiracy to deprive a person of his civil rights, and Reding Quiñones has become the go-to prosecutor against former Obama administration Director of National Intelligence James R. Clapper Jr. and CIA Director John Brennan, as well as former FBI officials Andrew McCabe, Lisa Page and Peter Strzok, and the Trump administration is recruiting outside prosecutors to replace those who have fled the office.

“I have never seen anything like this before,” said one recently departed assistant U.S. attorney with decades of experience in the office. “People are now asking, ‘If I touch this or that case, will I be fired?’”

The investigation appears to focus on a longtime Trump grievance about a 2017 intelligence assessment that found Russians had taken steps to promote his first campaign for president, and Davis has proposed a work-around for the statute of limitations that would otherwise place the alleged offenses against Trump out of reach for prosecution.

“Davis has floated the idea, however, that prosecutors could try to prove that the assessment was just one part of a long-running conspiracy by government officials to deprive Trump of his rights,” the Post reported. “He asserts the conspiracy included the 2022 raid of the president’s Mar-a-Lago resort, which was authorized by a court to retrieve sensitive government materials that Trump took when he left the White House, which would be within the time limit.”

The chosen venue for potentially charging those crimes also seems to have been chosen with the Mar-a-Lago classified documents case in mind.

“Davis has also posted court documents on social media showing that a grand jury has been impaneled at Redding Quiñones’s request at the Fort Pierce courthouse — which is part of the Southern District of Florida — and suggested it would be hearing evidence in the conspiracy case,” the Post reported.

“By impaneling the grand jury in Fort Pierce, the proceedings, set to begin in January, would likely be overseen by U.S. District Judge Aileen M. Cannon — a conservative favorite who made the controversial ruling to dismiss the federal mishandling of documents indictment against Trump,” the report added.

Our Analysis:

The Troubling Ascension of Jason Reding Quiñones: A Critical Examination

In what reads like a script torn from the pages of a political thriller, the rise of Jason Reding Quiñones from a floundered prosecutor to the U.S. Attorney for the Southern District of Florida under President Donald Trump is a narrative fraught with controversy and questionable integrity. This saga, as reported by the Washington Post, lays bare a concerning trajectory of political appointments and a blatant disregard for meritocracy and professional competency in favor of political loyalty and agendas.

The Dubious Comeback

Reding Quiñones’s initial stint as a prosecutor in the Southern District of Florida was notably lackluster. His failure to impress with either work ethic or legal acumen, resulting in poor performance evaluations, should have been a career-stifling blow. Yet, in a dramatic pivot, Trump’s administration saw fit to elevate him to the very pinnacle of the office he once struggled to find footing in. The decision to appoint someone who previously flunked out of an entry-level job as the head of federal prosecutions in one of the most significant districts in the United States is not just shocking; it’s a blaring alarm over the erosion of merit-based advancement in the justice system.

A Political Pawn?

The motivations behind Reding Quiñones’s sudden rise are thinly veiled. His elevation appears to be a direct result of his associations with conservative circles, notably his interaction with Mike Davis, a conservative lawyer and online activist. Davis’s endorsement of Reding Quiñones, predicated not on his prosecutorial prowess but on his perceived loyalty to conservative causes and willingness to engage in legal battles against Trump’s political adversaries, underscores a disturbing trend of valuing political allegiance over legal competence and ethics.

The panel discussion at a conservative legal conference, wherein Davis and Reding Quiñones participated, seems to have been a pivotal moment. The conversation, ominously titled “Modern Lawfare and the American Democracy,” laid the groundwork for what appears to be a politically motivated cleansing of Trump’s perceived enemies within the legal system. Davis’s assertion that there must be “severe consequences” for Trump’s foes and the subsequent targeting of figures like James R. Clapper Jr. and John Brennan signal a dangerous politicization of the justice system.

Legal Maneuvering and Ethical Quandaries

The strategic maneuvers by Davis and Reding Quiñones, particularly the proposal to circumvent the statute of limitations by framing the 2017 intelligence assessment as part of a long-running conspiracy, are as cunning as they are ethically dubious. This tactic, coupled with the choice to impanel a grand jury in Fort Pierce, where proceedings would likely be overseen by a conservative favorite judge, reveals a calculated effort to exploit the judicial system for political vendettas.

A System Under Siege

The chilling effect of these developments on the Southern District of Florida’s U.S. attorney’s office cannot be overstated. The departure of seasoned prosecutors, the climate of fear among remaining staff about the repercussions of handling politically sensitive cases, and the recruitment of outside prosecutors to replace those who have fled—all paint a picture of an office in turmoil, its integrity compromised and its function as a bastion of impartial justice dangerously undermined.

In Conclusion: A Call for Accountability

The ascent of Jason Reding Quiñones to the helm of the Southern District of Florida’s U.S. attorney’s office, underpinned by questionable ethics and political maneuvering, is a stark reminder of the fragility of the justice system when subjected to partisan influences. This saga is not merely about the failings of an individual but a systemic aberration that threatens the very foundations of justice and democracy. It underscores the imperative for a judiciary that remains insulated from political pressures, guided by merit, and committed to the impartial administration of justice. The case of Jason Reding Quiñones should not be seen as an isolated incident but a cautionary tale of what happens when the scales of justice are tipped by the heavy hand of political interests.
