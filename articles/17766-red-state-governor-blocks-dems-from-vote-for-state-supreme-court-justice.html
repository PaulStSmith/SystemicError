A Judicial Farce: The Exclusion of Democratic Voters in Louisiana’s Supreme Court Race

Power Play in the Bayou

The recent maneuvering in Louisiana’s judicial elections underlines a stark power grab by the Republican Governor Jeff Landry. By implementing a semi-closed primary system, Landry has effectively barred nearly 100,000 registered Democrats in District 1 from voting in the upcoming Supreme Court race. This isn’t just a tweak in electoral mechanics; it’s a deliberate exclusion of a significant portion of the electorate, ensuring that the judicial appointment reflects a narrow, partisan selection rather than a broader, more democratic choice.

The Institutional Culpability

This exclusionary tactic reveals a calculated decision by those wielding institutional power. Governor Landry, backed by the Republican legislative apparatus in Louisiana, pushed for this electoral system change. It’s a strategic move, ensuring that the only voters influencing the selection of a state Supreme Court justice are those aligned or unaffiliated with the Republican Party. This isn’t a mere oversight; it’s a tailored disenfranchisement.

Misdirection and Its Implications

The narrative surrounding the race, focusing on the personal backgrounds and campaign fundraising of the two Republican candidates, Judges Billy Burris and Blair Downing Edwards, serves as a smokescreen. While their personal stories and judicial philosophies are standard campaign fodder, they distract from the larger issue: a significant slice of the electorate is systematically sidelined. This misdirection shifts the focus from systemic disenfranchisement to individual candidate profiles, diluting the severity of the electoral manipulation.

The Money Trail and Its Influence

Further complicating the democratic deficit in this election is the influx of significant sums of money, with candidates drawing hefty donations from sectors that frequently have stakes before the Supreme Court. Edwards’ ties to personal injury lawyers and the nursing home industry, alongside Burris’ backing from the business and energy sectors, underscore how monetary influence is as much a part of this race as the votes—except, in this case, the votes are conspicuously skewed by partisan gating.

Anonymous Agendas

Compounding the lack of electoral fairness is the role of dark money, exemplified by groups like the First Principles PAC and Moms Protecting Children, which have spent large sums on attack ads without disclosing their donors. This anonymity in campaign finance allows hidden actors to shape the judicial landscape without public accountability, further eroding trust in the electoral process and the impartiality of the judiciary.

Conclusion: A Reflection of a Larger Pattern

Louisiana’s current Supreme Court race is not an isolated incident but a reflection of broader, more disturbing trends in American politics where electoral integrity and judicial independence are under threat. Such maneuvers not only undermine democratic principles but also instill a judiciary that may not reflect the will of the entire populace but rather the interests of a powerful few. As this scenario plays out, it becomes ever more critical to scrutinize not just who is running and their qualifications, but who is allowed to participate in the democratic process and who is not—and to what end.
