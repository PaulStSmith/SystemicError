How Museums Can Stand Firm When the Demagogue Comes for Them

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 19, 20255 min read

In our previous article, we argued that museums must stand firm against Trump’s threats to erase history. But “stand firm” is not a slogan; it’s a challenge. Museums are vulnerable precisely because they depend on federal grants—or, in the case of the Smithsonian, are federally owned. Survival itself can be used as leverage against them. Courage alone won’t cut it. If museums are to endure the next authoritarian lunge, they need not only resolve but strategy.

The Stakes: Museums as Memory Keepers

Museums aren’t neutral warehouses of artifacts; they are battlegrounds of memory. They curate the stories we tell about ourselves, the narratives that shape how future generations understand the past. Trump and his ilk know this. That’s why they want to neuter museums into propaganda halls—monuments to mythology rather than truth.

If museums cave, history becomes Disneyfied. Slavery becomes “an unfortunate labor practice.” Jim Crow becomes “regional traditions.” Civil rights activists become “agitators.” And America becomes a lie, wrapped in glossy brochures. That’s why standing firm matters. But standing firm without resources is suicide. So how do they resist?

1. Public Outcry as Armor

The first defense is outrage. Museums must make clear that an attack on them is an attack on the public’s right to know its own history. They should not issue mealy-mouthed statements about “political differences.” They should frame censorship as what it is: a war on truth. That means mobilizing allies—teachers, librarians, veterans’ groups, unions, civil rights organizations—to blast any political interference into the headlines.

Authoritarians thrive when institutions fight alone. But if every budget cut becomes a public scandal, every legal threat becomes a rallying cry, the cost of repression skyrockets.

2. Diversify the Money

Yes, museums rely on federal grants. But dependence is vulnerability. To stand firm, museums need to build lifelines outside Washington. That means aggressively cultivating private donors, endowments, and partnerships. Billionaires who fancy themselves philanthropists should be forced to put their money where their mouths are. Foundations that claim to care about truth and democracy should be pressured to step up.

It also means making the case to state governments and city councils that local cultural institutions are worth saving. Museums aren’t just national treasures; they’re local economies. They drive tourism, education, and community pride. Mayors and governors can be enlisted as allies if the fight is framed in those terms.

3. Flood the Zone with Transparency

Trump’s lie is that museums dwell only on “how bad slavery was.” The counter is obvious: show the world what museums actually do. Video tours, podcasts, lesson plans, online exhibits—push the full picture. Show the horrors of bondage, yes, but also the music, art, literature, political victories, cultural triumphs. Force-feed the public the truth Trump wants to erase.

The goal is to preempt the attack. If the narrative is already set—that museums celebrate resilience and achievement as well as oppression—then Trump’s caricature collapses faster under scrutiny.

4. Sue Back

Trump’s threats to sic lawyers on museums should be met with lawsuits of their own. The First Amendment doesn’t vanish because an orange demagogue doesn’t like history. Academic freedom, artistic expression, and free inquiry are protected rights. If the administration tries to censor museums, museums must drag them into courtrooms.

The strategy should be clear: don’t play defense only. Make it costly. Make every attempt at censorship a legal quagmire. Force the administration to burn resources fighting unwinnable cases. Tie up their efforts until the political winds shift.

5. Internationalize the Battle

Museums are not just domestic institutions; they are global cultural landmarks. The Smithsonian, for example, attracts millions of international visitors. If Trump tries to vandalize them politically, the backlash will not be contained within U.S. borders. UNESCO, international cultural organizations, and foreign governments can all be leveraged to apply pressure. Authoritarians loathe international shame. Museums should weaponize it.

Imagine headlines in London, Paris, or Tokyo: American President Orders Museums to Whitewash Slavery. That’s the kind of humiliation Trump fears.

6. Localize the Resistance

Even federally owned museums exist in local communities. The Smithsonian is rooted in Washington, D.C.—a city with its own politics and leaders. Local governments can provide emergency funding, declare cultural sanctuaries, or simply refuse to cooperate with federal meddling. The same applies to state-funded museums. If governors want to look like defenders of free expression, this is their chance.

Museums should start cultivating these relationships now, not when the axe falls.

7. Weaponize the Irony

Museums should hammer the hypocrisy until it becomes unavoidable. Trump rants about “cancel culture” while trying to cancel history. He whines about censorship while demanding censorship. He markets himself as a champion of freedom while attacking the very institutions that preserve it.

That message needs to be repeated endlessly: the man who cries victim is trying to erase actual victims from history. Force the hypocrisy into the spotlight until even casual observers can’t miss it.

8. Build Coalitions Across Sectors

Museums should not fight alone. Universities, libraries, archives, publishers, journalists—all are in the crosshairs. A coalition of knowledge institutions is stronger than any single one. Coordinated campaigns, joint lawsuits, shared funding drives, mutual defense pacts—these aren’t luxuries, they’re necessities.

The authoritarian project thrives on fragmentation. Its enemy is solidarity. Museums need to link arms with every institution under threat and fight as one.

9. Embrace Militancy in Messaging

Museums are often cautious, bound by a culture of politeness. That instinct must die. When the attack comes, polite letters to Congress won’t cut it. Museums must go militant—not with violence, but with rhetoric. They must say, loudly and unapologetically: This is censorship. This is fascism. This is an attack on America’s soul.

If they shrink into bureaucratic timidity, they will lose. If they embrace militancy, they may not win every battle—but they will keep the fight alive.

Conclusion: Standing Firm Means Fighting Dirty

Standing firm is not passive. It’s not stoically waiting for the storm to pass. It’s active resistance—mobilizing the public, diversifying funding, suing back, rallying allies, internationalizing the shame, localizing the resistance, weaponizing the hypocrisy, and linking arms with every threatened institution. Museums can’t hide behind glass cases anymore. They are on the front lines of the culture war whether they like it or not.

Trump wants museums to cower. To whitewash. To bow. If they do, history dies. If they resist, loudly and strategically, they can survive—and in surviving, they keep the nation’s memory alive. Because when authoritarianism comes for history, silence is surrender. And museums cannot, must not, surrender.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
