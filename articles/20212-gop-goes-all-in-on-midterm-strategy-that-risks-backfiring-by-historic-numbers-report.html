GOP goes all in on midterm strategy that risks backfiring by historic numbers: report

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosJuly 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump Isn’t on the Ballot, But His Damage Is

The Summary

NOTUS reports that Republicans are betting on a familiar trick for the midterms: chase the irregular voters who backed Donald Trump in 2024 and lean harder into Trump’s brand rather than distance themselves from it. A senior GOP official said the party is now “completely different” from the first Trump era, when some committees were willing to tell candidates to run from him. The problem, as the report notes, is that Trump’s support has cratered, he keeps attacking members of his own party, and his appeal to the low-frequency voters Republicans need is no longer guaranteed.

Power Still Runs Through Trump

The key fact here is simple: Republicans are not building a strategy around congressional candidates, local issues, or persuasion. They are building it around Trump, even though he is not on the ballot. That tells you where the real institutional power sits. The party may wear the label “GOP,” but the operating center is still Trump and the voters he can activate or repel.

That is not a sign of unity. It is a sign of dependency.

The Decision Makers Know the Risk

This is not an accidental drift. Republican strategists are making a conscious choice to double down on Trump despite the obvious danger. They know he has become a liability for party cohesion. They know his approval is sitting in the 30s. They know some of the voters he once pulled in for economic reasons are now anxious about their finances. And they are still choosing to treat him as the message.

That is the political reality beneath the spin: party leaders are not being dragged by events. They are deciding to tie their own brand to a weakened, volatile figure because they believe the base still belongs to him.

The Misdirection Is the Point

The article’s framing does one useful thing: it shows how easy it is for Republican consultants to dress up a desperation play as a turnout strategy. Targeting “irregular” Trump voters sounds analytical. In practice, it is a bet that the same voters can be reanimated by nostalgia, branding, and tribal loyalty even as the economic promise that helped move them in 2024 looks less convincing.

That is where the misdirection lives. The story is not really about turnout mechanics. It is about a party trying to hide the collapse of its broader appeal behind the language of field operations and voter targeting. If the plan fails, the blame will almost certainly be aimed downward at volunteers, field staff, or “messaging,” not at the decision to remain welded to Trump.

The Party Has Chosen Submission Over Control

The more revealing detail is the anonymous GOP official’s admission that, in the first Trump administration, operatives could still imagine telling candidates to run away from the president. Not anymore. “Everyone’s bought in this time around” is less a statement of confidence than a confession of surrender.

That is how institutions rot. First they accommodate the strongman. Then they depend on him. Then they cannot tell the difference between strategy and obedience.

The Bigger Pattern

This story fits a familiar pattern in modern American politics: a party whose formal institutions still exist, but whose actual behavior is dictated by one dominant figure’s moods, power, and resentments. When that figure weakens, the party does not correct itself. It improvises a new justification for staying attached.

Republicans are not just gambling on Trump here. They are revealing how thoroughly they have internalized a politics of permanent personal loyalty, even when the person at the center is dragging the rest of the operation toward the ground.
