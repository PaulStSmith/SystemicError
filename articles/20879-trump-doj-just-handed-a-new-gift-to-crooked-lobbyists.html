Trump DOJ just handed a new gift to crooked lobbyists

Trump’s Anti-Corruption Reversal Is Not a Policy Shift. It’s an Access Sale.

The Point Was Never Enforcement

Pam Bondi’s early DOJ memos did not merely “change priorities.” They stripped away anti-bribery and anti-kleptocracy capacity that had accumulated over decades, then pretended the damage was bureaucratic housekeeping. Trump later finished the job by gutting the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act, killing the shell-company registry, and replacing it with a “golden visa” scheme. The throughline is not reform. It is permission.

Power Sat at the Top

The actors with actual institutional power were clear: Trump in the White House, Bondi at Justice, and the executive branch apparatus they controlled. They did not inherit a broken anti-corruption regime and reluctantly trim it. They actively dismantled the tools that let the U.S. trace foreign money, expose hidden ownership, and punish bribery. That matters because power here is not abstract. It is the ability to decide whether the state hunts corruption or clears a path for it.

The Misdirection Is the Mechanism

The framing around FARA is especially revealing. Bondi narrowed attention to cases “similar to more traditional espionage,” which is a verbal trick designed to blur a lobbying law into a spy story. That is not confusion; it is misdirection. FARA is about foreign influence and undisclosed political pressure, not cinematic espionage. Recasting it as a national-security sideshow helps normalize nonenforcement while keeping the language of vigilance intact.

Kleptocracy Was the Business Model

The programs Trump and Bondi targeted were not decorative. The Foreign Influence Task Force tracked foreign influence campaigns from Russia, China, Iran, Venezuela, and elsewhere. Kleptocracy efforts pursued dirty money, frozen assets, and illicit wealth tied to the Kremlin and other regimes. The shell-company registry made hidden ownership harder. Removing these systems does not create neutrality. It gives oligarchs, dictatorships, and their American facilitators a cleaner operating environment.

“Competitiveness” Is the Cover Story

Trump’s defense of shutting down FCPA enforcement was “American economic competitiveness,” the standard laundering phrase for legalized bribery. That argument only works if you accept that U.S. firms need the right to corrupt foreign governments to compete. That is not pro-business realism; it is state-sanctioned moral collapse dressed up as pragmatism. When the richest and most connected actors get immunity, the public gets the bill in the form of weaker institutions, more captured policy, and a federal government that serves illicit wealth before democracy.

What This Story Actually Shows

This is the modern authoritarian playbook in administrative form: disable the anti-corruption machinery, rename the wreckage as efficiency, and invite the beneficiaries to call it growth. The danger is not just that corruption increases. It is that the state itself becomes an enabler, a host, and eventually a broker. Once that happens, the problem is no longer a few bad actors. The problem is a governing coalition willing to turn public power into private cover.
