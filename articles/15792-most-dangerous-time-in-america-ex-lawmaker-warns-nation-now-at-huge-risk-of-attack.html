Host Kate Bolduan asked the Democrat, “Just from your perspective, what does it do when a secretary's longtime advisor, his deputy chief of staff, the chief of staff to the deputy secretary of defense, they all get pushed out of the Pentagon in the midst of all of this?”

“Well, I recently chaired the commission on national defense strategy. We said unanimously for Democrats, for Republicans, that this is the most dangerous time in terms of national security since 1945,” former Rep. Jane Harman (D-CA) responded.

“If I were any one of America's enemies, looking at our country, I would think this is my opportunity to do any number of things, think a massive cyber attack in the U.S. I worry a lot that we could have a cyber 9/11 any day,” Harman said.

The former Representative then pointed to several women in the military who could take Hegseth’s place, before talking about how our enemies could take advantage of his Signal scandals.

“And so if I'm China, Russia, anywhere, I think I've got open season on the U.S. and look at what Russia is doing in Ukraine.”

She added, “Russia is toying with the United States and obviously toying with Ukraine, and it is an absolute tragedy to see a place which was invaded in violation of international law after Russia promised repeatedly to respect its sovereignty, suffering this way. Ukraine is still fighting back.”

“Europe is going to step up and fight for Ukraine. But now that we have a casual comment from, I think it's our vice president, that we may not continue to pay attention to Ukraine, I think that is hugely damaging and really threatens U.S. national security,” Harman said.

In the latest scandal, Hegesth leaked Military information in a Signal chat, which included his wife and brother. It came just a few weeks after the editor-in-chief of The Atlantic was mistakenly added to a similar chat. Both instances were about operations in Yemen.

Our Analysis:

Analysis of the Pentagon Firings and Signal Scandal Involving Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth

The recent firings of senior Pentagon officials, coupled with Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth's involvement in a second Signal scandal, have thrown a spotlight on the fragility and potential vulnerabilities within the U.S. national security apparatus. The commentary from former Rep. Jane Harman (D-CA) on CNN underscores the gravity of these developments. Harman's insights, particularly her reference to the "most dangerous time in terms of national security since 1945," should not be taken lightly. However, it's essential to dissect these statements and the surrounding circumstances with a critical eye.

The Firings and National Security

The dismissal of key Pentagon officials in the midst of a scandal is alarming but not unprecedented. What's particularly concerning here is the timing and the backdrop of international tensions and cybersecurity threats. Harman's warning of a potential "cyber 9/11" is not mere fearmongering but a stark reminder of our digital vulnerabilities. Yet, one must question if this scenario is the result of systemic issues within the Pentagon and broader U.S. government, rather than the actions (or inactions) of individual officials.

Signal Scandals and the Implications

The leakage of military information via Signal, involving Hegseth and his close circle, is a glaring breach of operational security. This isn't just about the mishandling of sensitive information; it's a testament to a culture of carelessness that seems to permeate some echelons of the U.S. defense establishment. The fact that such leaks could occur not once but twice, with significant implications for operations in Yemen, speaks volumes about the Pentagon's internal security measures and personnel reliability.

The International Dimension

Harman's point about our adversaries, specifically China and Russia, seeing the U.S. as vulnerable is well taken. The ongoing situation in Ukraine, with Russia's blatant disregard for international law, indeed reflects a broader geopolitical chess game. However, the leap to suggesting that the U.S. is now open season for our adversaries due to these scandals and firings oversimplifies and overlooks the multifaceted nature of international relations and military deterrence.

The Role of Leadership

The potential for women in the military to step into Hegseth's role underscores a broader issue of leadership and representation within the Pentagon. Leadership in times of crisis is paramount, and diversity in leadership can bring fresh perspectives and solutions. However, the focus should be on competence, integrity, and the ability to secure the nation against all forms of threats, rather than merely filling a position based on gender or any other singular attribute.

Conclusion: A Systemic Malaise?

The revelations brought to light by the firings and the Signal scandal are symptomatic of deeper, systemic issues within the U.S. defense and security framework. It's not just about individual missteps or operational security failures; it's about a systemic malaise that seems to undermine the very foundations of our national security.

The casualness with which sensitive information appears to be handled, the apparent ease with which key officials can be dismissed amid such crises, and the potential for geopolitical adversaries to exploit these situations, all point to the need for a comprehensive overhaul of how security, both digital and physical, is managed and prioritized.

In the end, while Harman's insights are valuable, they only scratch the surface of the profound challenges facing the U.S. national security establishment. The focus should not merely be on the symptoms but on the underlying disease plaguing our defense mechanisms and strategic postures. Only then can we hope to fortify our nation against the multifarious threats that loom on the horizon.
