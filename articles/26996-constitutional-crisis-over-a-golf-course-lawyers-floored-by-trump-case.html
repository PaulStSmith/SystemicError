'Constitutional crisis over a golf course': Lawyers floored by Trump case

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 31, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that U.S. District Judge Ana Reyes said Friday that she would appoint a special master to investigate whether lawyers for the Justice and Interior departments misrepresented the Trump administration’s plans for East Potomac Park. The case arose after President Donald Trump sought to convert one of Washington’s three public golf courses into a PGA-style venue without the approval process described in the lawsuit, while existing National Links Trust agreements were broken.

The immediate confrontation concerns more than 400 cherry and other trees cut down in October. Reyes had permitted routine maintenance and expected notice of major changes, according to the report. The administration notified the court at 4:55 p.m. on the Friday before a holiday; the trees were gone the next day. The National Park Service said evidence indicated that a few may have descended from Japan’s 1912 gift.

The special master will examine whether government attorneys lied to the court; the reporting does not establish that conclusion. Plaintiffs include the D.C. Preservation League and two golfers. Lawyers quoted by AlterNet said possible consequences could include judicial sanctions or referrals to professional disciplinary bodies.

The power was federal

The concerned residents and golfers did not command agencies, control the land or deploy government lawyers. The administration did. The Interior Department oversaw the public property, while the Justice Department represented the government and carried obligations of candor to the court.

That hierarchy matters. Describing this chiefly as a preservation dispute risks treating citizens and federal officials as comparable participants in a disagreement. They were not. One side petitioned a court. The other possessed the machinery capable of changing the site before meaningful review could occur.

A deadline engineered by action

The administration’s reported sequence is more revealing than the landscaping debate. Reyes expected notice before major changes. Government officials delivered notice late on a pre-holiday Friday, and the trees were removed the next day.

That timing does not, by itself, prove a scheme to deceive the court. It does show how executive power can manufacture finality: disclose an impending act when effective intervention becomes difficult, then present the completed act as the new reality. The foreseeable consequence is that judicial review arrives after the chainsaws.

The golf course is not the constitutional issue

Calling this a “constitutional crisis over a golf course” captures the absurd setting but can misdirect attention from the institutional conduct. The constitutional concern is not golf. It is whether executive agencies respected judicial supervision and whether government lawyers gave the court an accurate account while irreversible work proceeded.

Nor has the court established “corruption,” despite plaintiff-side counsel Norm Eisen using that term. The confirmed development in AlterNet’s account is narrower and serious enough: Reyes ordered an independent inquiry into possible misrepresentations after already making what lawyers following the case described as a bad-faith finding.

Public property, private preference

Trump’s preference for a tournament-grade course carried institutional force because federal agencies could translate it into contracts broken, procedures allegedly bypassed and trees removed. That is the central power story. A presidential desire did not remain a suggestion; the state apparatus reportedly reorganized public land around it.

The damage also changes the legal stakes. Contracts can be litigated and plans revised. Mature trees cannot be restored by a favorable ruling. When officials act faster than courts can evaluate their authority, delay ceases to be neutral and becomes an advantage for whoever controls the bulldozers, crews and paperwork.

The systemic error

Institutions built on professional compliance are vulnerable when compliance itself becomes disputed. Courts normally presume that federal attorneys will disclose material facts faithfully and that agencies will not exploit procedural timing to outrun review. The special master signals that Reyes no longer considered ordinary reliance sufficient.

That is the larger pattern exposed here: concentrated executive power can turn an apparently minor administrative dispute into a separation-of-powers test by treating oversight as an obstacle to be managed after the result is secured. The setting may be a golf course. The consequence is a judiciary forced to investigate whether the government lawyers before it can be trusted.
