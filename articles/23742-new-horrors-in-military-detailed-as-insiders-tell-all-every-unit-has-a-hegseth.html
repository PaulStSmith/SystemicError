New horrors in military detailed as insiders tell all: 'Every unit has a Hegseth'

Based on an article by Alexandria Jacobson, Investigative Reporter

The Military’s Chain of Command Is Also a Chain of Impunity

An Institution Protecting Itself

The military has repeatedly failed survivors of sexual assault, then classified that failure as procedure. A Navy officer’s claim that she was raped aboard a transport ship was dismissed as a workers’ compensation matter. Retired Marine Dawn says her gang rape produced no investigation. Army veteran Katy Eilers says four military police officers assaulted her at Abu Ghraib; she was moved, while they escaped punishment.

These are not stories about an institution unable to determine what happened. They are stories about where the institution places the cost when its personnel are accused of violence. The accused remain in place. Survivors lose careers, health, fertility, security, and years of their lives.

The Command Structure Made the Choice

Survivors did not design reporting systems that force them to weigh an investigation against retaliation. They did not create commands where victims continue working beside alleged assailants. They did not decide that moving the person who reported an assault was easier than disciplining those accused of committing it.

Military leaders made or tolerated those arrangements. Restricted reporting may offer confidentiality, but it also means no investigation. For Eilers, that option was shaped by fear that military police—the same institutional force implicated in her assault—would retaliate. Calling such a decision “voluntary” ignores the coercive structure surrounding it.

The aftermath is therefore not an administrative defect separate from the assault. It is a second exercise of power: silence is rewarded, reporting carries risk, and institutional reputation takes precedence over accountability.

Hegseth Is Removing Restraints, Not Confusion

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth has accelerated investigations into equal-opportunity complaints while branding such programs “weaponized.” He has blocked promotions for women and minorities and intervened to remove female candidates from promotion lists. The result included a year with no active-duty female Navy officers promoted to one-star admiral, reportedly the first in more than a decade.

The administration presents this retreat as relief from “walking on eggshells.” That phrase performs a familiar political trick: it recasts safeguards against abuse as persecution of the people subject to them. Those with rank and command authority become the supposed victims; those vulnerable to retaliation become bureaucratic inconveniences.

Nothing in the survivors’ accounts suggests excessive protection. Their testimony describes the opposite: assaults left uninvestigated, perpetrators unpunished, careers constrained, and victims isolated. Weakening oversight in that environment is not correction. It is permission.

Statistics Cannot Carry the Moral Load

The Pentagon answered questions with a press release emphasizing that estimated unwanted sexual contact declined since fiscal year 2023 and that reporting rose from 25 percent to 33 percent. Those numbers matter. They do not answer the charge.

An estimated 20,492 service members still experienced unwanted sexual contact in 2025. More importantly, a reporting rate is not an accountability rate. The official language of dignity, discipline, readiness, and “lethality” avoids the central questions: Who was investigated? Who was removed? Who retained command authority? What happened to the people who reported?

The press release substitutes institutional branding for institutional accounting. It describes values while survivors describe consequences.

Recruitment Refusal Is a Political Verdict

Dawn now goes to recruitment offices and tells women not to enlist while Donald Trump is president and Hegseth runs the Defense Department. That is not generalized hostility toward military service. It is a judgment about the government’s willingness to protect the people it recruits.

The armed forces claim extraordinary authority over service members’ movements, work, medical care, and legal options. That control creates an equally extraordinary duty of protection. When leadership instead weakens safeguards and closes avenues of accountability, enlistment becomes a demand for trust from an institution demonstrating that it may not deserve it.

The proposed Military Sexual Trauma Accountability Act would allow service members and veterans to sue the government over sexual trauma and negligence. Its political importance lies in breaking the military’s control over the consequences of its own failures. Institutions rarely discipline themselves effectively when they also control the evidence, the careers, and the available remedies.

Impunity Is a Personnel Policy

This story reveals more than cruelty toward individual survivors. It shows how impunity is maintained as policy: narrow the route to justice, stigmatize oversight, preserve command discretion, and describe the resulting silence as cohesion.

The weakest framing would treat military sexual trauma as a stubborn cultural problem or a dispute over competing priorities. Culture does not block promotions, design reporting systems, dismiss claims, or leave alleged perpetrators untouched. People with institutional power do those things.

The pattern is deliberate in its effects even where individual intent cannot be established: authority is protected, accountability is displaced, and survivors absorb the damage. A military that calls this readiness is not solving its abuse problem. It is organizing around it.
