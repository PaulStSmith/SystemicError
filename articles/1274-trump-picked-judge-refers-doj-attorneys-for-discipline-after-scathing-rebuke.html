Trump-picked judge refers DOJ attorneys for discipline after scathing rebuke

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 9, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

DOJ’s Power, and Its Cowardice, Were the Story

The Institution Behind the Abuse

A federal judge appointed by Donald Trump has now referred Justice Department attorneys for discipline after concluding that the department misled courts, shopped for a friendlier venue in Texas, and pursued a subpoena for the medical records of minor patients who received gender-affirming care at Rhode Island Hospital. That is the core fact pattern. The rest is institutional damage control.

The power in this story sat with the Justice Department, not with the junior lawyer left to absorb questions in court. DOJ chose the target, chose the tactic, chose the venue strategy, and chose to push forward after the legal ground beneath it had already started to crack.

The Decision Was Made Higher Up

Judge Mary McElroy’s order makes the hierarchy hard to miss. A senior attorney “sat silently by” while a lawyer with about six months of practice was left to answer for the department’s conduct. That is not merely bad litigation management. It is how institutions shield decision-makers by placing the least powerful people closest to the blast radius.

McElroy also found that a senior DOJ official filed a declaration in the Texas proceeding that was “clearly misleading, if not utterly false.” That matters because it shows the problem was not confusion at the margins. The misconduct was embedded in the department’s strategy, not just in a stray courtroom error.

The Judge Saw Through the Frame

The department’s apparent goal was obvious: use federal power to pry open confidential medical records, then route the case through Texas in search of a more favorable forum. McElroy said that plainly, calling the move blatant forum shopping and finding the subpoena lacked a congressionally authorized purpose, was issued in bad faith, and violated children’s constitutional right to informational privacy.

That is where the official framing collapses. This was not some neutral administrative inquiry gone astray. It was a coercive attempt to obtain sensitive data about minors, followed by misrepresentation when the legal trail started turning hostile.

Misdirection as Method

The usual institutional trick is to hide deliberate conduct behind process language. “Negotiations.” “Venue.” “Records request.” “Litigation posture.” Those words soften the underlying reality: the department was trying to force access to private medical information about children, then obscured what it had done when challenged.

The most revealing detail is the junior lawyer left to field the court’s questions. That is not an accident of staffing; it is a familiar bureaucratic tactic. When the substance is indefensible, spread accountability downward and let the least powerful employee stand in front of the judge.

A Pattern of Federal Overreach

This case fits a broader pattern in which federal institutions use their authority not to resolve a problem cleanly, but to stretch, obscure, and weaponize process against politically vulnerable targets. Here the target was children receiving gender-affirming care, and the mechanism was a subpoena backed by a department willing to misstate facts to get its way.

McElroy’s referral is not just a rebuke of bad lawyering. It is a record of institutional bad faith. DOJ did not merely overreach; it treated candor as optional and privacy as expendable.

The Real Lesson

The political meaning here is simple: when a powerful agency wants something badly enough, it can turn procedure into cover and subordinate lawyers into sacrificial buffers. The corruption is not only in the false declaration or the misleading hearing. It is in the structure that makes such conduct feel usable.

The judge quashed the subpoena and barred DOJ from seeking, receiving, or using the records. That was the legal correction. The political correction is harder: naming the department’s conduct for what it was, instead of letting federal power dress itself up as confusion, routine, or good-faith enforcement.
