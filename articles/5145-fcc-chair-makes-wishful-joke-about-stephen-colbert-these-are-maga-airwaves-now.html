FCC chair makes wishful joke about Stephen Colbert: 'These are MAGA airwaves now'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 20, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Charlie Kirk Show/screen grab

Federal Communications Commission Chair Brendan Carr joked that Stephen Colbert had been prevented from airing an interview with Texas state Rep. James Talarico, a Democratic candidate for U.S. Senate, because “these are MAGA airwaves now.”

During a Thursday interview on the Charlie Kirk Show, co-host Andrew Kolvet asked Carr why CBS had prevented Colbert from airing the Talarico interview.

“Well, look, as soon as I found out that Talarico going to be on, I went down to the bowels of the FCC, the Media Bureau, I found the dump button, I pushed it, and I said, these are MAGA airwaves now,” Carr joked.

The FCC chair later revealed that CBS lawyers had prevented the interview from airing because Carr had previously threatened to change FCC rules that traditionally exempted talk shows from equal-time restrictions.

“So this is Dem-on-Dem violence that they’re just trying to merchandise as having something to do with Trump or the FCC,” Carr claimed, noting that his interpretation of the rules would have required Colbert to also promote Talarico’s Democratic primary opponents, including Rep. Jasmine Crockett (D-TX).

Co-host Blake Neff wondered if Carr’s interpretation of the law hindered free speech.

“Do you think there’s a case to be made [that] this is sort of an obsolete standard that it infringes a little bit on freedom of speech, and there’s so many ways to get your message out there now?” Neff asked.

“There’s a lot of reasonable people that say there’s such a diversity of mechanisms to get your perspectives out there that we don’t need those types of regulations,” Carr agreed. “If people want to do that then they can go to Congress and they can change the law but you know my job at this point is to enforce in a fair and balanced way the laws passed by Congress and that’s what we’re going to be doing.”

Our Analysis:

MAGA Airwaves and the FCC: A Scathing Analysis

In a recent spectacle that can only be described as a tragicomedy, Federal Communications Commission (FCC) Chair Brendan Carr jests about turning the airwaves MAGA, effectively using his position to bring political theater to new lows. During an interview on the Charlie Kirk Show, Carr indulged in a fantastical narrative that CBS’s decision to pull an interview with Democratic Senate candidate James Talarico from Stephen Colbert’s show was due to his, Carr’s, intervention — an action he claimed with a laugh, asserting dominion over what he called “MAGA airwaves now.”

The Joke That Wasn’t Funny

Let’s dissect this moment of absurdity: Carr “joked” about abusing his power to suppress a political voice, a joke that, in any sane world, would be considered grotesque and alarming. This is not merely a bad attempt at humor; it’s a glaring example of how those in power can trivialize the fundamental principles of democracy — freedom of speech and the press.

The Real Reason According to Carr: Dem-on-Dem Violence?

Carr later clarified, suggesting the actual reason for the interview’s cancellation was internal Democratic politics, as CBS lawyers feared FCC repercussions due to equal-time rules. These rules, ostensibly designed to ensure fairness, apparently necessitate that if Talarico were given airtime, Colbert would also have to promote Talarico’s Democratic primary opponents, such as Rep. Jasmine Crockett.

Here, Carr’s attempt to deflect the narrative to “Dem-on-Dem violence” is both cunning and misleading. It’s a classic tactic: imply internal conflict to distract from the broader issue at hand, which in this case, is the potential misuse of FCC regulations to influence media content.

An Obsolete Standard?

The conversation then veers into whether the equal-time rule itself is an antiquated standard that infringes on freedom of speech. Co-host Blake Neff and Carr ponder the necessity of these regulations in today’s diverse media landscape. Carr nonchalantly agrees that there’s a “diversity of mechanisms” for getting messages out there, thereby subtly undermining the very rules he is charged with enforcing.

This raises a pivotal question: If Carr himself acknowledges the potential obsolescence of these regulations, why play into them so eagerly when it conveniently aligns with stifling Democratic voices? His admission that the law could be seen as infringing on freedom of speech, yet choosing to enforce it “in a fair and balanced way,” rings hollow when juxtaposed with his earlier joke.

Conclusion: A Farce at the Expense of Democracy

In this farcical display, Brendan Carr has managed not only to mock the principles of free speech and fair access to media but also to highlight a profound cynicism at the heart of the FCC’s leadership. His cavalier attitude toward media manipulation — joking about it on a platform known for its partisan bias — reflects a dangerous trivialization of the responsibilities entrusted to him.

The issue here transcends a mere interview on Colbert’s show. It’s about the integrity of our media institutions and the ease with which political actors might seek to undermine them for personal or partisan gain. Carr’s actions and words should not be dismissed as mere jokes or internal politics. They should be seen for what they are: a systemic error in our democracy, where those in positions of regulatory power feel emboldened to flaunt their influence in ways that could erode public trust and democratic norms.
