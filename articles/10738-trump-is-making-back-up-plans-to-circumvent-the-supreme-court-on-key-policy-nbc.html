Trump is making 'back up plans' to circumvent the Supreme Court on key policy: NBC

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 8, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:Trump's Tariff Tumult: A Tangled Web of Legal Workarounds and Public Persuasion

FILE PHOTO: WASHINGTON, DC - MARCH 04: U.S. President Donald Trump greets Chief Justice of the United States John G. Roberts, Jr as he arrives to deliver an address to a joint session of Congress at the U.S. Capitol on March 04, 2025 in Washington, DC. Win McNamee/Pool via REUTERS/File Photo

Donald Trump is pursuing legal and logistical workarounds to use in case the Supreme Court rules against him, according to NBC's reporting.

After reporting that, "After losing in lower courts, President Donald Trump plans to take his case for the authority to unilaterally impose tariffs to the Supreme Court and the public square," the outlet notes, "His aides have also explored alternative methods for imposing import taxes on foreign goods, according to a senior White House official and two people familiar with the internal discussions."

The report continues to suggest that the administration has had "backup plans" in the works for some time.

"The backup plans have been part of internal discussions for many months because some White House officials anticipated the possibility that courts would intervene to stop Trump's tariffs, challenging the method he used to impose them — which was pushed by senior trade counselor Peter Navarro — according to two people familiar with the dynamics," NBC reported. "But even if the Supreme Court turns Trump down, he’ll be ready to move forward with other tariff options, these people said."

The report goes on to describe the part of the plan that remains unchanged.

"Regardless of the court outcome, the Trump administration will make the case to the public that Trump has a national security prerogative to continue levying tariffs, the senior White House official said," the report states. "Trump has used tariffs to attempt to compel other countries, including Russia, India and Pakistan, to end or avoid wars."

However, according to the report, the issues the administration is working through are keeping them from getting some important deals done.

"But the uncertainties around various aspects of Trump’s tariff policies — including the court fight and looming Section 232 investigations at the Commerce Department — are delaying the administration’s ability to cut deals with foreign countries, according to a person close to the White House who works on trade issues," according to the report.

“The whole thing is sort of interwoven together,” the individual stated. “It’s made it incredibly hard for the negotiators to land some of these agreements.”

Trump's Tariff Tumult: A Tangled Web of Legal Workarounds and Public Persuasion

In what reads like a playbook from an administration more inclined to bend the rules than to follow them, Donald Trump's latest maneuverings on tariffs illuminate a troubling disregard for legal boundaries and a concerning reliance on public opinion as a tool for policy enforcement. This revelation speaks volumes about the administration's approach to governance—where the ends seemingly justify the means, regardless of the legal or ethical implications.

Firstly, let's dissect the audacity of planning legal and logistical workarounds. It's one thing to explore all legal avenues to achieve policy goals; it's entirely another to treat the Supreme Court's potential ruling as a mere obstacle to be circumvented. This strategy, while maybe not surprising coming from Trump's White House, sets a dangerous precedent for executive overreach and undermines the very fabric of the U.S. constitutional system. The administration's readiness to pivot away from the Supreme Court's authority underscores a flagrant disrespect for the judiciary and its role in checks and balances.

Moreover, the reliance on Peter Navarro's controversial methods for imposing tariffs highlights an administration more concerned with results than process. Navarro's aggressive trade policies have been contentious, to say the least, and the fact that these strategies are being pursued despite legal challenges raises questions about the administration's commitment to lawful governance. It's as if Trump's team is playing a high-stakes game of chicken with the global economy, where the rules are made up and the legal norms don't matter.

The report's mention of backup plans being part of internal discussions for many months reveals a premeditated effort to sidestep legal constraints. This isn't just about being prepared for all contingencies; it's about anticipating a loss in court and having a workaround ready to go. Such a strategy, while tactically savvy, erodes trust in the government's commitment to the rule of law and sets a troubling example for future administrations.

In an almost Orwellian twist, the administration plans to make the case to the public that Trump has a national security prerogative to continue levying tariffs, regardless of the court's decision. This manipulation of public opinion as a tool for policy implementation is cunning, yet deeply problematic. It suggests that the court of public opinion could be used to justify or bypass legal judgments, further blurring the lines between lawful governance and populist rule.

It's not just a matter of legal gymnastics; these policies have tangible impacts on global trade, diplomatic relations, and the U.S. economy. It underscores a willingness to flout legal norms, manipulate public opinion, and jeopardize international relations for the sake of policy goals. This isn't just about tariffs; it's about the integrity of the presidency and the principles that underpin the United States' system of government.
