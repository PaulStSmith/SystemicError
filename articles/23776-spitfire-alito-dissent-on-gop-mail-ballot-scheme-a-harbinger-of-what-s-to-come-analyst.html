Spitfire Alito dissent on GOP mail-ballot scheme a harbinger of what's to come: analyst

The Ballots Alito Chose Not to See

A Rule Designed for Disenfranchisement

The Trump administration tried three times to revive a Postal Service rule that would impose an unfinished barcode-verification system during an election already underway. One mismatch could invalidate an entire batch of mail ballots. The administration offered fraud claims without supporting evidence; the predictable consequence was not election security but mass disenfranchisement.

The Government Created the Crisis

USPS had no working verification portal even after North Carolina began mailing ballots on September 4. That failure belongs to the administration and the agency attempting to enforce the rule.

Samuel Alito nevertheless blamed the states and voting-rights groups that sued to stop it. This reverses cause and effect: the government devised an unlawful, unready system, then treated resistance to that system as the source of delay. Litigation did not create the administrative chaos. It exposed it.

Judicial Misdirection

Alito also renewed insinuations that Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson had “slow-walked” the case. That accusation serves the same purpose as blaming the plaintiffs: it directs scrutiny away from the officials who chose to introduce a destabilizing ballot rule in the middle of voting.

This is not mere disagreement about scheduling. It is institutional misdirection. The administration acted; USPS was unprepared; Alito faulted the people trying to prevent voters from paying the price.

Power Without Voters

Alito and Clarence Thomas were the only justices prepared to let the rule take effect despite the immediate risk to millions of voters. Their position gave expansive deference to executive power while assigning no meaningful weight to the citizens whose ballots could disappear through no fault of their own.

Brett Kavanaugh’s separate opinion was less reckless, but hardly reassuring. He suggested the government might ultimately prevail on the law and blocked the rule because the practical equities favored voters “this time.” Voting rights treated as a temporary balancing factor remain voting rights on probation.

The Court Stopped the Scheme—For Now

The majority concluded that USPS had likely exceeded its authority and that the burdens on voters and election administrators outweighed unsupported fraud allegations. That prevented immediate damage. It did not redeem a Supreme Court with a long record of weakening voting protections.

Calling this proof that the institutional “center” held risks confusing a narrow refusal with durable principle. The court rejected one especially blatant attempt at election subversion only after the government pushed it there three times. Relief is warranted; confidence is not.

Sabotage, Then Scapegoating

The larger pattern is familiar: manufacture an administrative obstacle, justify it with unsubstantiated fraud rhetoric, force weaker actors to scramble, and blame them for the resulting disorder. When courts accept that sequence, bureaucratic incompetence becomes a tool of voter suppression. When judges erase voters from the equation, cruelty acquires the vocabulary of procedure.

This time, most of the Court declined to authorize the damage. Alito and Thomas revealed the more consequential fact: two justices were willing to make voters absorb the cost of an administration’s deliberate choices—and then accuse the people resisting those choices of causing the crisis.
