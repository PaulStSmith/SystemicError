Two red flags that completely blew up Trump's strategy

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 6, 20263 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that Donald Trump’s strategy collapsed under two liabilities political scientist Allan Lichtman identified as unusually important in the 2026 midterms: voters’ concern about authoritarian conduct and corruption. Those issues compounded disapproval of the economy and the unauthorized war against Iran, helping Democrats retake both the House and Senate.

Lichtman said Democrats gained at least seven Senate seats, a shift surpassed in recent history only by 1994. He attributed the Republican defeat partly to Trump’s approval rating in the 30 percent range and said voters held the administration responsible for economic conditions, while opposition to the Iran war appeared without the usual early rally around the president.

AlterNet also reports that Trump, despite earlier plans to contest the results through various means, did not attempt to overturn this defeat and instead expressed hope of working with Democrats. Whether the election produces a durable realignment, Lichtman cautioned, depends on how Democrats govern and whom they nominate in 2028.

The power was never mysterious

Trump held the presidency. Republicans controlled the governing apparatus associated with his administration. The war, the economic program and the conduct voters judged were not products of a powerless opposition or an unruly electorate. They belonged to the people exercising federal power.

That distinction matters because election coverage often turns government failure into atmospheric “discontent.” But voters did not manufacture the conditions they rejected. According to Lichtman’s account, they assigned responsibility to the administration already making policy—and then used the institutional power available to them to remove its congressional support.

The strategy met its authors

The defeat was not merely a communications problem. Lichtman’s analysis points to substantive decisions: an Iran war without congressional authorization, an economic record voters associated with Trump, assertions of sweeping presidential authority and conflicts of interest that he described as presidential self-enrichment.

Calling these “red flags” risks making them sound like warning signs noticed before the real failure. In this account, they were the failure. The administration’s exercise of power created the liabilities; campaign strategy could not detach Republican candidates from the government they had defended.

The reported scale of the loss also constrained Trump’s room to challenge it. That is not institutional restraint suddenly triumphing inside the administration. It is an indication that overwhelming margins can make anti-democratic maneuvering less practicable.

Misdirection through electoral folklore

The article’s historical comparisons are useful until they begin converting responsibility into analogy. Cleveland in 1894, Roosevelt in 1934 and Republicans in 1994 illuminate how voters allocate blame. They do not explain away the choices made in 2026.

A bad economy is not an autonomous political actor. Lichtman’s Roosevelt comparison makes the sharper point: voters distinguish inherited crisis from a government’s response to it. They rewarded Roosevelt’s visible intervention in 1934 and, according to this analysis, punished Trump because they associated him with both the hardship and the governing response.

The same clarity should govern discussion of the war. “Foreign policy” did not become unpopular. AlterNet describes a war pursued by the Trump administration without congressional authorization. Abstract language would conceal the chain of agency.

Rejection is not realignment

A rout can remove power without building a replacement. Democrats gained control because voters rejected the governing party, but Lichtman does not establish that they received a durable ideological mandate. His speculation about Jon Ossoff and 2028 is plainly prediction, not an election result.

Nor should warnings against Democratic “overreach” become the next mechanism for shifting scrutiny away from the defeated administration. Democrats will be responsible for what they do with congressional power. That responsibility should be measured through their decisions and consequences, not through a preemptive demand that they reassure the political forces voters just rejected.

Accountability has to remain specific

The larger pattern is that institutional power becomes harder to disguise when several consequences arrive together. Economic pain, an unpopular war, expansive claims of presidential authority and reported conflicts of interest can be marketed separately for only so long. Elections recombine them into a judgment about who governed and what that government produced.

But electoral punishment is temporary accountability, not structural repair. The underlying system still permits concentrated executive power, military action without congressional authorization and conflicts between public office and private enrichment. A landslide can change who controls Congress. It does not, by itself, remove the machinery that made those abuses possible.
