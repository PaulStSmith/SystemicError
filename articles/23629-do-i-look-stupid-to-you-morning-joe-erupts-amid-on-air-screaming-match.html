'Do I look stupid to you?' Morning Joe erupts amid on-air screaming match

Based on an article by Matthew Chapman

The Question Isn’t Whether Netanyahu Knew. It’s Who Benefits From Not Answering.

A Failure With an Author

The reporting discussed by Joe Scarborough makes a stark allegation: Benjamin Netanyahu’s government encouraged continued Qatari funding of Hamas while receiving an explicit warning that Hamas was preparing a major attack. Netanyahu has since claimed that officials failed to alert him before October 7. Those claims cannot be reconciled without determining who knew what, when—and who chose not to act.

Power Was in the Prime Minister’s Office

Hamas committed the October 7 atrocities. That fact does not erase the separate responsibility of the Israeli government entrusted with preventing them.

Netanyahu held institutional power: authority over national security policy, access to intelligence and influence over Israel’s posture toward Hamas. If the warnings described in the reporting reached his government, then the central issue is not an abstract “intelligence failure.” It is whether identifiable officials received actionable information and failed to respond.

Calling every catastrophe a systemic failure can become a convenient way to ensure that no powerful individual is held responsible for the system he controlled.

Funding Hamas Was a Political Choice

Scarborough’s most consequential charge is not that Netanyahu misunderstood Hamas. It is that his government supported the continuation of Qatari funds despite Hamas’s declared hostility toward Israel.

That policy was not administrative weather. It was a decision. The apparent strategic logic—tolerating Hamas as a counterweight to Palestinian political unity—has long served Netanyahu’s interests by obstructing any coherent Palestinian negotiating partner. The source does not establish Netanyahu’s motive, and motive should not be invented. But it does establish the question that loyalists prefer to evade: why did his government keep approving a policy that strengthened Hamas?

When a strategy produces catastrophe, its architects do not become bystanders merely because the enemy pulled the trigger.

Atrocity as a Diversion

Donny Deutsch’s response demonstrates the misdirection perfectly. Asked about Netanyahu’s conduct, he returned to Hamas’s massacre of roughly 1,200 people.

Hamas’s responsibility was never in dispute. Repeating its crimes did not answer the question; it displaced it. The victims were converted into rhetorical cover for the officials whose conduct was under examination.

This is how accountability is neutralized without openly opposing it. A specific question about state power is recast as an alleged failure to condemn terrorism loudly enough. The weaker actor’s brutality becomes a shield for the stronger institution’s decisions.

Television Anger Is Not Accountability

Scarborough was right to reject the diversion. But shouting “Do I look stupid?” also dragged the exchange toward television spectacle and his own indignation.

The relevant issue is not whether Scarborough can be fooled. It is whether Netanyahu’s account is false, whether warnings were suppressed or ignored, who authorized the funding policy, and what consequences followed inside the Israeli government. Personal outrage may puncture a rehearsed evasion, but it cannot substitute for records, testimony and institutional reckoning.

The source’s framing is therefore both useful and limited. It identifies a question that defenders of Netanyahu avoid, then packages that avoidance as a cable-news confrontation.

Impunity Depends on Changing the Subject

The larger pattern is familiar: leaders claim expansive power when policies appear successful and dissolve into helplessness when those policies end in disaster. Responsibility is pushed downward into “the intelligence services,” outward toward enemies, or sideways into arguments about whether critics have condemned the enemy sufficiently.

That machinery protects power by confusing two truths that can coexist. Hamas perpetrated the massacre. Netanyahu’s government may still bear grave responsibility for policies and decisions that left Israelis exposed to it.

The political function of the diversion is to make the first truth cancel the second. It does not.
