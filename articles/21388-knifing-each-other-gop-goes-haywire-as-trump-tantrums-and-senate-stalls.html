'Knifing each other': GOP goes haywire as Trump tantrums and Senate stalls

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

Paulo SantosAugust 16, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The GOP’s Infighting Is Not a Mood Problem. It’s a Governing Problem.

Power, Not Temperament

The reporting here is trying to package Republican chaos as a family spat: Trump is mad, Senate Republicans are mad, everyone is “knifing each other.” That framing is too soft for what is actually happening. The people with institutional power are the same people causing the disorder. Trump is still the party’s central force, Senate leaders still control the chamber’s machinery, and senior Republicans are still choosing which parts of his agenda to enable and which to obstruct.

This is not dysfunction from the outside. It is power fighting with itself over how much damage it can still pass through Congress.

The Votes Are the Real Story

John Thune’s complaint that he cannot “wave a magic wand” is accidentally honest. There are no votes because Republicans do not have the votes, or do not want to spend them on the specific projects Trump is demanding. That includes the SAVE America Act, a non-citizen voting ban critics call redundant and dystopian. The issue is not that the party is being misunderstood. The issue is that its agenda is so extreme, so unserious, or so politically radioactive that even its own Senate leadership cannot reliably assemble support.

When Thune says he cannot manufacture votes, he is not describing helplessness. He is describing a breakdown in party discipline under the weight of its own priorities.

Misdirection as Strategy

The GOP senator quoted by CNN says the party should be “talking up the wins” and “how crazy Democrats are” instead of “knifing each other.” That is the real reflex at work: project chaos outward, keep the base angry at Democrats, and avoid any accounting for what Republicans are actually doing in power. It is a familiar trick. When the party cannot govern cleanly, it tries to redirect attention toward a manufactured enemy.

But the article itself shows the fraud in that move. The conflict here is not Democrats forcing Republican instability. It is Republican leaders fighting over whether to approve Trump’s latest demands, including a controversial $1.8 billion Anti-Weaponization Fund that reads less like governance than patronage with a slogan attached.

Corruption in Plain Sight

Cornyn and Tillis are blocking Todd Blanche’s confirmation unless they get written proof the fund is dead. Trump, in turn, says he will push hard to pass it. That is not policy disagreement in any meaningful democratic sense. It is leverage politics around an opaque slush fund, with senators using one nomination to extort the burial of another project. Tillis calling it a “payout pot for punks” is ugly language, but the substance matters more than the insult: even Republican senators are describing the initiative as a payoff structure, not a public good.

This is what modern Republican rule increasingly looks like: loyalty tests, confirmation hostage-taking, and a state apparatus treated as a tool for settling factional debts.

The Missing Broker

The death of Lindsey Graham matters in the narrowest inside-baseball sense, but that narrowness is revealing. The article describes him as a crucial diplomat between Thune and Trump. That says everything about the party’s internal condition. It needed a broker to translate between its institutional wing and its strongman wing. Remove the broker, and the whole arrangement becomes more brittle, more personal, and more openly coercive.

A healthy party does not depend on intermediaries to keep its leader from blowing up its own Senate operation. A party built around domination does.

The Pattern Beneath the Noise

Jim Justice warns Republicans they will “lose seats” if they keep this up. He is probably right, but that warning still treats the problem as electoral malpractice rather than democratic degradation. The deeper pattern is that the GOP is trying to hold together an authoritarian political project with a conventional legislative institution that still requires votes, confirmations, and a residue of procedure. The result is constant friction, public tantrums, and desperate blame shifting.

That is the real story here: not a party distracted from its mission, but a party whose mission is so corrosive that it keeps colliding with the basic mechanics of government.
