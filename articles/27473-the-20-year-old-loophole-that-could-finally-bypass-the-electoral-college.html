The 20-year-old loophole that could finally bypass the Electoral College

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 26, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The National Popular Vote Interstate Compact offers states a way to ensure that the presidential candidate who receives the most votes nationwide wins the White House—without formally abolishing the Electoral College. AlterNet reports that Democratic gains in governorships and state legislatures have created a realistic path for the compact, nearly 20 years after Maryland became its first member. But the mechanism remains contingent: it takes effect only when participating states control at least 270 electoral votes, depends on those states honoring their commitments, and could face a court challenge.

The proposal is less a repeal of the Electoral College than an attempt to neutralize its capacity to overrule the electorate. States joining the compact agree to award their electors to the national popular-vote winner, regardless of which candidate finishes first within their borders. Once the participating bloc reaches the 270 electoral votes required to elect a president, its combined votes would guarantee victory for the national winner.

That is the loophole: using the states’ authority over electors to change the outcome produced by the Electoral College without amending the Constitution.

Maryland opened the route in 2007

Maryland joined the compact in April 2007, when Democratic Gov. Martin O’Malley signed it into law. O’Malley told AlterNet that former Indiana Sen. Birch Bayh, then living on Maryland’s Eastern Shore, had urged him to act.

According to O’Malley, Bayh argued that states did not have to wait for a constitutional amendment to address presidential elections in which the popular-vote winner lost. Maryland could move first and invite other states to follow.

O’Malley did not expect a rapid transformation. Governors and legislatures face crowded agendas, he said, and a proposal like the compact can “fall off the plate” unless a governor makes it a priority. That explanation also identifies where the relevant power sits: not with voters acting nationally, because the United States does not conduct presidential elections as a single national contest, but with state officials who determine how their electoral votes are allocated.

Nearly two decades of incremental progress reflect the compact’s basic political difficulty. Every participating state must agree to bind its electoral power to a national result, but the agreement has no effect until the coalition is large enough to decide the presidency.

The Electoral College’s failures are not historical curiosities

Five presidents have taken office without winning the popular vote: John Quincy Adams in 1824, Rutherford B. Hayes in 1876, Benjamin Harrison in 1888, George W. Bush in 2000 and Donald Trump in 2016. The clustering of two such outcomes in the modern era turned what defenders can describe as an antique irregularity into a present political problem.

The 2016 result is central to AlterNet’s framing. Trump won the presidency through the Electoral College despite losing the national popular vote. Had an operative compact controlled at least 270 electors, the national popular-vote winner would instead have received the decisive electoral majority.

That history exposes the misdirection embedded in familiar descriptions of the Electoral College as merely “indirect.” Its consequence is not procedural quaintness. It can transfer the presidency to a candidate rejected by more voters than their opponent, giving state boundaries and electoral arithmetic more authority than the national vote count.

The system also concentrates campaign power in competitive states. The compact would replace that winner-take-all map with one decisive measure: the total number of votes each ticket receives nationwide.

A workaround is not the same as abolition

O’Malley described the compact as a way to “retire” an institution that “no longer serves.” At another point, he said the Electoral College would cease to exist once enough states pledged their electors to the popular-vote winner. That is politically understandable shorthand, but it overstates what the compact formally does.

The Electoral College would remain in the Constitution. Electors would still cast the votes that legally select the president and vice president. The compact would change how participating states instruct those electors, converting the institution into the vehicle for enforcing the national result rather than defeating it.

Nor does the plan require a majority of states, as O’Malley suggested in the interview. The threshold described by AlterNet is electoral, not geographic: states controlling at least 270 of the country’s 538 electoral votes. A coalition could therefore activate the compact without including most states, provided its members possessed a majority of electors.

That distinction matters because the proposal does not remove state power from presidential elections. It organizes enough states to use that power differently.

The plan’s leverage is also its vulnerability

The compact avoids the formidable process of amending the Constitution, but that shortcut creates its own exposure. AlterNet notes that the arrangement relies on participating states acting in good faith and could be struck down by the courts.

Those risks are not incidental. A mechanism designed to prevent the losing candidate from gaining power would face its greatest pressure precisely when it changed an election’s apparent state-by-state result. Officials would be required to award electors according to the nationwide count even if another candidate carried their own state. The compact’s mathematical guarantee therefore depends on its legal and political commitments surviving the moment when they become consequential.

Still, the proposal identifies the institution responsible for the distortion and the officials capable of changing it. Voters did not design a system in which the national runner-up can become president, and they cannot repair it through a single nationwide vote. State governors and legislators possess the operative authority. Maryland used that authority in 2007; the remaining question is whether enough states will do the same to reach 270—and whether the resulting agreement can withstand the first election it determines.
