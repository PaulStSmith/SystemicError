She’s 'the most powerful woman in Washington' — and Trump is powerless to stop her

Trump’s Fury at the Parliamentarian Is Really a Complaint About Limits on His Own Power

A convenient villain

The source story centers on Elizabeth MacDonough, the Senate parliamentarian, after she ruled that $1 billion for Trump’s White House ballroom could not be tucked into the Senate’s budget bill. That is the context. The larger truth is simpler: MacDonough did not create the limitation, and she does not control the Senate. She applies the rules that senators themselves keep in place when it suits them.

Calling her “the most powerful woman in Washington” flatters the people who would rather rage at a procedural official than confront the elected Republicans actually holding the levers.

Who actually has power

Trump has no direct authority to fire MacDonough. Senate Majority Leader John Thune does have leverage, and the Republican caucus has the real institutional power to either back her, overrule her, or change the rules entirely. The article makes that plain: without Thune’s backing, Trump’s demand goes nowhere.

That is the hierarchy the spectacle tries to obscure. Trump can howl on Truth Social. The Senate decides whether the howl becomes policy.

Procedure as cover for political choice

MacDonough has a record of ruling against both parties: blocking a GOP church-speech repeal effort in 2017, rejecting a Democratic minimum wage move in 2021, and stopping a legalization pathway from being stuffed into reconciliation later that year. That pattern matters because it shows the office is not an ideological weapon so much as a choke point built into the chamber’s machinery.

The real decision is not made by the parliamentarian. The real decision is whether senators want the constraint removed. The source notes the obvious consequence: overruling her could open the door to ending the filibuster. That is why the GOP is posturing instead of acting. They want the appearance of grievance without paying the institutional price.

Trump’s complaint exposes his dependency

Trump’s attack on MacDonough is not a show of strength. It is an admission that he still needs Senate Republicans to do what he wants. He can demand punishment, but he cannot execute it alone. The rage is theatrical because the underlying problem is structural: he wants personal command over institutions he does not control.

So the target becomes the referee. That is how power protects itself from accountability. When the governing coalition cannot get the outcome it wants through the rules it has preserved, it blames the rule-enforcer rather than the lawmakers who refuse to change the rules.

What the framing misses

The weak frame here is the idea that MacDonough is somehow the central actor. She is not. The central actors are Trump, Thune, and the Senate Republicans deciding whether to defend procedure, discard it, or weaponize it selectively. MacDonough is the person absorbing pressure generated by a political class that wants contradictory things: constraint when convenient, impunity when not.

This is not a story about an overmighty parliamentarian. It is a story about elected officials outsourcing responsibility to procedural veto points and then pretending those veto points are the problem.

The larger pattern

This is the recurring logic of modern congressional power: leaders keep the rules when they block outcomes they dislike, then treat the rulekeeper as the enemy when the rules block them. The institution preserves its own cowardice by turning procedural staff into scapegoats and calling it governance.

Trump’s tantrum is revealing precisely because it is so limited. He is not attacking a sovereign rival. He is attacking a boundary he cannot personally cross. The Senate wrote that boundary, the Senate can remove it, and the Senate chooses not to. That is the real story.
