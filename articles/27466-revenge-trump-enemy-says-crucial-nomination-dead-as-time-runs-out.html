Revenge: Trump enemy says crucial nomination 'dead' as time runs out

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosNovember 26, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A rushed nomination meets a Senate vetoTrump’s leverage expired with Cornyn’s careerA replacement, if the clock allows

John Cornyn has blocked T. Elliot Gaiser’s nomination to a federal judgeship in Texas, telling MS NOW that the nomination is “not going anywhere.” The retiring Republican senator withdrew the home-state approval needed to advance Gaiser, frustrating the Trump administration’s attempt to fill the seat before Democrats take control of it. What AlterNet framed as revenge is more concretely an exercise of Senate power by a lawmaker whom Donald Trump can no longer punish politically.

A rushed nomination meets a Senate veto

Gaiser reportedly entered consideration only after Republicans suffered an overwhelming defeat in the midterm election. According to MS NOW, staff for Texas Sen. Ted Cruz first contacted him about a possible nomination on Nov. 4. Cornyn objected both to the compressed process and to Gaiser’s lack of meaningful ties to Texas.

He then revoked his “blue slip,” the Senate Judiciary Committee’s mechanism for securing approval from both senators representing a judicial nominee’s state. That decision effectively halted the nomination. Cornyn told people close to him that Gaiser was “dead” for the time being, MS NOW reported, though neither the White House nor Senate websites indicated that the nomination had formally been withdrawn.

Trump’s allies expected Cornyn to surrender. Mike Davis, a longtime Trump legal adviser, presented the choice as accepting the administration’s nominee or handing the seat to Democrats: “You can have a very good nominee now, who may not be your top choice, or the Democrats are going to take the seats.”

That argument reveals the administration’s governing premise. The urgency was not principally about Gaiser’s qualifications or a careful search for a judge rooted in the state. It was about controlling a lifetime appointment before the political window closed.

Trump’s leverage expired with Cornyn’s career

Cornyn is scheduled to leave office early next year after losing his Republican primary. Trump backed Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton, a loyal MAGA figure, against him. Paxton’s scandal-heavy record and deep unpopularity were then widely credited with helping Democrat James Talarico capture the seat during the midterm wave.

Trump therefore succeeded in removing a Republican who crossed him, only to help produce a Democratic successor and liberate Cornyn from the pressure that once made presidential retaliation effective. With no reelection campaign left to threaten, Cornyn has resisted Trump’s legislative priorities and nominations.

Calling the Gaiser blockade “revenge” supplies a tidy personal drama, but it also obscures the institutional mechanics. Cornyn did not merely nurse a grievance; he used a prerogative available to a sitting senator. Trump and his allies had counted on party discipline, electoral intimidation and the fear of Democratic control to overcome Cornyn’s objections. That calculation failed because they had already stripped him of the incentive to comply.

None of this makes Cornyn an insurgent against partisan court-packing. His objection concerns the nominee and the process, not the Republican project of securing the seat. He is already urging Trump to nominate another conservative candidate quickly.

A replacement, if the clock allows

The administration has begun vetting Steve Fahey, a Cornyn-approved prospect with Texas ties, according to MS NOW. Cornyn described Fahey as “a highly accomplished prosecutor” who earned “stellar reviews” from the Federal Judicial Evaluation Committee and called on Trump to nominate him swiftly.

The remaining obstacle is time. It was unclear from the reporting whether Fahey could be nominated and confirmed before Jan. 3. Gaiser’s nomination had not officially ended, but Cornyn’s refusal left it without a viable path while the administration pursued an alternative.

The episode is less a principled break than a collision among competing forms of Republican power: Trump’s control over party careers, senators’ control over home-state judicial nominations and the approaching transfer of a seat to a Democrat. Trump used the first power to drive Cornyn out. Cornyn is now using the second to deny Trump his preferred nominee. The third may leave both men without the judgeship they were racing to secure.
