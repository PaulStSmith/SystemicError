Top pollster uncovers why GOP caught in a ‘trap of their own making’

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosNovember 16, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The base learned what Republican leaders taughtManufactured fear becomes a governing demandThe party’s dilemma is about priorities, not optics

Sarah Longwell’s diagnosis is blunt: Republican politicians and right-wing media trained part of the GOP base to treat Muslims as a political menace, and those voters now expect the party to keep feeding them the same hostility. The resulting “trap of their own making” is not a messaging mishap. It is the predictable cost of using marginalized people as instruments of political mobilization.

Writing in The Bulwark, the center-right anti-Trump publication she publishes, Longwell said Republican efforts to drive turnout through fear of immigrants and transgender people failed in the midterms described by AlterNet. But the anti-Muslim rhetoric penetrated deeply enough among some voters to create a second problem: Republicans seeking to move away from Donald Trump’s openly racialized politics may find that their own base punishes them for doing so.

The base learned what Republican leaders taught

Longwell’s focus groups found Muslim candidates and public figures becoming objects of suspicion among Republican voters. She cited discussions of Abdul El-Sayed and Vivek Ramaswamy, with Zohran Mamdani also entering the conversation. The particulars varied; the organizing prejudice did not.

That fixation did not emerge independently of institutional power. Longwell pointed to rhetoric from elected officials, including Texas Gov. Greg Abbott, and right-wing media figures. Party leaders supplied the threat narrative, media personalities repeated it, and voters absorbed it. Those voters now demand further confirmation that their fear is justified—what Longwell called part of the Republican “Triangle of Doom.”

This matters because political coverage often describes bigotry as something that simply resides within “the base,” as though voters spontaneously generated the party’s agenda and officeholders merely followed. Longwell’s account describes a feedback loop with a clear starting advantage: politicians and media operators possess the platforms, money and authority to decide which groups will be portrayed as dangerous. Once that machinery has worked, those same leaders can cite audience demand as an excuse to continue.

Manufactured fear becomes a governing demand

After the election, Longwell said, more voters who had supported Trump multiple times expressed alarm over Muslims’ influence on election results. A 75-year-old Texan called it “a little heartbreaking” that people who might not have “America’s best interest” could vote, move into communities and change laws.

The voter also invoked elementary-school books, “Muslim politics” and sharia law. The remarks are revealing not because they establish any of those alleged developments—they do not—but because they show how suspicion can erase ordinary distinctions between religious belief, political participation and disloyalty. Citizenship and voting cease to count as legitimate democratic activity when the voters exercising those rights belong to the targeted group.

Longwell reported similar concerns among conservative respondents in Georgia, North Carolina and Pennsylvania. She attributed the pattern to inflammatory statements by elected leaders, while noting that it had appeared earlier and especially in Texas, where Republican officials have pushed anti-Muslim rhetoric particularly hard.

The party’s dilemma is about priorities, not optics

Longwell argues that Republicans now face two politically costly choices. They can keep indulging Islamophobia and repel voters who want attention paid to material concerns such as the cost of living. Or they can reduce the bigotry and risk angering supporters who have been taught to regard it as an urgent issue.

Calling that a “trap” is accurate so long as responsibility remains attached to the people who built it. Republican leaders chose scapegoating as an organizing strategy. The fact that it may now constrain them does not turn them into its victims. Muslim Americans remain the people burdened with suspicion about their loyalty, their votes and their presence in public life.

Nor did the strategy become defensible because the reported electoral payoff failed to materialize. The attempt to generate panic about immigrants, transgender people and Muslims treated vulnerable groups as raw material for turnout. Its failure merely exposed a recurring weakness in grievance politics: propaganda can shape an audience’s demands more durably than it can deliver an election.

Longwell concluded that Republicans hoping to distance themselves from Trump’s “brazenly racist GOP” may discover that base voters will not permit the recalibration. That is the party’s actual predicament. It spent years presenting prejudice as vigilance, then found that some of its voters believed the lesson.
