Trump performs creepy pantomime of US ally's 'beautiful' president

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 31, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower disguised as grievanceA counterpart reduced to a performanceThe renaming is the substanceProtest is not an explanationThe politics of invented helplessness

What happened

AlterNet reports that Donald Trump used a South Carolina rally to reenact an alleged conversation with Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum about his renaming of the Gulf of Mexico as the “Gulf of America.” Trump portrayed Sheinbaum pleading with him to reverse the change, then said that “everybody’s happy.” The report provides no confirmation from Sheinbaum that this exchange occurred as Trump described it.

During the performance, Trump repeatedly commented on Sheinbaum’s beauty and voice while claiming politicians are no longer permitted to call women beautiful. AlterNet notes that he has made similar remarks before, including about Sheinbaum and Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni.

Trump also misstated the relative ages of Mexico and the United States and understated how long the Gulf of Mexico has carried its established name. The rally was interrupted by two protesters, while a planned Kansas appearance was reportedly canceled; the article cites speculation about protests and polling but does not establish why the cancellation occurred.

Power disguised as grievance

The person exercising power here was not Sheinbaum, the protesters, or some imaginary censor policing compliments. It was the president of the United States, publicly celebrating his government’s decision to impose a new American name on a geographic feature shared with Mexico and Cuba.

Trump nevertheless cast himself as the constrained party. He complained that social rules supposedly prevent him from discussing a woman’s appearance, even as he did exactly that before a rally audience. This is manufactured victimhood: wield institutional authority, then redirect attention toward a trivial restriction that did not restrict him.

A counterpart reduced to a performance

Trump’s pantomime assigned every role in advance. He was decisive; Sheinbaum was pleading. He acted; she reacted. Because AlterNet offers no independent confirmation of the conversation, the reenactment establishes only how Trump chose to portray a neighboring country’s president.

The fixation on Sheinbaum’s appearance further diminished her institutional position. She is Mexico’s head of government, yet Trump presented her as a flattering voice attached to an anxious supplicant. That is not diplomatic analysis. It is a gendered display of hierarchy performed for domestic political consumption.

The renaming is the substance

The article’s “creepy” framing captures the personal conduct but risks making the spectacle seem like the whole story. The consequential act was the assertion of unilateral naming power: the United States changing its official terminology and Trump presenting Mexico’s objection as helpless protest.

His factual errors served that performance. Mexico is not 150 years older than the United States, and AlterNet reports that the Gulf of Mexico has carried its name since 1550. The inaccuracies matter because they strip history down to whatever makes domination sound natural. The message was not that Trump had mastered the past. It was that facts were subordinate to his declaration.

Protest is not an explanation

The story’s closing turn toward disrupted rallies and the canceled Kansas event is less secure. AlterNet reports two protesters in South Carolina and says the Kansas cancellation followed vocal protests at Trump’s previous appearance there. It also cites commentators who suspected polling played a role.

Those facts do not establish why the event was canceled. Treating speculation as an answer would reproduce the same problem visible in Trump’s alleged conversation: a politically useful narrative filling the space where verified evidence should be.

The politics of invented helplessness

The larger pattern is power presenting itself as persecution. Trump described a foreign leader as pleading, an old geographic name as disposable, and himself as endangered by etiquette while he controlled the stage, the story, and the federal naming decision.

That inversion is politically useful. It converts domination into defiance and accountability into censorship. The spectacle invites the audience to focus on whether the performance was strange or offensive. The systemic error is deeper: an officeholder used state power, historical fabrication, and a caricature of an allied president to make unilateralism look like victory—and grievance look like courage.
