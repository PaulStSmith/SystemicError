Legend Bob Woodward levels Trump with a chilling comparison

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 27, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Bob Woodward’s comparison was blunt: President Donald Trump’s reported initiation of war against Iran resembled the conduct of tyrants who designate an enemy and attack without having been attacked themselves. Speaking with CNN’s Kaitlan Collins, Woodward called Trump “the aggressor” and argued that Iran had provided no provocation for the war. His indictment was not that Trump had used impolite rhetoric or broken diplomatic convention. It was that the president wielded the United States’ military power to begin a conflict merely because his administration opposed another government.

“The best thing a president can do is stop wars, prevent wars,” Woodward said. Instead, he argued, Trump had done the reverse: “He actually started a war against Iran.”

The comparison was about aggression, not personality

Woodward did not defend Iran’s government, which he described as an unfavorable regime. He rejected the more consequential sleight of hand: treating hostility toward that regime as sufficient cause for war.

“For the United States to say, ‘well, we’re just starting a war because we don’t like what you’re doing,’” Woodward said, is “exactly what tyrants do.” He described the authoritarian formula plainly: declare another country the enemy, then use that declaration to justify attacking it.

That distinction matters because governments routinely attempt to collapse it. A state can be repressive without having attacked the United States. American officials can oppose its conduct without possessing an unlimited mandate to launch a war. When those separate propositions are fused, the targeted government’s character becomes a substitute for evidence of necessity, and presidential preference becomes a substitute for public justification.

Woodward invoked Russian President Vladimir Putin as a point of comparison while noting Trump’s friendliness toward him. But the sharper charge concerned Trump’s own exercise of power. Woodward was not alleging guilt by association. He was arguing that initiating an unprovoked war is itself the relevant authoritarian conduct, regardless of what its architect calls the enemy.

Congress still controls the money

Woodward predicted that the Iran war would leave a lasting stain on Trump’s record. “I know lots of former presidents, late presidents who would be twirling in their graves” at a commander in chief beginning a war without an attack or provocation, he said.

Presidential legacy, however, is the least immediate consequence. Wars are sustained by institutions, appropriations and legislators willing to keep supplying both. AlterNet reported that Robert Malley, who served as a Biden administration special envoy to Iran, and foreign-policy historian Stephen Wertheim were already urging Democrats to end the war after taking the majority in January. In a New York Times essay, they described that decision as an early test of whether Democrats had understood the electorate and intended to honor its message.

That framing puts responsibility where it belongs. Democrats cannot condemn Trump’s voluntary war as an abuse while treating its continuation as an inherited condition beyond their control. If they possess the votes or leverage to restrict funding, their choice is whether to use institutional power—not whether to issue sufficiently grave statements about the president’s behavior.

Republican support for financing the war was also showing strain, according to AlterNet. Senate Budget Committee Chair Ron Johnson of Wisconsin acknowledged that the GOP’s preferred reconciliation route carried political costs because Democrats were threatening a government shutdown if Republicans proceeded.

Johnson’s complaint is revealing. He described Democratic resistance as a “downside” of reconciliation, shifting attention from the war Republicans sought to finance to the confrontation required to stop them. That is standard Washington misdirection: the exercise of power by those sustaining violence is treated as ordinary governance, while resistance becomes the disruption.

Woodward’s warning therefore reaches beyond one brutal comparison. Trump may have initiated the conflict, as Woodward charged, but Congress determines whether the machinery keeps running. The next consequential judgment will not come from former presidents imagined turning in their graves. It will come from living lawmakers deciding whether an unprovoked war is intolerable—or merely inconvenient to defund.
