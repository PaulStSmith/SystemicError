Warning: A cornered Trump is ready to unleash more havoc

Based on an article by Terry H. Schwadron

Paulo SantosOctober 12, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet argues that Donald Trump’s administration was escalating the use of federal power against perceived enemies as voting began in several states. The article points to reported efforts targeting Federal Reserve governor Lisa Cook and former White House aide Cassidy Hutchinson, sanctions aimed at companies working with the International Criminal Court, and the administration’s defense of an ICE operation in which agents reportedly fired seven rifle rounds and wounded a migrant suspect while a five-year-old was inside the car.

The outlet links those actions to Trump’s assertion that he could “do whatever I want” and portrays the escalation as a response to political weakness before Election Day. It predicts possible interference with voting and certification, but supplies no evidence of an operational plan. Several other serious assertions—including alleged pay-for-access arrangements, Republican candidates’ private assessments and contemplated prosecutions—are presented without enough underlying documentation here to evaluate them independently.

Power is the relevant fact

Trump controls the executive branch. That means access to federal investigators, prosecutors, immigration agents, diplomats and the administrative machinery capable of turning a grievance into an inquiry, sanction or armed encounter.

Cook and Hutchinson do not possess remotely comparable power. Nor do migrants confronted by ICE or international jurists subjected to American sanctions. Treating these episodes as clashes among political adversaries would erase the decisive asymmetry: one participant commands institutions that can impose legal expense, professional damage, physical danger and international isolation.

The central abuse alleged by AlterNet is therefore not that Trump is angry. Presidents are allowed resentments. It is that official authority is reportedly being directed toward the objects of his resentment.

Process can become punishment

An investigation need not produce a conviction to inflict damage. AlterNet reports that a Trump-selected inquiry could revisit mortgage-fraud allegations against Cook after grand juries declined to act, and connects that pressure to her votes against lowering interest rates. The article does not establish that Trump ordered the inquiry because of those votes, so that motive remains its inference. The institutional danger it describes is concrete nonetheless: executive pressure applied to a Federal Reserve official whose decisions displease the president.

The reported pursuit of Hutchinson operates similarly. Legal analysts cited by the outlet reportedly consider a case alleging false congressional testimony weak, but weakness does not eliminate its coercive value. Lawyers cost money. Investigations consume years. Public accusations contaminate reputations. When the state repeatedly reaches for doubtful cases against dissenters, failure in court does not mean the tactic failed.

Misdirection protects the armed institution

The ICE episode shows the crudest version of political misdirection. According to AlterNet, officials answered questions about agents firing seven rounds on a residential street during school pickup by emphasizing the wounded man’s criminal record and attacking New York City’s sanctuary policies.

Neither point answers whether agents’ tactics endangered the child in the vehicle or nearby residents. A suspect’s record does not travel backward through time to justify every method used during an arrest. Blaming municipal immigration policy shifts attention from the actors who carried rifles and chose when to fire them.

That rhetorical substitution matters. It converts scrutiny of state conduct into a referendum on the person subjected to it, ensuring that the institution with guns is treated as background while the weaker actor becomes the entire story.

The case is stronger than the speculation

AlterNet weakens its argument when it leaps from documented or attributed actions to predictions of insurrection, election disruption and presidential psychology. A prediction market is not evidence of a government plan. Falling polls do not prove desperation. Speculation about mental health explains nothing that can be established through decisions and chains of command.

The article does not need those shortcuts. Its strongest evidence concerns observable uses of power: inquiries against disfavored officials and witnesses, sanctions against an international court, pardons for January 6 defendants and an official response that reportedly avoided confronting the danger created by an ICE operation.

Calling every development “havoc” can blur distinctions among them. The sharper indictment asks which official acted, under what authority, against whom, with what procedural safeguards and with what foreseeable cost.

Personalized rule is the pattern

The systemic issue is not volatility but the conversion of public institutions into instruments of personal loyalty. Independent officials become targets, witnesses become prospective defendants, armed agencies evade scrutiny and foreign policy becomes entangled with the president’s resentments.

Institutions do not restrain that pattern merely by existing. Courts can issue rulings, grand juries can reject charges and Congress can hold formal powers, yet executive officials still decide whether to honor those limits or keep searching for another mechanism. The people enabling the outcome are not only the president but every official who converts his grievance into a memorandum, investigation, sanction or public excuse.

That is how personalized government advances: not through confusion, but through identifiable decisions made by people with authority who know that imposing the process may be enough.
