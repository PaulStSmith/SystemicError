JD Vance suspects Antichrist 'among us' as he defends Trump depicting 'himself as God'

When Power Starts Talking Like a Cult

A Vice President in the Fog

JD Vance, the vice president of the United States, sat for an interview and used the platform to drift from end-times prophecy to UFOs to “dark energy” in Silicon Valley. That is the surface fact. The political fact is simpler: a top officeholder is helping launder a president’s self-deification into ordinary conservative discourse.

This is not harmless eccentricity. It is institutional speech. And it comes from the second-highest office in the country.

The Meme Was the Point

The source is straightforward: Donald Trump posted an AI-generated image of himself in a white robe and red sash, holding a glowing orb and extending a hand like a holy healer. Religious leaders objected. Some of Trump’s own supporters objected. Trump then offered a weak revisionist explanation, claiming he thought it depicted him as a doctor and linked it to Red Cross imagery.

Vance did what loyal power brokers do when the evidence is embarrassing: he denied the obvious and substituted motive theater for reality. He said Trump did not mean to mock or mimic God. That is not an analysis. It is cover.

Responsibility Gets Laundered

The article tries to present this as a weird conversation about spirituality. That framing is too soft. The issue is not whether Vance privately believes in demons or angels. The issue is that he is using supernatural language to smother accountability.

A president posts an image of himself as a sacred figure. The backlash is not treated as a sign of authoritarian vanity, but as confusion to be explained away. The deliberate act gets recast as misunderstanding. That is how power evades consequence: not by denying the event, but by draining it of meaning.

Cosmic Language, Political Function

Vance’s comments about the Antichrist, UFOs, and satanic AI companies are not random thought bubbles. They are political weather. They turn the public conversation away from governance and toward spiritual spectacle, where evidence can be waved off and authority becomes a matter of faith.

That matters because Vance is not some fringe podcaster. He is a sitting vice president with an ideological job to do. He is helping normalize a politics where the leader can present himself as sacred, critics can be brushed aside as confused, and the whole arrangement can be narrated as providence rather than power.

The Real Pattern

This story is not about religion getting mixed up with politics by accident. It is about a ruling coalition that benefits from dissolving material responsibility into mysticism. If Trump can be pictured as a god, if his defenders can say the image was merely misunderstood, if the vice president can float Antichrist talk while refusing to name the actual damage, then the public is supposed to stop asking who made the decision and what it was meant to signal.

That is the pattern: propaganda dressed as faith, cowardice dressed as interpretation, and authority demanding reverence while pretending not to know what its own symbols mean.
