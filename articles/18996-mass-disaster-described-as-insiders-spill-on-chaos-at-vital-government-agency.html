'Mass disaster' described as insiders spill on chaos at vital government agency

Current and former employees at the Centers for Disease Control paint a damning portrait of chaos, dysfunction, and unfulfilled commitments under Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., according to interviews with The New York Times.

Since his February 2025 confirmation, Kennedy has waged a campaign against the CDC itself, labeling it “the most corrupt agency at H.H.S. and maybe the government” while endorsing the mass terminations orchestrated by Elon Musk’s DOGE initiative. At least 2,400 CDC employees — representing 18 percent of the agency’s workforce — have been terminated or forced into retirement.

The toll on remaining staff has been severe. Former Chief Medical Officer Debra Houry captured the emotional impact bluntly: “I’m an E.R. doc, so I handle stress pretty well. But this was like being in a mass disaster nonstop for eight months.”

The damage extends beyond domestic operations. Multiple staffers highlighted the consequences of Kennedy and the Trump administration’s withdrawal from the World Health Organization, a move that has fundamentally compromised America’s ability to coordinate global health responses.

Daniel Jernigan, former director of the National Center for Emerging and Zoonotic Infectious Diseases, explained the strategic failure. “There was no understanding of the connectivities between the U.S. government and W.H.O. There was magical thinking that you could build a bilateral set of relationships that would take care of what W.H.O. is doing. For flu, we had 57 different bilateral cooperative agreements with countries. But to make things happen, we went through W.H.O., because they have the ability to bring people together. Without W.H.O. involved, there’s an uncertainty among some nations about whether or not to work with the United States at all.”

A core problem undermining the CDC’s effectiveness, staffers reported, is Kennedy’s apparent disdain for subject matter experts.

Demetre C. Daskalakis, former director of the National Center for Immunization and Respiratory Diseases, described a pattern of false promises followed by systematic exclusion: “Kennedy’s deputies would come and talk to C.D.C. in this very kumbaya way: ‘We really love you all. We think you’re so amazing. Talk to us, and we’ll make sure that we can make things happen.’ And every time they did that, I would say: ‘We’ve never briefed the secretary. We would love the opportunity to talk to him about vaccine-preventable diseases, about measles, about flu, about Covid.’ And they would say: ‘Well, thank you so much. Just reach out.’ And then when we reached out, we would just get crickets.”

Karen Hacker, former director of the National Center for Chronic Disease Prevention and Health Promotion, emphasized how leadership constraints have made basic operations impossible. “A big part of being a senior leader is going into the field, doing site visits, talking with Congress,” she said. “And to have all of that curtailed leaves you where you literally can’t do your job.”

Trump appointee Jay Bhattacharya, serving as acting CDC director, has drawn criticism for underperformance even as standards have collapsed. One staffer offered this assessment. “Our bar has gotten so low that we are praising him for doing things that should be a bare minimum,” they said. “He has come to campus and talked with staff. He believes in the measles vaccine. And that’s all great. But that’s also part of what any leader should be doing.”

Our Analysis:

The Real Story Behind the Chaos at the CDC

The recent exposé by The New York Times regarding the turmoil within the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) under the stewardship of Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and the broader Trump administration paints a dire picture of an institution in distress. However, to navigate through the storm of accusations and emotional testimonies, it’s crucial to dissect the layers of institutional power, decision-making, and misplaced blame to uncover the root causes and accountability.

Institutional Power and Decision-Making

Robert F. Kennedy Jr., as the Health and Human Services Secretary, undoubtedly wields significant power over the CDC, an agency within his department. His public critiques of the agency and support for drastic workforce reductions under the so-called DOGE initiative spearheaded by Elon Musk indicate a top-down approach to reform, one that bypasses traditional bureaucratic processes and, evidently, the input of subject matter experts.

The Trump administration’s decision to withdraw from the World Health Organization (WHO) further exacerbates the CDC’s challenges, undermining its global health mission. This strategic blunder, rooted in a misunderstanding of international health diplomacy and operational mechanics, lies squarely with the administration’s highest echelons, which includes but is not limited to Kennedy. The decision to withdraw from WHO reflects a broader administration agenda, implicating a collective failure rather than the missteps of a single entity.

The Misdirection of Responsibility

The framing of the article suggests a chaotic environment fostered directly by Kennedy’s disdain for expertise and the administration’s policies. While Kennedy’s leadership style and the broader administrative ethos play central roles, the article inadvertently skirts around the systemic issues plaguing federal health agencies: political interference, chronic underfunding, and a culture that often sidelines scientific discourse for political expediency.

Moreover, Jay Bhattacharya, positioned as the acting CDC director, is criticized for underperformance, yet the narrative fails to acknowledge the inherent limitations of acting in a capacity constrained by higher-up directives and an already demoralized workforce. Bhattacharya’s efforts, albeit minimal, are a symptom of a larger disease - a systemic decay fostered by years of political manipulation and neglect.

The Role of False Promises and Systematic Exclusion

The experiences shared by former CDC directors like Demetre C. Daskalakis and Karen Hacker highlight a disturbing pattern of engagement, or the lack thereof, with the agency’s senior leadership. These testimonies reveal a deliberate strategy to isolate and ignore the agency’s expertise, a tactic that cripples the CDC’s operational capacity. The blame for this systematic exclusion can be attributed to Kennedy’s direct actions (or lack thereof) and, by extension, the administration’s broader disregard for scientific expertise in governance.

Conclusion

The chaos and dysfunction within the CDC are symptomatic of a larger crisis in American public health governance, where political agendas overshadow scientific expertise and operational efficacy. While Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and the Trump administration bear significant responsibility for the current state of affairs, it’s imperative to recognize the complexities of institutional power dynamics and the systemic issues that predate and likely will outlast any single administration.

To move forward, a recalibration of values that places scientific expertise at the heart of public health policy and administration is paramount. Only then can the CDC and similar entities reclaim their stature and efficacy on both the national and international stages. Criticism, while necessary, must be accurate and constructive, targeting the roots of systemic failure rather than its most visible symptoms.
