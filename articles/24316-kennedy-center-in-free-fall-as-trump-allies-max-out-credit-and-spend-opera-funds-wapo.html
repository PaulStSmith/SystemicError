Kennedy Center 'in free fall' as Trump allies max out credit and spend opera funds: WaPo

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Paulo SantosOctober 1, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory, citing The Washington Post’s review of confidential board documents and tax filings, reports that the Kennedy Center’s Trump-appointed leadership exhausted its $21 million bank credit line, depleted much of its debt reserve and withdrew $10 million from the Washington National Opera’s $10.3 million endowment. Arts-management professor Karen Gahl-Mills described the institution as appearing to be “in free fall.”

Ticket revenue fell 15 percent and donations nearly 25 percent in the fiscal year ending September 2025. The center expected ticket income to decline by more than two-thirds this year, while gifts in early 2026 were reportedly down about 40 percent from the previous quarter. It also wrote off $48 million, mostly in pledges it no longer expects supporters to honor.

The Justice Department told an appeals court on Monday that the center could pay its bills only because of $17 million raised by President Donald Trump and that the money was being spent quickly. The Kennedy Center declined the Post’s questions and had not released its audited 2025 financial statements, which normally appear by March.

The takeover has owners

This is not an institution mysteriously losing altitude. Trump took control, installed new leadership and attached his political project to the center’s governance. That leadership then made the consequential choices: doubling the credit line, borrowing all of it, drawing down reserves, taking nearly the opera’s entire endowment and shrinking the fundraising staff from 94 people to 16.

Those decisions belong to people with institutional authority. Patrons who stopped buying tickets or fulfilling pledges affected revenue, but they did not control the balance sheet. They did not authorize borrowing or decide that restricted cultural assets should become emergency operating cash.

A rescue from a crisis of their making

Trump has claimed that the Kennedy Center was losing hundreds of millions of dollars when he took over. Experts consulted by the Post said the previous leadership instead left a complicated but comparatively stable organization.

That contrast matters because the administration’s preferred narrative casts Trump as rescuer while its appointees preside over accelerating financial distress. The $17.5 million raised through the rebranded Trump Kennedy Center Foundation may temporarily cover bills, but unnamed donors and emergency fundraising do not erase the management choices that made such support necessary.

Calling that money a rescue without naming the preceding depletion reverses cause and effect. Political control creates the crisis; politically branded fundraising then becomes evidence of indispensable leadership.

The board’s silence is governance

Gahl-Mills asked where the trustees were. The reported figures make that more than a rhetorical question. A board exists to supervise executives, protect institutional assets and demand credible financial disclosure. When leadership exhausts available credit, consumes reserves and taps an endowment on this scale, absent oversight becomes an enabling condition.

The missing audited statements deepen the problem. Their absence does not by itself establish wrongdoing, but it deprives donors, artists and the public of the ordinary accounting needed to evaluate an institution already showing obvious signs of distress. The center’s refusal to answer the Post compounds that opacity.

Culture as collateral

The transfer of $10 million from the opera’s $10.3 million endowment is the clearest expression of the power structure. Money intended to sustain one artistic institution was treated as a reservoir for leadership’s broader cash emergency. The opera has since severed ties with the Kennedy Center, and the parties dispute who owes whom money.

That is the consequence of politicized institutional control in material form: artistic programs lose autonomy, donor commitments become uncertain, and long-term assets are consumed to cover immediate obligations. The damage is not an abstract quarrel over prestige. It is reduced financial capacity, fractured partnerships and fewer resources available for the work the institution exists to support.

Capture, depletion, rebranding

The larger pattern is familiar. Political leaders seize an institution, discredit its previous stewardship, install loyal management and then present emergency patronage as proof that only the new regime can keep the doors open. Accountability is displaced by branding; structural damage is narrated as inherited failure.

The Kennedy Center’s reported condition is therefore not merely an arts-management story. It is a case study in institutional capture: authority concentrated at the top, oversight rendered ineffectual, reserves converted into operating cash and responsibility redirected toward audiences and donors reacting to choices they did not make. The people with the least control are left to absorb the consequences of decisions made by those with the most.
