SYSTEMIC ERROR / COMMENTARY

The untold origins of Trump's plan to sharply restrict mail-in voting

Based on an article by Jen Fifield, Propublica

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power was in the White HouseSecurity was the pretextInstitutional independence without resistanceChaos had a constituencyThe systemic error

What happened

RawStory, citing ProPublica’s reporting, says the Trump White House considered requiring every mail ballot to travel by certified mail—a system that would have required voters to be home to sign for delivery. The administration abandoned that proposal after fierce Postal Service objections that it could not be executed, then issued a March 2026 order imposing other sweeping restrictions without meaningfully involving the agency expected to carry them out.

The order directed postal workers to match ballots against state voter lists and reject mailings over problems including outdated information or unreadable barcodes. According to a former official, the White House insisted that even one mismatch could stop an entire batch. Postal officials rushed out a 95-page implementation rule on Aug. 21, less than a month before the first ballots were due to be mailed.

The Supreme Court blocked the order on Sept. 14. The reporting says postal governors had avoided confronting the administration because they feared removal, while Postmaster General David Steiner ordered preparations to proceed and left the dispute to the courts. The White House denied improper interference and described the measures as election safeguards.

The power was in the White House

This was not a misunderstanding among election administrators. The president issued the order. A White House team that included special presidential assistant J. Brian Sikma helped develop it. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick participated in an advisory and coordinating role, then received implementation authority despite having no formal control over the Postal Service.

The administration attempted to convert a delivery agency into an eligibility checkpoint. Postal employees ordinarily transport election mail; they do not decide who receives a ballot. The order would have forced them into that decision while making access depend on hastily assembled databases, newly approved envelopes and an untested tracking system.

The mismatch between authority and expertise was the point of failure. Career postal officials knew the operational limits. The White House excluded them from crucial discussions, surprised the agency’s own lawyers with the final order and rejected an implementation proposal intended to keep postal workers from screening ballots.

Security was the pretext

The administration presented its intervention as a response to fraud. But the article supplies no evidence of widespread mail-voting fraud. It instead notes that independent audits found a system used for nearly one-third of ballots in the 2022 and 2024 elections generally worked reliably, despite occasional losses and delays.

Postal officials already had practical remedies for those failures: standardized envelopes, scannable barcodes and recognizable markings. A bipartisan bill incorporating those recommendations stalled in the Senate in 2024. Those measures were designed to help mail move accurately. The White House took pieces of that operational advice and attached punitive rules capable of withholding ballots from eligible voters.

That distinction matters. Improving mail identification is administration. Rejecting an entire mailing because one record fails to match is collective disenfranchisement disguised as quality control. The reporting does not establish how many voters would ultimately have lost access, because the order was blocked. It does establish a mechanism built to make minor data defects consequential on a massive scale.

Institutional independence without resistance

The Postal Service’s bipartisan Board of Governors possessed institutional authority, but fear narrowed its willingness to use it. According to people familiar with the board’s concerns, governors worried that a direct challenge could prompt Trump to try to fire them, particularly after a June 2026 Supreme Court decision expanded presidential removal power over leaders of many nominally independent agencies.

So the board stayed quiet and gambled that judges would intervene. The gamble succeeded this time. That does not make it responsible governance. Postal union president Jonathan Smith called reliance on the courts a dangerous calculation and accused agency leadership of lacking courage and integrity.

Career employees tried to reduce the damage from inside, including by proposing that voter lists be collected without using them to block ballot distribution. Their proposal was rejected as insufficient. This is how institutional submission becomes procedural: leaders preserve their positions, workers quietly blunt the directive, and everyone waits for another institution to accept the political risk.

Chaos had a constituency

Smith argued that the goal was to create chaos and doubt around mail voting. That remains his assessment, not a fact independently established by the reporting. The foreseeable effects, however, are difficult to sanitize: millions of identity checks under the certified-mail proposal; higher costs for local governments; possible rejection of entire ballot batches; and a rushed system that a whistleblower described as untested and error-prone.

The article’s central power story is therefore clearer than its language of “concerns” and “surprise” sometimes allows. The White House chose the policy, excluded relevant expertise and demanded a more aggressive screening regime when postal officials offered a narrower alternative. The weaker actors—voters, local election offices and frontline postal workers—would have absorbed the failures.

Calling that confusion would misdirect responsibility. The uncertainty concerned how the machinery could possibly work. Responsibility for ordering that machinery into existence was not uncertain.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is rule by administrative sabotage: take a functioning public system, invoke an unsupported crisis, impose duties outside the institution’s competence, and treat the resulting disruption as proof that the system was defective all along.

Courts can stop an order after lawsuits are filed. They cannot supply courage to officials who possess authority but decline to exercise it. Nor can judicial intervention erase the lesson delivered to future administrations: nominally independent institutions may bend long before they are formally captured.

The order failed because litigation interrupted it, not because the executive branch respected legal boundaries or postal leadership firmly defended its mandate. A system that depends on last-minute rescue has not neutralized the abuse of power. It has merely survived one attempt.
