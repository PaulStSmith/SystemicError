The 'Trump trap' that ruined conservative media —and is now blinding the President

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 19, 20263 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that conservative commentator Jonah Goldberg identifies the “Trump trap” as a feedback loop in which Donald Trump and pro-Trump media figures tell loyal audiences what they want to hear, then mistake that audience’s enthusiasm for the judgment of the broader public. Goldberg argues that Trump increasingly behaves as an entertainer serving fans rather than as a president evaluating reality beyond his base.

Goldberg says conservative hosts followed their audiences toward MAGA politics to preserve attention and influence. He points to Fox News host Jesse Watters, whose roughly 2.7 million nightly viewers include politically valuable Republican primary voters and small donors but represent only a narrow fraction of the country. Social-media algorithms, Goldberg argues, further encourage politicians and activists to confuse online approval with public consensus.

The audience did not seize the controls

Goldberg correctly describes an information system that rewards affirmation. But his framing lets its most powerful operators sound strangely captive to the crowd.

Viewers did not hire Fox hosts, design social-media algorithms or determine Trump’s political strategy. Media executives chose programming that retained audiences. Hosts chose accommodation over candor. Trump chooses which advisers, outlets and online reactions deserve his attention. Audience incentives constrain those decisions; they do not erase agency.

Trump is not merely performing

Calling Trump an entertainer captures his dependence on applause but understates the institutional stakes. A television host can misread ratings and damage a brand. A president who mistakes loyal spectators for the country can direct executive power according to information selected for emotional usefulness rather than representative value.

The problem is therefore not simply that Trump tells supporters what they want to hear. It is that the presidency gives material force to decisions made inside that distorted feedback system. Popularity within a faction may be enough to dominate a party while remaining a poor measure of national consent.

The respectable-pundit alibi

Goldberg says once-conventional conservative commentators followed their audiences toward populism and MAGA in pursuit of attention. That is less an accidental “trap” than a series of professional choices rewarded by the institutions employing them.

Describing pundits as captured by viewers softens their responsibility for reinforcing the very expectations they later claimed they had to satisfy. Hosts did not merely discover a radicalized market. By repeatedly validating its assumptions, they helped organize it into a dependable political constituency.

Intensity is power, not a majority

Goldberg’s distinction between intensity and quantity is useful but incomplete. A faction does not need majority support to exercise disproportionate power. It needs leverage over primaries, donations, media incentives and politicians’ careers.

Watters’s audience may be tiny compared with the national population, as Goldberg notes, but raw population comparisons obscure why it matters. A concentrated audience that votes in primaries, supplies money and punishes dissent can discipline Republican officials far more effectively than a larger but less organized public.

The system rewards blindness

The larger pattern is not simply that politicians become too online or too fond of cheering crowds. American political and media institutions repeatedly reward narrow constituencies that are intense, profitable and easy to mobilize.

Trump benefits from that machinery, conservative media monetizes it, and party actors accommodate it. Then the resulting distortion is described as a misunderstanding—as though powerful people merely wandered into a hall of mirrors. They built the mirrors, sold tickets and treated the reflection as the country.
