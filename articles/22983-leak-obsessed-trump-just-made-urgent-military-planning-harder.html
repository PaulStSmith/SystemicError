Leak-obsessed Trump just made 'urgent' military planning harder

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Secrecy as Discipline

The decision-maker is obvious

The Pentagon has pulled decades of classified material from a secret system used by defense officials and troops, shrinking access to the Secretary of Defense Orders Book after the Washington Post reported that senior generals and admirals were warning Pete Hegseth and Donald Trump that the war with Iran is unsustainable. That is the factual frame. The political frame is simpler: power did not like internal dissent, and it reached for the machinery of classification to make disagreement harder to sustain.

This is not about leaks

The stated motive is Trump’s obsession with leaks. That is the tell. When a White House treats scrutiny as contamination, it does not fix the leak; it narrows the circle, hides the evidence, and calls the damage security. The SDOB exists so top commanders can see where forces and weapons are and can “non-concur” when orders carry operational risk. Removing it from broader access is not neutral administration. It is an attempt to make dissent more expensive.

The people paying the price are not the people making the mess

The officers who raised objections did exactly what a functioning military chain of command is supposed to allow: they flagged consequences. Gen. Alexus Grynkewich, Gen. Francis Donovan, Adm. Samuel Paparo, Adm. Daryl Caudle, and others signaled that prolonged operations against Iran are draining scarce munitions and straining the fleet. The response from above is to make urgent planning slower and harder. The burden falls on commanders, staffers, and the logistics system. The cost is imposed by the political leadership that chose the war and is now making the institutions less capable of managing it.

Misdirection by classification

Calling this a leak problem is a convenient lie. The source material points to a more serious dynamic: the administration’s own war policy is generating institutional resistance, and the response is to reduce the visibility of that resistance. That is a familiar maneuver. If the facts are bad, restrict access to facts. If the planners object, make planning more cumbersome. If the process contains a brake, remove the brake and pretend the machine is now safer.

The pattern is the point

Trump said the war would take weeks. Seven months later, the military is burning through Patriot interceptors and Tomahawk cruise missiles while enforcing a blockade on Iran. The response is not strategic reconsideration; it is operational concealment. That is how brittle power behaves when it confuses control with competence. It punishes institutions for telling the truth about consequences, then presents the resulting opacity as discipline.

What this reveals

The real story is not a document transfer. It is the political management of a war by a leadership that treats internal warning as disloyalty and secrecy as a substitute for judgment. Democratic institutions depend on structured disagreement. Strip away the access that makes dissent actionable, and what remains is not command. It is blind obedience with better paperwork.
