'Have at it': Canada's response to Trump is not at all what he expected

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosSeptember 8, 20263 min read

Trump’s Trade War Is Really a Test of Whether Bullies Can Force Submission

The Source of the Damage

The reporting here is simple enough: Trump is escalating tariffs and insults against Canada, Canadian officials are answering with counter-tariffs, and Mark Carney is leaning into diversification instead of surrender. That is the context. The real story is not a diplomatic spat. It is a test of whether the United States can impose costs on a smaller ally and still demand obedience while calling it negotiation.

Power Is Not a Mystery

The people with real institutional power are not the Canadians being mocked for their size. It is Trump, backed by Scott Bessent and Howard Lutnick, setting the terms of conflict from the U.S. side. They control the tariff weapon, the rhetoric, and the threat environment. When Bessent sneers that Canada is too small to fight back, he is not describing economics. He is advertising the White House’s mindset: domination first, diplomacy later, if at all.

The Point Is Coercion, Not Commerce

This is not policy in any serious economic sense. It is coercive leverage dressed up as dealmaking. Trump’s floated threats of 100 percent tariffs, and his talk of ending trade with surplus countries, are the language of extortion, not administration. If the White House were pursuing stable trade policy, it would not keep using childish humiliation rituals like “Lake America” and the “51st state” joke. Those insults are not side noise. They are the point. They announce that the U.S. expects deference, not reciprocity.

Canada Is Refusing the Script

The administration seems to have assumed that Canada would absorb the punishment and keep negotiating on demand. That has not happened. Carney has public backing, according to the source, and his government is now targeting American imports, including products from states that reliably support Trump. That is not symbolic by accident. It is a reminder that trade conflict has political geography, and the pain can be directed back toward the coalition that created it.

The important detail is that Canadian officials are not trying to pretend this is a misunderstanding. They are treating it as a deliberate attack and responding accordingly. That clarity matters. It denies Trump the usual refuge of chaos theater, where reckless power blames confusion for what is plainly strategy.

The Framing Problem

The weak frame in stories like this is the temptation to treat the damage as a diplomatic mood swing or a personality clash. It is neither. The decision-makers are named in the article: Trump, Bessent, and Lutnick. They are the ones escalating. They are the ones trying to turn economic power into political submission. If the coverage softens that into “trade tensions,” it launders intent and blurs responsibility.

And the notion that Carney is just using the dispute for “political leverage,” as Lutnick claims, is a familiar diversion. It shifts attention from the initiator of the conflict to the victim of it. That is how coercive governments talk: they provoke, then accuse the target of responding strategically.

What This Pattern Reveals

This is the broader pattern: American power under Trump is less interested in building durable alliances than in proving it can still intimidate people who depend on the United States. That works until it doesn’t. Canada is resisting. Europe is getting angry. Inflation is made worse, not better. The fantasy of automatic submission is colliding with the reality that other governments have domestic legitimacy too.

The lesson is not that Trump is too crude for trade politics. It is that this is what trade politics becomes when an administration treats international relations as a shakedown. The system does not misfire by accident. It reveals itself.
