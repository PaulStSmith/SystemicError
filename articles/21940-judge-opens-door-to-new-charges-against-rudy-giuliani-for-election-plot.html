Judge opens door to new charges against Rudy Giuliani for election plot

Based on an article by David Edwards

Paulo SantosAugust 19, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Arizona Didn’t Clear the Election Plotters. It Refused to Let Them Escape on a Technicality.

This Was Never Innocence

An Arizona judge declined to make the fake-elector case against Kelli Ward, Rudy Giuliani, Mark Meadows, John Eastman, and others permanently disappear. That matters because the defendants are selling a fantasy: that a procedural setback is the same thing as vindication. It is not. The court did not bless their conduct. It said only that the case should not be dismissed with prejudice, leaving open the door to new felony charges.

That is the basic fact pattern here, and the political meaning is sharper than the legal choreography. A group of Republican operatives and Trump-aligned figures were charged with conspiracy, fraud, and forgery for trying to keep Trump in power after he lost Arizona. Joe Biden won the state by more than 10,000 votes. The effort was not confusion. It was an organized attempt to override a lawful result.

Procedure Became the Escape Hatch

The case ran into a prosecutor’s error: the original grand jury was not shown the full text of the 1887 federal law governing presidential certification. That mistake mattered, and it gave the defendants room to maneuver. But mistakes by prosecutors do not erase the underlying conduct, and they certainly do not transform a coordinated election interference scheme into some misunderstood administrative dispute.

This is where the system’s weakness becomes politically useful to bad actors. A narrow legal failure can be inflated into a public-relations victory. The defendants now point to delay, legal costs, and stress as if the burden of being charged is the real scandal. That framing is backwards. The burden exists because they allegedly tried to falsify the outcome of a presidential election. Stress is not exoneration. Expense is not innocence.

Persecution Is the Alibi

Kelli Ward’s complaint that Arizona Attorney General Kris Mayes is “persecuting” political opponents is not an argument. It is an alibi. It recasts accountability as partisan retaliation and tries to move the discussion away from the conduct itself. That is the standard defense of people who want the public to forget what they did: redefine consequences as oppression.

Mayes, for her part, moved to dismiss without prejudice so the state could re-present the case to a new grand jury on a reasonable timeline. That is not overreach. It is the state trying to preserve the ability to prosecute after a legal defect. The real overreach in this story was the original plot to submit false documents to Congress claiming Trump had won Arizona. The defendants and their allies are trying to invert that reality.

The Network Behind the Plot

The named defendants are not random foot soldiers. They are a political network: a former state party chair, a former mayor, a former White House chief of staff, a Trump legal strategist. That matters because the story is not about isolated zealots. It is about institutional actors using their status to lend authority to a fraud.

The Republicans now campaigning for Arizona attorney general who promised to drop the case if elected are part of the same pattern. They are not promising neutral justice. They are promising impunity in advance. That is the actual partisan divide in the story: one side is trying, however imperfectly, to enforce the law against an election subversion scheme; the other is advertising that it will bury the case.

Delay Is the Strategy

The AP notes that similar fake-elector prosecutions in Michigan and Georgia have already been dropped, and that the federal case against Trump himself was dropped in late 2024. That is how these operations survive: not by winning on the merits, but by grinding the system until the public moves on and the institutions blink.

This is the deeper pattern. Authoritarian tactics do not require a clean legal victory. They need enough procedural noise, enough elite protection, and enough political cowardice to convert attempted coup behavior into just another unresolved controversy. The Arizona case shows both the danger and the limitation of the moment: the courts can still refuse the clean escape hatch, but the political system keeps creating new ones.

What This Means

The real question is not whether the defendants are inconvenienced. It is whether people with money, media access, and partisan protection can treat election fraud as a temporary setback on the road to absolution. Arizona’s judge said no to permanent dismissal. Good. But the larger system remains polluted by the same message: if you are powerful enough, a failed attempt to subvert an election becomes a legal delay, then a talking point, then a campaign promise to make it all disappear.
