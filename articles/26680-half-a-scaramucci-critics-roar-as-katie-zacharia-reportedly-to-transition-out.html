'Half a Scaramucci': Critics roar as Katie Zacharia reportedly to 'transition out'

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 17, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Katie Zacharia was reportedly pushed out of the White House press secretary role after only several days—roughly “half a Scaramucci,” in the joke adopted by critics. AlterNet cites Wall Street Journal reporter Meredith McGraw and an unnamed White House official as reporting that Zacharia was “transitioning out.” President Donald Trump told reporters he had encouraged her to reconsider the job and suggested she might remain in the administration in another role.

According to AlterNet, Zacharia’s first briefing lasted under 15 minutes, included mostly questions about her, and favored far-right outlets. She also struggled to answer where she had passed the bar after previously describing herself as a corporate lawyer. Subsequent Fox News appearances did not settle questions about her qualifications or performance; Zacharia instead accused “legacy media” of using her to distract from Trump’s claimed achievements.

The decision belonged to Trump

Zacharia may have performed badly, but she did not appoint herself. Trump controlled the position, publicly described counseling her about whether she wanted it, and announced the possibility of moving her somewhere “more pleasant.” The institutional power was his: to select a spokesperson, define the standard, remove her, and potentially preserve her place in government despite an unsuccessful debut.

That distinction matters because personnel fiascos are often narrated as personal humiliation. The subordinate becomes the spectacle while the principal escapes scrutiny for choosing that subordinate. Zacharia had agency over her answers and conduct. Trump had agency over whether she represented the White House at all.

Incompetence was not the only problem

A press secretary’s job is not merely to survive hostile questions. The office controls access, conveys official claims, and helps decide which questions receive answers. AlterNet reports that Zacharia called only on far-right outlets during her brief debut. If accurate, that choice was an exercise of government communications power—not just stage fright.

Her response to criticism followed the same pattern. Rather than clarify her professional background or correct specific failures, she cast scrutiny itself as a media distraction from Trump’s agenda. That is a familiar propaganda maneuver: make accountability the offense and treat unanswered questions as evidence of persecution.

The ridicule obscures the institution

The Scaramucci and lettuce comparisons efficiently describe an absurdly short tenure. They also reduce a White House staffing failure to internet comedy. Jokes about Zacharia’s résumé may be deserved as commentary, but they do not explain why the administration placed her behind the government’s lectern or why Trump reportedly considered retaining her elsewhere.

AlterNet leans heavily on mockery from critics and influencers. That framing directs attention toward one employee’s embarrassment instead of the administration’s selection process, standards, and use of public office. The consequential question is not whether Zacharia could withstand another briefing. It is whether competence and candor were ever decisive qualifications.

Accountability flows downward

Trump’s reported remarks presented the episode as an overwhelmed employee discovering that his political world is unusually difficult. That interpretation turns an appointment failure into weather: unfortunate, impersonal, nobody’s responsibility. But the White House is not a workplace Trump merely inhabits. It is an executive institution he commands.

Keeping a failed appointee “in some form” would reinforce the same structure. Loyalty can remain employable even when performance collapses in public. The person at the bottom absorbs the ridicule; the person with appointment power retains discretion without accepting the cost.

The churn serves power

This episode reveals a broader pattern in personalized government: officials become disposable faces while authority stays concentrated above them. Rapid turnover can look like disorder, but it also prevents responsibility from settling on the official who repeatedly chooses the personnel and sets the terms of their service.

Zacharia’s reported exit is therefore more than a punch line about longevity. It shows how spectacle can misdirect accountability. The spokesperson fails, critics laugh, and the administration changes the cast. The system that produced the failure remains intact—and the person with the power to reproduce it remains in control.
