Dems stunned as John Fetterman shows signs of flipping again

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosAugust 19, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Fetterman Is Not Bridging a Divide. He Is Laundering One.

The Setup

The reporting here is straightforward enough: Sen. John Fetterman, already happy to collect GOP money while attacking fellow Democrats, is now saying he will not campaign against Republican Sen. Dave McCormick. The two men have appeared together, co-sponsored bills, and launched a joint fundraising committee, Common Ground PA, built to attract donors who want to bankroll both sides of a supposedly divided Senate delegation.

That is the surface story. The political meaning is sharper and less flattering.

Who Actually Holds Power

Fetterman is not some independent civic mediator floating above partisan conflict. He is a Democratic senator using the authority, visibility, and ballot access of the Democratic Party while signaling that he has no interest in using that power against the Republican who shares his state’s other Senate seat.

McCormick, meanwhile, gains something too: legitimacy. A sitting Democrat describing him as a “great guy” and refusing to oppose him does not neutralize partisan power. It normalizes it.

This is how institutional power works in modern politics: not just through formal votes, but through who is willing to defend whom, raise money for whom, and strip the opposition of any appetite to fight.

The Branding Scam

“Common Ground” is a useful label because it makes surrender sound like statesmanship.

The committee structure matters. This is not an accidental friendship between two lawmakers. It is a joint fundraising vehicle with named participants and donor pathways. That means the collaboration is not just rhetorical; it is organized, financial, and deliberate. The message to donors is clear: you can support a Democrat and a Republican at once, and nobody involved will make life difficult for the other.

That is not democracy in action. It is donor-friendly conflict management. It asks voters to admire the handshake while ignoring the policy reality that made the handshake profitable.

The Blame Shift

The weakest part of the public framing is the suggestion that this is simply a quirky example of cross-party comity in a split state. That framing launders agency.

Fetterman is choosing to do this. McCormick is benefiting from it. The donors are choosing to fund it. And the press coverage, by dwelling on the novelty of the pairing, risks treating a political decision as if it were a weather event.

There is nothing confusing here. A Democratic senator is helping a Republican senator become less politically accountable by refusing to oppose him. If the point is that both men are popular with the same donor class, then say that plainly. If the point is that Fetterman is increasingly less interested in acting like a Democrat, say that too.

What This Reveals

This is what institutional drift looks like when it wears a friendly face. Not the spectacular betrayal that announces itself with grand speeches, but the slow conversion of opposition into brand management.

Fetterman has already been rewarded for antagonizing Democrats while courting Republicans. That reward structure tells you more than any interview does. It tells you that parts of the political class value personal insulation and media novelty more than party discipline, governing coalition, or basic clarity about who is enabling whom.

The result is a familiar one: Republican power gets softened, Democratic accountability gets blurred, and the people with the least leverage are told to admire the “bipartisan” optics.

The Larger Pattern

The deeper pattern is not about one senator’s eccentricity. It is about a political culture that keeps mistaking elite détente for public good.

When elected Democrats treat collaboration with Republicans as an end in itself, they do the opposition’s work for free. They make Republican power look respectable, make donor networks look civic-minded, and make real conflict over power sound rude.

That is the actual scandal here: not that two senators from Pennsylvania can stand in the same room, but that one of them is using the Democratic label to make Republican power easier to live with.
