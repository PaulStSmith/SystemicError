The Veil, the Collar, and the Cage: On Voluntary Submission and the Illusion of Choice

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 12, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Last Saturday, I attended a dinner hosted by my father and his wife’s church. While I myself am an atheist and have not explicitly come out to them as such, I went out of courtesy, a social concession in the face of familial ties. The dinner was organized to commemorate 30 years of a local Christian AM radio station, a broadcasting relic sustained either by the parish or in support of it—I wasn’t sure, nor did I care to dig. It was the kind of event that peddles saccharine spirituality in disposable platitudes.

Live gospel music underscored the night, swelling with the kind of emotional manipulation that faith systems have perfected over millennia. I tuned most of it out as background noise—a blur of halos and harmonies—until one particular refrain snagged my attention and wouldn’t let go:

"What am I without Jesus? Nothing, nothing, nothing, nothing."

That wasn’t just worship. That was programming. It was self-erasure performed under the guise of piety. Repetition, that trusted hammer of indoctrination, wasn’t a creative choice—it was a feature of the machinery. This wasn’t just about believing in Jesus; it was about negating the self outside of him. Not faith. Dependency. Not transcendence. Dissolution. The religious mind doesn’t simply believe; it conditions itself to need belief, to tether identity to divinity so tightly that the very idea of autonomy feels like a fall from grace.

That lyric set me on a darker thought spiral. Was this psychological submission unique to Christianity, or was it part of a broader pattern? What of Muslim women who say they choose the veil, the niqab, or even the full burqa? What of those who defend the hijab as liberating while living in societies where removing it can mean shame, expulsion, or worse? And then another connection emerged: what about women in BDSM relationships who actively desire to be dominated, degraded, controlled, or punished?

What ties these forms of submission together? Is it empowerment? Is it autonomy? Or is it a more sophisticated form of internalized oppression wearing the costume of consent?

The Gospel of Erasure

The line that birthed this inquiry carries theological and psychological weight.

"What am I without Jesus? Nothing."

Not a child of God. Not a sinner in need of redemption. Just nothing. That’s not an admission of faith; that’s a surrender of selfhood. And let’s be clear—this brand of spiritual masochism is not the exclusive province of Christianity. Islam, particularly in its more conservative and patriarchal interpretations, operates on the same mechanics. Five daily prayers. Gender segregation. Modesty mandates. Entire systems designed to reinforce submission as a virtue, especially for women.

When a woman says, “I choose the niqab,” our liberal instinct is to applaud her agency. But we must interrogate what “choice” means when the cost of refusal is familial rejection, spiritual guilt, community exile, or the looming specter of hellfire. If the spectrum of choice is bounded by social penalties and divine threats, is it really a choice? Or just a velvet-lined prison?

Yes, some Muslim women genuinely embrace the veil as resistance to Western beauty norms or capitalist objectification. That nuance exists. But that doesn't absolve us from asking who built the frame within which that choice was made. If a woman internalizes the idea that modesty is synonymous with virtue, is her decision to cover empowering—or just another echo in the chamber of patriarchy?

The Masquerade of Consent: BDSM and the Fetishization of Subjugation

Let’s now wade into even more volatile territory: consensual BDSM. Often praised as the pinnacle of sex-positive empowerment, the BDSM world wraps submission in safety protocols: safe words, boundaries, aftercare, negotiation. In theory, it’s the most responsible playground of adult sexuality. In practice, it often hides the same old archetypes: master and servant, punisher and penitent, man as active agent, woman as willing object.

When a woman says, “I want to be used,” we need to ask where that desire came from. Is it a reclamation of control? A cathartic rewriting of trauma? Or is it a recitation of scripts learned from a world that equates female value with servitude?

There is a vast chasm between playing submissive and believing you are only lovable when you are submissive. The former can be theatrical. The latter is a wound, often hidden beneath layers of rationalization.

Acronyms like SSC (Safe, Sane, Consensual) and RACK (Risk Aware Consensual Kink) give BDSM a veneer of clinical legitimacy. But consent is not a moral shield; it’s a minimum threshold. One can consent to self-destruction if properly conditioned. One can choose the collar while still being shackled by trauma. This is not to say all submissives are victims—far from it. But the romanticization of domination needs to be tempered with the brutal honesty of why these dynamics appeal.

Was she taught to obey? To please? To accept pain as part of affection? Was she praised for being quiet, polite, self-sacrificing? Because if so, she didn’t choose the collar—she inherited it.

The Mirage of Autonomy

We fetishize choice in liberal democracies. Choice is holy. As long as someone chooses something, we must accept it, celebrate it, defend it. But this worship of choice ignores the upstream forces that shape it.

What if the menu was curated by patriarchy, trauma, and religious programming? What if all the available options are variations on submission—and the only real freedom is not choosing from that menu at all?

From the veil to the gospel to the dungeon, submission is sold as virtue. Sometimes it is. Sometimes it is healing, playful, honest. But far too often, it's the expected tax women must pay to be seen as good, pure, or desirable.

What we must scrutinize is not just the individual choices but the systems that teach women that obedience equals worth. Because when every story ends with the woman kneeling—before God, husband, or Dom—then we are not witnessing freedom. We are witnessing conditioning.

Truth Beyond the Chains

We must cultivate the discipline of doubt. Not to shame those who wear veils, sing hymns, or wear collars, but to ask who benefits when women “choose” submission. Freedom is not simply about permission. It’s about genuine alternatives. It’s about being able to say no without punishment. To disobey without exile. To remove the veil, silence the hymn, unbuckle the collar—and still be whole, still be loved, still belong.

Until women everywhere can say no without consequence, speak loudly without being dismissed, and love without being lesser—we cannot claim progress.

Submission, at its most beautiful, is a gift freely given, not a ransom for dignity. And until that distinction is universal, we should remain relentlessly skeptical of any system that praises obedience before understanding the cost at which it was bought.
