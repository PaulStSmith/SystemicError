'Big Short' legend names the one thing that could trigger the next meltdown

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 24, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The boom rewards spending before sustainabilityTwo companies support a much larger chainWall Street has made concentration look like diversificationThe midterm explanation is missing

Steve Eisman says the next financial rupture could begin with the failure of OpenAI or Anthropic. His argument is not merely that investors have overvalued artificial intelligence, but that a small group of technology companies has built a chain of spending commitments so concentrated that one broken link could halt chip orders, data-center construction and related investment—then push the United States into recession.

That is a warning about institutional choices, not technological fate. Executives, financiers and dominant cloud providers have tied an extraordinary share of corporate spending and market value to two AI developers whose long-term economics Eisman doubts. The risk, in his telling, comes from concentrated power and mutually dependent balance sheets masquerading as broad-based growth.

The boom rewards spending before sustainability

Speaking with Steven Bartlett on The Diary of a CEO, Eisman compared the AI boom with the dot-com bubble. The internet did create vast fortunes and durable infrastructure, he noted, but investors first poured money into young companies with little revenue. Many collapsed. Later companies such as Google inherited infrastructure financed during the wreckage and built successful businesses atop it.

AI may be following that pattern: a consequential technology surrounded by an investment mania. That distinction matters. A bubble does not require the underlying invention to be useless. It requires capital to outrun viable demand.

Eisman described an early phase of “token maxing,” when corporate leaders pushed engineers to use AI services without regard to cost. “I don’t care what the costs are. Just use it,” he said, characterizing the instructions some chief executives gave their employees. According to Eisman, Uber exhausted its AI budget in three months, while some companies spent as much as $500 million before appreciating the scale of their usage.

Those expenses could initially be recorded as evidence of surging demand. But compelled experimentation is not the same thing as stable consumption. When executives order indiscriminate use, providers receive revenue while customers are still discovering whether the product justifies its cost. Eisman said that token maxing stopped during the summer and that cheaper Chinese models began taking market share.

This is the boom’s central misdirection: spending generated by executive mandates is presented as market validation, while the durability of that spending remains unsettled.

Two companies support a much larger chain

At the top sits Nvidia, which Eisman described as the world’s largest company by market capitalization. He said its revenue grew by more than 100 percent in its most recent quarter—a measure of the scale of AI infrastructure spending, but not proof that the pace can continue.

The customer base is heavily concentrated. According to Eisman, five customers provide 70 percent of Nvidia’s revenue. They include Google, Amazon and Microsoft, along with Meta and Oracle. Those corporations have diversified businesses and can absorb substantial investments. But Eisman argued that their AI revenue is itself unusually dependent on OpenAI and Anthropic: roughly 70 percent of the AI revenue generated by Microsoft, Amazon and Google comes from those two developers, he said.

That creates a circular structure. OpenAI and Anthropic pay cloud companies; cloud companies order chips and equipment to fulfill commitments associated with those AI developers; chip sales and data-center construction then bolster the market’s AI growth story. Each layer can appear strong while much of the underlying demand traces back to the same narrow source.

“If either one of these two were to die, the entire chain would stop,” Eisman said. He predicted an immediate recession in that scenario as companies ceased ordering chips and related equipment.

That remains Eisman’s forecast, not an established outcome. But the concentration he describes exposes the weakness beneath claims that AI growth is distributed across the economy. Multiple corporate names do not necessarily mean multiple independent sources of demand.

Wall Street has made concentration look like diversification

The exposure extends far beyond companies branded explicitly as AI businesses. Eisman said technology accounts for 40 percent of the S&P 500. Including communications companies such as Google, Meta and Amazon raises the figure to 50 percent. Adding utilities, investment banks financing the buildout and industrial companies supplying power brings the AI-linked share to 65 percent, by his calculation.

Healthcare and consumer staples are the major sectors he identified as remaining outside the trade.

That composition turns a wager on a developing technology into a marketwide dependency. Investors buying a broad index may believe they are spreading risk across the economy while acquiring extensive exposure to the same capital-spending cycle through chipmakers, cloud providers, utilities, banks and industrial suppliers. Diversification on paper can conceal concentration in practice.

Eisman knows the architecture of such concealment. Recalling the subprime mortgage collapse, which he famously bet against, he said Wall Street had built an industry rewarded for loan volume rather than loan quality. Firms continued originating mortgages because their incentives paid them to keep the machinery running. He said his bet against that system earned about $1 billion.

The AI structure is not identical. Eisman said the dot-com crash primarily harmed investors and technology companies while consumers remained comparatively insulated. His present concern is that AI investment has become too large and too entangled with the broader economy for another collapse to stay contained.

The midterm explanation is missing

Eisman said he had considered betting that the AI bubble would burst, but that the 2026 midterm elections changed his thinking. AlterNet’s account does not explain why the elections altered the wager. The headline’s promised trigger is therefore clearer than the reported political reason for declining—or reconsidering—the bet.

What the interview does establish is Eisman’s preferred failure scenario: one of the two dominant AI developers falters, infrastructure orders freeze, the economy contracts and growth eventually resumes on a sounder footing. He called that the more likely course.

The political calendar may influence when investors expect officials, regulators or corporate leaders to tolerate a correction, but the supplied reporting does not provide Eisman’s reasoning. Inventing one would only replace the market’s speculative story with a political one.

The concrete warning is severe enough without embellishment. Corporate executives and financial institutions have concentrated enormous commitments around OpenAI and Anthropic, while the resulting spending supports much of the valuation attributed to the wider AI economy. If Eisman is right, the next meltdown will not be caused by ordinary users losing interest in a novelty. It will follow from powerful institutions building too much of the economy around two companies—and calling that concentration growth.
