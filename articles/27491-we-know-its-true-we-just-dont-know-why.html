We Know It’s True. We Just Don’t Know Why.

How artificial intelligence is separating discovery, verification, and understanding—and why mathematics is confronting a problem software engineering has been living with for decades.

I recently came across a cartoon shared by Erik Meijer on LinkedIn, illustrating the apparent difference between mathematicians and computer scientists when confronted with artificial intelligence.

On one side, a mathematician stands beside a mountain of paper representing 17 million lines of Lean code proving the Riemann Hypothesis.

“We can never understand that as humans. Ban AI!”

On the other side, computer scientists stand beside an even larger mountain representing 51 million lines of C++ code in Chrome.

“We can never understand that as humans. Bring on the AI!”

It is an amusing observation, but beneath the humor lies a fundamental philosophical question about the nature of human knowledge.

Does humanity need to understand something to know it?

Until recently, that question might have sounded like an exercise in academic philosophy. Artificial intelligence is rapidly turning it into a practical problem.

Seventeen Million Lines of Mathematics

On November 20, 2026, mathematician Levent Alpöge announced a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, developed in collaboration with Anthropic’s Claude.

For those unfamiliar with the Riemann Hypothesis, it is one of the most significant mathematical problems in history. First formulated in 1859, it concerns the distribution of the zeros of the Riemann zeta function and has profound implications for our understanding of prime numbers.

It has resisted mathematicians for 167 years.

The proposed proof was released not as a conventional mathematical paper, but as approximately 17.4 million lines of Lean, a formal mathematical proof language and verification system, distributed across 175,187 files.

Independent verification efforts have since reported successful checks of the formal proof.

Formal verification, of course, is distinct from the Clay Mathematics Institute’s process for officially recognizing a solution to a Millennium Prize Problem. That process remains separate.

But consider the extraordinary situation we find ourselves in.

We have a mathematical proof that independent verification systems have accepted, yet whose original implementation is far beyond what any individual human could reasonably read and comprehend in its entirety.

The formal mathematics has passed its verification.

Human understanding is still catching up.

Mathematics Has Entered the Refactoring Era

What happened next should amuse anyone who has spent a significant portion of their professional life developing software.

Mathematicians started refactoring the proof.

Within days, researchers working with AI had produced substantially smaller versions of the original formalization. Human-readable mathematical expositions also began appearing, attempting to extract the underlying reasoning from the enormous collection of machine-generated formal statements.

Any experienced software developer recognizes this process immediately.

Make it work. Establish that it works. Simplify it. Identify unnecessary complexity. Extract reusable components. Make it comprehensible to the next person who has to deal with it.

The difference is that, instead of refactoring an enterprise resource planning system, mathematicians are refactoring a proposed solution to one of the most important mathematical problems of the last two centuries.

And the original result does not become less valid simply because the subsequent implementation is more elegant.

Elegance is desirable. Correctness is indispensable.

They are not synonymous.

The Tyranny of Human Comprehension

For much of human history, our ability to discover things was closely tied to our ability to understand them.

A mathematician had to construct a proof. An engineer had to design a machine. A programmer had to write an algorithm.

The act of creation produced understanding as a natural consequence.

Artificial intelligence is disrupting that relationship.

A system can now generate a solution that exceeds the cognitive capacity of the person requesting it, even when that person possesses the expertise necessary to define the problem and evaluate the result.

This is uncomfortable because we have traditionally conflated three distinct intellectual activities:

  • Discovery: Finding a solution to a problem.

  • Verification: Establishing that the solution is correct.

  • Comprehension: Understanding how and why the solution works.

Historically, these activities were deeply intertwined because humans performed all three.

There is, however, no fundamental law of mathematics, physics, or epistemology requiring them to occur in a particular order—or to be performed by the same intelligence.

AI is making that separation increasingly consequential.

We can discover something, establish its validity, and only afterward begin the process of understanding it.

That represents a significant change in how humanity acquires knowledge.

Software Developers Have Been Here Before

As a software developer, I find the controversy particularly fascinating because our profession abandoned the expectation of complete human comprehension decades ago.

Consider what happens when I write a relatively simple C# application.

I rely on the .NET runtime, the operating system, database engines, networking libraries, cryptographic implementations, device drivers, compilers, firmware, and processor microcode.

I have not personally inspected every line of those implementations.

Neither has anyone else.

Yet nobody seriously argues that I am incapable of developing reliable software because I cannot explain every transistor-level operation occurring between my application issuing a database query and the database returning its results.

The entire history of software engineering is a history of progressively surrendering the need to understand implementation details in exchange for higher levels of abstraction.

We moved from machine code to assembly, from assembly to higher-level languages, from procedural programming to frameworks, and from manually implementing common functionality to consuming libraries.

At every stage, we surrendered some degree of intimate knowledge while gaining productivity and expressive power.

AI-assisted development is another step along that trajectory, albeit with a significant difference: generated implementations are not necessarily as predictable or as thoroughly validated as established libraries.

That difference deserves engineering attention. It does not invalidate the trajectory.

Management Still Wants the Punch Card

As I have argued before, there is a growing contradiction in the software industry.

Management wants developers to use artificial intelligence to increase productivity. Simultaneously, it expects those developers to possess the same intimate knowledge of every line of generated code that they would have possessed had they written it manually.

These expectations become increasingly incompatible as the scale of AI-generated software grows.

Writing code manually provides an intrinsic understanding of its structure. You remember why you made certain decisions, why you introduced a particular abstraction, and why you avoided a seemingly obvious alternative.

That knowledge is acquired through the act of creation.

When AI generates the implementation, acquiring that knowledge becomes a separate activity.

And asking AI to generate thousands of pages of documentation does not magically restore it.

Documentation nobody reads is not institutional knowledge. It is noise with headings.

The solution is not to prohibit AI or demand that developers manually reproduce everything AI generates.

The solution is to recognize that software development increasingly involves managing the relationship between discovery, verification, and comprehension.

We need to develop better mechanisms for establishing correctness, better abstractions for containing complexity, and better tools for understanding systems that exceed our individual cognitive capacities.

Insisting that developers return to an earlier model of intellectual labor is not quality assurance.

It is nostalgia masquerading as professional responsibility.

The Great Irony: Mathematics Is Better Equipped Than Software Engineering

Here is where the cartoon becomes particularly ironic.

Mathematicians actually possess something that most software developers would dearly love to have: a mechanism capable of formally establishing the logical correctness of an enormous artifact.

Lean is not simply running mathematical unit tests.

Its proof-checking kernel verifies that the formal derivation follows from the accepted axioms and inference rules of the system.

Provided the theorem has been correctly formulated and the verification mechanism is sound, its acceptance offers a guarantee that ordinary software testing cannot match.

I can write a million unit tests for a software application.

I can achieve 100 percent code coverage.

I can subject it to automated security analysis, integration testing, and millions of hours of real-world usage.

None of that establishes that the application is free of defects.

Testing can demonstrate the presence of errors. It generally cannot demonstrate their complete absence.

Formal verification can establish specific properties under explicitly defined assumptions. Software engineering can use formal methods, too, but applying them to large, general-purpose applications remains considerably more difficult than ordinary testing.

The mathematician standing beside 17 million lines of Lean code therefore has a significant advantage over the software engineer standing beside 51 million lines of C++.

The mathematician has a much stronger mechanism for establishing that the artifact satisfies its stated purpose.

The software engineer, comparatively speaking, has a collection of tests and varying degrees of confidence.

Perhaps mathematicians should be considerably less afraid of AI than software developers.

This Is Not Entirely Unprecedented

Humanity has encountered earlier versions of this problem.

The computer-assisted proof of the Four Color Theorem in 1976 generated controversy precisely because portions of its verification depended on computational work that humans could not practically reproduce by hand.

Modern experimental science routinely relies on instruments, statistical models, and computational systems whose complete operation no individual researcher understands.

Civilization itself is built upon distributed knowledge.

No individual knows how to construct a modern civilization from scratch.

We collectively know how to produce semiconductors, manufacture pharmaceuticals, generate electricity, construct aircraft, and operate global telecommunications networks.

Individually, we understand fragments.

What makes AI different is the possibility of accelerating this phenomenon beyond the rate at which human beings can absorb and organize the resulting knowledge.

Previously, our collective understanding was distributed among human minds, institutions, and accumulated records.

Now, we are beginning to produce intellectual artifacts whose correctness can potentially be established before their underlying reasoning has been absorbed into human knowledge.

The Emerging Comprehension Debt

Software developers are familiar with the concept of technical debt.

I would suggest that artificial intelligence is introducing another category: comprehension debt.

Comprehension debt is the gap between what we have established to be correct and what we understand sufficiently to explain, modify, generalize, or reproduce.

A formally verified mathematical proof can accumulate comprehension debt.

A functional AI-generated software application can accumulate comprehension debt.

A scientific discovery supported by strong experimental evidence can accumulate comprehension debt.

The existence of that debt does not invalidate the achievement.

But neither should we pretend that the debt does not exist.

Understanding remains essential for identifying incorrect assumptions, adapting solutions to changing circumstances, communicating knowledge, and making further discoveries.

If a mathematical proof contains a genuinely new technique, its value extends beyond the theorem it establishes. Understanding that technique could unlock dozens of other mathematical problems.

Likewise, understanding a software implementation allows us to modify it intelligently rather than blindly regenerating it every time requirements change.

The mistake is treating comprehension debt as evidence that the original achievement should never have occurred.

Debt can be managed. It can be reduced. It can even be an acceptable temporary consequence of progress.

But, like technical debt, comprehension debt becomes dangerous when it accumulates without recognition or management.

A software company that generates millions of lines of code without investing in verification and understanding is not necessarily becoming more productive. It may simply be accumulating liabilities faster than its engineers can identify them.

Similarly, a mathematical community that accumulates formally verified results without extracting their underlying techniques risks establishing an enormous catalog of truths while failing to capitalize on their intellectual significance.

The efforts to simplify and explain the Riemann Hypothesis proof are an excellent illustration of humanity beginning to pay down that debt.

The End of Human Intelligibility as a Prerequisite

There is a certain anthropocentrism underlying the insistence that knowledge must be immediately intelligible to human beings before it can be considered legitimate.

The universe is under no obligation to be comprehensible to the human brain.

Mathematical truths do not become true because we understand them.

Physical phenomena do not wait for humanity to develop an adequate theoretical framework before occurring.

Our cognitive limitations are characteristics of our species, not characteristics of reality.

If artificial intelligence can help us discover mathematical truths that we can independently verify but cannot yet comprehend, then rejecting those discoveries solely because they exceed our immediate understanding would be an extraordinary act of intellectual self-sabotage.

This does not mean abandoning skepticism.

It means redirecting skepticism toward the appropriate questions.

Is the result correct? Are the assumptions valid? Is the verification mechanism trustworthy? Have we actually solved the problem we intended to solve?

Those are legitimate concerns.

Whether the result fits comfortably within the intellectual capacity of a single human being is a different question altogether.

We must not confuse the limitations of the observer with the validity of the observation.

Conclusion: Discovery First, Understanding Later

For centuries, humanity’s capacity to discover was largely constrained by its capacity to reason.

Artificial intelligence is beginning to loosen that constraint.

We may increasingly encounter a world in which discovery happens faster than comprehension, where solutions precede explanations, and where the work of scientists, mathematicians, and engineers involves not only producing new knowledge but also making independently established knowledge accessible to human minds.

That is not the end of human intellectual activity.

It is a transformation of its purpose.

Perhaps the mathematicians in the cartoon should take a lesson from the software developers.

And perhaps software developers should take a lesson from the mathematicians about the importance of formal verification.

Neither profession needs to abandon understanding.

Both need to abandon the assumption that understanding every implementation detail is a prerequisite for progress.

For much of human history, discovery and understanding were inseparable companions.

We are entering an era in which we can increasingly discover, verify, and then understand.

The frontier of human knowledge no longer needs to coincide with the frontier of human comprehension.

And that may be one of the most profound consequences of artificial intelligence.
