Susan Collins 'angrily' blames corruption story bombshell on Senate colleague: report

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

The Scandal Is Not Susan Collins’s Anger

A Serious Allegation, Not a Proven Scheme

ProPublica reported that Martin Kao, a former defense contractor serving an 87-month sentence for money laundering and bank fraud, alleged that Senator Susan Collins’s office helped direct millions of federal dollars to Navatek in exchange for campaign contributions routed through a shell company. Collins and her office deny wrongdoing. She said the Biden Justice Department found no wrongdoing by her, her staff, or her campaign in 2021. ProPublica separately reported that a Justice Department investigation was closed after Donald Trump returned to the White House.

The Power Runs Through the Senate Office

The central issue is not whether Collins appeared angry when questioned. It is whether public appropriations and campaign money intersected improperly around an incumbent senator’s office.

Collins held the institutional power described in the allegation. Kao sought federal money; Collins occupied an office capable of influencing how that money was directed. That imbalance does not prove a quid pro quo. It does establish where scrutiny belongs: on the office controlling access to public resources, not merely on the convicted contractor making the allegation.

Coverage that foregrounds Collins “lashing out” turns a question about government power into a story about temperament. Her mood is politically vivid and substantively secondary.

A Denial Is Not an Explanation

Collins’s strongest defense was specific: she said the Justice Department and FBI investigated, and that the Justice Department found no wrongdoing by her or anyone associated with her office or campaign in 2021. That assertion matters and should not be erased by the report’s “bombshell” packaging.

But it does not resolve every question raised by ProPublica’s reporting. The account says the broader probe was later shuttered after Trump returned to office, while unnamed former officials alleged that the new administration had abandoned meaningful interest in public-corruption cases. The article does not provide enough evidence to establish why this particular investigation ended. Closure is therefore not proof of exoneration, just as investigation is not proof of guilt.

The unresolved political question is narrower: what conduct was examined, what remained unexamined, and who decided the inquiry was finished?

Schumer Is a Convenient Detour

Collins did more than deny the allegations. She blamed the report on Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer, saying it reflected tactics he “always uses.” The supplied reporting offers no evidence that Schumer directed ProPublica’s work.

That makes the accusation a diversion unless Collins can substantiate it. It shifts attention from federal funding, campaign contributions, and prosecutorial decisions toward an old Senate rivalry. The maneuver is familiar because it is useful: replace an evidentiary dispute with a partisan origin story, then invite supporters to judge the messenger’s presumed affiliation instead of the conduct alleged.

Schumer may be Collins’s adversary. That fact does not answer whether her office acted properly.

Oversight Cannot Depend on Institutional Reputation

The reporting presents two competing claims: Kao alleges an illicit exchange, while Collins says investigators cleared her and her associates. Kao’s criminal history bears on his credibility, but it does not automatically disprove his account. Collins’s position and denial deserve the same disciplined treatment: neither office nor indignation is evidence.

The larger failure is a political system that often asks the public to choose between personalities because its institutions do not produce sufficiently transparent conclusions. If prosecutors close a politically sensitive investigation without a public account detailed enough to settle its central questions, every faction gains room to manufacture certainty.

Accountability Dies in the Fog

This story reveals how power protects itself even before wrongdoing is proved or disproved. The allegation becomes “a hit job.” The journalist becomes a proxy for an enemy senator. The investigation’s end is treated as either total vindication or evidence of political suppression, though the available reporting establishes neither conclusion.

That fog benefits officeholders. It converts a concrete question—whether public money and campaign finance were improperly linked—into an argument about partisan motives and personal outrage. The systemic error is not skepticism toward Collins or toward Kao. It is accepting political theater as a substitute for an accountable record of who exercised power, what investigators found, and why the government stopped looking.
