The SAVE Act and the Collapse of Republican Federalism

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 10, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Federalism, Until It Isn’tThe Fiction of the ProblemTiming as SabotageConsistency as HeresyThe Asymmetry We Refuse to Name

There is a moment in every institutional decay when someone accidentally tells the truth. Senator Lisa Murkowski’s opposition to the SAVE Act is one of those moments—not because it is radical, but because it is painfully orthodox. She is repeating, almost verbatim, the Republican Party’s own stated doctrine from just a few years ago. And in doing so, she exposes how hollow that doctrine has become.

The SAVE Act is a sweeping federal election bill backed by Donald Trump and introduced by Senator Mike Lee. Its premise is familiar: require documentary proof of citizenship to register to vote nationwide and mandate voter ID at the polls. Its justification is even more familiar: “election integrity.” What Murkowski did—quietly, methodically, without theatrics—was remind her party that this exact kind of federal overreach was once described by Republicans as unconstitutional, dangerous, and tyrannical.

She is correct. And that correctness is precisely what makes the rest of the party’s position indefensible.

Federalism, Until It Isn’t

In 2021 and 2022, Senate Republicans mounted a unified blockade against Democratic voting‑rights legislation such as the For the People Act and the Freedom to Vote Act. The argument was simple and endlessly repeated: elections are administered by states, not Washington. Federal standards were framed as an assault on constitutional order. Mitch McConnell famously dismissed the entire debate as unnecessary, insisting “there’s nothing broken.”

That position has not evolved. It has been abandoned.

The SAVE Act does exactly what Republicans claimed to oppose: it federalizes voter registration standards and voting procedures. It substitutes local election administration with centralized mandates. It imposes uniform rules on states with radically different geographies, populations, and logistical realities. Alaska, which Murkowski represents, is a particularly stark example—but it is hardly unique.

If federal overreach was the sin in 2021, it cannot become virtue in 2026 merely because Donald Trump demands it. Constitutional principles do not change with polling data. When they appear to, what you are witnessing is not governance but instrumentalism.

The Fiction of the Problem

The SAVE Act purports to solve a problem that does not meaningfully exist. Voting by non‑citizens is already illegal under federal law. Registration requires attestation under penalty of perjury. Documented cases of non‑citizen voting are vanishingly rare—statistically irrelevant in a nation of hundreds of millions of voters.

This is not ignorance. Republican lawmakers are aware of these facts. They have been briefed on them repeatedly. The persistence of the claim, despite overwhelming evidence, tells us something important: the stated rationale is not the real one.

The actual function of the SAVE Act is to create friction. Documentation requirements disproportionately burden poor voters, elderly voters, rural residents, Indigenous communities, and women whose legal names have changed through marriage or divorce. These are not accidental side effects; they are predictable outcomes. The bill does not need to explicitly name its targets. Administrative complexity does the work for it.

Calling this “election integrity” is a semantic trick. Integrity is not enhanced by reducing participation. Trust is not built by erecting barriers and then declaring suspicion when people fail to clear them.

Timing as Sabotage

Murkowski’s most devastating critique is not ideological but operational. Election Day is approaching. States are already deep into preparation: training poll workers, printing materials, finalizing procedures. Introducing sweeping federal mandates at this stage is guaranteed to produce confusion, resource strain, and implementation failures.

In any other context, this would be recognized as reckless. In this one, it is better understood as strategic.

Disruption creates noise. Noise creates doubt. Doubt creates narrative space for claims of illegitimacy. A chaotic election benefits only those already prepared to reject its outcome. The SAVE Act is not designed to stabilize elections; it is designed to destabilize them in a controlled, politically useful way.

This is not a theory. It is an extrapolation from recent history.

Consistency as Heresy

What makes Murkowski’s intervention notable is how lonely it is. The SAVE Act boasts dozens of Republican cosponsors. Trump has personally demanded its passage. Party leadership has offered little resistance. The supposed guardians of federalism have gone silent as their own red lines are crossed.

Murkowski is not proposing a new framework. She is insisting on the old one. That insistence now reads as rebellion tells you everything about how far the party has drifted.

This is the broader pattern of the contemporary Republican Party: principles are not commitments but tools. They are deployed when useful and discarded when inconvenient. Federalism, free markets, free speech, even elections themselves are treated as contingent—valuable only insofar as they produce desired outcomes.

The Asymmetry We Refuse to Name

There is a persistent temptation in American political discourse to describe moments like this as “hypocrisy.” The word is accurate but insufficient. Hypocrisy implies a failure of character. What we are observing is a strategy of power.

The SAVE Act is not an aberration. It is part of a coherent project: constrict the electorate, centralize control, and pre‑emptively delegitimize any result that does not favor the movement advancing it. That project does not require consistency. It requires obedience.

Murkowski’s warning is unlikely to stop the bill. But it does something more important: it strips away the alibi. When the Republican Party claims to defend states’ rights, election integrity, or constitutional order, we now have a live, internal counterexample proving that those claims are conditional at best.

The collapse of Republican federalism did not happen because the Constitution changed. It happened because the party decided it no longer mattered.

And that, more than any single bill, is the real danger to American democracy.
