The Bureaucracy of Belonging: How “Just Show ID” Becomes Democratic Attrition

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 12, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There’s a certain smug simplicity in the phrase: “Why would anyone have a problem showing proper credentials to vote?”

It sounds reasonable. Responsible. Adult.

Until you follow the paper trail of a 76‑year‑old woman who has been an American citizen for 71 years and nearly gave up on flying to see her son because the federal government decided her identity was insufficiently crisp.

This is not a story about fraud. It is not a story about undocumented immigrants attempting to vote. It is not even primarily about elections.

It is a story about friction — deliberate, structured, bureaucratic friction — and how that friction functions as a quiet filter.

The Anatomy of a Paper Gauntlet

To obtain a REAL ID compliant driver’s license, Ismene Munch had to reconstruct her legal existence across decades:

  • A 71‑year‑old certificate of naturalization.

  • Two marriage licenses.

  • A divorce decree.

  • Potentially a death certificate.

  • Proof of address in a world that has aggressively pushed everyone to go paperless.

  • An online DMV appointment secured in a five‑minute digital scramble at dawn.

After assembling this archive of her own life, she was told her naturalization certificate was unacceptable because a number had faded where the paper had once been folded.

A faded number.

This is what “just show ID” looks like in practice.

It is not a single card. It is a documentary chain stretching back through marriage, divorce, widowhood, and bureaucratic record‑keeping practices that predate the internet.

The Gendered Trap

Women are disproportionately vulnerable to this kind of documentation cascade. Marriage still typically triggers surname changes. That means identity continuity must be proven at every pivot:

Birth name → Marriage certificate → Divorce decree → Second marriage → Updated ID.

Each link must exist. Each must be sealed. Each must be legible. Each must match.

If one document is lost, misfiled, faded, or never properly recorded in 1971, the entire chain fractures.

Men, statistically, face this burden far less frequently.

This is how structural bias operates. It doesn’t declare itself. It accumulates in requirements that seem neutral but compound unevenly.

The Citizenship Tax

When Homeland Security rejected her faded naturalization certificate, Ismene attempted to order a replacement.

The cost: $505 online. $555 by mail.

Five hundred dollars to prove a citizenship that has existed for seven decades.

This is not an administrative courtesy fee. This is a barrier.

For retirees on fixed incomes, $505 is rent, groceries, medication. For low‑income workers, it is catastrophic.

And that is before transportation costs, lost work hours, scanning equipment, compliant photographs, and repeated DMV visits are factored in.

If voter ID laws were truly about integrity, every related document required to prove eligibility would be free. Automatically issued. Easily replaced. Mobile.

They are not.

The Shutdown Irony

During her attempt to navigate the Immigration Department, the federal government was partially shut down. Calls went unanswered. Agencies idled.

The same political factions that champion ever‑stricter documentation requirements routinely destabilize the institutions responsible for issuing those documents.

This is not accidental contradiction. It is governance through attrition.

Make compliance mandatory.
Make compliance expensive.
Make compliance slow.
Then cut funding.

And finally ask, with a straight face, why anyone would object.

The Numbers Behind the Myth

Studies consistently show that millions of American citizens lack state‑issued photo identification. Even conservative estimates hover above 2% of the voting‑age population.

Two percent sounds small — until you translate it into raw scale: five to six million people.

They are disproportionately:

  • Elderly citizens.

  • Rural residents.

  • Low‑income Americans.

  • Black citizens in states that have closed DMV offices in majority‑Black counties.

  • Native Americans.

  • Women who changed names multiple times.

  • Naturalized citizens.

If a policy predictably burdens these groups more heavily, neutrality becomes a rhetorical shield rather than a factual description.

Impact defines fairness, not phrasing.

The Illusion of Administrative Neutrality

Defenders of strict voter ID laws often insist that the requirement is minimal: simply prove who you are.

But identity documentation in the United States is not a unified, automatic system. It is fragmented across federal and state silos, each with different standards and tolerances.

Ismene’s passport renewal was approved for $130 by the State Department.
Her naturalization replacement would have cost $505 through Homeland Security.
Her REAL ID attempt was blocked by a DMV clerk because of faded ink.

Same citizen. Same life. Three agencies. Three different burdens.

Consistency is not the priority. Compliance is.

“Carry Your Papers”

The rhetorical shift toward Americans needing to “carry their papers” is not accidental language. It normalizes the idea that citizenship is provisional until proven on demand.

In liberal democracies, citizenship is presumed.
In authoritarian systems, citizenship is conditional.

The distinction lies in who bears the burden of proof.

When the burden becomes expensive, time‑consuming, geographically distant, and document‑heavy, the system stops presuming belonging. It tests it.

And tests, by design, have failure rates.

Exhaustion as Strategy

The most important detail in Ismene’s story is not that she ultimately succeeded by renewing her passport.

It is that she almost gave up.

A persistent, literate, internet‑competent 76‑year‑old nearly abandoned the process because the friction became absurd.

Now imagine someone without broadband.
Without flexible hours.
Without spare funds.
Without decades of document storage.
Without the stamina to navigate automated phone trees and government portals.

“Just show ID” becomes “just endure attrition.”

Attrition is not dramatic. It is quiet. It does not make headlines. It lowers participation at the margins.

And margins decide elections.

If It Were About Integrity

If the objective were truly election integrity rather than filtration, the solution would be straightforward:

  • Automatic issuance of compliant ID at age 18.

  • Automatic updates upon address change.

  • Free replacement at any time.

  • Mobile issuance units in rural and underserved communities.

  • Home visits for elderly or disabled citizens.

  • Integrated federal‑state databases to eliminate documentary chain traps.

  • Elimination of all document fees related to proof of citizenship.

No political faction pushing strict ID laws has simultaneously championed this universal infrastructure.

That absence is not oversight. It is signal.

The Real Question

The public framing asks: Why object to showing credentials?

The more honest question is: Why design a credentialing system that predictably filters specific populations unless filtration is the point?

Democracy depends not merely on legal eligibility but on practical accessibility.

When participation requires reconstructing 50 years of paperwork, paying hundreds of dollars in fees, securing dawn‑released appointments, and surviving agency shutdowns, the system ceases to be neutral.

It becomes selective.

Ismene Munch’s story is not an anomaly. It is a case study in how bureaucratic friction functions as a political instrument.

The danger is not in a single faded document.
The danger is in a structure that turns belonging into an obstacle course and then pretends the obstacles are incidental.

That is not election security.

That is democratic attrition by design.
