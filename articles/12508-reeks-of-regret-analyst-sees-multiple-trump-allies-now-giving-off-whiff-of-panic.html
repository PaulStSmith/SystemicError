'Reeks of regret': Analyst sees multiple Trump allies now giving off 'whiff of panic'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 22, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Regretful Ensemble: Trump's Disenchanted Allies

FILE PHOTO: U.S. President Donald Trump speaks with the media after attending the FIFA Club World Cup final upon his arrival at Joint Base Andrews, Maryland, U.S, July 13, 2025. REUTERS/Kevin Lamarque/File Photo

Some of President Donald Trump's supporters, including possibly his own vice president, are showing signs of regret, according to a New York Times columnist.

A recent poll shows some Trump voters are unhappy with his handling of immigration, his signature issue, and Republican Senators Josh Hawley (R-MO) and Thom Tillis (R-NC) have each expressed disappointment with the president's agenda, with Tillis deciding to retire and Hawley introducing legislation to reverse the cuts to Medicaid that he'd just endorsed in Trump's megabill, wrote Times columnist Frank Bruni.

"It redefines the flip-flop," Bruni wrote. "And reeks of regret."

Conspiracy theorists have also been jolted by Trump's command to drop their questions about the Jeffrey Epstein case, and even Vice President JD Vance seems exhausted by his responsibilities.

"As the tawdry twists in the Epstein tale keep coming, Trump’s most obliged defenders are being driven to exhaustion," Bruni wrote. "Vice President JD Vance responded to The Wall Street Journal’s report that Trump had once written and doodled a special smutty, predators-in-arms birthday greeting to Epstein with a social media post that asked: 'Does anyone honestly believe this sounds like Donald Trump?'"

"Does anyone honestly believe it doesn’t?" Bruni added. "We’re talking Mr. Grab-Them-By-The-You-Know-What here. And Vance is giving off more than a whiff of panic, which is kissing cousins with regret."

It's not uncommon for voters to experience buyer's remorse, but Bruni said Trump's allies should have known better before tying their fortunes to his.

"Theirs was a willed gullibility — they have always known deep down who Trump is," Bruni wrote. "They wagered that they could live with that. They made a Faustian bargain, abetting him so that he didn’t eviscerate them. They just didn’t understand the full price they’d pay."

Hawley is already trying to undo the Medicaid cuts he approved, and Sen. Lisa Murkowski (R-AK) has also called for changes to the One Big Beautiful Bill Act she and her GOP colleagues passed earlier this month.

"Sorry, Senator Murkowski, that patient was dead, and you knew it," Bruni wrote. "Remorse and shame were etched on your face as you sought to explain yourself."

Our Analysis:

The Regretful Ensemble: Trump's Disenchanted Allies

The narrative unfurling within the GOP, as dissected by Frank Bruni in a recent New York Times piece, reads like a cautionary tale of political Faustian bargains gone awry. Bruni’s account of disillusionment among President Donald Trump’s staunchest supporters—and even those who merely aligned with him out of convenience or shared agenda—presents a tableau of regret, flip-flopping, and existential reevaluation that would be comical if it weren’t so deeply tragic for the state of American politics.

The Wavering Base and Disenchanted Senators

First, let’s address the elephant in the room: the apparent erosion of support among Trump's own voter base. This disillusionment, particularly concerning his handling of immigration—a keystone of his presidency—signals a profound disconnect between Trump’s promises and his delivery. It’s a classic tale of political overpromise and underdeliver, yet the irony here is rich considering the almost messianic fervor Trump managed to inspire regarding his immigration policies.

Then we have Senators Josh Hawley and Thom Tillis, emblematic of a GOP grappling with its own identity in the wake of Trump’s divisive leadership. Hawley's legislative backpedaling and Tillis’s retirement announcement are not mere political pivots; they are symptomatic of a deeper malaise within the party—a realization, perhaps too late, of the Pandora's box they’ve opened by supporting Trump’s agenda without foresight or, arguably, moral consideration.

The Epstein Controversy: A Mirror to Moral Bankruptcy

Bruni's highlighting of the Jeffrey Epstein controversy and its ties to Trump offers a glaring reflection of the moral bankruptcy at play. The attempt by Vice President JD Vance to downplay Trump’s smutty remarks about Epstein is portrayed not just as a feeble defense but as an act of desperation. It underscores a pivotal question poised by Bruni: "Does anyone honestly believe it doesn’t [sound like Trump]?" This rhetorical question pierces the veil of denial surrounding Trump's character, a veil that his supporters have clung to despite overwhelming evidence of his predilections and indiscretions.

The Faustian Bargain: A Willed Gullibility

Bruni’s most damning indictment lies in his characterization of the GOP’s alliance with Trump as a Faustian bargain. This metaphor aptly encapsulates the moral and ethical compromise made by Trump’s allies in exchange for political power and protection. Their "willed gullibility," as Bruni puts it, is a testament to the lengths to which political ambition can blind individuals to the corrosive influence of associating with figures like Trump.

The Unraveling of a Party

In their scramble to undo the damage—be it Hawley’s legislative efforts or Murkowski’s public remorse—one cannot help but see a party in the throes of an identity crisis. These actions, however, are akin to trying to resuscitate a patient long gone. The GOP, by hitching its wagon to Trump’s star, has traveled too far down a path of moral compromise and divisive politics to easily find its way back.

Conclusion: The Price of Political Complicity

Bruni’s piece, through its exploration of the regret permeating Trump’s circle, lays bare the consequences of political complicity. It serves as a cautionary tale not just for the GOP but for any political entity that chooses power over principle. The disaffection and regret within Trump’s ranks are not just symptoms of a failed presidency but markers of a deeper systemic malady that afflicts those who forsake ethical considerations for transient political gains.

In sum, the fallout from Trump’s presidency and its impact on his once-loyal supporters underscores a timeless truth: the price of aligning one's fate with a leader of dubious character and ethics is often a profound and public reckoning. The GOP, mired in its own existential quandary, now faces the Herculean task of reclaiming its soul—a task complicated by the shadows of compromise, regret, and the indelible stain of a Faustian bargain.
