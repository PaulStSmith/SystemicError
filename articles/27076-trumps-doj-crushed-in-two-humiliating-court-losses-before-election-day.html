Trump’s DOJ crushed in two humiliating court losses before Election Day

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 4, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power came from WashingtonSuspicion became the standardThe framing obscures the stakesA system built to intimidate

What happened

AlterNet reports that two federal courts halted the Trump administration’s effort to identify alleged noncitizen voters and pressure election officials handling their ballots. On the Monday before Election Day, the Ninth Circuit vacated a panel ruling that had temporarily freed the Justice Department to threaten local officials with prosecution, restoring U.S. District Judge Trina Thompson’s restraining order while the appeal is reheard en banc.

Thompson’s Oct. 30 order barred the Justice Department and Department of Homeland Security from forcing officials to reject, segregate or set aside ballots solely because a voter might be ineligible. The dispute concerns roughly 20,000 California mail voters whom the Justice Department described as noncitizens; state officials contested both the number and the list’s reliability. The order was set to last through Nov. 16, with another hearing scheduled for Nov. 13.

That same Monday, U.S. District Judge Tanya Chutkan stopped the departments from distributing suspected-noncitizen voter lists to states. She found that plaintiffs were likely to succeed in arguing that the policy exceeded federal authority and violated the Privacy Act. According to her opinion, the lists drew on records covering tens of millions of voters in at least 47 states and included Social Security numbers, birth dates and party affiliations. Plaintiffs alleged that the data produced numerous false positives.

The power came from Washington

The central actors were not confused poll workers or mysterious flaws in “the system.” They were the Justice Department and DHS, wielding federal data, prosecutorial authority and the implied force of criminal investigation immediately before an election.

Bill Essayli, the chief federal prosecutor in Los Angeles, warned that officials knowingly processing a noncitizen’s ballot risked “legal jeopardy.” Whatever the administration’s stated law-enforcement rationale, that warning placed local election workers under federal pressure while the underlying eligibility list remained disputed.

The institutional imbalance is stark. A voter wrongly flagged by a massive database has little practical power to correct the government before a ballot is isolated. A local official threatened with prosecution must weigh lawful ballot counting against personal exposure. The federal government created that pressure; weaker actors were forced to absorb its risks.

Suspicion became the standard

The California order addressed ballots challenged solely because a voter may be ineligible. That distinction is the story’s core. Possible ineligibility is not established ineligibility, and a database match is not an adjudication.

The administration’s approach, as described by AlterNet, joined two mechanisms: distribute lists of purported noncitizens, then bear down on the officials deciding how to treat their ballots. False positives were therefore not a minor technical defect. They were a foreseeable route by which eligible voters could be burdened or excluded at the moment when correction was hardest.

Chutkan’s ruling also exposes the scale of the machinery. Files containing sensitive information on tens of millions of voters across at least 47 states were being used to generate lists for state action. The government’s power rested not merely on what those records proved, but on the authority their official appearance could command.

The framing obscures the stakes

Calling the rulings “humiliating” turns an institutional confrontation into a scorekeeping exercise. The important result was not that the Justice Department suffered an embarrassing day. It was that judges temporarily blocked federal agencies from converting contested data into election instructions and prosecutorial pressure.

The administration portrayed its conduct as law enforcement. The courts did not finally resolve the underlying claims in the proceedings described here: the Ninth Circuit ordered rehearing, Thompson’s restraint was temporary, and Chutkan assessed plaintiffs’ likelihood of success. Precision matters. So does recognizing what had already occurred—the federal government had assembled and sought to circulate the lists while voting was underway.

The weaker framing is a familiar one: contested ballots become the source of disorder, election workers become potential wrongdoers, and the agencies issuing threats disappear behind abstract language about “integrity.” That reverses the chain of responsibility.

A system built to intimidate

This episode reveals how election power can operate before any final legal judgment. Officials with access to national databases can assign suspicion at scale. Prosecutors can make that suspicion personally dangerous for administrators. Even when courts intervene, the government has already forced voters and election workers to navigate uncertainty manufactured from above.

The larger pattern is rule by administrative accusation: label first, distribute the label widely, and make weaker institutions prove why they should not obey it. Judicial restraints can interrupt that process, as they did here. But the systemic failure begins earlier, when unproven government classifications acquire enough coercive force to endanger lawful participation before their accuracy has been established.
