Trump biographer on lookout for the 'literal death watch'

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosJune 21, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump’s Weakening Is Not the Point. His Weaponization of Weakness Is.

The Source

A cluster of political insiders is treating Donald Trump’s decline as if it were an imminent event you can schedule on a calendar. Michael Wolff calls it a “deathwatch” and predicts the collapse of Trump’s movement; James Carville says Trump may resign by spring; Peter Wehner says Trump has entered decline. The reporting leans on Trump’s poor poll numbers, his frail appearance at the G7, Republican pushback, and Wolff’s warning that a threatened Trump becomes more dangerous, not less.

Power Has Not Moved

The central fact is not Trump’s age, health, or theatrical deterioration. It is that he still holds the presidency. That means institutional power: the executive branch, the federal megaphone, and the ability to turn private grievance into public force.

The article gets closest to the real danger when it notes that Trump cannot win cleanly and may therefore wage an angry, aggressive campaign against the election itself. That is not confusion. That is a strategy. When a president cannot secure legitimacy through persuasion, he can still try to poison the process that would judge him.

The Body Is Not the Story

The commentary on Trump’s physical decline is easy material for insiders because it gives everyone a comforting illusion: if he looks bad enough, the problem will solve itself. That is the lazy part of the frame. It treats decay as destiny and collapses political power into personal frailty.

But a weakened strongman is not automatically a spent one. He can still damage institutions, weaponize loyalists, and turn every sign of weakness into an excuse for escalation. Wolff is right on that point, but the broader discourse around Trump’s “demise” risks reducing political violence to a medical sidebar.

The Blame Game Is Doing Work

Notice how much of the article’s energy goes into diagnosis, speculation, and personality reading. The burden falls on Trump’s body, Trump’s moods, Trump’s shrinking numbers. That keeps attention on the man while leaving the machinery around him less scrutinized.

The real enabling actors are not hidden. Republican lawmakers are described as pushing back, which means some in his own party can see the damage and still stop short of breaking with him decisively. That is not courage. It is managed discomfort. They want distance from the wreckage without paying the price of confronting the driver.

And the media’s fixation on whether Trump will “walk away” or whether his “end” has begun can blur a harder truth: he does not need to be coherent to be harmful. He only needs enough institutional leverage, loyal followers, and elite hesitation.

The Pattern Is Familiar

This is how authoritarian politics metabolizes weakness. It does not disappear when the leader becomes unstable. It gets louder, messier, and more dependent on spectacle. Collapse becomes a tactic. Decline becomes a cover for escalation. The leader’s deterioration is treated as the main event while the real action is the attempt to delegitimize elections, frighten opponents, and keep allies from defecting.

That is why the article’s most important line is not the prediction of Trump’s downfall. It is the warning that he becomes more dangerous when threatened. That is the operating logic of a movement built less on governing than on grievance, fear, and permanent conflict.

The System Behind the Man

The deeper story is not that Trump may be fading. It is that American institutions keep being forced to absorb his breakdown as though it were a personal drama rather than a political method. That is the misdirection. The system keeps narrating the problem as if it were about one man’s health, when the more serious question is how much damage a declining president can still do with the tools of office, a frightened party, and a political culture trained to mistake volatility for strength.
