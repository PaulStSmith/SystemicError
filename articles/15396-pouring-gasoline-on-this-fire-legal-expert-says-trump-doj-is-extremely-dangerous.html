'Pouring gasoline on this fire': Legal expert says Trump DOJ is 'extremely dangerous'

By Paulo Santos

A group of Democratic attorneys general from 20 different states recently authored an open letter to the legal community regarding President Donald Trump's executive orders targeting specific law firms. Now, one of those attorneys general is directly accusing the administration of undermining the Constitution in its ongoing attacks on the judiciary.

During a Wednesday interview on CNN, Connecticut Attorney General William Tong (D) took the administration to task over its continued defiance of court orders — particularly the ones handed down from judges that halted orders targeting certain law firms from taking effect.

Tong specifically condemned U.S. Attorney General Pam Bondi, who has said that federal agencies can still choose to not contract with law firms that sued the administration over its executive orders and won temporary restraining orders from judges pausing Trump's efforts to claw back security clearances and block their attorneys from federal buildings. Bondi even called on the Supreme Court to intervene and prevent lower court judges from constraining the administration.

"That's extraordinarily dangerous for the attorney general of the United States and a former state attorney general to say ... that the federal government doesn't have to follow court orders," Tong said. "She is pouring fuel, gasoline on this fire that's raging, this Constitutional crisis that's raging right now. We're not on the precipice of a Constitutional crisis. We're in one. And so she's saying, like the vice president has said, like the president has said, we don't have to listen to the courts. And that is dead wrong."

Trump's executive orders have targeted law firms for retribution that have either hired his political enemies, or represented them in court. Many of those firms have cut deals with Trump to contribute nearly $1 billion in pro bono work aimed at advancing the administration's goals. However, other major firms have successfully fought back with litigation, including firms like Janner & Block, WilmerHale, and Perkins Coie.

Tong cautioned other law firms and attorneys against "capitulating" to the administration in the future, arguing that it wouldn't just be harmful to the rule of law and the Constitution, but that it would ultimately jeopardize those law firms' ability to compete with other firms that chose to fight back.

"If i'm at a top law school in this country, and I'm looking at one of these firms that capitulated, why would I go there when I can go to any number of firms that haven't?" Tong said. "So if the defense is: 'He was going to destroy my firm,' well, you probably destroyed it anyway ... This is about checks and balances and being a check on this president. They know that. And they know to the extent they've capitulated that they're weakening it."

Our Analysis:

A Stark Warning from Connecticut: The Constitution Under Fire

In a recent CNN interview, Connecticut Attorney General William Tong (D) didn't mince words when he accused the Trump administration of engaging in activities that not only skirt the edges of authoritarianism but dive headlong into its murky depths. The issue at hand? A series of executive orders by President Trump targeting law firms for the sin of opposition, and the administration's chilling stance on court orders. Let's break down the audacious maneuvers of this administration and the implications of such actions on the very fabric of American democracy.

The Audacity of Defiance

At the heart of this controversy is U.S. Attorney General Pam Bondi's astonishing assertion that federal agencies could simply opt out of contracting with law firms that had the temerity to sue the administration and won temporary restraining orders against Trump's executive orders. These orders, among other things, aimed to strip security clearances and ban attorneys from federal buildings, effectively muzzling the opposition. Bondi's call to the Supreme Court to curb the power of lower court judges is not just a request; it's a glaring red flag signaling the administration's disdain for judicial oversight.

"Pam Bondi is pouring fuel on this fire," Tong declares, highlighting the dangerous precedent set by an administration that believes it can operate above the law. This isn't just about legal contracts; it's a direct assault on the checks and balances that underpin the U.S. Constitution.

Retribution and Capitulation

The executive orders in question are not merely administrative tools but weapons wielded with precision to target law firms that have either represented Trump's political adversaries or directly opposed him in court. The message is clear: align with the administration's goals (to the tune of nearly $1 billion in pro bono work, no less), or face retribution. Yet, as Tong points out, capitulation to these demands doesn't just undermine the rule of law; it threatens the very survival of these firms in an industry where integrity and resilience against pressure are prized commodities.

The Bigger Picture

What's at stake here is not just the future of a few law firms or the careers of ambitious attorneys. This is a test of the nation's commitment to the principles of democracy and the rule of law. When an administration openly defies court orders and seeks to punish its detractors, it challenges the foundational belief in a government of laws, not of men.

Tong's warning should not be seen as hyperbole but as a clarion call to all who value the constitutional framework that has guided the United States for over two centuries. "We're not on the precipice of a Constitutional crisis; we're in one," he states unequivocally. This is not just about resisting an administration's overreach; it's about safeguarding the very principles of justice and accountability that define a democratic society.

In Conclusion

The implications of the Trump administration's actions and the complicity of figures like Pam Bondi are as clear as they are terrifying. It's a straightforward message of power and punishment, aimed not just at law firms but at the judiciary and anyone else who dares to stand in opposition.

The question now is not whether the Constitution is under attack – it clearly is – but what we, as a nation, intend to do about it. William Tong's interview is not just an indictment of the current administration; it's a wake-up call to all Americans about the dire consequences of inaction in the face of authoritarian overreach. The time to speak out and stand firm is now, lest we find ourselves in a country where the rule of law is but a memory.
