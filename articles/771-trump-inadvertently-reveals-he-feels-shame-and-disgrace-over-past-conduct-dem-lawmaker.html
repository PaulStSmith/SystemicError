Trump inadvertently reveals he feels 'shame and disgrace' over past conduct: Dem lawmaker

Impeachment as Inconvenient Record

Power Wants Erasure

Donald Trump is not trying to argue that his impeachments were unjust. He is trying to make them disappear. According to the reporting, he and his allies are pushing Republicans toward a symbolic resolution to “void” both impeachments, one tied to the Ukraine pressure campaign and the other to Jan. 6. That is not defense. It is record management.

The distinction matters. A person who believes he was wronged seeks vindication. A person who seeks expungement is trying to rewrite the ledger after the damage is already done.

The Decision Maker

The institutional power in this story is obvious: Trump remains the central actor, and the Republican lawmakers he is pressuring are the available mechanism. The reported plan only exists because party officials are willing to entertain the fiction that a constitutional injury can be papered over by partisan theater.

Jamie Raskin’s point is the one the story cannot avoid: there is no constitutional cleanup crew for misconduct. The impeachments happened because Trump’s conduct happened. The effort to “void” them does not alter that sequence. It only shows who still has access to the party machinery and who is expected to accommodate him.

The Real Meaning of “Embarrassment”

Raskin’s language about shame and disgrace is useful because it strips away the usual soft-focus nonsense. If Trump feels embarrassment, that does not make him accountable. It suggests the opposite: he is irritated that the historical record still has teeth.

The source frames this as Trump’s feelings accidentally revealed by the effort. That is a generous reading. A more exact one is that he is trying to launder disgrace through Congress. He is not escaping the stigma of impeachment; he is asking Republicans to help him pretend it never happened.

Who Gets Blamed

The misdirection here is familiar. The story can easily be reduced to a parliamentary technicality, as if the problem were whether an expungement resolution is procedurally available. That is the safe, bloodless version. It treats the issue as a rules question instead of a power question.

But the harm is not procedural. The Ukraine episode was a shakedown. Jan. 6 left officers with injuries, including the fractured skull and traumatic brain injuries Raskin referenced. Turning that record into a dispute over symbolic resolution shifts attention away from the man who drove the conduct and onto the institutional helpers now trying to tidy up after him.

The Pattern

This is what authoritarian politics looks like when it has not yet been forced into open dictatorship: not only the abuse itself, but the demand that the abuse be forgotten. The same movement that excused the conduct now wants to erase the evidence. First comes the offense, then the cover story, then the ceremonial reset button.

That is the larger pattern. Institutions that should preserve accountability are pressured into assisting amnesia. Party loyalty is treated as a substitute for constitutional memory. And when power can no longer defend its actions honestly, it reaches for the next best thing: erasure.
