The internet erupted Monday after a new photo of President Donald Trump showed a significant rash on his neck, with new questions surfacing about his health.

Trump spoke for the first time live since the U.S. and Israel launched attacks on Iran and gave a Medal of Honor at the White House to 79-year-old Vietnam veteran Terry P. Richardson, who is credited with saving the lives of 85 soldiers during a 1968 battle.

Independent journalist Aaron Rupar pointed out photos that revealed a large rash on the right side of Trump’s neck at the event.

Dr. Sean Barbabella, physician to the president, told CBS News that the president was using a “very common cream on the right side of his neck.”

“The President is using this treatment for one week, and the redness is expected to last for a few weeks,” Barbabella added.

People quickly speculated about what could be behind the skin irritation.

“Vigorous handshaking strikes again!” Journalist Mike Rothschild wrote on X.

“Look forward to White House explanation that it’s hickeys,” Shannon Watts, author and founder of activist group Moms Demand, wrote on X.

“Sending all possible love and support to this rash,” tech and media journalist Evan DeSimone wrote on X.

“Imagine. If. This. Was. Biden,” Mehdi Hasan, founder of Zeteo, wrote on X.

“Every woman in America can advise you to say ‘it’s a curling iron burn’ and change the topic of conversation,” consultant Caitlin Legacki wrote on X.

“If President Biden’s neck looked like this, Fox News would be running 25th Amendment coverage non-stop,” journalist Aaron Parnas wrote on X.

“His ear looks in pretty good shape tho’,” media insider David Clinch wrote on X.

Our Analysis:

The Rash Heard ‘Round the World: Trump’s Neck and the Media Circus

In a spectacle that only the digital age could amplify to absurd heights, the internet became fixated on President Donald Trump’s neck rash. This seemingly innocuous health issue transformed overnight into a national conversation, punctuated by speculation, humor, and a glaring spotlight on the media’s, and by extension, the public’s, obsession with presidential health—or more accurately, their obsession with any perceived weakness in Trump.

The Rash in Question

The saga began when independent journalist Aaron Rupar highlighted photos from a White House event, revealing a conspicuous rash on Trump’s neck. Dr. Sean Barbabella, the presidential physician, attributed the condition to a “very common cream,” a mundane explanation for a phenomenon that was anything but. The rash, according to Barbabella, was a temporary side effect, yet this did little to quell the tempest of speculation and mockery that ensued on platforms like X (formerly Twitter).

The Media Melee

The reactions on social media were as swift as they were varied. From Mike Rothschild’s quip about “vigorous handshaking” to Shannon Watts’s sarcastic anticipation of a White House briefing on “hickeys,” the tone was predominantly one of mockery and schadenfreude. This collective glee at the president’s expense underscores a broader trend in political discourse: the trivialization of the personal as a means of scoring political points.

Journalist Mehdi Hasan’s commentary, “Imagine. If. This. Was. Biden,” touches on a crucial double standard. It’s a pointed reminder of the asymmetrical media landscape, where the coverage of similar incidents can diverge wildly based on the political affiliation of the subject. This isn’t mere speculation; Aaron Parnas’s observation about Fox News potentially invoking the 25th Amendment if Biden were in Trump’s place is rooted in a well-documented history of biased coverage.

The Underlying Issues

Beneath the surface level of humor and partisan jabs lies a deeper, more disconcerting reality about our collective engagement with politics. First, there’s the obsession with the superficial, a fixation on personal quirks and health scares that often overshadows substantive policy discussion. It’s a reflection of a society increasingly driven by the sensational, where a rash can overshadow geopolitical maneuvers and acts of valor, as evidenced by the scant attention paid to the Medal of Honor ceremony that served as the backdrop for the rash’s revelation.

Second, there’s the weaponization of health, an age-old tactic but one that has found new life in the era of social media. Health issues, real or imagined, are used as cudgels, tools to undermine or ridicule political figures. This not only detracts from meaningful discourse but also perpetuates a culture of ableism and ageism.

Conclusion

In the grand scheme, Trump’s rash is a non-story, a fleeting curiosity that should have merited no more than a passing mention. Yet, its elevation to a matter of national intrigue speaks volumes about the current state of media and public engagement with politics. It’s a circus, one where the performers and the audience are locked in a symbiotic dance of sensationalism and schadenfreude, all while the real issues await outside the tent, neglected and gathering dust.

As observers, participants, and critics, it’s incumbent upon us to recognize these distractions for what they are and refocus the conversation on matters of substance. After all, it’s not the rash on the president’s neck that will shape the future of the country, but the decisions he makes and the policies he enacts. Let’s not lose sight of the forest for the trees—or in this case, the rash.
