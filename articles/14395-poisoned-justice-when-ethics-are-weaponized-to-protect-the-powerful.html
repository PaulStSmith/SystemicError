Poisoned Justice: When Ethics Are Weaponized to Protect the Powerful

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 26, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

It all begins with a video clip: a dramatic courtroom takedown titled “This lawyer is personally destroying his career”. In it, attorney Mr. McBride is eviscerated by a judge for committing what seems like an unforgivable breach of legal ethics. Twenty-five years ago, McBride represented a pharmaceutical company—Zach’s Pharma—in a substantially related case. Now, he’s back in court, on the other side, using protected information to attack his former client.

The judge’s fury is palpable. “You are in complete breach of your ethical responsibilities,” she declares. “You’re off the case, effective immediately.” McBride doesn’t protest. He explodes. He calls out the courtroom, the legal system, the farce of justice. “They’re killing people for profit, and I want justice!”

At first glance, it seems like a cautionary tale about a disgraced attorney losing control. But the truth cuts far deeper. This isn’t just about McBride’s poor judgment—it’s a brief, furious window into the machinery of legal manipulation that protects the worst offenders in plain sight.

A comment beneath the video reveals the rot: Zach’s Pharma has employed a long-term strategy known as “poisoning the well.” For decades, they’ve cycled through law firms—each one representing them briefly in isolated cases. The result? No qualified attorney can ever oppose them. Every competent litigator has at some point held privileged information, making them ethically barred from future lawsuits. The very rules designed to uphold justice have been turned against it.

This isn’t fiction. It’s a dramatized mirror of real-life legal strategies used by powerful corporations to game the system. The practice is rarely called out so directly, but it lives in the shadows of our most influential legal battles.

Take Polsinelli, for instance. In 2025, the firm was barred from representing clients against Blue Cross Blue Shield after former defense attorneys joined its ranks. Their presence created a conflict of interest, effectively shutting down litigation from an entire group of plaintiffs. The court didn’t have to prove malice—just that the appearance of impropriety was enough to derail the pursuit of justice.

Or look at Vinson & Elkins. The firm was tossed from the Enviva bankruptcy case in 2024 due to conflicts stemming from its history with the company’s private equity backer. No matter how thick the ethical firewall, courts wouldn’t risk it. And why should they? The system is built to err on the side of corporate protection.

And then there’s the case of Jackson Walker, where undisclosed personal relationships between judges and attorneys led to a sweeping ethics scandal that undermined the integrity of multiple bankruptcy proceedings. The legal world acts surprised, but anyone watching closely knows this is business as usual—only now, caught on camera.

These examples show that the problem isn’t rogue attorneys breaking rules—it’s corporations learning how to turn those rules into armor. Legal ethics, meant to protect the vulnerable and ensure fairness, are now used as corporate weapons to preempt opposition. It’s a game of legal chess, where every move is calculated not for truth, but for impunity.

This is how power protects itself: not just with money, but with procedural traps, ethical minefields, and preemptive strikes. The “justice system” becomes a fortress where truth is unwelcome if it threatens the bottom line. The rules become rituals; fairness becomes theater.

McBride’s outburst—his so-called “professional suicide”—wasn’t just rage. It was a cry from inside the machine. He saw the game for what it was. And in breaking the rules, he exposed a truth more disturbing than his ethical breach: that the law, as it stands, often serves those who know how to corrupt it best. That sometimes, the only ethical thing left to do is scream.

If we are to have justice at all, then the legal profession must reckon with this reality. It must find a way to defend not just clients, but the public interest, against a system being played like a rigged casino. Reform can’t come only from within law schools or bar associations—it must come from pressure outside the walls of privilege.

We must ask: who are the rules really protecting? And when ethics become tools of suppression rather than protection, are they still worth following?

Otherwise, the well isn’t just poisoned. It’s toxic beyond redemption. And we’re all drinking from it.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
