The President Stole the Dead to Enlarge Himself

A Memorial Turned Into a Prop

At a ceremony marking 25 years since September 11, Donald Trump placed himself inside the catastrophe. He claimed that he sent a large construction crew to ground zero, stood beneath a dangerously unstable building, and was physically carried to safety by firefighters. The accounts cited in the reporting found no basis for those claims.

This was not incidental embellishment. The president used a national memorial—and the authority of his office—to appropriate the courage of people who actually entered the ruins, performed the recovery work, and absorbed its lasting costs.

Power Makes the Lie Consequential

Trump holds the institutional power here. He was not an entertainer telling a self-serving story in private. He spoke as president at an official commemoration, with the state supplying the platform and the dead supplying the moral gravity.

That distinction matters. A presidential lie at a memorial does more than falsify one man’s biography. It attempts to revise public memory from the most powerful lectern in the country. Trump cast firefighters as supporting characters in his invented ordeal and converted workers he apparently never dispatched into evidence of his supposed leadership.

The lie’s purpose can reasonably be inferred from its design: everyone else acts, suffers, rescues, or dies; Trump remains the central figure.

“Weirdness” Is Institutional Cowardice

Describing this conduct as “a bit of weirdness” is not neutral wording. It launders an abuse of public memory into another episode of Trumpian eccentricity.

The problem is not that Trump behaved strangely. The problem is that he fabricated proximity to sacrifice and delivered the fabrication under presidential seal. Coverage that emphasizes spectacle, oddity, or personality protects him from the harsher and more accurate frame: a powerful official exploited a memorial ceremony to claim honor he did not earn.

Treating deliberate self-mythology as confusion also obscures agency. Trump chose the story. He supplied its cinematic details. When challenged, he attacked the publication identifying the falsehood. That is not a misunderstanding. It is propaganda followed by retaliation against the fact-checkers.

The Public Did Not Tell This Story

The source is right that Trump’s political success has enablers, but its claim that “we the people” are his accessories spreads responsibility too evenly.

Voters who returned him to office bear political responsibility for empowering a figure whose habits were already extensively documented. News organizations that normalize his fabrications bear responsibility for reducing calculated deception to style. Officials who provide ceremonial legitimacy while declining to confront obvious falsehoods bear responsibility for institutional submission.

But Trump remains the actor who told the lie. Collective culpability must not become another device for blurring the decisions of the person with the most power. The electorate did not invent the firefighters, the collapsing-building drama, or the purported work crew. The president did.

Valor as Presidential Property

The larger pattern is Trump’s treatment of every institution, tragedy, and human sacrifice as material available for personal acquisition. Expertise becomes loyalty. Public office becomes private branding. History becomes autobiography.

That pattern is authoritarian in method even when the immediate act is rhetorical: the leader claims a privileged right to define reality, then portrays contradiction as hostility or fraud. The object is not merely to persuade. It is to establish that his status outranks the record.

The most revealing failure is therefore larger than one grotesque anniversary speech. A president can seize the honor of firefighters and recovery workers because political and media institutions have repeatedly treated factual reality as negotiable whenever enforcing it would require a direct confrontation with power. The theft begins with Trump. The system completes it by calling the evidence “weird.”
