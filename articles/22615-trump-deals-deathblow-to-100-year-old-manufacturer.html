Trump deals deathblow to 100-year-old manufacturer

Trump’s Tariff Theater Closes Another Factory

A Century Ended by Choice

Leed-Himmel Industries did not collapse because of abstract “headwinds.” It is closing after more than a century because presidential policy made ordinary business arithmetic impossible. The owner says rising costs, insurance, electricity, and especially tariffs pushed the company past the point of survival. That is the basic story. The more important story is that a White House can still break a manufacturing business, then ask to be credited for caring about manufacturing.

The Decision Maker Was Not the Market

This closure should not be narrated as a sad but mysterious act of economics. The relevant power sat in the White House. President Donald Trump imposed tariffs that the Supreme Court has already rejected as illegal because they lacked congressional authorization, yet he continued anyway. That matters. When a president uses unilateral punishment to reshape prices, supply chains, and cash flow, the result is not a natural disaster. It is policy working exactly as designed for whoever it is designed to serve.

Goldfarb said the tariffs were “the final nail in the coffin,” and his account is plain enough to stand on its own. Tariffs raised the cost of aluminum and other materials. They also forced him to tie up more cash in inventory before customers paid invoices. That is not an accounting footnote. That is the state reaching into the balance sheet of a private employer and squeezing until the operation stops breathing.

The Real Victims Are Hidden in Plain Sight

The factory floor went empty, but the damage does not stop at one owner’s frustration. This company was paying about $160,000 a year in local property taxes. It supported jobs. It supported suppliers. It supported a working industrial ecosystem in Hamden that now faces conversion to storage space if a buyer cannot be found.

This is where political coverage often gets lazy: it frames closures as unfortunate outcomes of “expenses” and “uncertainty,” as if the source of the harm were weather rather than governance. But the cost spikes here were not random. Health insurance rose. Building, vehicle, and workers’ compensation insurance rose. The power bill topped $500,000 even after operations were reduced. Then the tariffs landed on top. The result was foreseeable, and the administration owns that foreseeability.

Illegality as Economic Strategy

The Supreme Court rejected Trump’s tariffs, calling them illegal because they lacked congressional authorization. Trump kept going. That should be treated as the central constitutional fact in the story, not a side note. A president who persists after the court says no is not just testing policy boundaries. He is teaching the country that law is optional when executive appetite is strong enough.

That is the deeper pattern: authoritarian economics rarely arrives as a single dramatic seizure. It arrives as repeated acts of illegal leverage, wrapped in nationalist rhetoric, with the costs pushed downward onto firms, workers, and local tax bases. The point is not efficiency. The point is domination.

The Iran Escalation Exposed the Same Pattern

Goldfarb said a European buyer had nearly rescued the company, then backed out after Trump unilaterally declared war on Iran. That detail matters because it shows how presidential recklessness spills beyond tariffs and into the broader confidence needed for investment and dealmaking. A factory sale can be torpedoed by a president who treats international escalation as personal theater.

This is the same political logic that runs through the tariffs: instability as a method, law as an inconvenience, and private actors left to absorb the blast radius. When the White House manufactures uncertainty, it is not an accident if buyers walk away and lenders hesitate. That uncertainty is the product.

What This Closure Really Shows

The most revealing part of this story is not that one manufacturer is closing. It is that a sitting president can inflict concrete economic harm, do so in defiance of legal limits, and still have the damage reported as if it were just another unfortunate business decision. That framing protects the powerful by dissolving agency.

The agency is not mysterious. Trump chose the tariffs. Trump ignored the court. Trump escalated abroad in ways that spooked a buyer. The consequence is a shuttered factory, lost jobs, and one more hole in the industrial fabric of a town that did not ask to subsidize presidential lawlessness.

The larger pattern is blunt: when executive power is used like a wrecking ball, local economies become collateral damage and constitutional limits become scenery.
