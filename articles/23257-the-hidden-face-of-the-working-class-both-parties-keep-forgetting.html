The hidden face of the working class both parties keep forgetting

Based on an article by Chabeli Carrazana, 19thnews.org

Paulo SantosSeptember 9, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Working Class Was Not Lost. It Was Ignored.

Power, Not Phrasing

The source story is about JoAnna Mendoza, Kaela Berg, Peggy Flanagan, and other working-class women trying to make Democrats understand the voters the party spent years talking past. That is the surface. The real story is who had the power to set the terms and who paid the price: party strategists, donors, and national political professionals shaped the message, while working women absorbed the costs of rent, groceries, health care, childcare, and unstable jobs.

This is not a branding problem. It is a governing failure that got disguised as a communications problem.

The Party Kept Talking Upward

Democrats did not merely fail to “connect.” They centered college-educated liberals and policy language built for people who already had some cushion. That is the article’s own diagnosis, and it is the correct one. But the consequence is bigger than bad messaging. It is that a party claiming to represent working people let the most precarious workers become an afterthought.

Working-class women now make up a huge share of the labor force, especially in service, retail, and care work. They are not a niche constituency. They are the country’s economic infrastructure. Yet political coverage still treats them like a special-interest subgroup instead of the people most exposed to economic instability.

Women Are Doing the Arithmetic

The article’s strongest evidence comes from the women it quotes, because they are not speaking in abstractions. Mendoza knows grocery prices down to the cucumber. Jenny Cook describes living one broken dryer away from collapse. Roxy Hoda is paying $2,100 a month for health insurance with an $8,000 deductible after her child was hospitalized.

That is not anecdote as embellishment. It is the political ledger. Women are doing the accounting for households, and they are seeing exactly who leaves them to balance impossible numbers. When politicians reduce this to “affordability messaging,” they flatten lived crisis into a campaign theme. The crisis is not symbolic. It is administrative, financial, and relentless.

Trump Sells Fantasy, Then Delivers Pain

The article is right to note that Trump’s economic promises have not delivered for working people. His second term, as described here, has meant war in Iran and tariffs that pushed prices up instead of bringing relief. That has eroded support among some of his working-class backers, especially White voters who now disapprove of his handling of the economy.

But the larger point is that Trump’s appeal never depended on delivery alone. It depended on performance: directness, grievance, and the promise of power for people who feel shut out. He did not solve the material conditions he exploited. He just gave them a villain and called it representation.

Democrats Need More Than Better Tone

Mendoza says Democrats should stop talking like consultants and start talking like human beings. She is right, but only partly. The problem is not that Democrats use “$50 words.” The problem is that too many of them govern and campaign as if working-class women are a demographic to be reactivated rather than a class to be served.

A better tone without materially better politics is just cleaner packaging for the same neglect. Candidates like Mendoza, Berg, and Flanagan matter because they bring lived experience into campaigns the party has sterilized for too long. But the party cannot nominate its way out of a structural problem it has spent years building.

The Real Lesson

This story is not mainly about women becoming more visible in politics. It is about the consequences of treating the people who keep households and workplaces running as an afterthought until election season. Working-class women are not waiting for permission to understand politics. They understand it through bills, labor, care work, and repeated instability.

The system keeps asking them to believe that representation is a matter of nicer language. It is not. Representation means power answering to the people who actually carry the cost of broken policy.
