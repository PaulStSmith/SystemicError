'Grave mistake': Supreme Court justice slams colleagues for refusal to hear case

Supreme Court Nominee Judge Neil Gorsuch meets with Senator Cory Gardner (R-CO) on Capitol Hill in Washington, U.S., February 1, 2017. REUTERS/Joshua Roberts

Right-wing Supreme Court Justice Neil Gorsuch is outraged that his colleagues declined to take up what has become one of his notoriously idiosyncratic pet issues on Tuesday, Newsweek reported — calling the refusal to hear the case a "grave mistake."

"The case, brought by the group Apache Stronghold, sought to prevent the federal government's planned transfer of Oak Flat, a site in Arizona sacred to the Apache, to a mining company," reported Jenna Sundel. "Gorsuch argued that the Court should have agreed to hear the case, saying the lower court's decision 'rests on questionable legal footing.' The Court's order left intact a Ninth Circuit ruling that permitted the land transfer to proceed."

Apache Stronghold had sued under the theory that the mining company desecrating the sacred site was prohibited under the U.S. Religious Freedom Restoration Act. The Ninth Circuit had ruled against this legal reasoning.

Gorsuch, appointed to the court by President Donald Trump after a controversial battle in the Senate following the GOP's decision to hold the vacant seat open until former President Barack Obama had left office, is one of the few justices who served on a court in the West, where tribal issues and federal land use have been particularly thorny issues. He has made the rights of tribal groups one of his most passionate issues on the court, often breaking with his right-wing colleagues to join liberal opinions — or if he has to, standing on his own.

Gorsuch was the author of the Supreme Court's explosive McGirt v. Oklahoma decision in 2020, which forced the U.S. government to honor an 1866 treaty with the Muscogee Nation essentially making the entire eastern half of Oklahoma sovereign tribal land for the purposes of law enforcement.

That ruling was partially weakened, although not formally overturned, by Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta, from which Gorsuch issued a bitter dissent alongside the liberal justices.

Our Analysis:

Gorsuch's Lone Battle for Tribal Rights: A Surprising Stand from the Right

In a move that can only be described as unexpectedly principled, Justice Neil Gorsuch, a stalwart of the Supreme Court's right-wing faction, has once again diverged from his conservative colleagues in defense of tribal rights. This time, the issue at hand is the proposed desecration of Oak Flat, a site sacred to the Apache, which the federal government plans to hand over to a mining company. Gorsuch's outrage at the Supreme Court’s refusal to hear the Apache Stronghold case is not only justified but highlights a grave inconsistency in the court's priorities.

Gorsuch's Idiosyncratic Crusade

Labeling Gorsuch’s dedication to tribal rights as "notoriously idiosyncratic" is an understatement. In an era where political lines are starkly drawn, Gorsuch's deviation from his conservative roots in favor of tribal sovereignty is a rare and commendable anomaly. His sharp critique of the Court's decision to sidestep the Apache Stronghold case underscores a "grave mistake" that goes beyond legal oversight; it's a moral failure.

A Questionable Legal Footing

Gorsuch's assertion that the lower court's decision "rests on questionable legal footing" is not just a legal argument; it's a wake-up call to the judiciary's inconsistent application of the U.S. Religious Freedom Restoration Act. When a mining company's interests are prioritized over the sacred sites of indigenous peoples, one must question the integrity of the court's commitment to religious freedoms. Why does the desecration of a sacred site not trigger the protections of religious freedom?

The Controversial Path to the Bench

It's impossible to analyze Gorsuch's stance without acknowledging the controversial circumstances of his Supreme Court appointment. Appointed by President Donald Trump after the GOP's calculated blockade against Obama's nominee, Gorsuch's path to the bench was mired in political warfare. However, unlike many might have expected, Gorsuch has proven that his judicial philosophy cannot be easily pigeonholed into a conservative agenda, especially when it comes to tribal and land use issues in the West.

Breaking with the Right for Tribal Rights

Gorsuch's track record, including the landmark McGirt v. Oklahoma decision, paints a picture of a Justice willing to prioritize the rights and sovereignty of Native American tribes, even when it means standing alone or siding with the Court's liberal justices. His dissent in Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta further solidifies his position as an unexpected but steadfast ally for tribal rights.

Conclusion: A Call for Consistency and Integrity

Justice Gorsuch’s frustration with the Supreme Court’s refusal to hear the Apache Stronghold case is more than a matter of legal disagreement; it's a testament to the need for a consistent and principled approach to justice. In a court increasingly perceived as polarized and politicized, Gorsuch’s stance is a reminder that issues of sovereignty, religious freedom, and moral integrity should transcend political affiliations.

The Supreme Court’s decision to let the Ninth Circuit’s ruling stand is not just a setback for the Apache Stronghold; it's a glaring example of systemic disregard for the rights of indigenous peoples. In this context, Gorsuch’s dissent is not just noteworthy for its legal implications; it's a bold statement on the values that should define our judiciary. It's high time the rest of the court took note.
