Republicans lost the Cheesecake Factory vote — by a lot

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 7, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

According to AlterNet, citing Roll Call’s calculation, Democrats won the combined vote by 28.8 points across 141 counties containing a Cheesecake Factory. Of the chain’s 215 locations in 175 congressional districts, 171 locations will be represented by Democrats, up from 108.

The exercise followed Barack Obama’s joke at a Michigan rally that Donald Trump’s gold-heavy Oval Office looked “like Saddam Hussein opened a Cheesecake Factory.” Republicans denounced the remark as elitist; Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent posed outside one of the restaurants. AlterNet also reported that Trump’s election-night gathering ordered Cheesecake Factory catering, much of which went uneaten as returns disappointed his team.

The statistic beneath the stunt

The “Cheesecake Factory vote” is not a recognized constituency, and the reporting provides no evidence that restaurant preference shaped anyone’s ballot. It is a geographic correlation assembled around where a commercial chain operates.

But the underlying geography punctures the Republican performance. The cited districts containing a Cheesecake Factory had a median household income of $93,000, compared with $76,000 in districts without one. Republicans tried to cast the chain as a symbol of ordinary people insulted by an affluent Democrat. The available numbers instead associate its locations with relatively wealthier districts that voted heavily Democratic.

Who held the power

The people exercising institutional power were not restaurant customers. Trump controlled the presidency, Bessent led the Treasury Department, and Republican strategists had access to a national political communications apparatus. Obama had prominence and a campaign stage, but the administration chose to elevate his joke into a political dispute.

That distinction matters. A former president delivered a mocking line about White House décor. Administration officials and allies decided to answer it as though defending a restaurant chain were government business. The outrage did not rise spontaneously from diners; political professionals manufactured and distributed it.

Class politics as costume

GOP strategist Matt Whitlock accused Obama of snobbery and contrasted Cheesecake Factory customers with his access to a private chef. Bessent—whom AlterNet describes as worth hundreds of millions of dollars—then posed outside the restaurant. The maneuver substituted consumer branding for material politics: standing near a chain restaurant became evidence of solidarity with people facing affordability problems.

It proved nothing of the kind. The reporting identifies no policy response attached to the performance. It shows powerful officials converting criticism of Trump’s priorities into a culture-war argument about taste. Obama’s actual contrast—ornamentation versus affordability—was displaced by a staged dispute over whether Cheesecake Factory deserved respect.

The misdirection worked backward

The framing invited readers to treat restaurant geography as electoral insight. It is, at most, a comic proxy. Counties contain voters with different incomes, preferences, and reasons for voting; a storefront cannot explain their choices.

Still, the gimmick exposed the hollowness of the original attack. Republicans claimed ownership of a supposedly insulted public, yet Democrats dominated the places selected to represent that public. Even Trump’s election-night catering turned the chain into a prop: food ordered for an image, then reportedly left largely untouched when the political mood soured.

Politics by purchased symbol

This episode reveals a recurring method of power: evade a substantive criticism by inventing an aggrieved cultural constituency, then appoint yourself its defender. The weaker actor—in this case, an imagined mass of supposedly scorned restaurant customers—becomes camouflage for officials who actually control policy.

The vote tally does not establish a new electoral bloc. It establishes something more limited and more useful: Republican operatives built a class grievance around a restaurant whose relevant political geography did not support their story. The outrage was not analysis of economic power. It was branding deployed to prevent such analysis.
