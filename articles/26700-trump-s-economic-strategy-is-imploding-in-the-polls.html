Trump's economic strategy is imploding in the polls

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 19, 20263 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump’s economic strategy is collapsing in the polls because voters associate his policies with renewed price increases. Inflation was 3.7 percent in September from a year earlier, after progress reportedly stalled during 2025 as tariffs raised the cost of imported goods and the war with Iran pushed up energy prices.

The article attributes broader affordability pressure to record diesel prices, higher electronics costs, and expenses that companies may not yet have passed to customers. It also says Canada imposed retaliatory tariffs during Trump’s trade conflict and that Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz after the United States attacked it, further disrupting oil markets.

Former Clinton adviser Robert Shapiro disputed the administration’s suggestion that war-related oil costs had already been priced in. He pointed to a $10 increase before the attack as evidence that prices were responding to changing risk, not merely reflecting an old expectation.

The power sits in the White House

Voters do not control tariff schedules, initiate wars, or direct relations with major trading partners. The president and his administration do. Whatever other forces affect prices, the decisions highlighted here came from the institution with the greatest power to alter trade and foreign policy: the executive branch.

That matters because inflation is often presented as economic weather—unwelcome, complicated and nobody’s fault. But tariffs are taxes imposed through policy, and military escalation can predictably disturb energy markets. These are choices with identifiable authors and foreseeable costs.

The bill arrives through the checkout line

Trump’s strategy asks consumers to absorb the consequences of presidential power while the administration markets those consequences as strength. Importers encounter tariffs first, but households ultimately face higher prices when businesses pass costs along. Expensive diesel can then spread through transportation and food distribution.

The same structure applies to energy shocks. The government chooses escalation; consumers pay at the pump and across an economy dependent on fuel. The distance between the decision and the receipt makes political evasion easier, but it does not erase causation.

Confusion is a political alibi

Shapiro’s criticism exposes more than a technical dispute. If officials claim an oil-price increase was already “baked in” despite prices moving as the risk of war intensified, they are offering a story designed to detach the administration’s action from its economic consequence.

Markets are complex, and the reporting does not establish that Trump’s policies caused every price increase. It does, however, identify specific mechanisms connecting tariffs and war to higher costs. Treating those mechanisms as mysterious would misdirect attention away from the officials empowered to activate them.

Polls are the consequence, not the scandal

The polling collapse is politically important, but it is not the central failure. The underlying failure is an economic program that exercises federal power aggressively while shifting the resulting costs onto people with far less control over the outcome.

Michael Strain’s comparison is blunt: voters blamed Biden-era policies when they believed those policies raised prices, and they now make the same judgment about Trump. That is not inconsistency. It is voters assigning responsibility to the president who holds power when their living costs rise.

Power wants credit without liability

This story reveals a familiar governing pattern: concentrate authority, advertise every intervention as proof of dominance, and describe the damage as an impersonal market reaction once the consequences become unpopular.

The economic strategy is not merely suffering from bad messaging. According to the reporting, its signature decisions are colliding with the administration’s promise of affordability. A government cannot plausibly claim mastery when announcing tariffs and war, then plead helplessness when those decisions reach household budgets.
