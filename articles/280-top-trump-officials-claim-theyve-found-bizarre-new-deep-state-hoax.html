Top Trump officials claim they’ve found bizarre new 'deep state hoax'

The Deep State Hoax as a Governing Style

Power Knows What It Is Doing

The story is simple enough to name plainly: two officials inside Donald Trump’s administration used the machinery of office to resurrect Watergate as propaganda. Pardon attorney Ed Martin and chief of protocol Monica Crowley were not speculating from the sidelines. They were speaking from power, giving institutional weight to a narrative that tries to erase Nixon’s recorded, documented wrongdoing and replace it with a fantasy of persecution.

That matters because power is doing what power always does when it wants to escape accountability: it does not deny the facts directly, it attacks the existence of facts themselves.

The Source of the Lie

The article’s own evidence makes the target clear. There were taped conversations, a paper trail, testimony from Nixon administration figures and allies, and a unanimous Supreme Court ruling against Nixon’s attempt to keep the tapes secret. This was not a fog of partisan interpretation. It was a record.

So the decision was not confusion. The decision was to relabel settled history as a “deep state hoax,” because that label is useful. It turns documented abuse into victimhood and converts responsibility into conspiracy. Crowley’s claim that Nixon “will be vindicated” is not historical analysis. It is institutional laundering.

Blame Directed Upward, Then Sideways

The rhetorical move is familiar. When elites face evidence of corruption, they do not answer the evidence. They redirect blame toward invisible conspirators, the press, investigators, and the administrative state. Martin’s post goes so far as to accuse the Washington Post of “leading” rather than reporting, as though journalism were the crime and burglary, obstruction, and cover-up were incidental.

This is misdirection in its cleanest form. It shifts attention away from the men who ordered, covered, and lied, and toward the institutions that exposed them. The effect is not accidental. It trains audiences to mistrust accountability mechanisms more than the abuse those mechanisms uncover.

Nixon as a Cover for Trump

The article also tells on itself. Trump officials have spent years calling their own scandals “deep state hoaxes,” including Russian interference, the 2020 election results, and the classified-documents investigation. That repetition is the point. Nixon is not being defended for his own sake. He is being recycled as precedent, a historical decoy that normalizes Trump’s method: deny, smear, discredit, repeat.

That is why the language matters. “Vindicated” is not a neutral word here. It is an attempted rewrite of the political record, one that treats exposure as persecution and evidence as theft.

What This Story Actually Reveals

The deeper pattern is not about Watergate alone. It is about an executive culture that uses office to invert reality whenever accountability closes in. First comes the claim of hidden proof. Then comes the accusation of a rigged process. Then comes the attempt to recast proven misconduct as a smear by enemies with institutional power.

That is not confusion. It is administrative propaganda. And when officials inside a presidency use it, they are not defending history. They are teaching the public to accept impunity as a political identity.
