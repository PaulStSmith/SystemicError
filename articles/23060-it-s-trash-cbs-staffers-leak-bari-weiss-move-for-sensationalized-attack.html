'It's trash': CBS staffers leak Bari Weiss move for 'sensationalized' attack

Based on an article by David Edwards

Paulo SantosSeptember 4, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

CBS Didn’t “Cover” Abdul El-Sayed. It Manufactured Suspicion.

The Power Was Inside CBS

The important fact in this story is not Abdul El-Sayed’s deleted posts. It is that CBS News, under Bari Weiss’s editorial authority, chose to elevate them into a political weapon. Staffers say Weiss wanted the headline pushed harder, and that the network’s own promotion echoed the insinuation. That is not neutral editing. It is institutional power deciding who gets framed as suspect and how aggressively.

El-Sayed is the Democratic nominee for U.S. Senate in Michigan and would be the first Muslim elected to the Senate. That matters because the article did not land in a vacuum. It arrived in a media environment where Islamophobia is a ready-made frame, and CBS helped load the weapon.

A Smear In Search Of Cover

The source material shows a simple pattern: a small set of old pandemic-era posts was inflated into something much larger than the facts justify. CBS’s own account says the posts did not show contempt for 9/11 victims or defend the attacks. Yet the headline and promo language invited readers to infer exactly that.

That is the trick. You do not need to state the lie directly if you can force the audience to assemble it themselves. “Scrutiny” becomes a laundering term for suspicion, and “deleted tweets” becomes an insinuation factory. The article did not need to prove hostility. It only needed to make Muslim political ambition look contaminated.

Why 9/11 Was Chosen

The choice of subject was not random. 9/11 remains one of the most potent triggers in American political propaganda, which is precisely why it is used so often to discipline Muslims and justify smear campaigns. El-Sayed compared the scale of the Covid death toll to another national catastrophe and argued for a public-health response on that scale. Fox hosts made similar analogies during the pandemic. That context is not a footnote; it is the whole point.

CBS knew exactly what kind of reaction this framing would produce. So did Fox, which immediately ran with the headline and turned it into a claim that El-Sayed “doesn’t love America.” The article did not merely report on that attack. It supplied the raw material.

The Newsroom Saw The Problem

The staff backlash is the other key fact. Employees reportedly called the piece “trash,” “horribly Islamophobic,” and “shameful.” Senior staffers took objections to standards. The report says little came of it because Weiss was deeply involved. That tells you where accountability actually sat: not with a detached standards process, but with an empowered editor choosing the frame and overriding resistance.

CBS later changed the story multiple times and, per the reporting, has not offered an editor’s note. That is the behavior of an institution trying to manage fallout, not correct a mistake. If management had simply misread the issue, an apology and correction would follow. Instead, the network appears to be waiting for the outrage cycle to pass while preserving the original damage.

What This Reveals About Media Power

Weiss was installed atop CBS despite never having worked in broadcast news or run a major news operation. The result is exactly what critics of such appointments warned about: a newsroom leadership structure that treats provocation as editorial seriousness and lets ideological instinct stand in for judgment.

This is not just about one headline or one nominee. It is about elite media organizations converting vulnerable targets into content, then pretending the outrage they create is merely the market’s response. That logic serves power, not journalism. It normalizes the idea that Muslim candidates can be framed through inherited suspicion, that newsroom dissent can be overruled from above, and that manufactured controversy counts as news judgment.

The Pattern Beneath The Story

The deeper pattern is authoritarian in miniature: identify a minority candidate, attach a national trauma, force the public to process the candidate through fear, then let downstream propagandists do the rest. CBS supplied the opening. Fox turned the knob. The political target was obvious from the start.

If this is what “editorial leadership” looks like, the problem is not confusion. It is the deliberate use of institutional authority to launder prejudice into publishable form. That is how reputations are damaged, coalitions are narrowed, and democratic representation is policed before voters even get to the ballot box.
