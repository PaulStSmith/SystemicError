Profit Vs Healthcare

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 31, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The cartoon making the rounds, where a politician tours a hospital and cannot comprehend why no one is "getting rich" off the sick, is not satire so much as a mirror. It reveals the gaping ideological chasm between the idea of healthcare as a public good and the neoliberal obsession with profit as the only metric of value.

In the dialogue, doctors explain that patients are treated, cured, and sent home. The politician, exasperated, keeps asking: But who makes money? Who gets the cash? The answer, repeated with growing frustration, is always the same: no one. The money goes to saving lives. That’s the point.

This is not some wild exaggeration. It’s the daily reality of how politicians and profiteers view institutions like the UK’s National Health Service. The NHS was built on the radical idea that healthcare is not a commodity but a right. It is funded collectively, delivered universally, and, crucially, not structured to generate profit. That is its strength—and also why it has become the target of decades-long privatization schemes.

The neoliberal worldview is incapable of parsing this logic. In its eyes, an enterprise without profit is a failure. If no one is siphoning money to shareholders or MPs’ donor circles, then something must be wrong. This cartoon captures that mindset perfectly: the utter bewilderment at the idea of value beyond capital accumulation.

And yet, when measured by any humane metric, the NHS is a success. It saves millions of lives, provides care without bills or bankruptcy, and embodies the principle that a society’s health is a collective responsibility. But to the political class enthralled by markets, those achievements mean nothing if they don’t line pockets.

This ideological blindness is why austerity measures have gutted hospital budgets, why outsourcing has crept into the system, and why politicians speak in doublespeak about "reform" that usually means selling off public goods. Bit by bit, the profit motive worms its way in: private contractors for cleaning, outsourced services for diagnostics, creeping charges for formerly free treatments. The goal isn’t efficiency. The goal is extraction.

The cartoon’s humor lands because it’s painfully accurate. Ask an investment banker what a hospital is for, and they’ll say it’s an underutilized revenue stream. Ask a minister of health captured by corporate interests, and they’ll tell you it’s ripe for "modernization"—their code for privatization. Ask a doctor or nurse, and they’ll tell you it’s for healing the sick. Those answers cannot be reconciled.

This clash matters, because it’s not a hypothetical. If the profiteers win, healthcare ceases to be a service and becomes a market. Patients become customers. Illness becomes a business opportunity. And we know where that road leads: look at the United States, where medical bankruptcy is a leading cause of financial ruin and insulin prices are dictated by shareholder greed rather than human need.

The NHS—and public healthcare systems more broadly—represent a different vision of society. One where we acknowledge that some things are too vital to be left to the whims of the market. One where we recognize that health is not a product but a foundation of dignity. And one where the answer to the politician’s endless question, who gets the money? is and must remain: no one. It all goes to the patients.

That’s not inefficiency. That’s morality. That’s civilization.

The cartoon ends with the politician shouting in frustration: THE MONEY!!! WHO GETS ALL THE CASH?! The answer should be shouted back, louder than ever: it isn’t about the cash. It’s about people. And if our leaders can’t grasp that, then they have no business running healthcare at all.
