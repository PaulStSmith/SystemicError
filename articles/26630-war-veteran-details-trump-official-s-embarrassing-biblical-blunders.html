SYSTEMIC ERROR / COMMENTARY

War veteran details Trump official's embarrassing Biblical blunders

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 14, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth repeatedly used biblical passages to cast American military service and national purpose in sacred terms. Citing Paul D. Miller of Georgetown University, the article identifies the central blunder as Hegseth’s removal of verses from their original context—equating service members with the prophet Isaiah and treating the United States as though it were God’s covenant nation.

Miller argues that Hegseth turned passages from Isaiah, Psalms, and Ephesians into patriotic slogans during a military speech and recruitment campaign. AlterNet also recounts a separate speech in which Hegseth adapted dialogue from Pulp Fiction—loosely associated with Ezekiel 25:17—as though it were Scripture. The supplied reporting describes Miller as an international-affairs professor and military-ethics scholar, but does not substantiate the headline’s characterization of him as a war veteran.

The office matters more than the errors

The embarrassing part is not merely that a senior official mangled the Bible. The defense secretary controls a vast military bureaucracy and speaks with the authority of the executive branch. When he presents military missions through a Christian script, the practical issue is not theological literacy. It is the use of government power to privilege one religious vocabulary and wrap state violence in divine approval.

Hegseth reportedly described physical war as part of a spiritual battle and invoked the “Armor of God” while addressing the work of the Defense Department. That collapses distinctions a civilian-led military needs: between policy and revelation, national interest and divine command, public service and religious mission.

Scripture as state branding

Miller’s criticism is precise: Hegseth’s quotations do not humble or judge the nation; they sacralize it. Scripture becomes stage dressing for recruitment, hierarchy, combat, and obedience.

That use is politically convenient. A policy argument can be questioned. A strategic decision can be judged by evidence, law, cost, and results. But once officials imply that the nation’s battles participate in God’s battle, dissent is easier to portray as weakness, disloyalty, or spiritual failure. The reporting does not establish that Hegseth consciously designed that effect. His rhetoric nevertheless creates it.

The spectacle misdirects

AlterNet frames the story around “Biblical blunders,” inviting readers to laugh at ignorance and hypocrisy. The errors deserve scrutiny, especially the cinematic pseudo-Scripture. But ridicule can shrink a structural abuse into a personality flaw.

The consequential actor is not a confused congregant. It is the defense secretary. The consequential choice is not misremembering a verse. It is using the institutional platform of the Pentagon to fuse Christianity, American nationalism, and military force. Focusing on whether Hegseth passes a Bible quiz risks diverting attention from what his rhetoric authorizes.

Exclusion follows sanctification

The same official who reportedly invoked sacred language also promised a military with “no fatties,” “no trannies,” “no beardos,” “no weirdos,” “no wimps,” and “no radicals.” That list is not theology. It is a political sorting mechanism: define the ideal warrior narrowly, mark disfavored people as contamination, and present exclusion as institutional restoration.

Biblical imagery supplies grandeur to that project. It turns a program of discipline and exclusion into a story about righteousness. The foreseeable consequence is a military culture in which favored identities are associated with strength and divine purpose while targeted groups are cast as evidence of decline.

The larger pattern

Christian nationalism does not require sophisticated Christianity. It requires symbols that can convert political authority into moral authority. In this account, Scripture is valuable to Hegseth precisely because fragments of it can be detached from history, theology, and restraint, then redeployed as patriotic certification.

That is the systemic error exposed here. Coverage fixated on hypocrisy asks whether the official understands the text. The harder political question is what happens when an official with command authority uses that text to sanctify the nation, militarize faith, and make exclusion sound holy.
