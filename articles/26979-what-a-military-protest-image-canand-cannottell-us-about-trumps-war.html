What a military protest image can—and cannot—tell us about Trump’s war

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 20, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A Protest Image Is a Warning, Not a Measure of Military Morale

Power, Not Fog

The core fact in this story is not a viral photo. It is power. Donald Trump initiated a war against Iran, and the relevant institutional machinery now belongs to the administration that ordered it. That makes responsibility plain: the decision came from the top, and the consequences flow downward to the people told to carry it out.

Trita Parsi’s account matters because it names what elite coverage often obscures. A war can be officially commanded and still be politically rotten from the first day. When the policy serves narrow interests, or a foreign patron’s priorities, the state can still mobilize force. It just cannot manufacture legitimacy.

The Limits of the Photograph

The article centers on a photograph of messages in dust on a military aircraft. Parsi reads it as a sign of dissent. One photograph cannot establish how widely that view is held, who wrote every message, or the morale of the armed forces as a whole. It is an anecdote supporting his argument, not a representative survey.

Parsi interprets the messages as criticism of the war’s purpose and of its relationship to Israeli interests. That interpretation should remain attributed to him; the photograph cannot establish the motives or beliefs of military personnel generally.

Misdirection Is the Point

The usual Washington move is to hide deliberate choice inside the language of necessity. Wars are framed as drift, reaction, or obligation. But this one is presented in the source as a “war of choice,” which is exactly the point: someone chose it. Someone enabled it. Someone then asked the military to launder that choice into national duty.

Morale matters politically, but measuring it requires evidence beyond this image. The broader criticism here is Parsi’s argument about legitimacy and executive decision-making, not a demonstrated finding about widespread resistance among personnel.

Unpopular by Design

Parsi’s bigger argument is that the war was deeply unpopular from the outset among Americans and that defeat is now a matter of time. The important part is not the prophecy. It is the structure. A war with no democratic mandate, weak strategic rationale, and visible partisan or foreign-policy capture has to rely on inertia, coercion, and propaganda to survive.

That is not a strength. It is administrative decay. A government can still issue orders while losing the moral capacity to sustain them. The military can keep moving long after legitimacy has evaporated. But the dust on that aircraft suggests the lie has already reached the people expected to enforce it.

The Coalition Behind the War

The source’s most politically revealing line is the claim that the war was started on behalf of Israel. That is Parsi’s analysis, not a settled fact from the article, but it points to the real question: whose interests are treated as synonymous with America’s, and who gets to make that substitution without public consent?

This is where the usual national-security theater becomes useful to power. It turns alignment into necessity and criticism into disloyalty. It permits a president to dress a geopolitical favor in the costume of U.S. strategy. The result is not confusion. It is managed deception.

What This Exposes

This story is not about one snide photograph or one angry Substack post. It raises the question of how politically brittle a war becomes when those asked to fight it doubt its purpose. It also shows how little the public is supposed to know when elite institutions decide that war is the default tool for serving someone else’s agenda.

The deeper pattern is clear: American war-making is still built to protect executive power, not democratic consent. When that arrangement fails, the first cracks appear not in the press briefing room, but in the dust on the aircraft.
