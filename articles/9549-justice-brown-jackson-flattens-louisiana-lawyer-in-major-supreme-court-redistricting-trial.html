Justice Brown Jackson flattens Louisiana lawyer in major Supreme Court redistricting trial

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 16, 20256 min read

Judge Ketanji Brown Jackson testifies during the Senate Judiciary Committee’s confirmation hearing on her nomination to the U.S. Supreme Court, on Capitol Hill in Washington, on March 22, 2022. Credit: REUTERS/Michael A McCoy

Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson clashed with a Louisiana lawyer during oral arguments on Wednesday when the court heard Louisiana v. Callais, a case on whether a majority-minority district in Louisiana discriminated against white people.

Those advocating against the district frequently argued that the plaintiffs in the case demanded a second majority-minority district, but Jackson explained that this is not the case’s focus and does not constitute a retrial.

“Where in section two [of the Voting Rights Act] does it mandate another minority district?” asked Jackson. “My understanding, as I explored with Ms. Nelson, is that section two is the mechanism by which we determine equal electoral opportunity is not being provided for a certain minority group. And we’ve interpreted in Gingles, we’ve given some flesh to how one goes about identifying that set of circumstances. But I thought that’s the end of it in terms of the court’s announcement under section two. And then the court turns to the state and says, ‘How do you want to remedy this?’”

She explained that sometimes that means adding another majority minority district, and sometimes it does not.

“Your answer to Justice Barrett was, well, everybody just knows that’s the automatic remedy. So, can you help me figure out that disconnect?” Jackson asked Benjamin Aguiñaga, Louisiana solicitor general.

“Justice Jackson, I think there’s a reason why this court’s voting precedents, going all the way back to Shaw 1, are so tied up with race, it is because the remedy as parties and the courts have understood section two to operate is almost always going to be race,” said Aguiñaga.

“They’re so tied up with race because that’s the initial problem, right? That is the beginning,” said Jackson, explaining how the cases were brought. “The beginning is the claim that a person makes under section two, because of their race, they are not being afforded equal electoral opportunity. It is a separate question as to how we go about remedying that...”

Aguiñaga agreed and attempted to continue going on with his statement, but was stopped in his tracks.

“Wait, why is that not a compelling interest to identify areas [where] the problem is occurring?” she asked.

Aguiñaga cited a case that allowed states to remedy the problem, but Jackson paused him again, noting that it isn’t the question.

“I’m not talking about the remedy. I’m talking about identify — alright? So, if I’m right, that section two is about identifying the problem and then requiring some remedy,” said Jackson. “I don’t understand why your answer to Justice Kagan’s question about, ‘is this a compelling state interest’ is ‘no.’ The answer is obviously yes. That you have an interest in remedying the ills of all discrimination that we identify using this tool, when you go too far in your remedy, is another issue, right?”

That’s when things took a more confrontational tone.

Aguiñaga claimed that “step zero” was that the plaintiffs came in and said that they wanted another majority-minority district.

“I thought they came in and said we’re not receiving equal electoral opportunity because our votes are being diluted,” Jackson shot back.

Aguiñaga claimed it’s saying the same thing as saying “we deserve” another district.

“It’s not,” Jackson shot back. “Trust me on this. Because the second district is a remedy that one could offer for a problem that we have identified. And the whole Robinson litigation was about identifying the problem. Is it really happening?”

In most cases, she said, the court says “you’re fine” and “go away. In this case, the court said, I see. I’m looking at the factors. I appreciate what you’re saying. You’ve proven that we have this problem. And so, the next question is how do we go about remedying it?”

Aguiñaga again brought up “intentional discrimination,“ but Jackson questioned why it even needed to be discussed, as it was not the question before them.

Madiba K. Dennie, who writes for “Balls and Strikes” called the exchange “striking.”

“To me, Louisiana was basically like ‘here come these Black people always asking for more’ and Justice Jackson was like, ‘that’s not what the f--- they’re asking for, they’re saying: stop discriminating against us,’” Dennie wrote on Bluesky.

Our Analysis:

Analysis of Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson’s Clash Over Voting Rights

Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson’s recent exchange during the oral arguments of Louisiana v. Callais not only spotlighted her formidable presence on the Supreme Court but also underscored the nuanced complexities of the Voting Rights Act. This confrontation, particularly with Louisiana Solicitor General Benjamin Aguiñaga, reveals a profound disconnect between the legal obligations to address racial discrimination in voting and some parties’ interpretations of these obligations.

Misinterpretation of Voting Rights Act Section 2

At the heart of this dispute is a gross misinterpretation—or perhaps a deliberate misrepresentation—of what the plaintiffs in the case are demanding and what the law, specifically Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act, necessitates. Justice Jackson’s pointed questioning aimed to clarify that the case wasn’t about mandating another majority-minority district for the sake of it. Instead, it was about ensuring that minority voters are not denied equal electoral opportunities due to systemic discrimination—a fundamental right that seems to be perennially on trial in various forms across the United States.

Justice Jackson adeptly dismantled the opposition’s argument, emphasizing that Section 2’s intent is to identify and remedy instances where minority groups are not afforded equal electoral opportunities. This is not, as Aguiñaga suggested, an automatic trigger for creating majority-minority districts. His reduction of the issue to such a simplistic formula—implying that minority plaintiffs are just demanding more districts—strikes as both misinformed and misleading.

The Essence of Remediation

The exchange further highlighted a critical aspect of the Voting Rights Act: the flexibility in remediation. Justice Jackson stressed that the creation of another majority-minority district is only one possible remedy among others, contingent upon the specific circumstances of discrimination identified. This is where Aguiñaga’s argument crumbles under its own weight. By fixating on the remedy rather than the problem—electoral discrimination—he missed the forest for the trees.

Justice Jackson’s insistence on focusing on the identification of discrimination before discussing remedies exposes a crucial judicial philosophy: the importance of understanding and addressing the root cause of a problem rather than jumping to conclusions about its solutions. This approach is not only logical but imperative in ensuring that measures taken are both appropriate and effective.

A Confrontation of Ideals

What this confrontation ultimately reveals is a clash of ideals. On one side, there is a push for a narrow, literal interpretation of the law that ignores the lived realities of racial discrimination in voting. On the other, as represented by Justice Jackson, there’s a call for a more nuanced, principled application of the law that genuinely seeks to address and remedy discrimination.

Madiba K. Dennie’s commentary on the exchange, suggesting that Louisiana’s stance reeked of a dismissive attitude towards legitimate claims of discrimination (”here come these Black people always asking for more“), captures the essence of the frustration many feel when confronting systemic racism. Justice Jackson’s rebuttal, as paraphrased by Dennie, “that’s not what the fuck they’re asking for, they’re saying: stop discriminating against us,“ serves as a potent reminder of what’s at stake: the fight against discrimination and for equal rights.

Conclusion

Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson’s engagement in Louisiana v. Callais not only highlighted her legal acumen but also her commitment to ensuring the Voting Rights Act serves its intended purpose. This exchange serves as a microcosm of the broader struggles facing the United States in its endeavor to reconcile its democratic ideals with the reality of systemic racial discrimination. It’s a stark reminder that the journey towards justice is fraught with challenges, misunderstandings, and, at times, confrontations, but it’s a journey that must be undertaken with vigilance and an unwavering commitment to equity.
