Trump's colossal botch has wrenched open an unbelievable opportunity

The Populist Divide Is Really a Struggle Over Who Pays

Power Recognized the Crisis First

America’s collapsing faith in government is not merely a mood. It is a political resource, and the faction that understood this first now controls the federal government and roughly half the states. Donald Trump, his billionaire allies, and the media platforms that amplify them recognized that institutional failure could be converted into power—provided public anger was redirected away from concentrated wealth and toward designated enemies.

The reported context is stark: trust in government has fallen from nearly 90 percent in the Eisenhower-Kennedy era to 17 percent today, while the share of Americans identifying as middle class has contracted sharply. That is enough summary. The real question is who exploited the collapse, who helped create it, and who still refuses to acknowledge what it means.

Neoliberalism Was a Choice, Not a Weather Event

The economic order producing this resentment did not descend on Washington like fog. Republican officials cut taxes for the wealthy, weakened unions, reduced social provision, and transferred public capacity to private power. Democratic administrations then preserved much of that architecture.

Bill Clinton declared the end of “big government” and dismantled welfare guarantees. Barack Obama routed healthcare reform through dominant private insurers. Later Democratic governments left Reagan-era tax assumptions substantially intact. These were decisions made by officeholders with institutional authority, encouraged by donors and corporations that benefited from them.

Calling the result “polarization” obscures responsibility. Working people did not engineer wage stagnation, monopoly power, expensive healthcare, or the political dependence on billionaires. They experienced the consequences. The governing class designed, defended, or declined to reverse the system.

The Right Sells Plutocracy as Rebellion

Trump’s movement presents itself as an uprising against elites while handing state power to wealthy patrons and deploying masked federal agents as instruments of social control. Its trick is not ideological subtlety. It is scapegoating.

The right identifies genuine injuries—economic insecurity, institutional contempt, political exclusion—then assigns blame to immigrants, racial minorities, universities, and other comparatively weak targets. Billionaires become tribunes of the people; people with little power become the conspiracy supposedly ruining the country.

That inversion is authoritarian propaganda. The source points to tech and fossil-fuel fortunes, more than 200 billionaire families and foundations heavily financing the 2024 election, and media power concentrated around figures such as Elon Musk and Rupert Murdoch. Those actors possess capital, distribution systems, and direct access to government. An undocumented worker does not. Any account that treats them as equivalent sources of political power is laundering hierarchy into “division.”

Democratic Leaders Keep Mistaking Donors for Reality

Hakeem Jeffries’s hostility toward Medicare for All is politically revealing because it shows a party leader defending the boundaries of the order that generated the revolt. If voters distrust institutions that repeatedly protect insurers and wealthy interests, responding by protecting those interests more emphatically is not moderation. It is institutional cowardice dressed as practicality.

The Democratic split is therefore not mainly stylistic. Bernie Sanders, Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, Zohran Mamdani, Abdul El-Sayed, and other economic populists argue that government should directly reduce the costs of healthcare, education, and housing while taxing concentrated wealth. Corporate-aligned Democrats offer a narrower program constrained by the donors and industries that prosper under scarcity.

This is where weak political coverage tends to misdirect blame. It portrays disappointed voters as irrational, impatient, or culturally alienated while treating party leaders’ allegiance to wealthy interests as a neutral fact of electoral life. The powerful become background scenery; the powerless become the problem.

A Choice Between Two Uses of the State

The source frames the moment as a binary contest between fascism and democratic socialism. History is rarely that tidy, and the article’s claims about the 1980 hostage crisis and the Democratic treatment of Sanders are presented more confidently than the supplied evidence can independently establish. But its central distinction is sound: rival populisms can share an indictment without sharing either a program or a moral structure.

Progressive populism proposes using public authority against concentrated wealth to provide universal goods. Right-wing populism proposes consolidating authority while preserving concentrated wealth, policing vulnerable groups, and calling the arrangement liberation. One expands material membership; the other distributes punishment.

The symmetry ends at the slogan. “The system failed you” can lead to social provision, or it can become permission for oligarchs to govern through resentment.

The System Protects Itself by Misnaming the Conflict

The deeper pattern is not that America has become mysteriously angry. It is that decades of policy transferred security upward and risk downward, until distrust became the most abundant force in politics. The authoritarian right weaponized that distrust. Much of the Democratic establishment tried to manage it without disturbing the interests that caused it.

That is the systemic error: institutions manufacture desperation, elites deny agency for the manufacture, and political coverage studies the resulting anger as though it were an inexplicable defect in the population. The decisive conflict is over whether public power will discipline wealth or serve it—and whether the casualties of that choice will receive material repair or merely a new scapegoat.
