Secretary of State Marco Rubio speaks to the media on the day of a briefing for Congressional leaders on the situation in Iran, on Capitol Hill in Washington, D.C. on March 2, 2026. REUTERS/Kylie Cooper

Secretary of State Marco Rubio argued Monday that the U.S. faced a significant and “imminent threat” that prompted strikes in Iran.

Reporters asked Rubio in Washington, D.C., whether Congress was notified about the national security threats, and Rubio said he told the “gang of eight“ leaders that strikes were coming.

“There absolutely was an imminent threat,” Rubio said Monday afternoon.

He claimed that the Iranian military was growing its nuclear weapons program and planning to launch attacks against America and its allies in the region. In the days since, Rubio said Iran has attacked civilian areas in the Middle East in retaliatory strikes since the attacks started Saturday.

“We would love to see this regime be replaced. And ultimately, as the president has said, he would love for the people of Iran to use this as an opportunity to rise up and remove these leaders,” Rubio said. “They’ve been wanting to remove them for a long time, we’ve seen successive waves of protests and we’ve seen them slaughter people. But the objective of this mission is to make sure they don’t have these weapons that can threaten us and our allies in the region. That’s why we’re doing what we’re doing now.”

“And while we would love to see a new regime. The bottom line is no matter who governs that country, a year from now, they’re not going to have these ballistic missiles and they’re not going to have these drones to threaten us.

Rubio said he knew that oil prices would be impacted as a result of the strikes and that the Trump administration was aiming to mitigate against that.

“They are a terroristic regime,” Rubio said. “They sponsor terrorism and they participate in terrorism.”

He did not know how long the conflict would last.

“The hardest hits are yet to come from the US military,” Rubio said. “The next phase will be even more punishing than it is right now.

Our Analysis:

The Dubious Justification for Strikes in Iran: A Critical Examination

In a recent development that has sent rumbles across the international stage, Secretary of State Marco Rubio has positioned the U.S. strikes in Iran under the glaring spotlight of imminent threat rhetoric. The claim of an “imminent threat” as a justification for military action is a narrative we’ve seen unfurl with alarming regularity, often with scant evidence to support such drastic measures. Let’s dissect Rubio’s statements and the underpinnings of this latest military endeavor with the critical eye it desperately needs.

The Immediacy of the Threat: Fact or Hyperbole?

Rubio’s assertion of an “imminent threat” from Iran, specifically referencing its nuclear weapons program and alleged plans to launch attacks against America and its allies, is a hefty claim. It’s a narrative thread that’s all too familiar - reminiscent of the justifications used for the Iraq War in 2003, which were later debunked. The danger of such claims lies not just in their potential to mislead but in the irreversible consequences they usher in. The lack of detailed evidence presented to the public raises the question: Are we seeing a repetition of history, where the drumbeats of war are sounded on shaky grounds?

Congressional Notification: A Check on Executive Power

Rubio’s interaction with the “gang of eight” - a term referring to the leaders of Congress and intelligence committees - is a nod to the procedural steps taken before such military actions. However, informing a select few does not equate to a thorough debate and authorization by Congress, a fundamental step in ensuring that decisions of war and peace are not monopolized by the executive branch. This approach skirts dangerously close to bypassing the very essence of democratic oversight.

The Regime Change Rhetoric: A Slippery Slope

The Secretary of State’s comments on regime change in Iran are particularly troubling. While the aspiration for a democratic Iran, free from the clutches of a repressive regime, is valid, history is littered with the consequences of U.S. interventions under the guise of promoting democracy. From Iraq to Libya, the chaos that often follows such interventions serves as a stark reminder of the complexities involved in reshaping sovereign nations. Rubio’s casual linkage of military strikes with the hope for regime change is a cavalier stance on an issue that warrants far more caution and humility.

The Promise of Punishment: Ignoring the Human Cost

Rubio’s ominous warning of “the hardest hits are yet to come” from the U.S. military is a chilling reminder of the human cost of military conflicts. The reference to more punishing phases without a clear endgame in sight raises serious concerns about the potential for prolonged conflict and civilian suffering. The rhetoric of punishment overlooks the dire consequences for the people caught in the crossfire, further exacerbating the humanitarian crises that often accompany military interventions.

Terrorism and the Price of Oil: Oversimplification of Complex Issues

Labeling Iran as a “terroristic regime” that sponsors and participates in terrorism oversimplifies the complex geopolitical dynamics at play. While Iran’s involvement in regional conflicts and support for proxy groups is well-documented, reducing the narrative to one of pure villainy overlooks the nuances required for diplomatic resolution. Additionally, the acknowledgement of oil prices being impacted by the strikes, with a vague assurance of mitigation, betrays a concerning lack of foresight into the economic repercussions of such military actions.

Conclusion: A Call for Rigorous Scrutiny

In conclusion, Rubio’s justification for the strikes in Iran, wrapped in the language of imminent threat and regime change, demands rigorous scrutiny. The echoes of past misadventures in foreign policy loom large over the present situation. It is imperative that we demand transparency, evidence, and a lawful basis for actions taken in our name. The stakes are too high, and the cost too great, to accept anything less.
