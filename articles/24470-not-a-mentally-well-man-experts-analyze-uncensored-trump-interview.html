'Not a mentally well man': Experts analyze uncensored Trump interview

Based on an article by Chauncey DeVega

Paulo SantosOctober 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that, in a 75-minute interview with Time, President Donald Trump rejected unfavorable polling, blamed the press for public understanding of his statements, defended “annihilating” Iran as a means of creating peace, and declined to rule out invoking emergency powers around the approaching midterm elections. When asked whether ICE agents would be sent to polling places, he said he had not considered it; when asked about the Insurrection Act, he said he “could use it.”

AlterNet asked psychiatrist Bandy Lee and psychologist Vincent Greenwood to assess the interview. Lee described Trump as “not a mentally well man” and argued that his potential for violence was increasing. Greenwood said Trump’s public record and interview responses displayed traits associated with clinical psychopathy. Those are their professional opinions, not diagnoses established by an examination or legal finding; Greenwood argued that his chosen assessment method does not require a personal interview.

The report says Trump also disputed low approval ratings, offered sweeping claims about his economic record, and became angry that Time’s journalists had not asked sufficiently positive questions. The interview, as presented by AlterNet, left unresolved what Trump would actually do during the midterms.

Power is the relevant diagnosis

The psychiatric commentary is arresting, but the institutional fact is more important: Trump is president. He commands the executive branch and can influence the deployment of federal law-enforcement personnel and the military. His refusal to identify limits on those powers therefore cannot be treated as merely strange or emotionally revealing.

Neither Lee nor Greenwood decides whether ICE appears near polling sites or whether an administration attempts to invoke the Insurrection Act. Trump and the officials empowered to execute his directives do. A fixation on his mental state can obscure that chain of agency, converting a question about government power into a spectacle of individual pathology.

Ambiguity serves the officeholder

“I don’t rule anything out or in” is not a policy announcement. AlterNet’s evidence does not establish that Trump had ordered election-related deployments or decided to invoke emergency authority. It does establish that he declined opportunities to state clear limits.

That ambiguity has political utility regardless of its psychological origin. It warns opponents, encourages loyalists to imagine escalation, and preserves deniability if no action follows. The weaker actors in this story—voters, protesters, journalists and targeted communities—do not create that uncertainty. The president does, while holding powers that can give it material force.

The mental-health frame has limits

AlterNet’s headline directs attention toward whether Trump is “mentally well.” That framing risks medicalizing conduct that is already intelligible in political terms: rejecting adverse evidence, demanding flattering coverage, describing mass destruction as peace and presenting coercive authority as personally discretionary.

A clinical label can also become an alibi. If every abuse of power is explained as illness, then advisers, agency leaders, legislators and judges become background scenery. Yet presidential power is never exercised by one nervous system alone. It moves through institutions staffed by people who can authorize, implement, resist or legitimize it.

The record is serious enough

The article is strongest when it stays with Trump’s words and the authority attached to them. His rhetoric about Iran treats catastrophic violence as a solution. His comments about polling and the press cast unwelcome information as corruption. His answer on emergency powers offers no limiting principle. Those facts require scrutiny without turning expert inference into proven diagnosis or a hypothetical deployment into a completed act.

AlterNet weakens its case when it jumps from the interview to the assertion that Trump is capable of “almost anything” to retain power. The interview supports concern about his stated posture and refusal to impose boundaries on himself. It does not establish every future act the article imagines.

A system built around permission

The larger pattern is not simply an erratic president saying alarming things. It is an executive system in which consequential coercive powers can hinge on a president’s judgment, followed by political and legal institutions deciding whether to cooperate.

Personal instability, if present, would magnify that danger; it would not create the machinery. The decisive question is who grants permission when a president tests the boundary. Authoritarian practice becomes governmental reality through compliance: an aide drafts the order, an agency adopts the rationale, a lawyer supplies the justification, and an official carries it out.
