Trump is ‘shrieking behind his own eyes’ as MAGA crumbles: insider

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 8, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower is not psychologyRepublicans made the bargainThe war deserves evidence, not adjectivesA coalition is not propertyThe system behind the spectacle

What happened

Michael Wolff told the Inside Trump’s Head podcast that Donald Trump’s political coalition appears to be deteriorating as the consequences of his second-term decisions accumulate. As reported by AlterNet, Wolff argued that Trump’s escalation in Iran, rising fuel and produce costs, and a reversal in Hispanic support could produce severe Republican losses in the approaching midterms.

Wolff further claimed that Trump understands his position is weakening but remains driven to place himself at the center of the campaign. He also said Brazil’s Bolsonaro family asked Trump not to intervene in Flavio Bolsonaro’s race because his involvement could hurt the candidate. AlterNet provides no polling, election forecasts, price data, or response from Trump or the Bolsonaros to substantiate those claims.

Power is not psychology

The story’s central figure is not merely an anxious politician watching his popularity collapse. Trump is the president of the United States. He commands the executive branch, directs foreign policy within the powers available to his office, dominates his party and can impose consequences on Republicans who resist him.

That institutional power matters more than Wolff’s speculation about what is happening “behind his own eyes.” Presidential psychology may be colorful podcast material, but it is a poor substitute for tracing decisions through the institutions that execute them. The relevant question is not whether Trump is privately shrieking. It is what he authorized, who carried it out and who had the power to stop or constrain it.

Republicans made the bargain

AlterNet describes the Republican Party as having “hitched itself” to Trump, language that makes a sustained political choice sound almost accidental. Republican officials sought the MAGA electorate, accepted Trump’s control of the party and tied their own careers to his. They were not passive passengers.

If Republicans suffer major midterm losses, those losses would not simply be collateral damage from one man’s recklessness. They would be the foreseeable cost of a party structure built around presidential loyalty. Trump may dominate that structure, but elected Republicans and party officials enabled it because it offered them nominations, voters and power.

The war deserves evidence, not adjectives

Wolff calls Iran Trump’s worst political disaster and attributes higher fuel and produce costs—especially pressure on diesel-dependent farming—to the conflict. Those are consequential claims. AlterNet does not supply the underlying price figures, dates, causal analysis or details needed to measure them.

The article is therefore strongest when identifying the political contradiction: Wolff says Trump campaigned against “forever wars” and then entered a conflict without an exit strategy while continuing to escalate. It is weakest when it converts Wolff’s sweeping assessment into settled fact. Calling the war entirely Trump’s creation without documenting the decisions and sequence of events does not expose power; it skips the work required to prove responsibility.

A coalition is not property

Wolff’s discussion of Hispanic voters reveals the contempt embedded in political talk about demographic blocs. He says Trump behaved as though he had gained “ownership” of the Hispanic vote in 2024 and that economic conditions are now driving those voters away. Even if the reported reversal is real, AlterNet offers no supporting survey data or definition of which Hispanic electorates, places or contests are involved.

Voters are not permanent assets acquired by a politician. They make contingent choices under particular conditions. Treating a diverse electorate as something Trump possessed—and then lost—repeats the same political arrogance the article means to criticize.

The system behind the spectacle

This is a story about concentrated power disguised as a story about personal unraveling. Wolff supplies the diagnosis of Trump’s temperament; AlterNet amplifies it. Neither replaces a documented account of the officials, legislators and party institutions that converted presidential impulses into policy.

The larger pattern is institutional cowardice rewarded until the costs arrive. A party surrendered independent judgment to a leader because his coalition delivered power. Now, according to Wolff, war, economic pressure and voter defection may be turning that bargain against it. The scandal is not that one “larger than life” personality reached the presidency. It is that an entire political apparatus repeatedly chose submission and then presented the consequences as one man’s psychological drama.
