Trump is on 'death’s door' and no one’s covering it: ex-MSNBC host

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Joy Reid’s claim is blunt but unproven: President Donald Trump “looks like he’s on death’s door,” she said Wednesday on Shannon Sharpe’s Club Shay Shay podcast. Reid offered no medical evidence that Trump is dying. Her substantive charge was about the press—namely, that journalists soften or ignore signs of decline in the 80-year-old president while having subjected Joe Biden to relentless scrutiny over his age and cognitive fitness.

That distinction matters. Trump’s health cannot be diagnosed from a podcast or his public appearance. The conduct of news organizations, however, can be examined. Reid alleged that Trump’s remarks are frequently edited into coherence and that visible signs of frailty receive little attention. Her criticism targets the institutions deciding what audiences see, what gets cleaned up and which presidential liabilities become sustained national stories.

The comparison Jake Tapper helped create

Reid singled out CNN’s Jake Tapper, co-author of Original Sin, the bestselling 2025 book about Biden’s cognitive decline and efforts by his White House to conceal it.

“Where’s Jake Tapper? Jake, where are you?” she asked.

The point was not that Biden deserved less scrutiny. A president’s capacity to perform the job is plainly legitimate news. Reid’s accusation is that the standard changed when a Republican returned to the White House: Biden’s deterioration became a major accountability story, while Trump’s age, appearance and often disjointed speech are treated less aggressively.

That allegation is more consequential than Reid’s “death’s door” flourish, which outruns the evidence presented. Trump’s health status remains a medical question. Whether editors repeatedly rescue his public statements from their original disorder is an editorial choice—one that can give audiences a more competent version of the president than the president himself supplied.

Editing can become political insulation

Reid said Trump’s statements are often “re-edited to seem coherent.” If broadcasters reduce wandering or confused remarks to polished excerpts without conveying their character, they are not merely shortening material. They may be changing the public’s understanding of how the president communicates and performs.

Trump’s age makes that choice especially salient. He turned 80 in June and was the oldest person ever sworn in as president. He also spent years attacking Biden’s mental fitness. A press corps that amplified those attacks but treats comparable questions about Trump as impolite or peripheral would not be maintaining neutrality. It would be preserving an asymmetry that Trump himself exploited.

Reid argued that the imbalance extends beyond Fox News. In her account, Democratic politicians face more demanding interviews, harder questions and stricter expectations, while Republicans routinely receive greater latitude. “There is no liberal media,” she said. “That is just not real.”

Reid is not a detached observer of that system. She formerly hosted The ReidOut in prime time on the left-leaning MSNBC, which canceled the program in early 2025. She now hosts The Joy Reid Show through YouTube, Substack and podcast platforms. That history does not settle her argument, but it does locate it: this is a former cable host accusing the national press—including institutions adjacent to her former employer—of applying its standards unevenly.

Petty scrutiny for Obama, deference for Trump

Reid contrasted Trump’s treatment with the coverage Barack Obama received. She recalled controversies over Obama wearing a tan suit, saluting while holding a coffee cup and presiding over the failed rollout of the Affordable Care Act website. Those incidents varied greatly in substance, but each became an occasion for extended judgment about Obama’s competence or respect for the presidency.

If Obama had used presidential power as Trump has, Reid argued, he “probably would have been impeached and convicted.”

“He’s black,” she added.

That is Reid’s interpretation, not a demonstrated counterfactual. But the comparison identifies how political misdirection works. Attention can be lavished on symbolic breaches by one president while far more consequential conduct by another is normalized through repetition. The distortion does not require journalists to endorse Trump. It requires only that they treat his abnormalities as familiar, convert his incoherence into usable clips and reserve their sharpest institutional scrutiny for politicians operating under stricter expectations.

Reid’s most defensible conclusion was therefore narrower than her most explosive line. “If the press covered Trump like they would have covered Obama or Biden, we might have a chance of a more educated public,” she said.

The public does not need reporters to pronounce Trump terminally ill. It needs them to show his remarks and conduct accurately, investigate credible questions about his fitness and apply the same evidentiary standard they used when Biden held power. Anything less turns editing, framing and selective intensity into political protection.
