'Desperate' Trump is 'just making a lot of this up': Wall Street expert

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 17, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power belongs to the presidentBlame as economic policyThe article’s own loose arithmeticThe larger pattern

What happened

AlterNet reports that investor and former journalist Steve Rattner accused President Donald Trump of misrepresenting inflation and gasoline prices while blaming former President Joe Biden for current economic conditions. Appearing on MSNBC’s “Morning Joe,” Rattner said inflation was below 3 percent when Biden left office and had since risen; the article gives both 3 percent and 3.7 percent as the current figure without resolving that discrepancy.

Rattner also rejected Trump’s description of Biden-era inflation as the “worst” in American history, pointing to substantially higher rates in 1980 and the 1920s. He attributed a 25 percent increase in inflation to Trump’s tariffs, while host Joe Scarborough characterized Trump’s claims as lies.

On gasoline, Rattner said the price shown in his comparison was about $4.35 per gallon, versus roughly $3 when Biden left office—a difference he calculated at about 44 percent. The report does not establish whether those figures are national averages, regional prices, or prices from another defined market.

The power belongs to the president

Trump is not a spectator interpreting somebody else’s economy. He occupies the presidency and has made tariffs a flagship policy. That gives him institutional power to shape trade policy and substantial power to influence the economic agenda, even though no president directly controls every price.

The relevant political question is therefore not whether Biden once presided over higher inflation or gasoline prices. It is whether Trump’s claims accurately describe the conditions he inherited, the conditions under his administration, and the effects of policies he chose. According to the comparisons Rattner presented, they do not.

Blame as economic policy

Blaming Biden serves a clear political function: it assigns responsibility backward while Trump exercises power in the present. Pandemic-era inflation becomes a permanent alibi, even as the reported measurements move in the wrong direction under the current administration.

That maneuver also exploits a basic confusion between price levels and inflation. Slower inflation does not mean prices return to their earlier levels, and isolated historical peaks do not establish who is responsible for today’s movement. A president claiming credit for every favorable number while treating every unfavorable one as his predecessor’s inheritance is not offering an economic account. He is constructing immunity from accountability.

The article’s own loose arithmetic

AlterNet’s central case is weakened by imprecision. It alternates between current inflation of 3 percent and 3.7 percent, and it presents gasoline figures without identifying their geographic or statistical scope. Those omissions matter because a factual rebuttal depends on cleaner evidence than the claim it is rebutting.

The piece also says tariffs “caused” inflation to jump 25 percent when what it actually establishes is that Rattner made that attribution. His analysis may be correct, but the article does not provide the methodology needed to treat causation as independently demonstrated. Scathing television commentary is not a substitute for clearly sourced measurements.

The larger pattern

The underlying pattern is more durable than any single chart: political power is used to make policy, while political messaging is used to relocate responsibility. Trump’s account, as described by AlterNet, asks the public to judge Biden by the worst conditions during his term and Trump by conditions he says he intends to create.

That asymmetry is the point. The stronger actor claims agency over good news and helplessness before bad news, while the predecessor becomes an all-purpose culprit. Coverage should expose that allocation of power and responsibility with disciplined evidence. Otherwise, it risks turning accountability into another televised argument over who delivered the sharper insult.
