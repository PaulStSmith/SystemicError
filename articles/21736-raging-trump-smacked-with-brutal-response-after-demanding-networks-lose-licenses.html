Raging Trump smacked with 'brutal' response after demanding networks lose licenses

Based on an article by Nicole Charky-Chami

Paulo SantosAugust 18, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump Wants the Regulator to Do the Dirty Work

The Setup

Trump did not just complain about ABC and NBC refusing to air his primetime address. He asked the FCC to punish them by revoking their licenses. Anna Gomez, the commission’s only Democrat, responded with the obvious: the agency has no authority to slap broadcasters for declining to carry a blatantly political speech that remained available online anyway.

That is the whole story in miniature. The networks made an editorial decision. Trump tried to turn it into a licensing threat.

Where the Power Actually Sits

The important fact is not whether ABC or NBC said yes. It is that Trump reached for state power after being denied airtime. He used a federal pressure point to try to discipline private media into carrying his message.

That is not a principled dispute over access. It is an attempt to convert public authority into leverage over speech. The decision-maker here is Trump. The enabling instrument, if he could get it, would be the FCC. The target is not just two networks. It is the idea that broadcasters should understand refusal as a risk.

The Fake Controversy

The article gestures toward “editorial decisions” as if this is a routine programming quarrel. It is not. Editorial independence is not the scandal. The scandal is a president trying to punish it.

Trump’s speech itself shows the logic. He was not offering a neutral public-service address. According to the report, he used the platform to promote his SAVE America Act ahead of the midterms, push claims about Chinese election interference, and attack the networks that declined to air him. That is political messaging, not some civic emergency the public was deprived of.

The Real Target

Gomez’s response matters because it cuts through the usual institutional fog. She named the move for what it was: a naked attempt to bully broadcasters. That should not be controversial language. It is the correct description of a president demanding punishment for editorial refusal.

The networks, meanwhile, are being treated as though they committed some breach by declining to carry a partisan speech. They did not. The stronger claim in the article is actually the simpler one: other presidents have also not been guaranteed live coverage. That is how press discretion works. The issue is not whether every presidential address must be televised. The issue is whether a sitting president can use the state to coerce compliance when the answer is no.

The Pattern

This is the same authoritarian pattern every time: manufacture grievance, recast resistance as illegitimacy, then threaten institutions until they submit. The rhetoric is always about fairness, access, and public duty. The method is always intimidation.

The misdirection here matters. The story can be told as a media etiquette dispute. It should be understood as a test of whether a president can weaponize a regulator against broadcasters for refusing to become his stenographers. That is the political meaning of the episode. Not confusion. Pressure. Not misunderstanding. Coercion.

The System

Democratic institutions do not fail only when the law is broken. They fail when powerful officials normalize the use of state machinery to punish independent judgment. Trump is doing exactly that, and the FCC becomes dangerous the moment anyone inside it treats his demand as a serious policy question instead of the abuse it is.

The lesson is not that broadcasters need better manners. It is that concentrated executive power will always try to turn dissent into a licensing problem, a staffing problem, or a “process” problem. That is how democratic erosion works in practice: not with a sudden coup, but with routine threats aimed at making obedience feel administrative.
