Trump official tries to do an end-run around Supreme Court justice

Due Process Is Not a Deportation Ban

The Power Sits With the Administration

The Trump administration controls the machinery of deportation. The Justice Department chooses which orders to challenge, the Department of Homeland Security determines how removals are conducted, and Immigration and Customs Enforcement places people on planes. Immigrants facing removal hold none of that institutional power. Their narrow protection is the opportunity to learn where the government intends to send them and argue that they could face persecution or torture there.

Politico reported that the Justice Department asked the Supreme Court to stay a lower-court ruling governing removals to third countries. Because emergency appeals from the 1st Circuit are assigned to Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson, the filing also requested referral to the full Court if Jackson did not grant relief immediately.

Notice Recast as Obstruction

Attorney General Todd Blanche described the ruling as blocking “all third-country removals.” But Aaron Reichlin-Melnick of the American Immigration Council disputed that characterization, pointing to the judgment itself.

As quoted in the reporting, the judgment required DHS to pursue removal first to countries with which a person has an identified legal connection, provide effective notice of any proposed third-country destination, and allow a meaningful opportunity to raise a reasonable fear of persecution or torture there. Those are constraints on how the government exercises its power—not, on the text presented, a prohibition against exercising it.

That distinction is the story. The administration’s framing turns procedural safeguards into an attack on enforcement. It treats the government’s inability to deport someone without adequate warning or a meaningful fear review as though the government had been stripped of authority altogether.

The Emergency Is Government Inconvenience

The filing’s asserted urgency depends on collapsing delay into paralysis. If even a short period for a response is presented as intolerable, then ordinary judicial consideration becomes an obstacle by definition.

The foreseeable consequence is a legal system in which speed belongs to the state and risk belongs to the person being removed. The administration seeks immediate freedom to act; the immigrant may be sent somewhere unfamiliar before a claim of persecution or torture can be meaningfully heard. Calling that arrangement “efficient” would merely sanitize the imbalance.

An End Run Around the Assigned Justice

The request concerning Jackson is unusual because the Court’s own assignment system directs these emergency appeals to her. Politico interpreted the filing as anticipating that Jackson might allow immigrant-rights advocates time to respond while leaving the lower-court ruling temporarily intact—and as suggesting that the full Court might be more receptive to the administration’s urgency claim.

That does not establish improper motive by Justice Department lawyers. It does expose the institutional tactic: ask the assigned justice for immediate relief while simultaneously preparing to move around her if she permits even brief adversarial process.

Jackson has previously accused the Court of applying unstable rules in Trump administration cases, describing its approach in 2025 as “Calvinball” jurisprudence. The present filing does not prove her broader charge. But the structure of the request reinforces the concern behind it: procedure appears negotiable when it might slow executive power.

The Weaker Party Becomes the Culprit

The administration’s language directs attention toward “illegal aliens” and away from the officials deciding whether people receive notice and a chance to describe feared harm. That is classic scapegoating architecture. The state presents its own procedural obligations as a burden imposed by the people subject to its force.

The framing is especially revealing because the reported judgment did not transfer deportation authority to immigrants. It required the government to disclose its intended destination and hear a limited objection grounded in persecution or torture. Describing those requirements as disabling enforcement substitutes resentment for legal precision.

Rights That Exist Only When Convenient

The larger pattern is not confusion about what the lower court ordered. It is the political conversion of due process into alleged sabotage whenever process constrains executive speed.

Institutional power becomes harder to scrutinize when officials describe safeguards as bans, review as obstruction, and vulnerable people as the cause of administrative difficulty. The government still holds the power to remove. The dispute is whether it must exercise that power with notice, a hearing, and some regard for the consequences. A system that treats those minimal conditions as intolerable is not merely enforcing immigration law. It is arguing that executive convenience should determine when legal protections count.
