Judge lashes out at DOJ lawyer claiming 'executive privilege' on Trump’s posts

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 31, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that a federal judge sharply rejected Justice Department lawyer Stanley E. Woodward Jr.’s claim that “executive privilege” prevented him from interpreting President Donald Trump’s public Truth Social post saying the East Potomac Golf Course would close on Nov. 15. The judge emphasized the obvious defect in that position: Trump had published the statement for everyone to read.

The dispute followed the federal government’s removal of more than 430 trees from Hains Point, which includes the public golf course, trails and parkland. The court had ordered the government to provide notice before major action on the island, but, according to AlterNet’s account of earlier reporting, officials notified the court only minutes before the close of business on a holiday weekend.

At the hearing, the judge called Woodward to testify about who directed the government to circumvent her earlier ruling. Woodward maintained that there was no closure plan on Oct. 26, referred eventually to a “directive,” and declined to assign meaning to Trump’s post. The account does not establish who issued that directive or fully explain the authority behind it.

Power was not ambiguous

The institutional power here belonged to the executive branch. Federal officials controlled the land operation, possessed the relevant internal information and decided when the court would learn what they were doing. The judge could issue an order, but compliance depended first on officials treating that order as binding rather than as an obstacle to manage.

That distinction matters. This was not a misunderstanding among equally situated parties. The government reportedly cut down hundreds of trees and supplied notice at the edge of a holiday weekend despite a judicial requirement for advance warning. Whatever internal chain of command produced those actions, the executive branch held both the machinery and the information.

Privilege as an escape hatch

Executive privilege ordinarily concerns confidential presidential communications. Trump’s social-media post was public. Invoking privilege to avoid explaining the government’s position did not protect a secret communication identified in the reporting; it attempted to make a public presidential statement unusable the moment a court asked what it meant.

Woodward’s refusal also converted a concrete accountability question into a semantic fog. The judge was not asking him to speculate about an overheard rumor. She was confronting him with the president’s published declaration and the government’s denial that a plan existed. Calling that conflict a problem of “meaning” shifted attention away from the government’s conduct and toward an artificial debate over whether plain words can be acknowledged.

The missing decision-maker

The central unresolved question is not whether the judge became angry. It is who ordered the action, who knew about the court’s instruction and who chose the timing of the notice. AlterNet’s account shows the judge trying to identify that chain of responsibility, but it does not provide the answer.

Coverage centered on courtroom fireworks can inadvertently help the government by turning institutional defiance into personality-driven spectacle. The judge’s exclamation is vivid; the executive branch’s opacity is consequential. One produces a headline. The other determines whether judicial orders constrain officials in practice.

Government by fait accompli

The broader pattern is rule by completed action: exercise executive power first, notify the supervising institution when intervention is least practical, and then resist efforts to reconstruct responsibility. Courts can review decisions, but review loses force when the government creates irreversible facts before meaningful scrutiny can occur. More than 430 felled trees cannot be restored by a later clarification.

The political danger lies in that imbalance between the speed of executive action and the slowness of accountability. When officials can treat disclosure as negotiable, public statements as uninterpretable and decision-making authority as untraceable, formal oversight remains on paper while power operates through faits accomplis.
