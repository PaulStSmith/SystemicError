'What a coward!' Scorn hits single Trump ally for failing to halt 'most dangerous' move

President Donald Trump's second term has been mired by scandals from the very beginning. However, New York Times foreign affairs columnist Thomas L. Friedman, a three-time Pulitzer Prize winner, highlighted on Monday what he considers to be the "most dangerous" among all of the "terrible" actions taken by the president.

Friedman pointed to the firing of Erika McEntarfer, the Senate-confirmed head of the Bureau of Labor Statistics, as the most alarming action of Trump 2.0. McEntarfer's tenure ended after she released a jobs report that Trump didn't like.

"Trump, in effect, ordered our trusted and independent government office of economic statistics to become as big a liar as he is," wrote Friedman in a New York Times column.

Friedman called the ouster the most dangerous thing Trump has done — then expressed astonishment at the second "most dangerous" thing to happen.

Senior Trump officials "most responsible for running our economy — people who in their private businesses never would have contemplated firing a subordinate who brought them financial data they did not like — all went along for the ride," he noted.

Friedman scolded those officials for immediately providing cover for the move, rather than calling it out.

"So Trump’s rotten character is now a problem for our whole economy," Friedman railed.

He called out Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent by name.

"Shame on each and every one of them — particularly on Bessent, a former hedge fund manager, who knows better and did not step in. What a coward!" he wrote.

He ended with a dire warning for America, concluding that while he's a "congenital optimist, for the first time I believe that if the behavior that this administration has exhibited in just its first six months continues and is amplified for its full four years, the America you know will be gone."

"And I don’t know how we will get it back," wrote Friedman.

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Undermining of American Democracy: Trump's Dangerous Precedent

In what can only be described as a chilling chronicle of authoritarian overreach, Thomas L. Friedman's recent column in the New York Times throws a scorching spotlight on President Donald Trump's most alarming action yet: the firing of Erika McEntarfer, the Senate-confirmed head of the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS). This move, as laid bare by Friedman, isn't just a scandal; it's a siren warning of a democracy in peril.

Trump's Assault on Economic Integrity

At the heart of this debacle is Trump's insatiable desire for affirmation, even at the cost of the integrity of American economic statistics. By dismissing McEntarfer over a jobs report that wounded his ego, Trump essentially mandated the BLS to morph into a propaganda machine, echoing his own distortions of reality. "Trump, in effect, ordered our trusted and independent government office of economic statistics to become as big a liar as he is," Friedman asserts. This isn't merely a firing; it's an outright assault on the factual foundation upon which policy, investment, and economic confidence are built.

The Complicit Silence of Economic Officials

What amplifies this dangerous precedent even further is the deafening silence from the senior officials who, under any other administration, would have safeguarded such institutions. The collective shrug from Trump's economic team, as they provided cover for an action so fundamentally corrosive to the principles of good governance, is staggering. Friedman's disdain for Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, and others in positions of economic stewardship who failed to intervene, is both justified and necessary. By prioritizing loyalty to Trump over duty to country, these officials have not only eroded their own legacies but have laid bare their cowardice for public record.

The Rotten Core of Trump's Administration

Friedman's diagnosis of Trump's "rotten character" becoming a systemic issue is spot on. The implications of such character flaws at the highest levels of government are far-reaching, potentially corrupting the very essence of American democracy and its economic system. This isn't merely about one man's hubris; it's about a contagion of moral and ethical decay that threatens to undermine the bedrock of the nation.

A Dire Warning for America

The conclusion of Friedman's column is nothing short of apocalyptic for the health of American democracy. The fear that the America known and cherished could vanish under the weight of such continued abuse of power is a harrowing prospect. Friedman's warning isn't just the pessimistic musing of a disillusioned observer; it's a clarion call for vigilance and action.

In Conclusion

Friedman's column is a scathing indictment of not just President Trump, but of a broader systemic failure to protect the integrity of crucial institutions. The firing of Erika McEntarfer is emblematic of an administration that views truth as a malleable inconvenience. This incident, and the complicity surrounding it, should alarm anyone concerned with the health of American democracy.

The question now is not just about how to resist such overreach, but how to rebuild and ensure the resilience of American institutions in the aftermath. Friedman may profess to be a "congenital optimist," but optimism alone will not suffice. It will take concerted effort, accountability, and perhaps most importantly, a rekindling of civic duty to navigate out of these troubled waters. The America we know is at stake, and the time for action is now.
