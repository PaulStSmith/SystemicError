'Ambush': CNN's Dana Bash shocked by Trump's 'set-up' of South African president

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 22, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:Analyzing the Ambush: Trump's Meeting with RamaphosaThe SetupThe AmbushThe DiscomfortThe False ClaimsThe Orchestrated EffortThe Conclusion

CNN's Dana Bash described president Donald Trump's meeting with his South African counterpart Cyril Ramaphosa as an "ambush."

The two world leaders met Wednesday afternoon in the Oval Office, where White House aides showed a video of speeches made by the Economic Freedom Fighters party calling for violence against white farmers – which Ramaphosa insisted was "not government policy" and represented the views of "a small minority party."

"As we monitor what is happening in the Oval Office, you see a video playing that is in the Oval Office of the White House," Bash said. "We're going to again monitor it, but luckily we have White House reporters here who have on their phones running, explanation and reporting from the White House pool about what is happening, and as you saw, if there's a video playing there, it is a pretty good indicator that things have devolved in this meeting."

Ramaphosa sat in visible discomfort as Trump made false claims about genocide against white South Africans, and his invitation to discuss the issue was ignored.

"There's been some arguing inside the Oval Office about those claims that South African president has encouraged Trump to listen to other voices that he said would dispute those claims," said New York Times reporter Tyler Pager. "Trump didn't seem to be interested in hearing about that, and so now he has asked his aide, Natalie Harp, to play this video in the Oval Office. It's obviously playing right now, and our understanding is the point of this video is to back up some of the claims that Trump has made about South Africa. It seems this is clearly a well-orchestrated effort. They brought screens, there's not usually TVs in the Oval Office, and and they were prepared to do this."

Associated Press reporter Seung Min Kim said it was more than that.

"It's an ambush," she said."

"I was going to say set-up," Bash agreed, "but, you know ambush works, too."

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Ambush: Trump's Meeting with Ramaphosa

CNN's Dana Bash didn't mince words when she described President Donald Trump's meeting with South African President Cyril Ramaphosa as an "ambush." This wasn't your garden-variety diplomatic tête-à-tête. Here's why:

The Setup

In the heart of the Oval Office—a place historically reserved for discussions of utmost diplomacy—Trump and his aides decided to play a video of the Economic Freedom Fighters party calling for violence against white farmers. This move is as subtle as a sledgehammer to the face of international diplomacy. Ramaphosa was quick to clarify that these views represented a "small minority party" and were "not government policy." Yet, the damage was clear. The intention? To paint a narrative, regardless of its accuracy or the discomfort it causes.

The Ambush

Let's call a spade a spade, or in this case, an ambush an ambush. The term used by Associated Press reporter Seung Min Kim and later endorsed by Bash, perfectly encapsulates the essence of the meeting. It wasn't a dialogue; it was a deliberate attempt to corner Ramaphosa, employing a strategy that reeks of bad faith.

The Discomfort

The discomfort of Ramaphosa was palpable, a testament to the unnerving nature of the meeting. Trump's false claims about genocide against white South Africans were not just uncomfortable; they were a blatant disregard for truth and a slap in the face to diplomatic decorum. Ramaphosa's attempt to introduce other voices and perspectives was ignored, showcasing a refusal to engage in meaningful dialogue.

The False Claims

Trump's false claims and the decision to play the inflammatory video underline a worrying trend of using misinformation as a political tool. This isn't just about being ill-informed or misinformed—it's about actively choosing to spread falsehoods to serve a particular agenda.

The Orchestrated Effort

Tyler Pager of the New York Times notes the well-orchestrated effort to play this video in the Oval Office, highlighting the premeditated nature of this ambush. Screens were brought in, a clear indication that this was not a spur-of-the-moment decision but a calculated move designed to put Ramaphosa in an uncomfortable position.

The Conclusion

What we witnessed was not diplomacy. It was a spectacle, a show designed to embarrass and pressure. It was a misuse of the Oval Office's power and prestige, turning what should have been a meeting of mutual respect into a stage for domestic political theater.

The decision to describe this meeting as an ambush is not just accurate; it's necessary. It calls out the maneuver for what it was: a deliberate attempt to manipulate and misinform for political gain. In the landscape of international relations, where the stakes are high and the repercussions far-reaching, such tactics are not just regrettable; they're reprehensible.

Diplomacy should be about building bridges, not burning them. Unfortunately, this meeting was a stark reminder of how far we've strayed from that ideal.
