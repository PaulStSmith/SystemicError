“I can tell you right now, what people want more than anything in those communities is they don’t want performative, they want performance,” Moore told “Fox and Friends” on Wednesday.

“They’re not looking for someone to walk around with military fatigues and a big gun. They’re looking for someone who actually understands how to make people feel safe and how to make people actually safe in their communities. Our National Guards are not trained for this.”

Moore said he was happy to stand on his record of success in fighting crime in the Old Line State, stressing that his success did not come from deploying the military to police the streets of his state.

“And so all I’m saying is this, if we want to have a serious conversation about violent crime, we can have a serious conversation about violent crime, and I would love to be able to share the multitude of things that we are doing in the state of Maryland, where we are surging support for local law enforcement, surging support for predictive analytics and technologies, surging support for community groups and allowing more supports for the mayor and the county executive, and the people on the ground doing the work, and we were able to receive some really encouraging and historic results in Baltimore and around the state of Maryland, and I never once had to mobilize the National Guard in order to accomplish accomplish those feats.”

In April, Baltimore’s homicide rate dropped to a historic low, according to The Baltimore Banner. Last month, Moore wrote that “In the first half of 2025, Baltimore had the fewest homicides ever recorded. Compared to this time in 2022, we’ve seen a 62% decrease.”

Moore’s emphasis on his record of success without deploying the National Guard did not appear to please host Lawrence Jones.

“I’m way over time, Governor,” Jones said, closing down the interview.

Our Analysis:

Maryland Governor's Bold Stance on Military Deployment: A Critique

In a recent appearance on "Fox and Friends," Maryland’s Democratic Governor, Wes Moore, delivered a scathing critique of former President Donald Trump's decision to deploy National Guard troops and federal officers onto the streets of Washington, D.C. His words were not just a mere dismissal but a pointed condemnation of what he termed a “performative” act—a stark illustration of political theater over substantive, long-term solutions to crime.

"What people want more than anything in those communities is they don’t want performative, they want performance," Moore asserted. This statement cuts to the heart of the issue, emphasizing a preference for genuine, effective crime-fighting strategies over the superficial display of military might. Moore’s critique underscores a fundamental misunderstanding—or perhaps, disregard—by the Trump administration for what it actually takes to make communities safer.

Moore's argument that the National Guard is not trained for municipal policing is not just an opinion; it's a fact. Military personnel are prepared for combat and national defense, not neighborhood patrolling or engaging in community policing efforts. Deploying them in such a capacity is not only a misallocation of resources but a dangerous oversimplification of the complexities surrounding urban crime and community safety.

The Success of Maryland's Approach

Moore didn’t stop at criticism. He contrasted Trump’s approach with his own record in Maryland, offering a glimpse into proactive measures that have yielded "encouraging and historic results" in reducing crime. He spoke of "surging support for local law enforcement, predictive analytics and technologies, community groups," and more. These strategies, grounded in understanding and addressing the root causes of crime, starkly contrast with the heavy-handed, militarized approach criticized.

The results speak for themselves. Moore cited a significant drop in Baltimore’s homicide rate, reaching a historic low, with a 62% decrease in homicides compared to the previous year. These numbers are not just statistics; they represent lives saved, communities stabilized, and a step toward dismantling the cycle of violence that plagues many urban areas.

A Missed Opportunity for Meaningful Dialogue

The response from "Fox & Friends" host Lawrence Jones, who hurriedly ended the interview citing time constraints, was disappointingly predictable. This interaction was a missed opportunity for a meaningful dialogue on effective crime-fighting strategies. Instead of delving deeper into Moore's successful approach in Maryland—a potential blueprint for other states—the conversation was cut short, leaving viewers with more questions than answers.

Conclusion

Governor Wes Moore's critique of the Trump administration's deployment of the National Guard in Washington, D.C., highlights a critical divide in approaches to public safety. Moore’s emphasis on performance over performative actions, coupled with tangible results in Maryland, offers a compelling argument for reevaluating how we address crime in American cities.

The conversation between Moore and Jones on "Fox & Friends" may have been brief, but it was revealing. It shed light on the ongoing debate over the best methods to ensure public safety—a debate that should prioritize effective, evidence-based strategies over superficial shows of force. As Moore’s experience in Maryland demonstrates, when it comes to fighting crime, substance should always triumph over spectacle.
