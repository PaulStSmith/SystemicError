More Republicans are weighing new career options as chaos devours GOP power

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 23, 20265 min read

(REUTERS)

With Democrats having enjoyed a series of double-digit victories in recent elections and President Donald Trump dogged by weak approval ratings, a long list of GOP lawmakers won’t be seeking reelection in the 2026 midterms. Former Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene (R-Georgia) left Congress altogether in early January, and Republicans ranging from Sens. Mitch McConnell (R-Kentucky), Thom Tillis (R-North Carolina) and Joni Ernst (R-Iowa) to Rep. Don Bacon (R-Nebraska) decided against seeking reelection but plan to serve out the rest of their terms.

In an article published on February 22, Washington Post journalists Marianna Sotomayor, Eric Lau and Anna Liss-Roy report that many GOP lawmakers are weighing their options as chaos and uncertainty plague their majorities.

“Like no time in recent memory,” the Post reporters explain, “lawmakers are setting their sights anywhere but Capitol Hill — setting the stage for crucial midterm elections that will feature fewer incumbents than usual and ensure more fresh faces in Congress next year. As of late February, 68 Senate and House members have said they will not seek reelection. An unprecedented 31 of those lawmakers have filed to run for another office. In some cases, members exiting the House are hoping to enter the Senate. In others, lawmakers are seeking to return home as governor.”

Sotomayor, Lau and Liss-Roy explain, “While looming midterms often prompt a swell of retirements, the number of announced departures at this point in the election cycle is notably higher than in other recent cycles, according to a Washington Post analysis…. Some who are leaving Capitol Hill are frustrated with how little is getting done.”

The reporters note that GOP lawmakers are “retiring or running for another office in larger numbers than Democrats.”

“Twenty-nine House Republicans are not seeking reelection, but only 10 are retiring from public office,” Sotomayor, Lau and Liss-Roy observe. “Ten are running for governor, while eight are hoping to move to the Senate. House Republicans have been plagued by internal battles over the past four years that they have been in the majority, including the historic ousting of Speaker Kevin McCarthy (R-California) in 2023. Several Republicans surveyed said that the fights and legislative stagnation are not why they are retiring. In other cases, those unsavory parts of the job are a factor.”

Bacon still resents the way McCarthy was ousted as House speaker after far-right then-Rep. Matt Gaetz (R-Florida) tripped a “motion to vacate.”

The Nebraska Republican told the Post, “I was so mad when eight people out of 223 take down Kevin McCarthy, who most of us liked. That was a tough one, that takes a little toll.”

Our Analysis:

GOP Exodus: A Symptom of Deeper Malaise?

In a political landscape where chaos and uncertainty reign supreme, the recent Washington Post article unpacks an unfolding drama that could very well reshape the American legislative branch in years to come. The decision by a substantial number of GOP lawmakers to not seek reelection in the 2026 midterms is a glaring beacon of the turmoil within the ranks, a turbulence that has far-reaching implications beyond mere party lines.

The Great GOP Flight

The article mentions an astonishing figure: 68 Senate and House members have announced they will not seek reelection. Even more striking, a significant portion of these departures are from the Republican side, with 29 House Republicans bowing out. This isn’t just a trickle; it’s a flood. The fact that many are not just retiring but actively seeking other offices suggests a strategic retreat rather than a wholesale abandonment of political aspirations. But why the mass exodus?

Internal Battles and Legislative Stagnation

The internal strife within the GOP, notably the historic ousting of Speaker Kevin McCarthy, is highlighted as a contributing factor. The chaos instigated by far-right elements within the party, such as the “motion to vacate” led by then-Rep. Matt Gaetz, underscores a factional warfare that has rendered governance secondary to ideological purity tests. The frustration expressed by Rep. Don Bacon over McCarthy’s ousting is indicative of the broader disillusionment felt by some members over the direction their party has taken.

Running From or Toward?

While some Republicans surveyed in the article claim that the internal fights and legislative stagnation are not their primary reasons for leaving, it’s hard to ignore these factors as significant contributors to the current climate of dissatisfaction. The pursuit of other offices could be seen as an attempt to find relevance and fulfillment in roles perceived as more impactful or less contentious. This trend of seeking gubernatorial or senatorial positions reflects a strategic recalibration within the GOP, possibly hinting at a desire to influence from different vantage points.

The Implications for the GOP and American Politics

The departure of seasoned lawmakers and the ensuing introduction of fresh faces in Congress could lead to a significant shift in the legislative landscape. On one hand, this could be an opportunity for rejuvenation and the introduction of new ideas. On the other, it could further exacerbate the polarization and instability within the halls of power, as inexperienced legislators navigate the complex and often treacherous waters of American politics.

This mass exodus and the reasons behind it paint a picture of a party at a crossroads, struggling with its identity and direction in the face of internal divisions and external pressures. The GOP’s ability to adapt and unify in the aftermath of these departures will be crucial in determining its future viability and effectiveness as a political force.

In conclusion, the unfolding drama of GOP retirements is more than a mere political sideshow; it’s a symptom of a deeper malaise plaguing the party and, by extension, American politics. The 2026 midterms loom large not just as a test of electoral might but as a referendum on the soul of the Republican Party. Whether this will lead to a course correction or further descent into chaos remains to be seen, but one thing is clear: the stakes could not be higher.
