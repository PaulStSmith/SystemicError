'Dystopian': Leaker unveils Trump’s step-by-step scheme for power

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 31, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Miles Taylor, a former Homeland Security deputy chief of staff in Donald Trump’s first administration, predicts that Trump will use continued Republican control of Congress after the midterms to consolidate executive power. Taylor’s scenario includes contesting unfavorable election results, pursuing political critics, defying courts, deploying troops domestically, restricting voter registration, pressuring the press and the Federal Reserve, and resisting the eventual transfer of power.

The article cites Justice Department attempts to challenge roughly 20,000 mail ballots in California and Washington—an effort it says a judge blocked—and House passage of the SAVE Act as evidence underlying Taylor’s forecast. But its most alarming claims, including ballot seizures, permanent troop deployments and an uncertifiable 2028 election, are predictions rather than reported events. Taylor argues that electing a Congress willing to investigate and constrain the administration is the remaining practical check.

The power is institutional

Trump holds the presidency. His administration directs the Justice Department, oversees the FBI and DHS, commands the military and possesses emergency authorities. Congress controls legislation, appropriations and oversight. Courts can block unlawful measures, while election officials administer voting under federal and state law.

That hierarchy matters because the story is not fundamentally about MAGA voters, protesters or political rhetoric. It is about whether officials controlling federal institutions can convert ordinary legal powers into instruments of political entrenchment—and whether Congress and the courts will stop them.

Congress is not scenery

Taylor places the decisive enabling role with congressional Republicans. His argument is conditional: if the party retains control, it could pass voting restrictions, frustrate oversight and protect the administration from meaningful investigation. AlterNet’s framing sometimes compresses that chain of responsibility into Trump “taking total control,” as though power simply accumulates around one man.

It does not. Legislators vote. Justice Department officials authorize litigation. Agency leaders carry out directives. Judges decide whether challenged acts survive. Treating those actors as background understates their agency and makes institutional surrender look inevitable rather than chosen.

Forecast is not fact

The article’s strongest evidence is narrower than its headline. A judge reportedly blocked the attempt to invalidate the mail ballots, the Senate had stalled the SAVE Act, and the Supreme Court had blocked early stages of the troop-deployment effort described by Taylor. Those are real constraints, not proof that every remaining check will collapse.

Taylor may be identifying a coherent trajectory, but AlterNet presents his sequence with too little separation between documented conduct, plausible escalation and worst-case projection. “Leaker unveils” suggests disclosure of a settled operational plan. The supplied reporting instead establishes that a former official published a forecast based on his experience, interviews and interpretation of recent actions. Precision strengthens the warning; inflated certainty weakens it.

Fear can misdirect responsibility

The article repeatedly describes what Trump “will” do, but its own evidence supports “Taylor predicts” rather than certainty. That distinction does not absolve anyone. It identifies where accountability belongs: with officials who initiate abusive actions, lawmakers who authorize or excuse them, and institutions that decline to enforce their own limits.

Apocalyptic language can also obscure the immediate decisions beneath it. The relevant questions are concrete: Who authorized the ballot litigation? Which legislators support restrictions on registration? Which officials would execute an unlawful order? Who possesses the authority to refuse? A democracy is not dismantled by atmosphere. It is weakened through identifiable decisions by people with power.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is executive aggrandizement enabled by institutional loyalty to a leader over the limits of an office. Taylor’s scenario depends less on one dramatic seizure than on repeated cooperation: partisan legislation, selective enforcement, intimidation of critics and gradual erosion of independent review.

AlterNet is right to center the stakes, but it blurs warning and confirmation. The sharper conclusion is not that collapse has already been proved. It is that constitutional restraints function only when officials empowered to enforce them choose enforcement over submission—and that every vague account of an inevitable “power grab” risks concealing the specific people whose choices would make it possible.
