Trump just dug up some 1790's laws to grab more power

Loaded Guns in the Code

The Trick Is Not Breaking Everything

The reporting here is plain: Trump is not only bulldozing law; he is scavenging old statutes, dusting them off, and using their neglect as a weapon. The Justice Department is moving to revive the Alien Terrorist Removal Court for deportations in classified settings and prize courts for seizing Iranian oil tankers. Ford’s central warning is sharper than the usual “he is lawless” script: the administration is exploiting what Congress left lying around.

Power Belongs to the People Filing the Motion

The real institutional power sits with the executive branch. Trump’s team decides what to revive, how to frame it, and whom to target. The Justice Department is not discovering neutral legal tools; it is choosing to activate dormant machinery for coercive ends. That is the point. These statutes do not spring to life by accident. Someone with authority decides to turn old law into fresh force.

Congress Built the Trap and Walked Away

Ford’s criticism of Congress matters because it identifies the other half of the problem. Lawmakers passed these authorities and then abandoned them without serious repeal or revision. That is not a procedural footnote. It is institutional negligence that leaves constitutional junk in the closet for a future strongman to weaponize. If Congress does not want buried powers revived, it cannot keep pretending legislative garbage collection is optional.

The Framing Is Too Soft on Intent

The familiar story says Trump “violates” law. That is true, but incomplete. He also uses law strategically, especially the old, obscure, and barely remembered kind. Calling this confusion or opportunism misses the deliberate structure of the project. The Alien Enemies Act invocation against Venezuelan nationals, and the move toward closed deportation proceedings through the ATRC, show an administration not merely ignoring restraints but searching for legal forms that can launder aggression.

Old Statutes, New Cruelty

There is nothing innocent about reviving moribund law to target immigrants or seize vessels in wartime settings. The obscurity is part of the advantage. Classified deportation procedures reduce scrutiny. Prize courts turn state violence into administrative process. This is how authoritarian politics often works in the American system: not always by smashing institutions outright, but by using their neglected corners to make coercion look technical.

The Larger Pattern

The deeper lesson is that democratic breakdown is not only produced by presidents who break rules. It is also produced by legislatures that leave dangerous powers on the shelf and call that governance. Trump is exploiting a preexisting institutional failure: a legal order full of dormant weapons and a political class too lazy or cowardly to disarm them. If democracy is going to survive authoritarian operators, it will need more than outrage at lawbreaking. It will need Congress to stop leaving loaded guns in the code.
