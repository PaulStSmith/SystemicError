Hassett on Tuesday told CNBC, “We’re comfortable that inflation has come way down — the 5% on average, for Joe Biden.”

“It’s probably a little less than half of that right now,” he continued. “And the trajectory is really, really, really good.”

Inflation for December 2024, President Joe Biden’s last full month in office, was 2.9%. It increased to 3.0% in January.

Inflation for September 2025, the last month for which there is Bureau of Labor Statistics data, was 3.0%.

Hassett went on to say that “inflation is one of those things that has a lot of momentum, if you look at the charts…”

“Even though it’s been increasing for five straight months as of September?” CNBC’s Carl Quintanilla asked.

“Well, I guess if you look at it from January, there’s ups and downs and seasonals, but yeah, it surprised on the downside, people were expecting it to accelerate it and it didn’t.”

Voters one week ago took to the polls and delivered a resounding message to Republicans and President Donald Trump. Exit polls show that voters’ number one concern was the economy and affordability, as they decided to put Democrats into office.

And national polls show the same result: the high cost of living, the state of the economy, and affordability are all top of mind for voters, who give President Trump low marks in those areas.

One week ago, on Monday, the day before the election, CNN reported, “61% of Americans think Trump has made the economy worse. Could that impact tomorrow’s elections?”

The New York Times shows President Trump’s current average approval rating is 42%, and his disapproval rating is 55%.

In mid-October, CNBC reported that on the economy, Trump’s approval was “the lowest of any CNBC survey during either of Trump’s two terms.”

Our Analysis:

The Illusion of Economic Stability: A Closer Look at Inflation Claims

In a recent display of selective optimism, Kevin Hassett, director of the White House’s National Economic Council, attempted to paint a rosy picture of the current inflation situation, insisting that inflation is “way down.” This assertion, made during a CNBC interview, not only flies in the face of recent data but also seems to be at odds with the perceptions of the American electorate, as reflected in both polls and recent election outcomes.

The Dubious Decline of Inflation

Hassett’s claim that inflation has significantly decreased during President Joe Biden’s tenure—boasting a reduction to “probably a little less than half” of the previous 5% average—is misleading at best. Despite his confident assertion that the trajectory of inflation is “really, really, really good,” the reality, as per the Bureau of Labor Statistics, tells a different story. Inflation in December 2024 was recorded at 2.9%, inching up to 3.0% in January and maintaining this level through September 2025. This stubborn persistence of inflation rates contradicts Hassett’s overly optimistic narrative.

Economist Justin Wolfers’ response, featuring a graph mocking Hassett’s description of the inflation trajectory, highlights the disconnect between the administration’s portrayal and the actual economic indicators. This graphical retort underscores the skepticism shared by many regarding the administration’s economic assessment.

Voter Sentiment and the Economy

Adding insult to injury, recent elections and polls have served as a referendum on the Republican handling of the economy under President Donald Trump. With the economy and affordability topping voter concerns, the electoral shift towards Democrats signifies widespread discontent with the current economic policies. CNN’s report that 61% of Americans believe Trump has worsened the economy further cements this dissatisfaction. Trump’s approval ratings, especially concerning the economy, are at their nadir, reflecting a clear disapproval of his economic stewardship.

The Real Picture

Hassett’s attempts to downplay the continuous rise in inflation over five months as mere “ups and downs and seasonals” is not only a gross simplification but also an evasion of accountability. The administration’s reluctance to confront economic realities head-on is evident in their responses to inflation concerns.

The disconnect between the administration’s economic narratives and the electorate’s perceptions is stark. The insistence on a favorable inflation trajectory, in the face of contrary evidence and public sentiment, is indicative of a broader misalignment with the American public’s priorities and experiences.

Conclusion

In summary, Kevin Hassett’s assertions regarding the state of inflation and the economy lack grounding in the current economic climate and public sentiment. The administration’s portrayal of an improving economy does little to address the concerns of voters who are feeling the pinch of the high cost of living and economic instability. As recent elections have shown, voters are increasingly unwilling to accept rosy narratives at face value, especially when their daily experiences paint a markedly different picture. The political repercussions of this disconnect are a testament to the fact that when it comes to the economy, perception is reality—and the current reality is not as optimistic as Hassett would have us believe.
