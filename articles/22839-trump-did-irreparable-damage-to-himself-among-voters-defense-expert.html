Trump did 'irreparable damage' to himself among voters: defense expert

Trump Turned Iran Into a Loyalty Test, Not a Strategy

The Source Says This Is a Credibility Problem. It Is Bigger Than That.

Robert Fox’s column says Trump’s Iran war has badly damaged his standing, stretched the military, depleted munitions, and pushed peace talks into the ditch. That is true as far as it goes. But the central fact is not Trump’s “credibility.” It is that the people with actual power chose escalation, then dressed the consequences up as momentum.

This was not some fog-of-war mishap. Trump resumed attacks on Iran. Pete Hegseth helped turn the Pentagon into a loyalty filter. Netanyahu stands to benefit politically from continued confrontation. Those are decisions made by actors with institutional authority, and the damage lands on everyone else.

The Command Chain Is the Story

Fox points to depleted stockpiles and overworked personnel aboard the USS Abraham Lincoln. That matters because it shows the state being used as a consumable. The military is not being treated as a public institution with finite capacity. It is being treated as a blunt instrument for presidential theatrics and electoral positioning.

That is the real political meaning of “near its breaking point.” It means the costs of executive choices are being pushed down the chain onto service members, logistics, readiness, and ordinary people who will pay more at the pump. The system is not failing abstractly. It is being strained by design.

Hegseth’s Loyalty Purge Is the Mechanism

Fox’s mention of Hegseth’s “constant purges” of officers and promotions for MAGA loyalty is not a side note. It is the mechanism that makes the whole project more dangerous. A military chain of command that rewards factional obedience over competence does not produce discipline. It produces a politicized apparatus that mistakes loyalty for capability.

That is how institutional cowardice becomes policy. The purge is not merely personnel management. It is an attempt to align military power with movement politics. Fox’s framing nods at this, but the deeper point is that Trump’s war effort is being filtered through a domestic authoritarian logic: reward the loyal, discard the independent, and treat dissent inside the state as disloyalty.

The Calendar Is Driving the War

Fox notes that meaningful peace talks are unlikely before the U.S. midterms and Israel’s October election. That detail matters because it strips away the pretense that the timing is incidental. The conflict is being managed on electoral clocks, not diplomatic ones.

This is where the article’s “damage to Trump” framing becomes too polite. The war is not just hurting Trump politically; it is being conducted in a way that treats politics as the battlefield and the battlefield as campaign material. Netanyahu, Fox says, may profit from further confrontation in election debates. That is not strategic clarity. It is elite opportunism wrapped in national-security language.

Iran Is Not Being Read, Only Provoked

Fox describes Iran as a harsher, less pliable adversary than Trump expected, and notes that Ayatollah Mojtaba Khamenei responded with a call for Muslim unity against a named enemy. Whether one likes either side is irrelevant. The point is that Trump appears to have entered a long war with an opponent whose leadership can also turn confrontation into legitimacy.

That is the trap of macho politics: it assumes the other side will blink first, then discovers that escalation can harden resistance. The result is not victory. It is open-ended damage, rising energy prices, and a U.S. military being stretched to preserve a narrative.

What This Really Shows

The article is right that Trump’s Iran war is wrecking his standing. It is too narrow if it stops there. The larger pattern is more corrosive: executive power is being used to convert military force into a loyalty-performance system, while political elites on both sides of the conflict exploit escalation for their own calendars and domestic advantage.

That is the sickness here. Not confusion. Not accident. Not a few bad decisions in isolation. A deliberate political architecture that makes war serve factional power, treats institutional strain as acceptable collateral, and counts the human and economic costs only after the damage is already done.
