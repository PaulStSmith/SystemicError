Trump's biggest 'cheerleader'​ raging over broken promise — and set to strike him down: Religion expert

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

Paulo SantosOctober 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that some evangelical voters who strongly backed Donald Trump are turning against him over what religion professor J. Derrick Lemons describes as a broken promise of foreign-policy isolationism. Lemons said anger over an unspecified war, rising gas prices, and the lack of an exit strategy could damage Trump and Republicans in the midterms, though the article provides no direct evidence that these voters are “set to strike him down.”

Lemons identified the disaffected “cheerleaders” as evangelical supporters who helped elect Trump after religious leaders rallied behind him. RawStory cites a June Reuters poll in which about 54 percent of evangelicals said the war conflicted with their Christian values and Trump’s approval among them was 52 percent. The report does not give the poll’s year, methodology, sample size, or wording, and it does not establish that evangelicals are abandoning the Republican Party.

The bargain was political

Trump’s evangelical coalition was not built solely from spontaneous religious enthusiasm. Lemons’ research describes a 2016 closed-door meeting where organizers vetted questions with Trump in advance, allowing him to give answers tailored to evangelical priorities. According to Lemons, that event helped persuade millions of uncertain voters.

That is political organization dressed in devotional language. Religious leaders supplied credibility and voter access; Trump supplied policy victories. The actual institutional power remained with the president, his administration, and the party controlling government. Pastors could bless the arrangement and mobilize congregations, but the White House decided which promises became state action.

Power made the promise expendable

Lemons argues that Trump retained evangelical loyalty because he delivered major first-term wins. In that transactional relationship, the movement’s influence depended on its ability to reward compliance and punish betrayal.

The reported foreign-policy reversal tests whether that leverage was real. Evangelical voters may feel deceived, but the administration controls military policy. Their anger matters only if it becomes organized electoral defection—and RawStory offers an expert’s assessment and one polling snapshot, not proof that such a rupture has occurred.

The headline outruns the evidence

The article converts qualified dissatisfaction into a dramatic electoral revolt. “Set to strike him down” is not supported by the reporting. Lemons says the midterms look bad and describes anger within evangelical circles; the cited poll records disapproval. Neither establishes how those voters will cast ballots, whether they will stay home, or whether their views caused a broader partisan shift.

That framing also relocates agency. Evangelicals are presented as the decisive force poised to punish Trump, while the administration’s decision to depart from the isolationism Lemons says Trump promised receives little concrete examination. The story does not identify the war, its dates, the relevant military decisions, or which officials designed and executed the policy. The weaker actors’ reaction becomes the spectacle; executive power escapes scrutiny.

Faith as campaign infrastructure

Lemons’ account reveals a durable pattern: political operatives cultivate religious authority, religious leaders translate partisan goals into sacred obligation, and voters are told that supporting a politician serves a divine project. His description of efforts to “re-Christianize” America and portray Trump’s 2024 return as a resurrection shows how conventional power seeking can be elevated beyond ordinary political accountability.

When policy disappoints, that machinery does not necessarily disappear. It can redirect the same moral absolutism toward betrayal. The danger in such an arrangement is structural: leaders teach followers to evaluate power through prophecy and loyalty, then discover that a politician who secured their votes still controls the bargain.

A coalition is not its ruler

The larger story is not that evangelicals suddenly acquired the power to bring Trump down. It is that a constituency repeatedly treated as his political bedrock may be confronting the limits of access without control.

RawStory’s evidence supports discontent, not electoral collapse. But the distinction exposes the system clearly: organized religious leaders helped manufacture consent, voters supplied legitimacy, and executive officials retained the authority to choose policy. When coverage turns that hierarchy into a melodrama about angry former cheerleaders, it obscures the central fact of political power—those who make the decision bear responsibility for its consequences.
