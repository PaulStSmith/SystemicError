Economist blows whistle on multi-billion dollar plot to rob Social Security

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosSeptember 19, 20264 min read

Social Security Is Being Sabotaged in Plain Sight

The Damage Is Administrative

The Trump administration publicly promises to protect Social Security while closing regional offices, reducing staff, cutting independent research, and leaving vulnerable people without accessible help. Economist Teresa Ghilarducci describes this as an acceleration of a staffing decline that began under the Obama administration. The relevant question is not whether benefits have already been formally abolished. It is whether the government is preserving the machinery required to deliver them. It is not.

Neglect Is a Policy Choice

The people making these decisions hold institutional power: the president directs the executive branch, administration officials set priorities, and Congress controls the revenue decisions that determine the program’s future. Retirees, disabled people, and overburdened field-office workers do not.

Closing offices and starving the system of expertise can then be presented as mere inefficiency. That framing conceals agency. An institution made harder to use will produce delays, confusion, and public frustration. Its architects can point to the resulting dysfunction as evidence that government does not work—after ensuring that it cannot.

“Reform” Without Revenue Means Cuts

Ghilarducci offers a useful test for the language of modernization: does the proposal bring additional revenue into Social Security? If not, “reform” is usually a euphemism for reducing benefits or forcing people to wait longer for them.

Raising the retirement age is especially dishonest. Ghilarducci’s research finds that people in their late fifties have little chance of remaining employed through 65 and scarcely any realistic chance of working until 70. Telling them to work longer does not create jobs, reverse age discrimination, or repair worn-out bodies. It simply converts a benefit cut into a lecture about personal responsibility.

The White House’s claim that Trump delivered “No Tax on Social Security” does not answer the charge. A tax provision for many current seniors is not proof that the administration is maintaining Social Security’s staffing, accessibility, research capacity, or long-term revenue. It changes the subject from institutional erosion to a marketable slogan.

The Young Are Being Prepared for Surrender

Political elites have spent decades teaching younger workers that Social Security’s decline is inevitable. That message is useful because resignation makes retrenchment easier. A public convinced that a program will disappear is less likely to defend it and more likely to accept private accounts, delayed retirement, or smaller benefits as unavoidable.

Younger workers would absorb the damage from both directions. They are being told to finance retirement individually after the collapse of pensions and the failure of 401(k)s and IRAs to provide adequate security for most workers. At the same time, weakened Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid would leave them supporting older relatives with their own stagnant wages and accumulated debt.

That is not generational conflict. It is a transfer of responsibility from public institutions and employers onto families, packaged as fiscal realism.

Insecurity Is Profitable

The broader record described by Ghilarducci is consistent: union power declined, profits rose, wages failed to match productivity, pensions disappeared, healthcare costs migrated onto workers, and debt became the financial system’s substitute for security. Social Security remains one of the few major institutions that interrupts that arrangement by guaranteeing income outside the discretion of employers and financial markets.

That makes administrative sabotage politically useful. A dependable public benefit limits the leverage of private finance. A weakened one creates customers—for investment products, credit, and whatever privatized substitute can be sold to people frightened about old age.

Claims that Trump allies intend to profit personally from privatization remain allegations in the source, not established facts. The structural incentive requires no speculation, however: dismantling a public guarantee opens more of retirement to private extraction.

Manufactured Failure Is the System

The looming benefit shortfall is routinely described as though it were a natural disaster. It is not. According to Ghilarducci, roughly 35 percent of retirees depend on Social Security for essentially all their income, yet the president and Congress have not supplied the revenue needed to prevent projected cuts when the trust fund reaches its deadline.

The political pattern is larger than Social Security. Officials weaken a public institution, describe its deterioration as inevitable, and then prescribe further withdrawal as the cure. Responsibility is redirected toward aging workers who supposedly failed to save, younger people who supposedly expect too much, or an abstract demographic crisis that no identifiable officeholder caused.

But institutions do not hollow themselves out. Presidents choose administrators and priorities. Congress chooses whether to fund benefits. Employers choose whether to provide retirement security. The cruelty lies not only in the prospective cut, but in the elaborate effort to make a sequence of political decisions look like nobody’s decision at all.
