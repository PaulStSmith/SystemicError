Melania tells Fox she’s 'not really' into Trump’s 'tacky' obsession

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 21, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A joke built on presidential excessThe People’s House as personal brandingA carefully managed counter-image

Melania Trump did not call her husband’s gold-heavy White House makeover “tacky.” What she did, during a Friday appearance on Fox & Friends, was reject host Brian Kilmeade’s joking prediction that the Christmas decorations would feature “more gold.” Raising a hand, the first lady answered: “No. Not really.” It was a small exchange, but a revealing one: even Donald Trump’s wife would not endorse extending his gilded aesthetic into the holiday display.

A joke built on presidential excess

Melania Trump appeared on Fox to promote her new series about life in the White House. Near the end of the segment, host Ainsley Earhardt praised the Christmas decorations, and the first lady invited the panel to visit. Kilmeade then supplied the gold joke.

It worked because Trump’s fixation is not subtle. According to AlterNet, he has added gold-painted molding and ceiling details, urns, coasters and a thick “Trump” paperweight to the White House during his second term. Maggie Haberman and Jonathan Swan reported in Regime Change that the president personally glued gold ornaments to the Oval Office fireplace.

AlterNet also reports that Trump proposed placing a golden winged figure atop a 250-foot triumphal arch and had a collection of Washington horse statues coated in gold. The recurring theme is less decoration than self-mythology: public space treated as a setting for the president’s preferred image of wealth and grandeur.

Melania’s “not really” should not be inflated into a political break or even a comprehensive verdict on the renovations. She was responding to a joke about this year’s Christmas decorations. But her dismissal punctured the premise that Trump’s taste automatically defines every part of the White House simply because he occupies it.

The People’s House as personal branding

Presidents inevitably alter the building where they live and work. The political question is what those alterations communicate. Trump’s additions turn the executive mansion toward the visual language of his private brand, complete with his own name rendered as décor.

That matters because the White House is not a personal resort or a monument awarded to its current tenant. Trump holds the institutional authority to reshape its presentation, and his aesthetic choices repeatedly collapse the distinction between the presidency and the president’s ego. The gold does not establish strength or prosperity. It advertises his preferred version of both.

The media framing around Melania risks misdirecting attention toward a familiar marital sideshow: Does she secretly dislike his taste? Is she rebelling? The reported exchange establishes only that she did not want “more gold” associated with the Christmas display. The consequential actor remains the president, who has used his control over the executive mansion to impose that aesthetic throughout it.

A carefully managed counter-image

Holiday decoration has traditionally fallen largely within the first lady’s domain, and AlterNet describes Melania Trump’s choices as mostly conventional. Some displays nevertheless became spectacles of their own. Her stark 2019 design was criticized as joyless; the 2018 scarlet trees prompted comparisons to The Handmaid’s Tale; and commenters likened the 2017 décor to the horror film The Babadook.

Her Fox appearance was itself part of another image-making project. The series she was promoting was produced and released by Jeff Bezos’ Amazon, which had already produced a documentary about her. AlterNet reports that the documentary held a 9 percent Rotten Tomatoes rating, while reviews of the newer series were harsh: The Guardian called it a “dead-eyed advert that barely manages to masquerade as entertainment,” and The Telegraph compared it to an “audition for a movie supervillain.”

That context makes the gold exchange more than idle banter, but less than dissent. Melania was selling a controlled account of White House life on a friendly network when an unscripted joke invoked the president’s conspicuous vanity. Her response briefly separated her presentation from his. It did not challenge the power that allowed him to turn the nation’s executive residence into another extension of the Trump name.
