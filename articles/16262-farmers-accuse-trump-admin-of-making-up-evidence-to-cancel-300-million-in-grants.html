Farmers accuse Trump admin of making up evidence to cancel $300 million in grants

By Paulo Santos

USDA’s Grant Purge Wasn’t Reform. It Was Administrative Cruelty with a Press Release

The Target

The source story is straightforward enough: the USDA canceled 49 of 50 grants in a $300 million land-access program, giving recipients two business days’ notice and leaving one grantee in limbo. The program was designed to help farmers of color and historically underserved producers acquire and hold land. Agroecology Commons, which had already won an injunction in earlier litigation, was the lone grantee not immediately terminated.

Who Held Power

This was not an accident, and it was not a mystery. The real power sat with the Trump administration, USDA leadership, and the officials executing the purge, including Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins and associate administrator Steven Peterson. They controlled the money, the notices, and the narrative. The grantees were not powerful actors with leverage over the agency; they were recipients dependent on federal approval that the USDA could delay, deny, or destroy.

That matters because the article’s surface framing leans on the language of “wasteful spending” as if the issue were confusion over administrative standards. It wasn’t confusion. It was a decision to weaponize bureaucracy against programs the administration disliked.

The Pretext Machine

Peterson’s letter does the usual rhetorical work of modern state sabotage: it takes a broad political attack and dresses it up as fiscal hygiene. The administration says the program did not align with USDA priorities and was not in keeping with congressional intent, then cites lurid examples like gazebos, massages, a camper, and a $130,000 office-supply budget.

But the reporting is clear on the crucial point: the grantees quoted here say those expenditures were not theirs, and several say they have no idea where those figures came from. The USDA declined to explain its claims. That is not evidence of misuse. It is an accusation launched from behind the shield of bureaucratic authority, then repeated as if repetition were proof.

Blame Directed Downward

The pattern here is familiar. Power commits the harm, then points at the weakest participants and calls them irresponsible. The recipients are made to look suspect for asking to administer grants they were already awarded. The agency delays approvals, starves the projects of basic support, then treats the resulting dysfunction as evidence that the projects were flawed from the start.

Even the appeal process reflects the insult. Many grantees were told their terminations were not appealable because the decision was “general applicability,” not tied to specific program criteria. That is a bureaucratic confession disguised as procedure. If the decision was general, then the point was political cancellation, not case-by-case oversight.

The Real Damage

The article keeps coming back to delay, obstruction, and uncertainty because that is the actual mechanism of harm. Kansas Black Farmers Association says nearly a year passed before the USDA supplied needed approvals. Other grantees describe scarce communication and a lack of institutional support. That is not merely inefficiency. It is a method of strangling programs without the political cost of openly repealing them.

This is how modern administrative power works when it wants to erase a policy but avoid owning the repeal: underfund it, delay it, accuse it, terminate it, and let the target spend months proving it is not guilty of the crimes invented to justify the cutoff.

The Broader Pattern

What this story reveals is not a isolated dispute over grant management. It is a governing style built on scapegoating, selective outrage, and bureaucratic force. Programs meant to broaden land access for farmers of color are recast as fraud risks. Concrete public goods are slandered with vague scandal language. The state then uses its own paperwork to make the damage look procedural.

That is the deeper political meaning here. The administration did not just cut funding; it used the language of waste to punish the people least able to absorb the blow, while keeping the officials who made the decision safely out of frame. The real waste was not the grant program. It was the deliberate destruction of public capacity by people who call sabotage accountability.
