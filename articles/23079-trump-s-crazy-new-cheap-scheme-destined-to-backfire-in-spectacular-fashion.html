Trump's crazy new cheap scheme destined to backfire in spectacular fashion

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Trump’s Oil Heist Is Not Policy. It’s Plunder.

Power, First

The source describes a simple chain of power that the political press likes to blur: Trump is using U.S. military force, congressional inaction, and a sham “deal” to seize control of Venezuelan oil. That is the story. Not diplomacy. Not stabilization. Not even crude realpolitik. It is coercion dressed up as management.

When a president can turn the military into an investor, the question is not whether the rhetoric sounds reckless. The question is who is authorizing extraction, who is profiting from it, and who is being told to absorb the consequences.

The Deal Is the Crime

The source says Trump is calling this “the biggest oil deal in world history” while claiming a 100-year lease and pretending the proceeds will refill the Strategic Petroleum Reserve. That is not a policy program; it is a laundering story for theft. The reserve claim is especially telling because the source itself notes the logistics don’t work for Venezuelan heavy crude. In other words, the excuse collapses under basic scrutiny.

What remains is the actual function of the operation: seize a sovereign country’s asset, hand the contract to a politically connected billionaire with an arrest warrant hanging over him, and treat the public like fools who won’t notice the extraction.

The Old Colonial Script

The source is right about the pattern, even if its language is sometimes overheated: this is the same imperial script the U.S. has used for generations. Backing austerity, crushing democratic outcomes, protecting capital, then calling the wreckage unfortunate. Venezuela’s oil has been treated as an entitlement by outside powers for decades, and the source ties this directly to the 1989 Caracazo and the 1953 U.S. overthrow of Mossadegh in Iran.

That is the larger political logic here. Resource nationalism is acceptable only until a poor country insists on controlling its own wealth. At that point, democracy becomes negotiable, constitutions become disposable, and force gets recast as policy.

The Enablers

The source does not describe a lone Trump caper. It describes a coalition of enablers. Delcy Rodríguez is depicted as clinging to power after losing an election, while the U.S. military props up the arrangement. Republican leaders in Congress, according to the source, know the scheme is absurd and stay quiet because silence may pay better later. That is not oversight. That is anticipation of a lobbying payout.

The same applies to the lawyers, financiers, and political fixers orbiting the deal. Alejandro Betancourt López is presented as the kind of figure these operations always attract: accused, connected, useful, and protected. Rudy Giuliani’s presence in the story is not incidental. It is the signal that corruption has graduated from side effect to operating principle.

Misdirection as Cover

The source also catches the other classic maneuver: move money in secret, then let the public argue about everything except the theft. The $13 billion allegedly collected from seized crude, the Qatar account, the missing money, the unanswered questions after the earthquakes in June, the refusal to return funds to the country that needed them most. That is the anatomy of plunder followed by denial.

And when the damage becomes obvious, the blame will be sprayed downward: on chaos, on incompetence, on foreign resistance, on abstract instability. But deliberate extraction is not confusion. It is a decision. The institutions around Trump are not confused either; they are yielding.

The Real Lesson

The political meaning of this story is not that Trump is uniquely reckless, though he is. It is that American power still behaves as if weaker countries are inventories to be managed, not sovereign societies to be respected. The source’s outrage is justified because the underlying structure is familiar: militarized theft, elite complicity, and a Congress that discovers principle only after the money has moved.

That is the system. The scandal is not only the robbery. It is how many institutions make robbery feel procedural.
