In June, an individual posing as House Intelligence Committee Chairman Rick Crawford (R-AR) reached out via Telegram to at least two members of Congress — Rep. Eric Burlison (R-MO) and former Rep. George Santos (R-NY), the Washington Post reported Saturday. The impersonator claimed involvement in a “project” with First Lady Melania Trump and urged them to install a suspicious “Phoner App” to learn more.

“There is a Project I am working on with Melania Trump. I recommend to make you the coordinator,” the fake Crawford messaged Santos, according to The Post.

While Burlison ignored the request, Santos did install the app — though he says the interaction ended before any of his personal information was compromised. The exchange took place just days before Santos is set to begin a seven-year prison sentence for aggravated identity theft and other charges, per the report.

He told The Post: “I’m dealing with my own issues with the law, and now I have to be a victim of this garbage."

Santos was expelled from Congress in December 2023 following revelations of widespread fabrications in his personal biography and numerous ethics violations. In April, he was convicted in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of New York on charges of aggravated identity theft and wire fraud.

Santos received an 87‑month prison sentence and is required to report to a federal facility by July 25.

This June incident fits into an increasing pattern of scams — some employing AI — targeting government figures and their staff by impersonating them. Investigators are probing a separate June campaign that used AI-generated voice and text messages, supposedly from Secretary of State Marco Rubio.

Crawford’s office confirmed that others had received voice memos imitating his voice, likely produced via AI.

“Over the last few months, we have seen this happen to several high-ranking Administration officials,” Crawford told The Post.

He continued: “It is very easy for anyone to fall victim to these nefarious efforts. I strongly encourage all Americans to stay informed on the threat and take proactive action to protect themselves by reviewing the public service information provided by the FBI on AI-powered impersonations.”

Our Analysis:

Capitol Hill's Latest Drama: The Sophisticated Impersonation Scams

Capitol Hill has once again found itself embroiled in a narrative that reads more like a subplot of a cyberpunk thriller than the day-to-day operations of the United States government. The latest episode involves sophisticated impersonation scams targeting lawmakers and their staff, with the impersonators employing everything from Telegram messages to AI-generated voice memos. Let's dissect this circus of deception and incompetence, shall we?

The Plot Thickens with Telegram and the Phony Phoner App

In what seems to be a laughably audacious move, an individual masquerading as House Intelligence Committee Chairman Rick Crawford (R-AR) reached out via Telegram to at least two members of Congress. The targets? Rep. Eric Burlison (R-MO) and the infamous former Rep. George Santos (R-NY). The impersonator dangled a carrot in the form of a "project" with none other than First Lady Melania Trump, urging them to install a suspicious "Phoner App".

Let's pause for a moment and ponder the absurdity. The idea that a project of any legitimacy involving the First Lady would be coordinated through a Telegram message and a dubious app is the stuff of satire. Yet, here we are.

George Santos, a figure already mired in controversy and on the brink of serving a seven-year prison sentence for aggravated identity theft and other charges, took the bait and installed the app. The irony of Santos, a man expelled from Congress for fabrications and ethics violations, falling victim to a scam is almost poetic. Almost.

AI Impersonations: The Future of Scams

The article then shifts focus to a broader, more disturbing trend of scams employing AI to impersonate government figures. A separate incident in June involved AI-generated voice and text messages purportedly from Secretary of State Marco Rubio. The technology to create convincing fakes is not only here, but it's also accessible enough to target high-profile individuals in government.

Rick Crawford's office confirmed receiving voice memos imitating his voice, likely produced via AI, highlighting the ease with which these digital doppelgangers can be crafted. Crawford's warning to Americans to stay informed and protect themselves against these "nefarious efforts" is a stark reminder of the evolving landscape of cybersecurity threats.

The Bottom Line

What this saga underscores is a glaring vulnerability at the heart of our government's operations and their approach to digital security. The fact that these scams can not only be attempted but can reach as far as they have, speaks volumes about the preparedness (or lack thereof) of our elected officials to tackle the digital age's unique challenges.

Moreover, the involvement of figures like George Santos in these narratives only adds layers of irony and incompetence to an already concerning situation. It's a wake-up call — or at least, it should be — for tighter security measures, better education on digital threats, and a more discerning approach to communication within the halls of power.

In a world where technology blurs the lines between truth and fiction, vigilance and skepticism are invaluable allies. The Capitol Hill impersonation scams are not just a series of unfortunate events; they're a symptom of a larger issue that needs immediate and serious attention. Let's hope this latest episode sparks more than just outrage but action to safeguard the integrity of our government's operations against the digital age's dark arts.
