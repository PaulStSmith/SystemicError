The Anachronism of Compromise: When the Center Cannot Hold

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 11, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

In a previous analysis, we framed the divide within America’s political discourse as a clash between arithmetic and absurdity: Lawrence O’Donnell’s quiet faith in the machinery of government versus Stephen Colbert’s scathing laughter at its decay. O’Donnell, the institutionalist, argued that five Democrats breaking ranks to end the government shutdown represented strategy, not surrender. Colbert, the satirist, saw farce—proof that Democrats had once again mistaken appeasement for progress. Both perspectives carried truth, but we placed our conclusion squarely between them, acknowledging the necessity of O’Donnell’s process and the emotional truth of Colbert’s despair.

That middle ground made sense then. It no longer does.

Because now, with California Governor Gavin Newsom erupting in fury over those very same defections—calling the compromise “pathetic” and a “betrayal of working Americans”—it’s clear that something fundamental has shifted. The arithmetic no longer adds up when one side is using dynamite instead of a calculator.

The Old Faith in the System

Lawrence O’Donnell’s worldview is forged in a Senate that no longer exists. His measured defense of procedural compromise comes from a time when institutional respect still held the line—when politicians could disagree ferociously and still inhabit a shared reality. His calm, deliberate tone reflects the ethos of a man who still believes in the power of the deal, in the dignity of process, in the machinery of democracy grinding toward justice, however slowly.

But that assumption—of a functioning system that rewards patience and punishes extremism—has collapsed. The GOP of today is not the party of negotiation; it is a cult of demolition. It doesn’t want government to function, because dysfunction is the product. Every shutdown, every court blockade, every norm shattered is a feature, not a flaw. To treat them as equal partners in governance is to pretend you’re working with colleagues while standing across from arsonists.

O’Donnell’s approach, in that light, feels less like realism and more like ritual. His Senate arithmetic depends on a common moral denominator that no longer exists. The GOP has walked away from the table, yet O’Donnell still sets out the plates.

Gavin Newsom and the Politics of Fury

Newsom’s eruption over the shutdown deal wasn’t calculated—it was visceral. He wasn’t raging at Trump; he was raging at Democrats who, in his view, handed Trump a victory. His outrage wasn’t about the numbers, or even the policy specifics. It was about the symbolism of caving—about what it means when a party that claims to defend democracy keeps normalizing its dismantling.

And here’s the telling detail: none of the eight Democratic defectors are up for reelection. A few are retiring; the rest are safe enough to afford moral ambiguity. That’s precisely what infuriates the activist base—the sense that the establishment treats moral courage as a luxury item, exercised only when politically convenient. They can afford to compromise because they no longer have to answer to voters. It’s a betrayal that cuts deeper than ideology; it’s existential.

Newsom’s fury speaks for a generation of Democrats—and independents—who are done mistaking civility for courage. His “don’t bend the knee” moment may sound performative, but it captures a raw, necessary truth: you can’t compromise with a movement that wants to erase compromise itself.

Colbert’s Laughing Dirge

Colbert, ever the cultural mirror, saw it coming. His satire turned the Senate’s surrender into farce because the alternative was despair. The joke—that the longest shutdown in American history ended with “jack squat”—wasn’t just about Democratic weakness; it was about the futility of playing by rules that no longer constrain the other side.

Colbert’s laughter was elegiac. It was the laughter of a man attending democracy’s open-casket funeral and cracking jokes to stay sane. In his hands, the absurdity of compromise becomes the punchline of an empire in denial. His comedy works because it captures what people feel: that every procedural victory is a moral loss, and every bipartisan handshake is another inch of rope.

The End of Symmetry

What O’Donnell, Colbert, and Newsom each reveal—in their own ways—is that America is trapped in an asymmetrical conflict pretending to be a debate. The GOP treats power as conquest; Democrats treat it as stewardship. Republicans weaponize chaos; Democrats negotiate over its terms. And the result is a slow, orderly surrender dressed up as governance.

When O’Donnell defends compromise, he’s defending an ecosystem that assumed both predators and prey were bound by rules. But the predators learned long ago that the rules don’t apply when no one enforces them. The filibuster, the courts, the norms of oversight—these were meant as guardrails. Now they’re the very instruments of obstruction.

Newsom’s fury, then, is the emotional response to an institutional failure. He’s watching his own party cling to outdated civility while the other side sharpens knives. His outrage isn’t reckless; it’s diagnostic. He’s naming the disease O’Donnell refuses to see: that bipartisanship, in an age of anti-democratic extremism, is not virtue but self-delusion.

The Naïveté of Nostalgia

Calling O’Donnell’s stance “anachronistic” is generous. It’s not just out of date—it’s dangerously naive. His kind of faith in process is the civic equivalent of believing that if you follow the rules hard enough, the people breaking them will eventually stop. But authoritarian movements thrive precisely because proceduralists keep waiting for fairness that never comes.

Compromise only works when both sides want a functioning state. When one side wants power at any cost, and the other keeps bargaining in good faith, you don’t get balance—you get collapse. Democracy doesn’t die when the authoritarians seize control; it dies when the defenders of democracy refuse to admit the threat is real.

The tragedy of O’Donnell’s worldview is that it’s not cynical enough for the world he lives in. His Senate is gone, his math no longer works, and his faith in the institution has outlived the institution itself.

The Limits of Good Faith

What, then, is the alternative? Newsom’s fury and Colbert’s mockery aren’t solutions; they’re symptoms of a broken equilibrium. Rage and ridicule may expose the rot, but they can’t repair it. Yet between naïve proceduralism and performative outrage lies a middle path—one that requires a new kind of realism.

It starts with acknowledging that compromise is not inherently virtuous. In an environment where one party seeks to dismantle democracy, the duty of the other is not to find common ground but to defend the ground itself. “Meeting halfway” with fascism is not bipartisanship; it’s surrender. The moral imperative now is asymmetrical resistance—a willingness to use power aggressively in defense of the republic, not apologetically in defense of optics.

That doesn’t mean abandoning the rule of law; it means wielding it. It means understanding that the Constitution isn’t a suicide pact and that procedural purity means nothing in a graveyard.

When Compromise Becomes Collapse

The shutdown deal exposed the fracture line within the Democratic Party: the institutionalists versus the insurgents, the senators who still believe in decorum versus the governors who know it’s killing them. It’s not just a policy debate—it’s a philosophical one about whether democracy can survive without abandoning some of its old habits.

O’Donnell would say yes, if we just keep faith in process. Newsom would say no, that the only way to save the system is to break the illusion that it’s still functioning. Colbert, ever the cynic, would say it’s already too late—and at times, he’s not wrong.

But if democracy is to endure, it cannot do so on the fumes of nostalgia. The Senate that O’Donnell loves has become a stage set, its rules weaponized against itself. The only path forward is through hard, unapologetic realism: to recognize that the age of polite compromise ended the moment one party decided democracy was optional.

Epilogue: The Arithmetic of Survival

In the final calculus, O’Donnell’s arithmetic was elegant but obsolete. His equations assumed a balance of power that no longer exists. Colbert’s laughter was bitter but honest. And Newsom’s fury, though messy, might be the first honest sound the Democratic Party has made in years.

Democracy will not be saved by those waiting for the old Senate to reappear. It will be saved—if it is saved at all—by those willing to stop pretending the arsonists can be reasoned with. The arithmetic has changed. The question now is whether the defenders of democracy are willing to do the math.
