Trump's 'incredibly vulgar' threat reveals secret tactic: Ex-State official

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

Trump’s Private Rage Is Public Policy

The President as the Threat

The source material is built around one fact with real institutional weight: Donald Trump is not a commentator yelling into the void. He is the president of the United States, and he used that office’s platform to threaten to “decimate and destroy all areas of Iran” after a report about an alleged assassination plot. That matters because power, not tone, is what turns language into danger.

Richard Stengel’s alarm is justified, but the larger problem is not that Trump sounded “vulgar.” It is that a sitting president treated mass punishment as a legitimate response to a threat against himself.

Personal Injury as State Doctrine

Trump’s post collapses a private grievance into a state threat. “ME” is the point. The response is not framed as diplomacy, restraint, or law. It is framed as retaliation by imperial fiat: missiles “locked and loaded,” with more to follow. That is not confusion. It is deliberate escalation language from the highest office in the country.

The source presents this as a reaction to a Wall Street Journal report that Israeli intelligence may have uncovered an Iranian assassination plan. Even if that report is true, it does not justify collective annihilation rhetoric. The president chose to answer a disputed security story with language that implies mass destruction against a nation and its people.

The Framing Problem

The article’s weakest move is the familiar Washington habit of treating authoritarian cruelty as if it were mainly a communications issue. “Undiplomatic” is anemic here. What Trump threatened, in plain terms, was not diplomacy gone wrong but state violence deployed as theater.

That framing also hides agency. The blame does not belong to some fog of international tension. It belongs to the president who posted the threat, to the political operation that normalizes his impulses, and to the institutions too cowed to say this plainly: a president who talks like this is not restoring deterrence. He is advertising his willingness to use national power as personal revenge.

War by Delay

Stengel says Trump and his team are “playing for time,” kicking the problem down the road with “occasional flare ups” instead of any serious nuclear deal. That reads less like strategy than managed crisis: enough menace to keep the story alive, not enough discipline to resolve anything.

The source also notes Trump’s months-long war with Iran and the “ceasefire” he declared “over” last week. That is the architecture of endless instability: declare a break, claim control, then leave the underlying conflict intact so it can be reactivated whenever useful. Peace becomes a branding exercise. War becomes background noise.

The Larger Pattern

This is not just about Iran or one reckless post. It is about a political order that lets a president convert narcissism into doctrine and then calls the result “foreign policy.” It is about a system that repeatedly downplays deliberate harm by laundering it through process language, as if the main issue were optics rather than violence.

The real story is simple: power is concentrated in one office, and Trump uses it to turn personal grievance into public threat. That is the pattern. The misdirection is everything around it.
