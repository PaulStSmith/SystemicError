The Fragile Strongman: From Qi Hong’s Defiance to the Cults of Xi and Trump

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 2, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Authoritarian regimes always pretend to be eternal. Their leaders strut as if they’ve bent history into permanence, as if the future has been signed over to their will. But every dictatorship, every cult of personality, carries within it the seeds of its own demise. Today, in two very different theaters — Xi Jinping’s China and Donald Trump’s America — we see this paradox playing out in real time. Both men thrive on personality cults that seem unshakable to their followers, yet both movements are hollow at the core. When the figurehead falters, the edifice will collapse.

Turning the Lens Back on Power

The story of Qi Hong, a dissident from Chongqing, reads like a parable of defiance in an age of omnipresent surveillance. In the face of China’s suffocating security apparatus, Qi staged an act of protest that was also a performance, flipping the tools of the state against itself. With nothing more than a projector and a camera, he projected slogans denouncing the Communist Party onto a building on the eve of a major military parade. While the police scrambled to shut it down, Qi recorded their every move and released the footage online. The hunter became the hunted; the surveillance state was caught surveilled.

Qi’s message wasn’t just in the words he beamed across Chongqing’s skyline — “No more lies, we want the truth. No more slavery, we want freedom.” It was in the humiliation of the police themselves, documented as they fumbled with curtains and cables, watched by the very eye they thought they controlled. To cap it off, Qi left behind a letter to the police: “Even if you are a beneficiary of the system today, one day you will inevitably become a victim on this land. So please treat the people with kindness.”

Of course, the regime struck back in its predictable, cowardly way. Unable to seize Qi, who had already fled to Britain, they interrogated his frail mother in front of her wooden home, hoping that punishing the family would deter the son. Instead, the act exposed the cruelty and absurdity of the CCP’s power — five officers standing over a bent old woman as though she were a national security threat. If Qi’s projection was performance art, Beijing’s reaction was farce.

What matters here isn’t just the courage of one dissident, but the weakness his act revealed. A regime that pours billions into total surveillance still cannot stop one man with a lightbulb and a plan. Its supposed airtight control is riddled with cracks.

The Cult of Xi: Absolute Control, Absolute Fragility

Xi Jinping has spent the last decade building his image into something approaching infallibility. His face dominates billboards, classrooms, and party meetings. His slogans are repeated like scripture. The message is simple: Xi is China, and China is Xi.

But here lies the trap. By eliminating rivals, suppressing independent voices, and centralizing power around himself, Xi has hollowed out the very Party he claims to embody. The Communist Party once prided itself on “collective leadership,” where factions balanced each other and successors were groomed in advance. Under Xi, those mechanisms are gone. The result is a brittle hierarchy in which no one dares to emerge as a potential heir.

This makes Xi’s cult of personality a double-edged sword. As long as he is healthy, the illusion of unity holds. But what happens when he falters? There is no successor, no designated future. Instead, there will be a free-for-all among opportunists, each lacking the charisma or authority to replicate Xi’s dominance. Whoever emerges from that struggle will inherit a throne that is broken, a Party weakened by years of purges and paranoia.

History is clear on this point: personality cults collapse into chaos when the personality disappears. Stalin’s death unleashed a brutal scramble inside the Soviet Union. Mao’s death saw the Gang of Four swiftly dismantled. Franco’s Spain, Pinochet’s Chile, even Perón’s Argentina — every strongman who tried to fuse his image to the state left behind instability. Xi is not the exception; he is the rule, only amplified by modern surveillance.

Trumpism: A Hollow Empire of Grievance

Across the Pacific, in a democracy that once styled itself as a beacon of stability, another cult of personality festers. Donald Trump may not command the apparatus of a one-party state, but his movement operates on the same logic: loyalty to the man, not to institutions or ideas.

Trumpism is not a coherent ideology. It is a stew of grievances, conspiracies, and resentments, all funneled through Trump’s persona. He doesn’t just lead the movement — he is the movement. Every attempt to manufacture a successor, from Ron DeSantis to Vivek Ramaswamy, collapses under the weight of comparison. They can mimic the sneer, repeat the slogans, but they can’t replicate the aura. It’s karaoke Trump, and the crowd knows it.

Like Xi, Trump has gutted the structures that might have carried his movement forward. The Republican Party, once a traditional political organization, has been reduced to a personality vehicle. Candidates sink or swim not on policy but on their ability to flatter the dear leader. When Trump eventually exits the stage — whether by age, illness, or legal fate — the movement will splinter. Some will chase a new strongman. Others will drift away. But no one will ever be the “original article.”

This is the paradox of Trump’s hold on his base: it feels unbreakable, but it is deeply fragile. A movement that relies on one man’s charisma cannot outlive him. And unlike Xi’s CCP, Trumpism doesn’t even have the scaffolding of a bureaucratic party-state to hold things together.

The Double-Edged Sword of Cult Politics

What unites Xi and Trump is not ideology but method. Both have transformed politics into theater of loyalty, where the leader is the sun and everyone else mere orbiting satellites. Both mistake the cheers of the faithful for permanence. And both are blind to the rot this creates.

Cult politics is like political fentanyl: an intense high that numbs institutions, hollows out succession, and poisons the body politic. It works brilliantly in the short term to consolidate power. But it guarantees chaos in the long run. When the leader dies or disappears, the movement dies with him, leaving only rubble behind.

For Xi, that rubble may be a fractured Communist Party scrambling to maintain legitimacy in the face of economic decline and public disillusionment. For Trump, it may be a Republican Party consumed by infighting, unable to decide whether to keep worshiping his memory or move on.

Cracks in the Wall

Qi Hong’s act of defiance matters here because it underscores the fundamental truth that authoritarians can never fully seal off: control is never airtight. One projector, one protest, one act of ridicule can ripple across millions of screens. For all its billions in surveillance, Beijing couldn’t stop Qi from exposing its absurdity. For all his bluster, Trump cannot stop his movement from eventually facing the void he leaves behind.

Authoritarianism always ends the same way: slowly, then suddenly. The wall looks solid until one day it crumbles. And the more a leader invests in making himself the wall, the more catastrophic the collapse will be.

Xi’s China and Trump’s America may look like opposites on the surface, but they are bound by this same paradox. Their leaders mistake loyalty to the man for loyalty to the system. When they go, the systems they hollowed out will go with them. The only question is how much damage they’ll do before gravity catches up.

Conclusion: The Unwritten Future

Predicting the fall of regimes is a perilous business. The Soviet Union looked eternal until it vanished in a puff of smoke. Trump looked finished after January 6th, only to claw his way back to dominance. Xi looks invincible, but so did Mao and Stalin. Yet if history teaches us anything, it’s that personality cults cannot endure. They burn hot, but they burn out.

Qi Hong’s protest reminds us that even in the darkest systems, cracks appear. And those cracks spread. The cults of Xi and Trump may look towering today, but they are built on sand. When the strongman falls, the tide will come rushing in.
