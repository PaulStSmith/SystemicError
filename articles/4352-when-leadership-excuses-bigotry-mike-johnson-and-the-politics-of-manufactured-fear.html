When Leadership Excuses Bigotry: Mike Johnson and the Politics of Manufactured Fear

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 12, 20266 min read

The Easiest Test of Leadership

There are moments in politics that require no nuance, no constitutional law seminar, and no complicated policy analysis. They require only a single, unambiguous sentence. When a member of Congress publicly declares that an entire religious group does not belong in American society, the correct response from leadership is painfully simple: That statement is wrong, and it does not represent our values.

House Speaker Mike Johnson was given exactly that opportunity when Representative Andy Ogles declared that “Muslims don’t belong in American society.” It should have been the easiest leadership test imaginable. Instead of rejecting the statement outright, Johnson chose a different path: rhetorical laundering. He distanced himself from the tone of the remark while validating the paranoid narrative that fuels it.

The result was not a clarification. It was a justification.

Johnson’s defense leaned on an old and thoroughly discredited conspiracy theory — the idea that Muslim Americans are attempting to impose Sharia law on the United States. According to Johnson, the anger driving anti-Muslim rhetoric in his party stems from legitimate fears that immigrants will arrive in America and try to replace the Constitution with religious law.

There is just one problem with that claim.

It is completely fictional.

The Zombie Myth of “Creeping Sharia”

The panic over “creeping Sharia law” is not new. It surged through right‑wing political media during the early 2010s, driven by talk radio hosts, partisan blogs, and opportunistic politicians searching for a convenient cultural enemy.

The theory was simple: Muslims were supposedly infiltrating American institutions and preparing to replace constitutional law with Islamic legal doctrine.

Reality, however, refused to cooperate.

There has never been a credible political movement in the United States advocating the replacement of the Constitution with Sharia law. None. No political party. No coalition of elected officials. No legislative movement. Not even a fringe ballot initiative.

The idea survived anyway because it was never meant to describe reality. It was designed to manufacture fear.

Moral panics operate on a specific formula: identify a minority group, attribute to it an existential threat to national identity, and then present discrimination as a defensive reaction rather than prejudice. The mechanics are old. American history is filled with them.

Catholics were once accused of plotting to impose papal authority over the United States. Jewish immigrants were accused of secretly controlling financial institutions and global politics. Japanese Americans were portrayed as inherently disloyal during World War II. Each case followed the same script: a minority community framed as an internal civilizational threat.

Today, that role has been assigned to Muslims.

Johnson’s comments did not merely fail to challenge the narrative. They revived it.

The Constitutional Hypocrisy

Johnson attempted to cloak his argument in constitutional language, claiming that Sharia law would conflict with the U.S. Constitution. That framing collapses under even the most basic understanding of American law.

The United States already contains countless religious legal traditions that operate voluntarily within communities. Jewish arbitration courts settle disputes according to religious law. Christian churches operate under canon law in matters of internal governance. Religious mediation systems exist across denominations.

None of these systems threaten the Constitution because they function within its boundaries.

The First Amendment guarantees both religious freedom and the separation of church and state. Individuals may follow religious law in their personal lives. The government, however, cannot enforce religious doctrine as public law.

That arrangement has worked for more than two centuries.

If Johnson genuinely believed that religious legal traditions posed a constitutional threat, he would have to oppose not only Muslim communities but also large segments of American Christianity and Judaism. He does not do that, of course, because the supposed concern is selective.

The target is not religious law in general.

The target is Muslims.

The Assimilation Dog Whistle

Johnson also invoked another familiar rhetorical device: assimilation.

According to his statement, the real problem arises when immigrants come to the United States and attempt to impose their beliefs rather than assimilating into American culture.

On its surface, this sounds reasonable. In practice, it functions as a coded accusation that minority communities are culturally incompatible with American identity.

That accusation has appeared repeatedly throughout American history.

Irish Catholics were once described as incapable of becoming true Americans because of their loyalty to the Vatican. Jewish immigrants were accused of forming insular communities that refused to integrate into American life. Chinese immigrants were portrayed as permanently foreign, regardless of how long they lived in the country.

Each of those claims now reads as transparently bigoted.

Muslims occupy the same position today.

The irony is that Muslim Americans already demonstrate the same patterns of civic integration seen in every previous immigrant wave: participation in elections, professional achievement, military service, and community leadership. Millions of Muslim Americans live ordinary lives within the framework of American law.

But the assimilation narrative persists because its purpose is not descriptive. It is political.

It creates the impression that prejudice is merely a reaction to cultural incompatibility rather than an act of discrimination.

The Politics of Plausible Deniability

Johnson’s response illustrates a broader strategy that has become common within the modern Republican Party.

Extremist rhetoric is rarely endorsed directly by leadership. Instead, it is normalized through a sequence of carefully calibrated steps:

  1. A fringe figure makes an openly discriminatory statement.

  2. Leadership declines to condemn it clearly.

  3. The discussion shifts to hypothetical threats allegedly posed by the targeted minority.

  4. The original bigotry becomes reframed as a legitimate cultural concern.

By the end of the process, the discriminatory claim remains intact, but its proponents can insist that the debate is really about values, security, or constitutional principles.

Johnson followed that script precisely.

He acknowledged that he would not use the same language as Ogles, but he immediately validated the conspiracy theory that produced the remark in the first place. The message to the party’s base was unmistakable: the prejudice may have been phrased too bluntly, but the underlying suspicion remains justified.

This approach provides political cover for extremist narratives while allowing leadership to maintain a veneer of respectability.

The Religious Freedom Paradox

Johnson’s stance carries a deeper irony. He frequently describes himself as a defender of religious liberty. That commitment, however, appears to apply selectively.

In the American constitutional tradition, religious freedom is universal. It protects believers of every faith and those with no faith at all. The principle is not conditional on popularity or cultural familiarity.

When a political leader defends the right of Christians to express their beliefs but refuses to defend the equal place of Muslims in American society, the concept of religious freedom collapses into sectarian favoritism.

That is not liberty. It is religious hierarchy.

The founders of the United States explicitly rejected that model. They understood that a government capable of privileging one religion could just as easily persecute another.

Johnson’s comments move in the opposite direction.

The Cost of Normalizing Prejudice

The most troubling aspect of the episode is not the existence of bigotry within Congress. American politics has always contained individuals willing to exploit fear for power.

The real danger emerges when leadership refuses to confront it.

Every time discriminatory rhetoric goes unchallenged, it becomes easier for the next politician to go further. What once sounded extreme gradually becomes ordinary.

Political language shapes political reality. If Muslims are repeatedly portrayed as cultural invaders or constitutional threats, policies targeting Muslim communities become easier to justify. Surveillance programs, travel bans, and discriminatory legislation begin to appear reasonable to voters conditioned by years of fear‑based messaging.

History shows where that path leads.

A Failure of Moral Clarity

Mike Johnson had an opportunity to demonstrate that religious bigotry has no place in American political leadership. The test required nothing more than a simple affirmation that Muslims belong in the United States as fully equal citizens.

He chose not to say it.

Instead, he resurrected a conspiracy theory that has circulated for more than a decade and used it to rationalize the prejudice expressed by a member of his own party.

Leadership reveals itself not through slogans but through decisions made in moments like this one.

Johnson’s decision was unmistakable.

He chose to excuse the bigotry rather than confront it.

And in doing so, he reinforced the very narrative that produced it.
