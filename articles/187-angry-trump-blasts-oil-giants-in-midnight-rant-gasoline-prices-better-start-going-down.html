​Angry Trump blasts oil giants in midnight rant: 'Gasoline prices better start going down'

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Paulo SantosJune 25, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Power Blames Its Favorite Target

The Real Actor

Trump is not a bystander here. He is the president, and he used the presidency as a threat display: a midnight social post ordering the Department of Justice to go after oil companies over gas prices. That is the center of the story. Not consumer relief, not market analysis, not even law enforcement. It is executive power performing outrage.

What Happened

The trigger was simple enough: Trump said oil companies were not passing lower crude costs through to pump prices, accused them of “gouging,” and claimed he had instructed the DOJ to “immediately” look into it. The reporting also notes that gasoline prices have fallen for six straight weeks, though they remain above January levels, and that the White House and DOJ had not confirmed any formal probe by Wednesday morning.

The Blame Shift

The framing does a familiar job: it narrows a messy price chain into a morality play with a villain the public already resents. Oil companies are easy to denounce; pricing lags, state and local taxes, and supply mechanics are harder to explain and easier to weaponize. Trump’s post treats complexity as if it were fraud. That is not clarity. It is misdirection with a badge on it.

Why the DOJ Matters

The choice of target matters as much as the accusation. Trump did not announce a policy fix, a tax change, or a regulatory explanation. He invoked the Justice Department, because a DOJ probe sounds like action even when it may be only a political gesture. That is the point of the stunt: to convert an administrative weapon into a campaign message, especially with midterm politics hanging over the price of gasoline.

The Price He Wants

The article notes that pump prices are still above the roughly $2.76 a gallon seen in January, before the Iran conflict began, and that easing tensions after a U.S.-Iran agreement have helped some consumers. That context matters because it undercuts the fantasy that a president can reduce a volatile market to a single villain on demand. He can pressure institutions, but he cannot erase the fact that prices move through a chain of production, taxation, timing, and geopolitics.

The Pattern

This is the old executive trick: when the system is inconvenient, blame the nearest large actor and pretend the state is a referee instead of a participant. Trump is not exposing market abuse so much as staging a public reprimand for political gain. The deeper pattern is not just scapegoating oil companies. It is the steady conversion of institutional power into spectacle, where the presidency behaves less like a governing office than a megaphone for selective indignation.
