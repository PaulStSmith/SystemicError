'Diabolical taste': Celebrity NYC restaurateur dishes dirt as he recalls serving Trump

Long before he was president, Donald Trump was a New York celebrity who frequented the Big Apple’s restaurant scene — and he left an indelible mark.

In an exclusive interview with People, celebrity restaurateur Keith McNally recalled his interactions with Trump.

For two years, the now-president was a regular at McNally’s NYC hotspot, Balthazar, after its 1997 opening.

“Even then, he seemed like a caricature of a rich, pushy New Yorker with diabolical taste. But he wasn't offensive. In fact, he was very decent to me,” McNally told People.

“All the same, he wasn't too bright and if someone had told me that one day he'd be president I'd have thought they were certifiable.”

McNally is now promoting his new book in which he recalls his most infamous run-ins with the who’s-who of New York City.

“Even though I missed meeting Henry VIII by four hundred years, I did meet his modern-day equivalent, Donald Trump,” McNally wrote in the book.

He recalls that Trump had invited him to come look at a restaurant space to rent in a building he owned on Manhattan's Upper West Side.

"Walking through a series of overdecorated spaces, we passed one that was noticeably less gaudy than the others. I asked the Don if that restaurant space was also for rent. ‘No, that one’s taken. I guaranteed it to someone else a month ago.”

McNally added, “There was a pause before Trump added with a smile: ‘But just because it’s guaranteed doesn’t mean it’s locked in.’”

The restaurateur ultimately did not end up renting the space.

Our Analysis:

The Trump Caricature: A Glimpse into the Past through the Eyes of Keith McNally

Long before the world grappled with the phenomenon of Donald Trump as President of the United States, he was carving out a reputation in the New York City restaurant scene. This isn't just any anecdote from the past; it's a revealing snapshot provided by none other than Keith McNally, a titan in the world of celebrity restaurateurs. In an era when Trump's every move wasn't dissected with the ferocity it is today, his interactions at McNally’s NYC hotspot, Balthazar, offer a fascinating glimpse into his character.

Trump: The "Decent" Patron with Questionable Taste

McNally's reflections, as shared with People, paint a picture of Trump that's both familiar and slightly disarming. Describing Trump as a "caricature of a rich, pushy New Yorker with diabolical taste," McNally nonetheless credits him with being "very decent" to him. This decent behavior, however, is overshadowed by McNally’s incredulity at the thought of Trump ascending to the presidency. "If someone had told me that one day he'd be president I'd have thought they were certifiable," McNally remarks, a sentiment likely echoed by many both during and after the 2016 election.

The Modern-Day Henry VIII?

McNally doesn't stop at casual observations. In promoting his new book, which promises to be a treasure trove of encounters with New York's elite, he draws a bold comparison between Trump and Henry VIII. This analogy is less about direct behavioral parallels and more about the symbolic stature and controversy both figures evoke. It's a comparison that's as audacious as it is thought-provoking, suggesting that Trump's larger-than-life persona and impact on his milieu are as significant as those of the infamous Tudor king.

The Art of the Deal, Trump Style

A particularly telling anecdote involves McNally's exploration of a restaurant space in a building owned by Trump. As they toured, they came across a less gaudy space, already "guaranteed" to someone else. Trump's response? "But just because it’s guaranteed doesn’t mean it’s locked in." This statement is emblematic of Trump's approach to business and, by extension, politics. It's a mindset where assurances are fluid, and commitments are negotiable, revealing a flexibility with words and promises that would become all too familiar during his political career.

Concluding Thoughts

Keith McNally's reflections on Trump offer more than just a peek into the past; they provide a lens through which we can understand the complex figure that Trump remains today. His behavior at Balthazar, his self-comparison to historical figures, and his business dealings underscore a consistency in character that has played out on the national and international stage.

What we learn from McNally's account is that Trump has always been the man we see today: bold, controversial, and unapologetically himself. Whether one views that as a positive or a negative trait, it's clear that Trump's impact, much like Henry VIII's, will be discussed and debated for generations to come. In the grand tapestry of New York City's history, Trump's thread is as colorful and complex as it gets, woven into the fabric long before his political ascendancy. And in this tapestry, McNally's anecdotes are but a few stitches that stand out, highlighting the intricate pattern of a man who would become one of the most divisive figures in modern American history.
