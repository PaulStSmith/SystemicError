Sen. Markwayne Mullin (R-OK) defended the practice of “dueling” — even though it has been illegal for over 100 years.

During a Senate confirmation hearing on Wednesday, Sen. Rand Paul (R-KY) questioned whether Mullin was the right person to lead the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) because of his history of promoting violence. He played video of a 2023 Senate floor interaction in which Mullin threatened to fight Sean O’Brien, the president of the International Brotherhood of Teamsters.

“In the days after the fight, you did many interviews in which you justified the violence as historically justified by precedents, such as caning and dueling,” Paul said of one argument in the Senate.

“Is it today your opinion that the caning of Charles Sumner was not only justified but argues still for resolving our political differences with violence?”

In 1856, Senator Charles Sumner of Massachusetts was brutally beaten by South Carolina Representative Preston Brooks on the Senate floor. Sumner had delivered a scathing anti-slavery speech criticizing Brooks’ cousin. The caning left Sumner severely injured, becoming a pivotal moment that intensified sectional tensions between North and South preceding the Civil War.

“What I was simply pointing out is some of the rules that still applied to this body,” Mullin claimed. “For instance, dueling with two consenting adults is still there.”

“It’s been illegal for 170 years,” Paul pointed out. “There’s no precedent for legal dueling. Even then, they fled the country.”

“Do you realize that the man that beat Charles Sumner with a cane, he beat him until he was unconscious?” he added. “You know why no senators intervened? Because his friend held a gun on the other senators, and he kept beating him and beating him until he crushed his skull. That’s what you’re insinuating as the president of the Senate, and that’s what you live by.”

“That is a very, very dangerous sentiment.”

Our Analysis:

Dueling Debates: Unpacking the Mullin Controversy

In an era where political rhetoric frequently teeters on the brink of hyperbole, the recent defense of “dueling” by Sen. Markwayne Mullin (R-OK) during a Senate confirmation hearing has ignited a firestorm of controversy. However, to dissect this incident with the precision it demands, we must first navigate through the layers of institutional power, decision-making, and the framing of the narrative.

Institutional Power and Decision-Making

At the heart of this controversy is Sen. Markwayne Mullin, a figure nominated to lead the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), a key institution with significant power over national security, immigration, and anti-terrorism efforts. Mullin’s comments were made in response to criticisms from Sen. Rand Paul (R-KY) regarding Mullin’s suitability for the role, given his history of promoting violence. This context is crucial; the DHS leadership position is one of substantial institutional power, where the holder’s views on violence and conflict resolution are not just academic but could influence policy and culture within the organization.

Mullin’s Defense of Dueling: Mullin defended his stance by referencing historical precedents of violence among political figures, notably the caning of Charles Sumner in 1856. His argument suggests a belief in the legitimacy, or at least the historical precedent, for resolving political differences through violence—an alarming stance for someone nominated to lead a major federal agency.

Sen. Rand Paul’s Challenge: Paul’s questioning highlights the problematic nature of Mullin’s views, particularly in the context of leading a department tasked with ensuring the nation’s safety. Paul’s reference to the illegality of dueling for over 170 years and the brutality of past congressional violence serves to question Mullin’s judgment and suitability for the role.

Narrative Framing and Misdirection

The article frames the debate around the appropriateness of violence in political discourse, focusing on Mullin’s defense of dueling. However, it does not misdirect responsibility; rather, it places the spotlight directly on Mullin, the individual whose decisions and views are under scrutiny. The narrative is clear: a nominee for a high-level position has espoused views that seemingly endorse violence as a means of resolving political disputes.

Analysis

Rejecting False Equivalence: It’s crucial to differentiate between historical context and present-day applicability. Mullin’s reference to dueling and the caning of Charles Sumner invokes a period in American history where political violence was more commonplace, yet fails to recognize the evolution of political norms and the legal framework governing conflict resolution today.

The Core Issue: Mullin’s comments bring into sharp relief the tension between historical precedent and contemporary standards of conduct for public officials. The core issue is not just the endorsement of an antiquated and illegal practice but what such an endorsement signals about Mullin’s perspective on violence, conflict resolution, and suitability for a role that demands a commitment to upholding the law and ensuring public safety.

Conclusion

In evaluating the controversy surrounding Sen. Markwayne Mullin’s defense of dueling, it is evident that the criticism is neither misplaced nor a product of narrative misdirection. Instead, it raises legitimate concerns about the appropriateness of historical violence as a model for contemporary political conduct, especially from someone nominated to lead an institution as critical as the DHS. The framing of the debate, centered on Mullin’s judgment and views, underscores the importance of scrutinizing the beliefs and values of those in or aspiring to positions of significant institutional power. The responsibility lies squarely with Mullin, whose defense of dueling not only reveals a troubling perspective on violence but also calls into question his suitability for a role predicated on the principles of law, order, and civil discourse.
