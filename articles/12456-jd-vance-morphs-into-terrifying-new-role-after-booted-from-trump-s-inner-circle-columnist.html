JD Vance morphs into terrifying new role after booted from Trump's inner circle: columnist

Vice President JD Vance has been left out of the "room where it happens," ceding his vice presidential role to posts usually considered more junior and acting as Donald Trump's social media cheerleader, a New York Times columnist wrote Wednesday.

Locked out of real power while Deputy Chief of Staff Stephen Miller runs deportations and Director of the Office of Management and Budget Russell Vought terrorizes federal workers, Vance has become Trump's "official fanboy," spending his time crafting sophisticated arguments for the administration's cruelty, wrote Jamelle Bouie.

But his recent speech at the right-wing Claremont Institute revealed that he's bringing something to that role that's far more sinister than typical MAGA rhetoric.

"America is not just an idea," Vance declared at Claremont. "It is a group of people with a shared history and a common future" — meaning your passport might say American, but that doesn't make you equal, Bouie wrote.

Vance explicitly rejected what he called the "over-inclusive" nature of founding ideals, arguing that believing in the Declaration of Independence's principles would mean admitting "billions of foreign citizens." But his alternative is chilling: a hierarchy where "the people whose ancestors fought in the Civil War have a hell of a lot more claim over America than the people who say they don't belong," Bouie wrote.

This isn't just nativism—it's a direct echo of Chief Justice Roger Taney's infamous Dred Scott decision, which argued that Black Americans could never be citizens because the founders never intended it, Bouie argued. Taney, too, found the Declaration's egalitarian language "over-inclusive."

Vance's tiered citizenship extends to immigrants, who he says must show proper gratitude. He savagely attacked New York City's Democratic mayoral candidate Zohran Mamdani for describing America as "beautiful, contradictory, unfinished" on Independence Day.

"Has he ever looked in the mirror and recognized that he might not be alive were it not for the generosity of a country he dares to insult?" Vance sneered. "Who the hell does he think that he is?"

The message is clear: there are Americans who can critique their country, and Americans who cannot—determined by bloodline and deference, Bouie wrote.

Perhaps most grotesquely, Bouie finished, Vance invokes Civil War soldiers while rejecting everything they died for. Abraham Lincoln called the Declaration's promise of equality the "electric cord" linking all Americans. Vance wants to cut that cord, replacing it with what he calls connection to "soil and the dead," Bouie wrote.

Our Analysis:

JD Vance: A Vice President's Sinister Vision for America

In an age where political discourse often veers into the absurd, JD Vance's recent declarations carry a chilling echo of America's darkest historical chapters. As dissected in a New York Times column by Jamelle Bouie, Vance, ostensibly sidelined in the Trump administration, has found a niche that is not just troubling but downright sinister. Let's peel back the layers of Vance's statements and their implications, shall we?

The "Official Fanboy" Role

Vance, reduced to what Bouie calls an "official fanboy," seems to have embraced a role far removed from the power corridors, where his colleagues, such as Stephen Miller and Russell Vought, wield their influence through policies that terrorize and exclude. But don't be fooled; Vance's apparent relegation to Trump's cheerleader belies a far more dangerous agenda.

A Sinister Ideological Shift

At the Claremont Institute, Vance revealed a vision of America that is not just exclusionary but fundamentally antithetical to the nation's founding ideals. "America is not just an idea," Vance declared, but a "group of people with a shared history and a common future." This rhetoric is a veiled attempt to redefine what it means to be American in dangerously narrow terms, prioritizing bloodline and historical ties over the inclusive ideals enshrined in the Declaration of Independence.

Echoes of Dred Scott

Vance's rejection of the Declaration's "over-inclusive" nature is a direct echo of Chief Justice Roger Taney's infamous Dred Scott decision, which posited that Black Americans could never be citizens as the founders never intended it. This comparison is not made lightly. Taney's decision is a stain on America's history, and Vance's rhetoric dangerously aligns with this dark past.

The Hierarchy of Citizenship

Vance's notion that those "whose ancestors fought in the Civil War have a hell of a lot more claim over America" is not just nativism; it's a blueprint for a tiered citizenship that values some Americans over others based on ancestry and perceived loyalty. This vision is not only divisive but fundamentally un-American, contradicting the very principles upon which the nation was founded.

Attacking the Right to Critique

Vance's attack on Zohran Mamdani, a Democratic mayoral candidate, for describing America as "beautiful, contradictory, unfinished," reveals a belief that only certain Americans, those who align with Vance's narrow vision, have the right to critique the country. This stance is not only hypocritical but dangerous, suggesting a litmus test for who gets to participate in America's democratic discourse.

Cutting the "Electric Cord"

Perhaps most egregiously, Vance seeks to sever the "electric cord" of equality that Abraham Lincoln saw as linking all Americans. By prioritizing "soil and the dead" over the ideals of equality and inclusion, Vance not only rejects what the Civil War soldiers died for but also undermines the very foundation of American democracy.

Conclusion: A Dangerous Path Forward

JD Vance's vision for America is not just a departure from the nation's ideals; it is a roadmap to a more divided, hierarchical, and fundamentally unequal society. This is not mere political rhetoric; it is a sinister ideology that threatens the very core of what it means to be American. As such, it is incumbent upon all who cherish democracy and equality to recognize and resist this dangerous path Vance advocates for. The stakes could not be higher.
