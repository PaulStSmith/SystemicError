Internal GOP polling shows unmistakable 'downward trajectory' — handing Dems an opening

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 3, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:GOP Inflation Anxiety: A Recipe for Electoral Disaster?

Donald Trump signs a bill, surrounded by congressional Republicans. REUTERS/Nathan Howard

Republicans are alarmed by internal polling and focus groups showing deep voter concerns about a key issue, and they're worried that time is running out to correct the problem.

GOP lawmakers and insiders are increasingly anxious that inflation could wallop them in the 2026 midterms and want President Donald Trump to take more aggressive action on the issue, which is the top voter concern, reported Axios.

"We don't want Democrats making campaign ads about $8 Lucky Charms and grandma unable to get prescription drugs because of shortages from the tariffs," one GOP operative involved in drawing up the party's midterm plans told Axios. "Voters care a ton, and Republicans need to talk more about fighting this."

The conservative GrayHouse polling firm has handed out data showing 25 percent of voters see inflation as the most important issue, which is more than double the next-ranked concern, government corruption, and Senate Republicans have seen polling that shows voters don't believe they're focused enough on rising costs and product shortages.

"If we don't address some of [the economic issues], I think it will create headwinds going into a cycle where you already expect headwinds with a midterm after a presidential election and the change of party in the White House," said Sen. Thom Tillis (R-NC), who announced his retirement in June.

A recent Economist/YouGov survey also found costs were the single most important issue to voters, including 2024 Trump supporters, and only 34 percent of all voters said they approved of the president's handling of inflation, and more than two-thirds of all voters thought the problem would be the same or worse in six months.

"The president's approval rating has been remarkably resilient on handling of the economy, immigration, and foreign policy," said GOP pollster Robert Blizzard. "But since he took office, it continues a downward trajectory on handling inflation. This continued fatigue is providing an opening for Democrats to criticize the president on his signature issue of the economy."

The White House didn't directly acknowledge those concerns, but instead pointed to cost decreases in some sectors, such as lower gasoline prices this Labor Day weekend.

"President Trump pledged to end Joe Biden's inflation crisis, and the administration's supply-side policies have delivered: Inflation is trending towards an annualized rate not seen in years while the prices of gas, eggs, and other household essentials have declined," said White House spokesperson Kush Desai.

Our Analysis:

GOP Inflation Anxiety: A Recipe for Electoral Disaster?

In the labyrinth of political maneuvering and public perception, the Republican Party finds itself at a crossroads, haunted by the specter of inflation and its potential to derail their aspirations in the 2026 midterms. The recent revelations from Axios about internal polling and focus group findings should serve as a blaring alarm for the GOP. Yet, the response—or lack thereof—suggests a party scrambling, perhaps too late, to address the economic grievances of the American electorate.

Inflation: The Political Quicksand for Republicans

The GOP's internal polling is not just a wake-up call; it's a fire alarm in the night. The data, revealing that 25 percent of voters prioritize inflation above all other issues, including government corruption, should have sparked an aggressive policy and messaging overhaul. Yet, the Republican machinery appears to be caught in a quagmire of its own making, with an overreliance on past economic policies that are now failing to resonate with the current concerns of the electorate.

Senator Thom Tillis's acknowledgment of the looming "headwinds" the party faces encapsulates the predicament perfectly. The North Carolina Senator's retirement announcement in June could be read as a canary in the coal mine for the GOP, signaling a deeper recognition of the challenges ahead, particularly in a post-presidential election cycle marked by a change of party in the White House.

The Trump Factor

The reliance on President Donald Trump to address the inflation crisis is a double-edged sword. While the White House spokesperson Kush Desai points to "supply-side policies" and temporary price decreases in gasoline and other essentials as victories, this narrative fails to grasp the full picture. The Economist/YouGov survey’s findings—that only 34 percent of voters approve of Trump's handling of inflation—speak volumes. It's a stark indicator that temporary price dips are not enough to assuage voter concerns, especially when more than two-thirds believe the situation will remain the same or worsen in six months.

A Missed Opportunity for GOP Messaging?

The comment from a GOP operative to Axios about not wanting Democrats to capitalize on inflation fallout in campaign ads is particularly telling. It underscores a reactive rather than proactive approach to policy and messaging. Republicans are aware of the potent electoral weapon inflation can be in the hands of Democrats. Yet, their strategy seems to be more about damage control than presenting a convincing plan to combat inflation effectively.

Democrats' Opening

As GOP pollster Robert Blizzard notes, the president’s "continued fatigue" on handling inflation provides a clear opening for Democrats. The administration's resilience on the economy, immigration, and foreign policy matters little if they fail to address the primary concern of voters. Herein lies the irony: Trump's "signature issue of the economy" could very well be his—and the GOP's—Achilles' heel in the upcoming midterms.

Conclusion: A Call to Action or a Prelude to Defeat?

The GOP's alarm over inflation and its potential electoral consequences is justified. However, the path forward requires more than acknowledgment of the problem—it demands bold, innovative solutions that resonate with the electorate's current needs. The party's ability (or inability) to pivot and address these concerns effectively will not only shape the 2026 midterms but could redefine the Republican identity in the post-Trump era. Is this a moment of reckoning or a prelude to a broader existential crisis for the GOP? Only time will tell, but the clock is undoubtedly ticking.
