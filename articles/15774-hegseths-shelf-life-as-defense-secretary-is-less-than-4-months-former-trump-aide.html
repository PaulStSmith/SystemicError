Original story below.

John Bolton, who served as the national security adviser to President Donald Trump during his first term, said Monday Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth should resign after reports emerged that he shared sensitive military details in a second Signal group chat.

"I think Hegseth should resign," Bolton said, but added that he is not going to. "He may leave at some point, because I think he's used up his bank account with Donald Trump. Trump doesn't want to have to waste his resources defending his people, and that's now what he's doing on Hegseth," Bolton said during an appearance on CNN.

"But I think Trump is bound and determined not to show weakness, which is what he's worried he would do if he fires Hegseth or anybody else. So, I think Hegseth has a shelf life of undetermined length, but it's not going to be much longer....Three months, four months, something like that," the former Trump aide said.

Earlier on Monday, several media outlets reported that Hegseth provided specific details about upcoming strikes in Yemen on March 15 in a private Signal group chat that included his wife, brother, and personal lawyer.

Some of these individuals who confirmed the development noted that the information Hegseth disclosed in the Signal chat included the flight schedules for the F/A-18 Hornets that were targeting the Houthis in Yemen, which were essentially the same attack plans he shared in another Signal chat that day, which mistakenly included the editor of The Atlantic.

Hegseth's wife, Jennifer, is a former producer at Fox News. She is not an employee of the Defense Department, but she has traveled abroad with Hegseth during his official visits.

Meanwhile, NPR reported Monday that the White House has initiated the process of looking for a new secretary of defense. An official, whose name was not disclosed, confirmed the development, per NPR.

Our Analysis:

The Hegseth Fiasco: A Symptom of Trump's Chaotic Administration

In an era where the absurd has become the norm, the latest scandal engulfing the Trump administration could almost pass as mundane, if it weren't so blatantly indicative of the systemic rot at its core. Reports that Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth shared sensitive military details in a Signal group chat is not just a breach of security; it's a testament to the cavalier recklessness that seems to be a prerequisite for holding a position in the Trump White House.

John Bolton, the perennial hawk and former Trump national security adviser, has called for Hegseth's resignation. Yet, in the same breath, Bolton acknowledges the futility of such a call, given the administration's propensity to circle the wagons around its beleaguered members until the very last moment. Hegseth's indiscretion, involving the sharing of specific details about military strikes in Yemen, is not merely a lapse in judgment; it's a national security hazard. But the response from the "Official Rapid Response account of the Trump 47 White House" dismisses the report as "Lies from NPR," a tactic as predictable as it is worn.

The Denial Machine at Work

The White House's rapid dismissal of the NPR report as fake news is emblematic of a broader strategy: when faced with criticism or allegations, deflect and attack the credibility of the source. This knee-jerk reaction to deny and deride rather than address the substance of the allegations is a hallmark of Trump's presidency, a presidency where accountability appears to be an alien concept.

Hegseth's Alarming Indiscretion

The details of Hegseth's indiscretion are particularly alarming. Sharing flight schedules of F/A-18 Hornets targeting Houthis in Yemen with individuals who have no business knowing such information — including his wife, a former Fox News producer, and his personal lawyer — is not just a breach of protocol. It's a reckless endangerment of military operations and, by extension, the lives of those involved in them. The fact that such sensitive information ended up in a chat that mistakenly included a media editor only adds a layer of farce to the whole situation.

The Fallout

Bolton's prediction that Hegseth has "used up his bank account with Donald Trump" and may soon be ousted, not out of a concern for national security but because Trump "doesn't want to have to waste his resources defending his people," speaks volumes about the priorities in the Oval Office. It's not about the principle or the security breach but about the optics and the resources expended on damage control.

The call from a GOP congressman for Trump to fire Hegseth, branding the situation as "totally unacceptable," is a rare break in ranks that underscores the gravity of the situation. Yet, in the grand scheme of Trump's tumultuous tenure, it's likely to become just another footnote in a long list of controversies that have become all too common.

Conclusion

The Hegseth saga is more than a story about one man's folly; it's a reflection of an administration that operates with a cavalier disregard for norms, ethics, and, most alarmingly, national security. The Trump administration's handling of this situation — from the initial denial to the potential reluctant ousting of Hegseth — is a microcosm of its broader dysfunction and moral bankruptcy. It's a clear indication that the systemic error within Trump's White House isn't just a bug; it's a feature.
