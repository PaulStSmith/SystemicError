Trump DOJ caught using false claims in explosive court revelation

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 13, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Trump’s Justice Department admitted a false allegation appeared in federal affidavits used to seek warrants during its investigation of an anti-ICE protest at a Minnesota church. The affidavits said journalists Don Lemon and Georgia Fort, producer Jerome Richardson, and others were “chanting with agitators.” Prosecutors later acknowledged that Lemon and Fort were not chanting; Fort’s lawyers said the error appeared in at least 15 affidavits.

Federal judges in Minnesota and Pennsylvania authorized cell-site simulators to locate Richardson, although the records do not establish that agents deployed them. Investigators obtained the second warrant two days before Richardson—who had publicly indicated that he expected to enter custody—surrendered at a Philadelphia federal courthouse. He was released on bond that afternoon.

The investigation had already encountered judicial resistance. A magistrate judge denied five of eight arrest-warrant applications, and the chief judge found no evidence of criminal conduct by two targeted journalists. Prosecutors later obtained a grand-jury indictment. The released records do not explain why investigators pursued such intrusive surveillance or why the false claim repeatedly appeared in submissions to judges.

The government held the power

The institutional imbalance is stark. Federal prosecutors and investigators could seek arrests, obtain sealed surveillance authority, deploy technology capable of collecting identifiers from nearby people’s phones, and place their account of events before multiple judges. The journalists and producer could deny the government’s characterization only after that machinery had begun moving.

Richardson’s willingness to surrender matters because it weakens any obvious practical justification for extraordinary location tracking. It does not prove an improper motive, and the reporting does not establish that the devices were used. But it leaves the Justice Department owing an explanation for why such invasive authority was sought against someone publicly anticipating federal custody.

A false claim became infrastructure

The significant fact is not merely that one description was wrong. According to Fort’s lawyers, the same false allegation appeared in at least 15 affidavits. Repetition gave it institutional weight: the government’s characterization traveled from document to document as part of the asserted basis for surveillance.

The records do not establish that investigators knowingly lied. That distinction must be preserved. It is still inadequate to reduce the episode to an innocent “wrinkle.” When prosecutors ask courts to authorize coercive powers, factual accuracy is not clerical polish. It is the condition under which that power is supposed to be legitimate.

Journalism was recast as participation

The government sought to build cases involving people who said they were documenting the protest as journalists, while its affidavits described Lemon and Fort as chanting alongside protesters. That false description blurred the line between observing an event and joining it—the very line a court later treated as decisive when it found no evidence of criminal conduct by two journalists.

This is where the framing can misdirect. The central issue is not whether reporters stood near disruptive protesters or recorded a politically charged confrontation. It is whether federal officials had evidence that those reporters committed crimes. Proximity is not participation, and documentation is not conspiracy. Treating those categories as interchangeable expands prosecutorial discretion while forcing journalists to prove they were doing what the footage already showed them doing.

Judicial resistance was a brake, not a cure

Judges rejected most of the initial arrest-warrant applications, and the government failed to overturn that decision before withdrawing its request. That scrutiny mattered. But prosecutors subsequently secured an indictment, demonstrating how many routes the federal government possesses when one legal avenue closes.

The story therefore is not a comforting tale of checks and balances functioning automatically. It is a record of judges catching unsupported claims only after officials submitted them and sought coercive authority. Courts can refuse particular requests; they cannot erase the burden, expense, exposure, and chilling effect produced by the investigation itself.

The systemic error

This episode reveals how state power can convert an unverified—or simply false—premise into operational momentum. A claim enters an affidavit, recurs across filings, supports increasingly intrusive requests, and forces its targets to contest the government’s narrative from a position of radically lesser power.

No evidence in the reporting establishes deliberate falsification, and the unexplained surveillance requests do not by themselves prove retaliation. What the record does establish is serious enough: the Justice Department repeatedly presented a false account while pursuing journalists and a producer, even as courts questioned the criminal basis for doing so. In such a system, correction comes late. The warrant application, the investigation, and the intimidation arrive first.
