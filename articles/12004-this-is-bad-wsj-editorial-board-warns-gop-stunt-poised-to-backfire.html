'This is bad': WSJ editorial board warns GOP stunt poised to backfire

Texas Republicans called a special session to redraw the state's electoral map following the devastating floods in the state's Hill Country that killed more than 130 people and displaced hundreds more. Republicans have defended the move as legal while Democrats contend they are simply trying to rig the 2026 midterm election.

Democrats have fled Texas in protest of the GOP's efforts. The move prevents the state legislature from achieving a quorum, and therefore, they cannot vote on the new maps until Democrats return.

The WSJ editorial board suggested the situation is a race to the bottom because Democrats in states like California and New York will surely retaliate.

"This practice is bad for political competition, since each party draws itself 'safe' seats where voters reward loud, loyal partisans," the editorial board wrote in a recent op-ed.

While gerrymandering may seem like good politics now, the editorial board warned it could backfire. It could force Congress to intercede and take away some of the rights that states are using to redraw their maps in the first place.

"But telling states they can only redistrict once per decade might de-escalate the gerrymander wars, and it would mainly ratify the status quo of recent years. Both parties could benefit from this kind of disarmament treaty, and voters most of all," the editorial reads.

Our Analysis:

Gerrymandering: The Art of Political War

In a recent editorial, the Wall Street Journal editorial board has taken a stance that might raise eyebrows, especially among its conservative base. The topic at hand? Gerrymandering in Texas, a practice as American as apple pie, yet far more divisive. This time, it's the Texas Republicans attempting to redraw the state's electoral map under the guise of a special session, purportedly called in response to the devastating floods in the state's Hill Country. However, the real motive seems as clear as day: rigging the 2026 midterm election.

The Texas Tango: Republicans vs. Democrats

The GOP's move is as transparent as it is cunning. By calling a special session to redraw electoral maps in the aftermath of a natural disaster, they've shown a level of opportunism that would make Machiavelli blush. The Democrats, on the other hand, have resorted to a time-honored tradition of political protest: fleeing the state. This prevents the Texas legislature from achieving a quorum, effectively putting a temporary stop to the Republicans' plans. This is not a new tactic, but its use underscores the desperation and high stakes involved in this political chess game.

The Editorial's Warning: A Race to the Bottom

The WSJ editorial board warns of a "race to the bottom," suggesting that Democratic states like California and New York might retaliate. This is a valid concern, as gerrymandering has become a weaponized tool in the political arsenal of both parties. The board's critique is sharp, highlighting how gerrymandering erodes political competition, rewarding "loud, loyal partisans" at the expense of moderate voices and, ultimately, democracy itself.

A Backfire in the Making?

Perhaps the most intriguing part of the editorial is the warning that this strategy could backfire. The notion that Congress could step in and strip states of their redistricting powers is a fascinating one. It suggests a potential future where the federal government imposes restrictions on when and how states can redraw their electoral maps, possibly limiting this practice to once per decade. This, the editorial argues, could "de-escalate the gerrymander wars" and benefit both parties—and, most importantly, the voters.

Analysis: Between the Lines

While the WSJ's editorial board makes a compelling argument, it's essential to read between the lines. The call for a "disarmament treaty" in the gerrymandering wars is an attractive idea, but it's as likely as achieving world peace through a group hug. The underlying problem isn't just gerrymandering; it's the pervasive influence of partisanship that has poisoned the well of American politics. Yes, limiting redistricting to once a decade could help, but it's akin to putting a Band-Aid on a bullet wound.

Moreover, the focus on potential retaliation by states like California and New York feels like a veiled warning: "Don't push us too far, or we'll do the same." It's a reminder that in politics, the threat of mutual destruction is often the only deterrent against egregious actions.

The Bottom Line

The Wall Street Journal's editorial is a rare moment of clarity in an otherwise murky political landscape. However, it falls short of addressing the root causes of the issue. Gerrymandering is but a symptom of a larger disease: the utter lack of bipartisan cooperation and the prioritization of party over country. Until that fundamental problem is addressed, expect more maps to be drawn and redrawn, not in the interest of fair representation, but as weapons in an endless political war.
