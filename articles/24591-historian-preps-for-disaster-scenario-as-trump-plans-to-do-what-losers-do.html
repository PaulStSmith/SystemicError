Historian preps for disaster scenario as Trump plans to do 'what losers do'

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 10, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that historian Timothy D. Snyder predicted Donald Trump would claim the Nov. 3 election was rigged if Democrats won. The article does not establish that Trump had announced such a plan; “what losers do” was Snyder’s description of the anticipated claim, not a reported admission from Trump.

Speaking with former Republican strategist Steve Schmidt, Snyder urged voters and Democrats to frame a victory immediately: explain why they won, publicize what they expected, and focus on governing priorities including affordability, gas prices, and corruption. He argued that establishing this narrative before any fraud allegation could make Trump’s response appear stale and unconvincing.

The report also cites retired judge Michael Luttig’s warning that Republicans might refuse to seat elected Democrats. Snyder speculated that Trump had probably discussed that and other possibilities, but supplied no evidence of those conversations. He stressed that any attempt to overturn an electoral result would depend on other officials choosing to cooperate.

The headline outruns the evidence

AlterNet converts a forecast into a plan. Snyder says he expects Trump to allege fraud; the headline says Trump “plans” to do it. Those are materially different claims.

The reporting supports concern about a possible post-election narrative battle. It does not demonstrate a settled operation, identify participants in one, or document instructions from Trump. A publication cannot credibly criticize political disinformation while making its own most consequential assertion stronger than its evidence.

Power requires accomplices

Snyder’s strongest point is institutional rather than psychological: Trump cannot produce an electoral outcome by declaration alone. Any effort to nullify votes or deny elected lawmakers their seats would require cooperation from people controlling certification, litigation, congressional procedure, law enforcement, or other state machinery.

That distinction assigns agency where it belongs. Trump could make a claim. Officials with legal authority would decide whether to indulge it, amplify it, or convert it into government action. Treating those actors as scenery would conceal the chain of responsibility.

Narrative is not government

Snyder proposes rapid public messaging as a form of inoculation. Calling an anticipated fraud claim a loser’s maneuver may help prevent television coverage from treating an unsupported allegation as an unresolved mystery.

But messaging cannot substitute for institutional preparation. Explaining a victory does not seat lawmakers, certify results, or constrain officials willing to misuse their offices. A hopeful agenda may compete with propaganda; it cannot neutralize procedural obstruction by itself.

His criticism of Democrats nevertheless lands: a party expecting to win must be prepared to exercise power. Fear of responsibility is not caution. It is an invitation for opponents to define both the election and what follows it.

The disaster is conditional

The article gestures toward an authoritarian scenario, but the evidence it presents remains prospective. Luttig warned of Republicans refusing to seat Democratic winners; Snyder speculated about conversations and possible tactics. Neither warning establishes that the acts occurred or that a coordinated plan existed.

That uncertainty should sharpen the analysis, not soften it. The relevant question is which officials possess the authority to obstruct a result, what choices they make, and what consequences attach to those choices. “Chaos” is often a misleading label when identifiable people are using identifiable powers.

Democracy’s weakest verb

Political coverage habitually says institutions “failed” as though buildings made mistakes. Institutions act through officeholders. An unsupported fraud claim becomes politically dangerous only when broadcasters reward it, party leaders repeat it, lawyers dress it in procedure, or officials use public authority to enforce it.

The systemic lesson is therefore less dramatic and more demanding than the headline: watch the enablers. Personal bravado may start the spectacle, but institutional cowardice determines whether spectacle acquires force.
