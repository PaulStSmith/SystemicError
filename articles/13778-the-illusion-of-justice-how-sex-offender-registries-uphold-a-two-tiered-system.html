The Illusion of Justice: How Sex Offender Registries Uphold a Two-Tiered System

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 11, 20252 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Myth of SafetyTwo Systems, One LieA Society Addicted to ScapegoatsThe Cynics and the ShruggersWe Either Believe in Justice, or We Don’t

In a world where billionaires can traffic minors and walk free while low-income offenders face lifelong surveillance for crimes they’ve already served time for, it’s worth asking: Who is the registry really protecting?

The scene from Blue Bloods that sparked this discussion was clear in its subtext—Kenneth Tripp, a registered sex offender, tried to rebuild a life, only to be hunted, assaulted, and treated like a threat no matter what he did. Not because he posed danger now, but because of a label he can never shed. This isn’t public safety—it’s institutionalized vengeance.

The Myth of Safety

Let’s be blunt: There is no credible evidence that public sex offender registries reduce recidivism. Study after study has shown they do nothing to make communities safer. What they do is create targets. They fracture neighborhoods, increase homelessness, limit employment, and isolate people until reintegration becomes impossible.

Registries aren’t tools of protection. They’re weapons of punishment—after the punishment has already been served.

Two Systems, One Lie

Jeffrey Epstein ran a trafficking operation for years. He was convicted, “served” a laughable sentence, got VIP work release, and was coddled by a legal system that only got serious when public pressure finally boiled over. Trump has been accused by dozens of women and stood next to Epstein in photos and praise—yet walks free, gets re-elected, and rewrites his history with a magic marker.

Meanwhile, someone like Tripp, who served time, registered, and tried to live quietly, is assaulted in broad daylight—and there’s not a whisper of outrage. The legal system shows mercy to predators with private islands, but it’ll throw the book at someone who’s broke and already broke down.

A Society Addicted to Scapegoats

The registry functions as a modern scarlet letter, not a crime prevention tool. It exists because politicians love it—because it looks “tough on crime.” It makes people feel safe while doing absolutely nothing useful. And worse, it reinforces a cultural narrative: that we can only sleep soundly if someone else is constantly branded the monster.

Even when that someone is no longer a threat. Even when they’ve done everything asked of them.

The Cynics and the Shruggers

This is where the comment section comes in. Some people expressed outrage that the rich escape justice. Fair. Others just threw up their hands—“Well, the rich protect their own.” As if that’s the end of the conversation.

It’s not. That’s the problem.

The correct response to corruption at the top is not apathy at the bottom. It’s outrage. It’s organization. It’s refusing to accept that people who did time should be branded and hunted while oligarchs shake hands at golf tournaments.

We Either Believe in Justice, or We Don’t

If rehabilitation is a lie, say it. If our society only believes in vengeance, admit it. But don’t pretend these registries protect children or prevent crimes. They exist to create permanent underclasses of “acceptable” targets—poor, powerless, and legally doomed to be hated forever.

Justice is supposed to be blind. But in America, it’s just squinting at your bank account.
