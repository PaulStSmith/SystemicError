Trump retreats on taxpayer-funded ads as DOJ dodges legality questions

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 13, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the Trump administration said it would stop airing a $20 million taxpayer-funded advertising campaign after the Democratic National Committee and Common Cause sued and a judge demanded answers about its legality. A Homeland Security official told the court that the ads would air for the last time on Sunday, but the Justice Department did not answer the lawsuits’ allegations or the judge’s questions about the White House’s role.

According to a Customs and Border Protection official cited in the filing, CBP issued the purchase order on Sept. 20 through a previously awarded agreement with Maryland-based LMD Agency. The money came from a Republican law enacted in 2025 and appropriating funds for border-security activities. The ads appeared on major cable and broadcast networks.

The retreat followed broad public opposition: 86 percent of respondents in a Reuters/Ipsos poll conducted Sept. 30–Oct. 5 called the use of taxpayer money inappropriate, including 80 percent of Republicans surveyed. The administration reportedly has no plan to reimburse the government. Common Cause argues that ending the broadcasts is insufficient and is seeking to recover funds paid but potentially unspent.

The power was federal

This was not a dispute among comparably situated political participants. The administration controlled the agencies, the appropriation and the machinery used to purchase nationwide airtime. The public supplied the money; executive officials decided how to spend it.

Congressional Republicans enabled that discretion by passing the 2025 funding law. The article does not establish that the statute authorized campaign advertising. It does establish the institutional chain that mattered: lawmakers furnished the funds, CBP issued the purchase order, and the administration defended the result in court.

Withdrawal is not accountability

Stopping the ads after they aired does not answer whether officials lawfully commissioned them. Nor does it recover money already spent or establish limits on repeating the tactic.

The Justice Department’s position, as reported, was procedural evasion: dismiss the case because the challenged conduct was ending. That argument asks the court to treat the expiration of the purchase as a substitute for examining the purchase itself. Common Cause’s response identifies the obvious incentive—officials could run out the clock, cease when judicial review approaches and preserve the benefits of conduct that escapes a ruling.

The missing answers matter

The unresolved issue is not merely whether viewers disliked the commercials. It is who directed their creation, what role the White House played and whether public funds were converted into an electoral advantage. AlterNet reports that the Justice Department declined to address the allegations and ignored questions about White House involvement.

That silence is not proof of illegality or of an undisclosed scheme. It is, however, an exercise of institutional power: the government’s lawyers attempted to end scrutiny without supplying the facts the court requested. The administration announced cessation while resisting adjudication.

Backlash is not the governing check

The poll explains the political pressure, but popularity is a poor substitute for enforceable restraint. Taxpayer-funded political promotion would remain an abuse of public power even if polling found it popular; overwhelming opposition merely made retreat less costly than continued defense.

Framing this chiefly as Trump “waving the white flag” risks converting an accountability dispute into a story about tactical defeat. The weaker actors—the DNC, Common Cause and taxpayers—did not control the money or the agencies. They could only sue after officials had already committed funds and distributed the ads.

A system built for faits accomplis

The larger pattern is government by completed transaction: appropriate broadly, spend quickly, litigate slowly and declare the controversy moot once the benefit has been captured. Courts may eventually reject that maneuver, but delay itself favors the officeholder controlling the state.

Without disclosure, recovery of unused funds and a ruling on the legal boundaries, the lesson for future administrations is corrosively simple. Public money can finance politically useful messaging, and accountability may arrive only after the airtime is gone.
