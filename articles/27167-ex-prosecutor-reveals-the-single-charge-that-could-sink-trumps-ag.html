Ex-prosecutor reveals the single charge that could sink Trump’s AG

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 10, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Former federal prosecutor Harry Litman argues that House Democrats should pursue a single impeachment charge against Attorney General Todd Blanche: using federal prosecution to punish President Donald Trump’s perceived enemies. AlterNet reports that Litman pointed to cases involving James Comey, Letitia James, Cassidy Hutchinson, and six Democratic lawmakers who told service members they could refuse illegal orders.

Litman contends that impeachment is the only remedy capable of reaching Blanche personally because prosecutions impose costs before courts rule and prosecutors generally cannot be sued for damages. The reported proposal remains an argument for future House action: Rep. Hank Johnson has called for Blanche’s impeachment, while Rep. Jamie Raskin is expected to lead the Judiciary Committee where proceedings would begin.

The power is prosecutorial

The central actor is not the opposition lawmakers discussing impeachment. It is the attorney general, who controls a department with the power to investigate, charge, and financially exhaust people. Trump’s demand that the Justice Department examine Democrats for discussing impeachment further underscores the imbalance: elected legislators were considering a constitutional remedy, while the president answered by invoking federal law enforcement.

Blanche reinforced that pressure when CNN asked whether Democratic lawmakers could face prison. His answer—“depends on what the crime is and what they’ve done”—did not identify a crime. In the context AlterNet describes, however, it left prosecution hanging over lawmakers performing an expressly constitutional function.

Process can become punishment

Litman’s proposed charge isolates the alleged abuse that matters most: not merely losing cases or stretching legal theories, but directing prosecutorial machinery at political adversaries. His examples span former officials, a state attorney general, a congressional witness, and sitting lawmakers. The alleged pattern is institutional, not personal.

A prosecution need not end in conviction to inflict damage. Legal bills, public stigma, disrupted careers, and years of uncertainty arrive long before acquittal or dismissal. That asymmetry makes prosecutorial discretion unusually dangerous when partisan retaliation is alleged: the state can impose punishment through procedure while insisting that the courts will eventually sort it out.

The impeachment reversal

Trump called Democratic impeachment threats “extortion” and reportedly ordered the Justice Department to investigate. That framing reverses the relevant chain of authority. Congress possesses impeachment power; the executive branch possesses police and prosecutorial power. Treating legislative oversight as criminal coercion while mobilizing law enforcement against those discussing it converts accountability into the supposed offense.

Hegseth used the same maneuver in his response to pending impeachment, describing the proceedings as obsession and partisan harassment. The substance of the allegations disappears, replaced by a story in which powerful officials are victims because other institutions attempt to constrain them.

A narrow charge has limits

Litman’s strategy has political clarity. A single count would prevent a sprawling catalogue of misconduct from obscuring the question of retaliatory prosecution. It would force lawmakers to decide whether the Justice Department may be used against presidential enemies.

But impeachment is not self-executing. House action would begin a political process, not guarantee removal, and the article reports only proposals and preparations. The decisive agency therefore belongs not just to Blanche or Trump, but also to members of Congress who must choose whether alleged retaliation merits a formal institutional response.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is the conversion of state power into partisan insulation. Officials deploy or threaten coercive authority, then recast oversight as persecution. The weaker actors—the targets of investigation, individual lawmakers, and dismissed prosecutors—are made to look like the source of instability because they resist.

That is the misdirection. The constitutional problem described here is not that impeachment talk creates conflict. It is that officials entrusted with prosecution may be using—or threatening to use—that power against the people positioned to expose, oppose, or restrain them. When legal process becomes a political weapon, eventual courtroom outcomes cannot repair all the damage.
