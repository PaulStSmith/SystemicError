Conservative anti-Trump activist Rick Wilson, who co-founded the Lincoln Project and hosts the group's podcast, recently flagged Trump's decision to step back from one of his most crucial political positions.

"ICE and DHS are absolutely destroying farms and construction businesses by their sweeping and illegal raids and seizures of immigrants (legal and otherwise). MAGA noticed," Wilson wrote. "TACO Don started the walkback Thursday, no doubt leaving Stephen Miller (R-The Cuck Chair) in a fetal position, weeping."

Wilson highlighted Trump's own statement on the matter, which was, "Our farmers are being hurt badly by, you know, they have very good workers. They’ve worked for them for 20 years. They’re not citizens, but they’ve turned out to be, you know, great. And we’re going to have to do something about that."

Trump continued:

“We can’t take farmers and take all their people and send them back because they don’t have maybe what they’re supposed to have, maybe not. And you know what’s going to happen and what is happening?... And then you’re supposed to throw them out and you know what happens? They end up hiring the people, the criminals that have come in, the murderers from prisons and everything else.”

According to Wilson, "This was the central, crucial lie from the start: they all have to go. They’re all evil. They’re all lesser races of impure blood."

"This is the beginning of the end for Stephen Miller’s dream of a white ethnostate," Wilson added.

Our Analysis:

The Art of the Walkback: Trump's Immigration Pivot

In what can only be described as a monumental shift, Donald Trump appears to be backpedaling on one of his cornerstone political positions. This pivot, so dramatic in its departure from previous rhetoric, is said to have left his adviser Stephen Miller "in a fetal position, weeping," if former Republican strategist and conservative anti-Trump activist Rick Wilson is to be believed. The imagery alone of Miller, a staunch advocate for hardline immigration policies, reduced to tears over Trump's reversal, is enough to make one pause. But let's dissect the layers of this unfolding drama.

The Trump Reversal

Trump's sudden empathy for immigrant workers, specifically within the farming sector, marks a stark contrast to his administration's previous stance on immigration. His acknowledgment of the value and contribution of these workers, many of whom are not citizens, to the American economy is a far cry from the harsh narratives and policies pushed during his tenure. The statement, "Our farmers are being hurt badly by, you know, they have very good workers. They’ve worked for them for 20 years. They’re not citizens, but they’ve turned out to be, you know, great. And we’re going to have to do something about that," is a radical departure from the “build the wall” mantra.

The Underlying Irony

Rick Wilson's analysis of this situation sheds light on a crucial aspect of Trump's presidency and the broader Republican strategy on immigration. The narrative of all immigrants being "evil," "lesser races of impure blood," as Wilson puts it, was not just a political stance but a calculated move to stir fear and division. Trump's walkback exposes the hollowness and opportunism at the heart of such narratives. The sudden shift in tone suggests that political expediency, rather than any genuine concern for the welfare of immigrant workers or the economic realities facing American farmers, guides Trump's rhetoric.

The Fallout

Wilson's commentary predicts this pivot as "the beginning of the end for Stephen Miller’s dream of a white ethnostate," highlighting a potential ideological rift within the Trump camp. This episode underscores a critical point about the Trump brand of politics: it is inherently malleable, tailored to suit immediate needs or pressures rather than any steadfast principles. The fallout from this, particularly among Trump's base and his advisors like Miller, who have championed the hardline immigration stance, remains to be seen.

Conclusion

Trump's immigration walkback is not just a simple policy shift; it's a revealing moment that strips bare the mechanics of fear and division that powered his rise to and maintenance of power. It's a testament to the transactional nature of his political ethos, where allegiances, principles, and policies are all subject to change based on the whims of political expediency. The image of Stephen Miller, the architect of many of Trump's immigration policies, potentially in despair over this reversal, is symbolic of the broader ideological disarray that may follow. As observers, we must ask: what does this say about the future of Trumpian politics, and how will this affect the broader landscape of American political discourse? One thing is clear: in the Trump saga, loyalty is fleeting, and no position is too sacred for a walkback.
