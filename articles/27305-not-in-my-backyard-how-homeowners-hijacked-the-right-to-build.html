Not in My Backyard: How Homeowners Hijacked the Right to Build

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 16, 202616 min read

From San Francisco to London, the housing crisis isn’t simply a failure to build. It’s what happens when the people who already have homes are given the political power to keep everyone else out.

Nine Years. Zero Apartments. One Very Important Parking Lot.

In 2017, a developer proposed something apparently outrageous in San Francisco: replacing a downtown parking lot with 495 apartments.

Not demolishing a historic landmark. Not paving over a protected forest. Not displacing hundreds of existing families.

A parking lot.

The property, at 469 Stevenson Street, was supposed to become a 27-story residential tower, complete with affordable housing. The San Francisco Planning Commission approved the project in 2021.

Then came the neighborhood opposition.

A local group appealed the decision. The Board of Supervisors voted 8–3 to overturn the project's environmental certification, citing concerns including shadows on a nearby plaza and potential effects on neighborhood rents.

Apparently, San Francisco had discovered a radical new principle of urban planning:

If housing is too expensive, the solution is to prevent people from building more of it.

The State of California intervened, investigating whether the city's actions violated state housing law. The project was approved again in 2023.

And as of March 2026?

Still a parking lot.

The developer was requesting an extension of its approvals through 2029, citing challenging market conditions and rising construction costs.

Nine years of planning, approvals, appeals, reconsiderations, and political obstruction, and San Francisco had managed to preserve one of its most precious urban resources:

A place to park cars.

Meanwhile, hundreds of potential households remained without the apartments that could have stood there.

Welcome to the wonderful world of NIMBYism.

Not in My Backyard — But Especially Not Near My Property Value

NIMBY stands for “Not In My Backyard,” a term describing people who oppose developments near their homes even when they acknowledge that those developments may be needed elsewhere.

The phenomenon is not exclusive to homeowners, nor is every objection to development illegitimate.

But when housing construction threatens perceived neighborhood exclusivity, the familiar arguments emerge.

The building is too tall. It will create shadows. Traffic will get worse. Parking will become difficult. The neighborhood will lose its character.

And frequently lurking beneath those objections is another concern:

What will happen to my property's value?

Let's be clear about something.

Owning a home gives you property rights. It does not give you ownership of the entire neighborhood.

You are entitled to protection against dangerous construction, unlawful encroachment, and demonstrable damage to your property.

You are not automatically entitled to an unobstructed view forever, unlimited street parking, or a neighborhood frozen at the precise moment you purchased your house.

And you certainly shouldn't be entitled to prevent hundreds of other people from finding housing merely because their arrival might inconvenience you.

That's not the defense of private property.

That's the appropriation of everyone else's property rights for your personal benefit.

It's like an HOA, except considerably worse.

An HOA might fine you because your lawn is two inches too tall.

An organized NIMBY movement can help ensure that hundreds of families never have a lawn, an apartment, or even a front door in the first place.

And the truly remarkable part is that the political system often treats this as responsible civic engagement.

The People Who Don't Live There Don't Get a Vote

Here is the structural defect that makes NIMBYism so effective.

Existing homeowners vote.

They attend neighborhood meetings. They contact elected officials. They organize opposition campaigns. They challenge planning decisions.

But the people who would eventually live in those 495 apartments?

They don't live there yet.

They may be young professionals who cannot afford to move into the city. Teachers commuting from distant communities. Healthcare workers spending hours in traffic. Families sharing overcrowded housing because rents are prohibitive.

Some may already live in San Francisco but desperately need somewhere more suitable.

They are real people with real housing needs.

Yet they are often invisible in the local political process.

The people potentially inconvenienced by new construction are identifiable, organized, and politically influential.

The people who would benefit from it are dispersed, frequently unknown, and poorly represented.

The people who already have housing get to help decide whether the people who need housing will ever get any.

And politicians, naturally, respond to the voters standing in front of them.

This is how a legitimate democratic process can become a mechanism of exclusion.

Not because democracy itself is defective, but because the political constituency affected by a decision is larger than the constituency allowed meaningful influence over it.

The people excluded from a city by its housing policies are still affected by those policies.

They just don't get much of a say.

London: NIMBYism with a British Accent

Perhaps this is simply an American pathology?

Unfortunately, no.

Consider London.

Like San Francisco, London is an economically important city with enormous housing demand and punishing housing costs.

England's planning system provides statutory decision periods of eight weeks for ordinary applications and thirteen weeks for major developments, subject to exceptions and agreed extensions.

Sounds reasonable.

Until we remember that statutory deadlines and actual development timelines are not the same thing.

Local councils consult residents, consider objections, and sometimes refer controversial projects to elected planning committees.

Shadows, traffic, parking, privacy, and neighborhood appearance can all be legitimate planning considerations.

One important distinction, however, is that British planning guidance does not treat a reduction in neighboring property values as a legitimate reason to refuse permission.

Nor do neighbors ordinarily have a direct right to appeal an approval through the planning appeals system. Legal challenges generally concern whether the decision was lawful.

That's an improvement over a system where political opposition can repeatedly reopen approved projects.

Yet London still struggles to deliver enough housing.

Why?

Because even without an ordinary third-party appeal, politically sensitive planning decisions can become slow, contentious, and expensive.

Developers face uncertainty. Local councils face organized opposition. Politicians face homeowners who know exactly whom to blame if something changes in their neighborhood.

Different mechanisms.

Familiar outcome.

Paris: You Can Challenge the Law, Not Simply Your Neighbor's Existence

France offers another model.

In Paris, building projects generally require a permis de construire, assessed against applicable planning rules, building requirements, and heritage protections.

Paris is hardly a paradise of deregulation. Its historical architecture is extensively protected, and construction can be extraordinarily complicated.

However, the system makes an important distinction.

A neighbor who believes a permit is unlawful can challenge it through administrative procedures and the courts. The ordinary period for a third-party legal challenge is two months from proper public display of the authorization.

But an objection must rest on a legally recognized basis.

“I don't want those people living near me” is not, by itself, a planning-law argument.

Neither is “I liked my neighborhood better before someone bought the empty lot.”

France still allows development disputes, and legal challenges can be costly. But the governing principle is worth examining:

The question is whether construction complies with the law, not whether its neighbors find it socially acceptable.

That difference matters.

Berlin: There Is a Rule for That

Germany provides an especially revealing contrast.

Berlin has several construction authorization procedures, including an exemption mechanism called Genehmigungsfreistellung.

For qualifying projects that comply with the applicable development plan and other requirements, construction can generally proceed after one month following submission of complete documentation, provided the authorities don't intervene.

Larger or more complicated developments may require ordinary or simplified permits, technical reviews, and additional approvals.

A 27-story tower does not magically receive approval in thirty days simply because it is located in Germany.

But the underlying philosophy is different.

If a project qualifies under an established set of rules, those rules can provide a relatively predictable path forward.

Neighbors may challenge violations of legally protected interests. They do not possess a general entitlement to stop lawful construction simply because they dislike additional housing.

Germany certainly has housing problems of its own, including high construction costs, financing difficulties, and bureaucratic delays.

The lesson isn't that German bureaucracy is wonderful.

The lesson is that predictable rules are preferable to perpetual political renegotiation.

A developer should be able to determine what is legally permitted before spending years attempting to build it.

And a neighbor's personal preferences should not acquire the force of law merely because that neighbor is loud enough.

Brazil: The City Hall, the Permit, and the Absence of a Neighborhood Referendum

Now consider a system familiar to many Brazilians.

In municipalities such as Santo André, in metropolitan São Paulo, the ordinary approach is to submit construction plans to the municipal authorities for evaluation under the applicable building code, zoning rules, and land-use regulations.

Santo André even operates an electronic licensing system called Obra Fácil.

For straightforward projects, the process can be relatively quick, although approval times vary considerably with complexity. Large developments, environmental restrictions, and neighborhood-impact requirements can introduce additional scrutiny.

Brazilian bureaucracy can be maddening. Nobody seriously needs to be told otherwise.

But there is an important distinction between obtaining municipal approval and asking the neighborhood for permission.

For an ordinary, legally compliant project, the municipality generally doesn't conduct a popularity contest among neighboring homeowners.

The property owner submits the required documentation. The government evaluates the proposed construction.

If the project satisfies the applicable requirements, it can be authorized.

Neighbors retain legal protections against violations and harmful activities. Major developments can require public-impact assessments and consultation.

But disliking a proposal is not ordinarily sufficient to stop it.

Imagine having to ask everyone on your street whether you may build a house on land you already own.

And then imagine that, even after city hall approves it, a neighborhood association can drag the decision back into political proceedings because your house might cast a shadow across someone's favorite afternoon reading spot.

That is the absurdity San Francisco has repeatedly demonstrated.

China: The Opposite Extreme

China provides a very different warning.

For decades, Chinese local governments had strong incentives to expand construction and infrastructure investment.

The results were extraordinary.

Entire districts, vast transportation networks, and enormous residential complexes appeared at a pace difficult to imagine in most Western cities.

Sometimes, however, China built considerably more than local demand could immediately absorb.

Kangbashi, in Inner Mongolia, became internationally famous as a supposed ghost city.

Planned for approximately 300,000 residents, it had around 119,000 in the 2020 census.

Empty residential towers and sprawling streets make compelling television.

But the comparison raises a question that Western housing debates often ignore.

Why is excess housing considered an obvious planning failure, while chronic housing shortages are treated as an unavoidable fact of life?

Overbuilding can be economically disastrous, especially when financed through unsustainable debt or driven by speculative demand.

China's property-sector problems are substantial, and nobody should mistake government-directed construction for a universally successful model.

But a city can also suffer from underbuilding.

One system may create buildings without sufficient residents.

Another may have residents desperately seeking buildings that powerful interests won't allow to exist.

Neither represents sound planning.

Yet only one shortage is routinely defended as protecting neighborhood character.

The Economics NIMBYs Would Rather Ignore

There is another problem with the claim that building more apartments will necessarily make housing less affordable.

It conflicts with a substantial body of economic research.

A 2024 review by Vicki Been, Ingrid Gould Ellen, and Katherine O'Regan, published in Housing Policy Debate, examined recent research into housing supply and rental costs.

The authors found that increasing housing supply generally reduces rents or slows their growth at the regional level, with some studies identifying similar effects in surrounding neighborhoods.

A separate study by Andreas Mense, published in 2025 in the Journal of Political Economy Macroeconomics, found that new housing supply can reduce rents across different market segments.

Why?

Because housing markets are interconnected.

When someone moves into a new apartment, they often leave another dwelling available. Someone else moves into that one, potentially freeing another.

New housing creates chains of moves that can increase availability beyond the newly constructed building itself.

Of course, merely approving expensive luxury apartments will not magically provide decent housing for everyone.

Cities need genuinely affordable housing, subsidized construction, preservation of existing low-cost units, and protections against displacement.

But none of those objectives is served by preventing housing construction altogether.

And here's the particularly ugly part of the 469 Stevenson controversy.

The proposed development included affordable apartments.

The 2023 approval provided for 73 affordable units on-site.

The 2026 extension request sought to reduce the on-site affordable share from 19% to 15%, an unfortunate development that underscores how financial pressures can weaken affordability commitments.

That doesn't mean every dollar of increased construction cost can be attributed to NIMBY opposition. Inflation, financing conditions, labor, and materials all affect projects.

But prolonged political obstruction certainly doesn't make construction cheaper.

And when a development becomes financially impossible, the city doesn't get more affordable homes.

It gets nothing.

Or, in this case, a parking lot.

When NIMBYism Becomes the Law

There is something even more outrageous than homeowners organizing to stop apartment buildings from being constructed.

In much of the United States, they don't even need to organize.

The law has already prohibited the apartments.

Welcome to exclusionary zoning, one of the most effective mechanisms ever devised for creating housing scarcity while pretending to regulate urban development.

Consider Los Angeles.

Under its traditional R1 residential zoning, a property owner is generally permitted to build a single-family house, subject to applicable exceptions. A conventional apartment building, however, is not ordinarily permitted in that same zone.

And the restrictions don't end there.

The standard minimum lot size is 5,000 square feet.

That's approximately 465 square meters.

Imagine owning a perfectly suitable piece of land in a city suffering from a housing shortage, wanting to construct a modest four-unit apartment building, and being told that the law reserves your property primarily for one detached house.

Not because the apartment building would be structurally unsound.

Not because the land is environmentally protected.

Not because the infrastructure cannot accommodate additional residents.

But because the zoning designation says so.

California has begun loosening these restrictions. Since 2022, Senate Bill 9 has permitted qualifying two-unit developments and lot splits in single-family zones, with subsequent amendments expanding and refining the framework.

San Francisco has also begun dismantling some of its restrictions. Its Family Zoning Plan, effective January 2026, allows greater density and taller buildings in designated areas, especially along commercial and transit corridors.

These are welcome changes.

But consider what they reveal.

For decades, American cities legally prohibited people from building the very housing those cities desperately needed.

And this wasn't merely an unfortunate consequence of otherwise sensible regulations.

The historical record is considerably uglier.

In 1917, the United States Supreme Court struck down explicit racial residential segregation ordinances in Buchanan v. Warley.

But racial and economic exclusion did not disappear.

Instead, zoning restrictions became an alternative means of controlling who could afford to live in particular neighborhoods.

Minimum lot sizes. Single-family-only districts. Restrictions on apartments. Minimum building dimensions. Requirements that increased construction costs.

The language changed.

The exclusion continued.

In 1926, the Supreme Court upheld municipal zoning authority in Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co., helping establish the legal foundation for the restrictive residential zoning that spread throughout the country.

And the consequences were enormous.

A 2024 study from the University of California, Berkeley found that approximately 82% of California's residential land within incorporated jurisdictions was zoned exclusively for single-family housing under the zoning rules examined.

Eighty-two percent.

In a state synonymous with astronomical housing prices, vast portions of residential land were legally reserved for the least space-efficient conventional housing arrangement.

Think about the implications.

You cannot construct enough apartments because the zoning doesn't permit them.

You cannot easily change the zoning because existing residents oppose increased density.

You cannot accommodate the population without expanding outward, increasing commuting distances, infrastructure costs, and dependence on cars.

And when housing prices inevitably soar, politicians solemnly announce that the city is experiencing a housing crisis.

As though the shortage had fallen from the sky.

They didn't merely fail to build enough housing. They wrote laws restricting how much housing could be built.

Now, not every zoning restriction is illegitimate.

Separating hazardous industrial operations from residential neighborhoods is sensible. Protecting floodplains, ensuring structural safety, and maintaining adequate infrastructure are essential government responsibilities.

But preventing a duplex, triplex, or modest apartment building from existing in a residential neighborhood simply because it contains multiple households?

That deserves a much better justification than preserving neighborhood character.

Because “neighborhood character” has an extraordinarily convenient habit of meaning whatever the existing homeowners want it to mean.

And historically, those preferences have not always been innocent.

The particularly bitter irony is that many of the same political voices celebrating property rights and opposing government interference have little objection to government prohibiting another property owner from constructing additional homes.

Apparently, freedom extends all the way to the property line.

And then it becomes someone else's problem.

NIMBYism is bad enough when it takes the form of a neighborhood protest.

It is considerably worse when it has been written into the municipal code.

At that point, exclusion doesn't need angry homeowners standing outside city hall.

It has the force of law.

The Property-Rights Hypocrisy

This is where the American contradiction becomes almost comical.

The United States has an extensive political tradition celebrating private property, individual liberty, and limited government interference.

Politicians regularly lecture the public about the sanctity of ownership.

Until someone wants to build apartments.

Suddenly, private property is everyone's business.

The height of the building. The appearance of its exterior. The number of apartments. The hypothetical impact on somebody else's investment.

And the neighbors who demand government intervention to prevent construction may be the same people who insist that government should stay out of private affairs.

Apparently, property rights are sacred only when they belong to people who already own the surrounding property.

This is not an argument for abolishing zoning, environmental protections, or building standards.

Those rules exist for legitimate reasons.

The public has every right to demand structurally safe buildings, adequate infrastructure, environmental safeguards, and compliance with democratically adopted planning requirements.

But there is a world of difference between enforcing rules and allowing organized groups to manipulate those rules into instruments of exclusion.

A property right that depends on the political approval of every nearby property owner is not much of a right at all.

And a housing policy designed around preserving the comfort of existing homeowners is not a housing policy.

It's an exclusion policy.

Democracy Is Not a Neighborhood Veto

Supporters of extensive neighborhood participation often invoke democracy.

Residents deserve a voice, they argue.

And they do.

But having a voice is not the same as possessing a veto.

A democratic government must consider the interests of everyone affected by its decisions, including people who are not organized into neighborhood associations.

Including renters.

Including future residents.

Including workers who commute from miles away because they cannot afford to live near their jobs.

Including young adults who cannot establish independent households.

Including families who must choose between overcrowding and financial ruin.

When the political process repeatedly elevates the preferences of established homeowners over the housing needs of everyone else, it ceases to function as balanced public planning.

It becomes an institution for protecting incumbents.

And its consequences are not abstract.

Housing shortages increase financial pressure, constrain labor mobility, worsen long-distance commuting, and prevent people from living near employment and essential services.

The price of preserving one neighborhood's preferred appearance is distributed across people who may never have visited that neighborhood at all.

That's the part missing from so many local planning meetings.

The people denied housing don't disappear.

They move farther away, pay more, crowd together, postpone major life decisions, or leave the region entirely.

The costs are real.

They simply don't appear on the neighborhood association's balance sheet.

Stop Giving Parking Lots More Rights Than People

The solution is not to eliminate planning.

It's to restore its proper purpose.

Establish sensible zoning laws. Allow enough density to meet actual housing demand. Require appropriate environmental and safety protections. Build and subsidize affordable housing. Protect existing residents from genuine displacement.

And then, when a project complies with the rules, allow it to be built.

Public objections should be evaluated against identifiable legal and material concerns, not treated as an inexhaustible source of political delay.

Procedures should have meaningful deadlines. Unnecessary duplication should be eliminated. Approval should mean something.

Most importantly, governments should stop pretending that protecting the preferences of existing property owners is the same thing as protecting the public interest.

Sometimes those interests align.

Sometimes they don't.

And when hundreds of people need homes while a politically influential minority wants to preserve a parking lot, public officials should be capable of recognizing the difference.

San Francisco's 469 Stevenson Street development is not merely a story about one failed construction timetable.

It is a demonstration of how a city can acknowledge a housing crisis, approve a solution, obstruct that solution, approve it again, and then watch the economics of construction deteriorate while nothing gets built.

All while claiming to care deeply about housing affordability.

Nine years.

Nearly 500 proposed apartments.

A city desperate for housing.

And the parking lot survives.

The housing crisis wasn't inevitable. The obstruction was a choice.

And the people making that choice already had somewhere to live.
