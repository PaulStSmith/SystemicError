'Backfired': Expert warns GOP mutiny against Todd Blanche just 'made matters worse'

Based on an article by Matthew Chapman

Todd Blanche Got What the Senate Pretended to Resist

A blockade that strengthened the target

Todd Blanche’s confirmation fight is being sold as a test of Senate backbone. It reads more like a demonstration of how weak theatrical opposition can be when the people staging it are willing to bless the same corruption they claim to oppose.

According to legal expert Liz Oyer, the brief Republican holdout from Sens. Thom Tillis and John Cornyn ended up helping Blanche, not weakening him. Their “concessions” were minor, and the result was not a limit on his power but a stamp of approval.

Power was never uncertain

Blanche was already acting as attorney general. That matters more than the confirmation circus. The real institutional power sat with him and with Donald Trump, who picked him to run the Justice Department in the first place.

That is the basic political fact the Senate keep trying to obscure: the issue is not whether Blanche has access to the machinery of federal law enforcement. He already does. Confirmation does not create his influence; it launders it.

The Senate licensed the corruption

Oyer’s description is blunt because the record in the source is blunt. Tillis and Cornyn did not extract meaningful changes. They endorsed the corrupt arrangement Blanche brought forward, including the settlement fund and the immunity agreement tied to Trump.

That is the scam. The Senate can always posture as a restraint on executive overreach, but when its members accept a sham as a concession, they do not check power. They validate it. They convert a political liability into a governing mandate.

The language of oversight hides the real function

This was never serious oversight. It was a permissions process dressed up as scrutiny.

Blanche’s documents supposedly changed to satisfy the senators, but Oyer says they changed nothing as a matter of law. If that is true, then the blockade was not just ineffective. It was misdirection, a way for Republican senators to claim they fought corruption while handing Blanche the legitimacy he needed to keep operating in Trump’s interest.

The pattern is the point

This is how institutional decay looks in practice: not a dramatic collapse, but a series of small endorsements that turn abuse into procedure. Trump names a loyalist. Senators stage concern. A legal expert identifies the sham. The loyalist gets confirmed anyway with the veneer of legitimacy intact.

The larger lesson is not about Blanche alone. It is about a political system where Republican power is routinely used to shield Trump-aligned corruption, and where even nominal resistance is often designed to preserve the appearance of accountability while producing the opposite result.
