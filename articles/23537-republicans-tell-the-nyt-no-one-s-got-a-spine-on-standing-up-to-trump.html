Republicans tell the NYT: 'No one's got a spine' on standing up to Trump

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Congress Is Not “Broken.” It Is Being Used.

Fear Is a Power Structure

The immediate facts are simple: Republican lawmakers describe a Congress warped by Donald Trump’s command of the party, enforced through leadership pressure, White House retaliation, social-media threats, and political surveillance by loyalists. Democrats and departing Republicans describe an institution increasingly populated by performers who treat legislation as secondary to publicity. That is enough context. The important question is not why everyone has become so disagreeable. It is who benefits from making Congress incapable of acting independently.

Trump holds executive power and commands the Republican Party’s machinery from the national level down to state and county organizations. Congressional leaders transmit that pressure. Republican members then choose compliance, whether from ambition, ideological loyalty, or fear of losing their careers and personal safety. Calling the result “hyper-partisanship” launders this hierarchy into a mood afflicting everyone equally.

Cowardice Is Still a Decision

Fred Upton’s account is valuable because it describes enforcement, not mere polarization. A Republican who signals dissent can expect intervention from party leadership, pressure from the White House, online threats, and organized public harassment. Those are mechanisms of discipline.

The threats are real, and fear is not trivial. But elected officials still possess votes, salaries, staffs, platforms, and constitutional authority unavailable to ordinary citizens. When they surrender that authority to avoid political punishment, they are not passive victims of dysfunction. They are enabling the person applying the pressure.

“No one’s got a spine” is blunt, but incomplete. Institutional cowardice is not an absence of agency. It is the repeated decision to protect oneself while transferring the consequences to everyone governed by the institution.

“Gridlock” Conceals the Sabotage

The source framing leans too heavily on public disgust with acrimony and on Congress’s long history of unpopularity. Congress was unpopular before Trump; that does not explain away what lawmakers say has changed under him. A low approval rating dating back decades and a president’s present control over one party are different facts, operating at different levels.

“Gridlock” suggests two comparable forces accidentally locking gears. The reporting instead describes asymmetric coercion inside the Republican Party and a political culture that rewards obstruction as spectacle. When lawmakers refuse cooperation because the other chamber might receive a “win,” governing has been subordinated to factional scorekeeping. The failure is not mysterious. It is produced.

The Green Screen Caucus

Dave Joyce’s description of Congress as a “green screen” captures the institution’s degraded incentive system. Some members no longer use publicity to advance legislation; they use Congress as scenery for building personal brands. Julia Brownley’s distinction between the “scrappy fighter” and the legislator identifies the electoral reward behind the performance: conflict is legible, viral, and lucrative. Competent lawmaking is slow and often invisible.

Anna Paulina Luna’s posture makes the pattern explicit. She presents hostility from colleagues as proof of integrity and obstruction as legislation. Her extravagant praise of Trump strengthens the political brand: loyalty upward, combat outward, and contempt for cooperation presented as independence.

This is not simply a decline in congressional “quality.” It is a selection system working as designed. A party organized around one leader will elevate people skilled at demonstrating loyalty, attracting attention, and punishing internal dissent. Legislative competence becomes optional because actual policymaking is no longer the only—or even primary—route to power.

Irrelevance Serves the Executive

Jim Himes calls Congress somewhere between dysfunctional and irrelevant. But irrelevance is not evenly costly. A legislature that cannot deliberate, bargain, or restrain the executive leaves more room for presidential command. That makes congressional decay politically useful to a president who already dominates his party.

The arrangement also offers members a convenient escape from responsibility. They can blame polarization, hostile colleagues, the Senate, voters, social media, or Congress itself. The institution becomes the grammatical subject—“Congress is broken”—while the people breaking it disappear from the sentence.

The System Rewards Surrender

The larger pattern is not that American politics has become unpleasant. It is that institutional power is being reorganized through fear, loyalty tests, spectacle, and deliberate incapacity. Trump applies or benefits from party discipline; Republican leaders transmit it; compliant lawmakers validate it; political performers turn the resulting paralysis into content.

Congress’s collapse in public esteem then becomes self-reinforcing. The less the institution accomplishes, the easier it is for ambitious members to treat it as a stage rather than a legislature. The more it becomes a stage, the less capacity it has to resist executive domination. What looks like dysfunction from outside can function perfectly well for those whose power grows when Congress abdicates its own.
