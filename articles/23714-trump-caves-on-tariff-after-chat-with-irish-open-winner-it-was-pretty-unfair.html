Trump caves on tariff after chat with Irish Open winner: 'It was pretty unfair'

Based on an article by Robert Davis

Tariff Policy by Tee Time

The Favor

Donald Trump imposed a 15 percent tariff on Irish whiskey in April 2025. In September 2026, after attending the Irish Open trophy ceremony at his own golf resort in Doonbeg, he announced that Ireland alone would receive relief. His stated policy process was blunt: golfer Shane Lowry asked him about it, “everyone” had asked, and Trump decided the tariff was unfair.

That is enough context. The political substance lies in how casually presidential power was described.

The Power Was Always Trump’s

Irish distillers did not impose this cost. Shane Lowry did not possess the authority to remove it. Trump created the tariff and Trump lifted it. Yet his language turned executive action into something that merely happened around him: a policy was “unfair,” people mentioned it, and he graciously corrected the problem.

This is misdirection through informality. The official responsible for the burden recasts himself as the sympathetic listener who discovers it. The decision-maker disappears; the benefactor takes his place.

Government by Personal Access

The exemption was announced after a trophy ceremony at Trump International Golf Links & Hotel. The president said a professional golfer raised the matter with him. When asked whether the relief applied across the European Union, Trump answered: “Just Ireland.”

The source does not establish that the resort visit or Lowry’s request caused the exemption. But Trump’s own account presents proximity to him as politically meaningful. A tariff affecting commerce between countries appears negotiable through whoever reaches the president in the right setting.

That is not evidence of a coherent trade policy. It is evidence of personalized rule: broad economic powers exercised through private judgment, selective attention and direct access to one man.

“Unfair” Is Not an Explanation

Trump called the tariff unfair without explaining why imposing it had been justified, what conditions had changed, or why Ireland deserved treatment denied to other European countries. “Others have the relief” is not a governing principle. It is an after-the-fact excuse for an exception.

The article’s framing that Trump “caved” weakens the story. Capitulation implies that some force overcame him. His own description suggests something more revealing: he used presidential authority to impose a cost, then selectively withdrew it after personal appeals. The central issue is not embarrassment or retreat. It is arbitrary power.

The Exemption Is the Policy

Selective relief does not repair an arbitrary system. It demonstrates how that system works. Those outside the favor still bear the tariff; those granted an audience can become evidence that the president is generous. Harm creates leverage, and relief becomes a political gift.

The larger pattern is patronage disguised as responsiveness. First create the burden. Then make access to the ruler matter. Finally, claim credit for sparing a chosen group from a policy the ruler imposed. When public authority operates this way, inconsistency is not a glitch. It is the mechanism that makes personal power visible.
