Baptist pastor tears apart Trump — and his 'abusive' MAGA movement

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosAugust 14, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Pulpit Finally Says the Quiet Part Out Loud

Power Has a Name

A Baptist publisher and pastor has done something many Christian leaders spent years avoiding: he named Donald Trump as an abuser and described MAGA as an abusive movement built on racist and misogynistic tropes. That matters because the source of the crisis is not confusion, bad messaging, or overreaction. It is power. Trump is the political actor with the national megaphone, the governing authority, and the capacity to turn grievance into policy. MAGA is the vehicle that translated that authority into social permission for cruelty.

The article’s factual core is simple: Mark Wingfield, executive director of Baptist News Global, is telling Baptists that they are facing a crisis, that Trump is a predator and con man, and that the costs are not abstract. People are dying, starving, getting sick, and having their lives and families wrecked by the Trump administration’s cruelty and stupidity. That is the context. The rest is a question of why so many religious institutions took so long to say it.

The Enablers Were Not Bystanders

The damage Trump does is not possible without a support structure. The source makes clear that Evangelicals were among his most loyal voters in 2016, 2020, and 2024, and that Baptist voters are part of that broader bloc. Even with a reported drop in Evangelical support to 52 percent, the important fact is not the decline itself. It is that a majority still remains attached after years of open corruption, sexual abuse findings, and daily public lying.

That is not ignorance. It is institutional accommodation. Churches, media platforms, and clergy who treated Trump as a difficult topic instead of a moral emergency helped normalize him. They did not merely fail to stop the abuse. They helped create the conditions in which the abuse could be dismissed as partisan annoyance.

The Complaint Is About Accountability

Wingfield quotes the familiar evasion: why keep talking about Trump and Christian nationalism, why not change the subject, why not be more pleasant. That complaint is the politics of insulation. It is what privileged people say when the cost of naming harm finally reaches their comfort.

His analogy is useful because it exposes the lie in the request. Nobody tells the target of abuse to stop talking about the abuser because the real problem is the repetition of the complaint. The real problem is the abuser, and the people around him who prefer silence, plausibility, and access over truth. When critics are smeared as having “Trump Derangement Syndrome,” the point is not diagnosis. It is discipline. The goal is to make moral clarity sound irrational so that obedience can keep wearing respectable clothes.

Cruelty Is Not a Bug

The source does not describe random chaos. It describes deliberate harm. Wingfield says the movement preys on racist and misogynistic tropes and that the Trump administration’s cruelty is producing real-world suffering. That is the political content of authoritarianism at the level most institutions prefer not to name: it is not only top-down force, but permission structures that tell people whom to dehumanize and whom to ignore.

This is why the language of “mistakes,” “tone,” or “overreach” is so dishonest here. The movement’s harm is not accidental. Its abuse is how it consolidates loyalty. Cruelty is not a side effect of the brand; it is part of the product.

Baptist Delay Tells the Story

Wingfield’s intervention is notable precisely because it comes from inside the coalition that helped carry Trump. That gives the article its real force: not because one Baptist pastor has magically solved the crisis, but because the people closest to the damage are beginning to admit what the rest of the country has been told to soften.

Still, there is a limit to late repentance if it remains only rhetorical. Naming the abuser is the first responsibility, not the end of the work. Religious leaders who helped make Trump socially durable should explain that history plainly, not hide behind generalized sorrow or vague calls for healing. If a movement enabled predation and then asked everyone to stop talking about it, the institution is part of the mechanism, not separate from it.

The Pattern

The larger pattern is familiar: authoritarian politics survives when powerful intermediaries decide that preserving their own standing matters more than confronting abuse. In this case, a religious network that should have been morally resistant instead became one of the places where abuse was translated into devotion. That is how democratic norms rot in public: not only through the man at the top, but through the institutions below him that teach people to excuse him.

The real divide is not between people who are too harsh and people who are more civil. It is between those willing to name power accurately and those who benefit from pretending it is just another disagreement.
