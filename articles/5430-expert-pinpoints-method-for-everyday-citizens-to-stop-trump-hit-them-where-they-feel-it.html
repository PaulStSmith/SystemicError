Expert pinpoints method for everyday citizens to stop Trump: 'Hit them where they feel it'

U.S. President Donald Trump and Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg smile during a private dinner for technology and business leaders in the State Dining Room at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., September 4, 2025. REUTERS/Brian Snyder

A group Donald Trump is reliant on could be the president’s undoing, according to an expert who believes losing said support will cripple the president.

Scott Galloway, a marketing professor at NYU Stern School of Business, believes if technology company leaders turn on Trump then the president will have little power in office. He told the The Daily Beast about his plan to put pressure on Big Tech leaders like Mark Zuckerberg and Jeff Bezos.

He said, “This is a coin-operated president. And these individuals have decided that their one and only job is shareholder value. And I empathize with that, I get it. There’s a ton of pressure; nobody wants to go first.

“But there needs to be collective action from 10, 50, a hundred, or the Fortune 500 to say, ‘This has just gone too far.’ But what is it about? It’s about one thing. It’s about shareholder value—full stop.”

Galloway went on to note the funding for the ballroom as a way to appease Trump, though their actions will do nothing to help them with voters who can sway the efforts of companies with their wallet, the NYU professor believes.

He added, “They are saying, ‘Let’s wait this guy out, let’s kiss his a--, let’s give a million dollars to the East Wing, let’s go to the premiere, and let’s stay out of his crosshairs and make sure the good times keep going for us and our shareholders.

“These companies are highly sensitive to growth rates and projected growth rates. The easiest way to maybe save some money and have a really big impact and send a message to the markets, to Trump, and to other citizens that they can have an impact is to resist and unsubscribe.

“So I think if you can just take GDP down one or two points, or if Sam Altman or Andy Jassy or Tim Cook says, ‘This unsubscribe thing is getting a little bit uncomfortable for us and our shareholders,’ I think they’re going to find their backbone and maybe not show up for as many Melania premieres or decide that maybe we shouldn’t be as supportive of ICE as we have been, or maybe we’re gonna be a little bit more courageous with our views.

“I think you have to hit them where they feel it. And that’s in terms of shareholder value and a decline in subscriber growth. So what do I want from them? I want them to show some fidelity to the American values that made them so wealthy and built such great companies.”

Our Analysis:

The Illusion of Corporate Valor in the Face of Political Expediency

In a world where the lines between corporate interest and political power blur more with each passing day, Scott Galloway’s insights slice through the facade like a hot knife through butter. His analysis, as reported by The Daily Beast, paints a vivid picture of the current American political landscape, where Donald Trump’s presidency could potentially be undermined not by the electorate, but by the very corporate titans who have thrived under his administration. Galloway’s proposition? That technology company leaders, if they turn their backs on Trump, could significantly weaken his power. However, this notion is as idealistic as it is fraught with the complexities of corporate greed and moral ambiguity.

The Coin-Operated President and His Billionaire Enablers

Galloway’s metaphor of a “coin-operated president” is as scathing as it is accurate. It conjures the image of a presidency not led by the will of the people, but by the financial contributions of the ultra-wealthy. The suggestion that figures like Mark Zuckerberg and Jeff Bezos hold the keys to Trump’s political survival is a damning indictment of the state of American democracy. It’s a world where shareholder value trumps (pun intended) ethical considerations, and where corporate leaders are willing to engage in a delicate dance of appeasement with the administration, so long as their coffers remain full.

The Flawed Strategy of Corporate Resistance

Galloway’s call for collective action from Big Tech leaders is commendable but reveals a naivety regarding the capitalist machinery at work. He correctly identifies the obsession with shareholder value as the driving force behind corporate America’s actions but seems to believe that a hit to the GDP or a dip in subscriber growth would be enough to spur these leaders into action. This view underestimates the depth of complicity and the lack of moral fortitude among these corporate giants. The idea that taking GDP down a notch or affecting subscriber growth could lead to a significant political shift is wishful thinking at best.

The Mirage of Corporate Courage

The expectation that tech leaders might find their “backbone” if their profits are threatened is a testament to the perverse incentives at play in modern capitalism. Galloway’s hope that they might eschew “Melania premieres” or reconsider their support for agencies like ICE upon feeling the financial pinch is indicative of a larger problem: the expectation of corporate courage is a mirage. These entities are not structured to uphold American values out of a sense of patriotic duty; they are designed to maximize profits, often at the expense of those very values.

Conclusion: A Call for Real Valor

While Galloway’s analysis is sharp and his frustrations are well-founded, his proposed solution underscores a fundamental misapprehension of the corporate psyche. The reliance on Big Tech leaders to act in the interest of the American people, rather than their shareholders, is a miscalculated bet. The real undoing of the Trump presidency, or any future administrations that prioritize corporate interests over the will of the people, will not come from the boardrooms of Silicon Valley. It will come from a populace informed, enraged, and engaged enough to demand better not only from their leaders but from the corporations that dictate the American way of life.

In the end, Galloway’s insights, while pointed, serve more as a reflection of our societal ills than a viable path to political change. The expectation that corporate America will save us from the consequences of our political choices is a folly. Only through collective action, civic engagement, and a reevaluation of our societal values can true change be achieved.
