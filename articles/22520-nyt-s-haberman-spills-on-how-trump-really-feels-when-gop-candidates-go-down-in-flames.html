NYT's Haberman spills on how Trump really feels when GOP candidates go down in flames

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

Paulo SantosAugust 25, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump’s Trade War Is Not Policy. It Is Leverage.

What the reporting says

Maggie Haberman said on Morning Joe that Trump takes satisfaction when Republicans lose elections he is not on the ballot for, and that his new trade war with Canada is another example of him doing what he wants because he can. She also said he is indifferent to the party’s electoral damage, does not want to be impeached again, and has made clear he plans to pardon people who got close enough to the Oval Office.

Who Has Power

The key fact is not that Trump is “erratic.” It is that he sits at the center of institutional power and uses it as a personal instrument. He is not drifting from policy to policy in confusion. He is exercising presidential authority in ways that force Republicans to absorb the fallout while he keeps the payoff.

That is the structure here: Trump holds the office, the attention, and the coercive tools of the state. GOP lawmakers hold the consequences. The party is not governing him; it is being used by him.

Misdirection as Method

The usual commentary frame treats moves like this as impulsive, random, or hard to decode. That framing is convenient for everyone trying to avoid naming intent. Haberman’s account points in the opposite direction. “Because he can” is not a joke. It is the operating principle.

This is what misdirection looks like in real time: the media asks what Trump is “doing,” as if the puzzle were his motivation. The answer is simpler and more damning. He is demonstrating control, punishing weakness, and reminding his own party that it exists under his permission, not as an equal partner.

Republicans Are the Target

Trump’s willingness to damage Republicans when he is not personally on the ballot is not some side effect of his politics. It is the point. A party that depends on one man’s personality cult has already surrendered its agency. It can be rewarded, humiliated, or threatened depending on how useful it is in the moment.

Haberman’s note that he does not want to be impeached again matters for the same reason. The trade war is not just about Canada. It is about keeping Republicans obedient, avoiding oversight, and preserving a zone where personal loyalty outranks institutional constraint. That is not party leadership. That is hostage politics.

Pardons, Protection, and the Message

The pardon warning is the ugliest part because it reveals the logic underneath the spectacle. If Trump is signaling that he will pardon “any number of people” who got close to the Oval Office, then the presidency is being treated as a shield for insiders, not a duty to the public.

That is how corruption hardens into procedure. The people near power are not asked to account for themselves; they are promised cover. The law becomes selective, and selective law is not law in any meaningful democratic sense. It is patronage with a seal on it.

The Pattern

The larger pattern is simple: Trump does not govern like a president constrained by institutions. He governs like a dominant faction leader testing how much damage he can inflict while everyone else explains it away as temperament, strategy, or chaos.

That is the real danger in stories like this. The problem is not that he is misunderstood. The problem is that too much political coverage still treats deliberate consolidation of personal power as noise. It is not noise. It is the system working exactly as a demagogue wants it to work: with fear above principle, loyalty above oversight, and everyone else left describing the wreckage after the fact.
