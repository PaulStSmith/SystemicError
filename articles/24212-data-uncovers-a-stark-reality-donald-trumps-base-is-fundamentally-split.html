Data uncovers a stark reality: Donald Trump’s base is fundamentally split

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe presidency has the powerLoyalty filters experienceThe fracture has consequencesThe story misplaces the spotlightThe systemic error

What happened

AlterNet reports that polling shows a sharp divide among people who voted for Donald Trump in 2024: those who still identify with MAGA are much more likely to say the economy has improved, while non-MAGA Trump voters are far more likely to describe severe cost-of-living pressure.

Citing Economist/YouGov data, Robert Reich writes that roughly 53 percent of Republican voters identify as MAGA Republicans or members of the MAGA movement. A Politico poll found that 28 percent of MAGA-identifying voters said expenses had become less affordable since Trump took office. By contrast, 52 percent of 2024 Trump voters who reject the MAGA label said their local cost of living was the worst it had ever been; nearly half were undecided about the midterms or said they would not vote Republican.

Reich says surveys show no meaningful difference in cost-of-living conditions between the two groups and that MAGA-identifying Trump voters have lower incomes. He concludes that loyalty to Trump best explains the gap in their economic perceptions. The polling establishes the divide, but the article does not present evidence proving that Trump’s claims caused it.

The presidency has the power

The relevant authority does not belong to voters answering surveys. Trump, described here as serving a second presidential term beginning in January 2025, holds executive power and the country’s largest political platform. His administration owns its economic decisions; voters merely experience and interpret their effects.

That distinction matters because political coverage often turns governance into a personality study of the governed. The central question is not why struggling people give inconsistent answers. It is what the administration has done about prices and wages—and why partisan identity may protect those in power from accountability when material conditions disappoint.

Loyalty filters experience

The polling suggests that MAGA identification functions as more than a policy preference. Among these respondents, affiliation correlates with a rosier economic assessment even though Reich says their wages and living costs do not materially distinguish them from other Trump voters.

But “cult” is Reich’s interpretation, not a finding produced by the cited polls. The evidence supports a narrower and still politically significant conclusion: partisan identity can mediate how people describe their own circumstances. It does not, by itself, establish that respondents surrendered critical judgment or accepted a particular falsehood because Trump uttered it.

Precision strengthens the indictment. Propaganda succeeds when loyalty becomes a filter through which evidence must pass. Polling can reveal the filter’s presence; it cannot, without more evidence, map every mechanism that created it.

The fracture has consequences

The non-MAGA Trump voters are the politically exposed part of the coalition. According to the reported figures, they are more willing to name economic pain and less committed to voting Republican in the midterms. Their uncertainty threatens the electoral durability of a coalition assembled in 2024.

MAGA identifiers appear more resistant to economic dissatisfaction translating into political defection. That gives Trump insulation unavailable to an ordinary officeholder: worsening perceptions need not produce proportional punishment if group identity can override retrospective judgment.

The story misplaces the spotlight

AlterNet correctly identifies a consequential split, but Reich’s framing concentrates blame on voters’ susceptibility. That risks turning a problem of presidential power and political communication into a diagnosis of individual irrationality.

The stronger question is how governing power escapes scrutiny when supporters are encouraged to treat favorable descriptions as tests of allegiance. Voters have agency and remain responsible for their choices. Yet the person occupying the presidency has vastly greater capacity to define the message, repeat it, and make loyalty to that message politically meaningful.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is an accountability system weakened by identity. One faction evaluates government partly through economic pain; another appears to evaluate economic pain through allegiance to the government’s leader.

That is the practical value of personality-centered politics to those holding power. It converts performance into affiliation and makes contradictory evidence easier to absorb without changing sides. The polling does not prove Reich’s full psychological explanation. It does reveal something more concrete: a governing coalition can fracture materially while remaining partly intact politically, because loyalty changes how failure is reported—even by the people living through it.
