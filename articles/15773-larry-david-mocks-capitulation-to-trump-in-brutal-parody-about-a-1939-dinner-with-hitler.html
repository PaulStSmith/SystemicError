Larry David mocks capitulation to Trump in brutal parody about a 1939 dinner with Hitler

Many of President Donald Trump's more outspoken critics believe that way too much "capitulating" is taking place —that Trump is genuinely dangerous and deserves total condemnation rather than having a lot list of universities, law firms and tech CEOs who were critical of him in the past trying to make peace with him.

Comedian Larry David has fun with that concept in a satirical op-ed published by the New York Times on April 21. In his op-ed, David describes a fictional dinner with Adolf Hitler in 1939.

"Imagine my surprise when, in the spring of 1939, a letter arrived at my house inviting me to dinner at the Old Chancellery with the world's most reviled man, Adolf Hitler," David sarcastically writes. "I had been a vocal critic of his on the radio from the beginning, pretty much predicting everything he was going to do on the road to dictatorship. No one I knew encouraged me to go. 'He's Hitler. He's a monster.' But eventually, I concluded that hate gets us nowhere. I knew I couldn't change his views, but we need to talk to the other side — even if it has invaded and annexed other countries and committed unspeakable crimes against humanity."

David continues, "Two weeks later, I found myself on the front steps of the Old Chancellery and was led into an opulent living room…. Everyone stiffened as Hitler entered the room. He was wearing a tan suit with a swastika armband and gave me an enthusiastic greeting that caught me off guard. Frankly, it was a warmer greeting than I normally get from my parents, and it was accompanied by a slap on my back."

In a separate op-ed also published on April 21, New York Times Deputy Opinion Editor Patrick Healy describes assigning the op-ed to David after he pitched it.

Healy explains, "Times Opinion has a high bar for satire — our mission is geared toward idea-driven, fact-based arguments — and we have a really, really high bar for commenting on today’s world by invoking Hitler. As a general rule, we seek to avoid Nazi references unless that is the literal subject matter; callbacks to history can be offensive, imprecise or in terrible taste when you are leveraging genocidal dictators to make a point. I also understood Larry's intent in writing this piece."

Healy adds, "We had spoken about American politics and how some on the left and in the center think it’s important to talk and engage with President Trump."

David's op-ed follows "Real Time" host Bill Maher's decision to meet with President Donald Trump in the White House. Although Maher has often been a scathing Trump critic, he decided to meet with him anyway. And Maher, on "Real Time," commented that Trump was a lot more low-key during their one-on-one conversation that he is publicly.

"Like many people," Healy notes, "Larry listened to Bill Maher talk about his recent dinner with Trump. Bill, a comedian Larry respects, said in a monologue on his Max show that he found the president to be 'gracious and measured' compared with the man who attacks him on Truth Social. Larry's piece is not equating Trump with Hitler. It is about seeing someone for who they really are and not losing sight of that."

Our Analysis:

The Satirical Edge: Dissecting Larry David's Op-Ed and the Trump Comparison

In a world where the lines between satire and reality blur with alarming frequency, Larry David's recent op-ed in The New York Times serves as a stark reminder of the dangers of normalizing the abnormal. David, utilizing his well-honed satirical blade, cuts deep into the fabric of a society too willing to extend an olive branch to figures who, in another era, would be unequivocally condemned.

The Satire: A Closer Look

David's piece, under the guise of a fictional dinner with Adolf Hitler in 1939, is not just a humorous jab at the past but a scathing critique of the present. The comparison between Hitler and Donald Trump is not made lightly, nor is it the crux of his argument. Instead, David masterfully uses the absurdity of breaking bread with a dictator to highlight the folly of attempting to find common ground with those who have demonstrated, through action and rhetoric, a disdain for the very principles of democracy.

The Warm Reception of a Dictator

The moment in David's narrative where Hitler greets him with a warmth unexpected from a tyrant is particularly jarring. It serves as a metaphor for the way figures like Trump have been normalized by some sections of the media and public. The swastika armband, a symbol of hate, sitting comfortably next to the tan suit, is a visual representation of how the unacceptable can become mundane if we're not vigilant.

The Reality: Engagement vs. Endorsement

The article mentions Bill Maher's real-life meeting with Trump, noting Maher's observation that Trump was "gracious and measured" in person. This anecdote is crucial, illustrating the dangerous allure of mistaking a dictator's charm for a softened stance. As history has shown, personal amiability does not absolve one of their public deeds or rhetoric.

The Editorial Decision: A Double-Edged Sword

Patrick Healy's defense of the op-ed's publication underscores a significant dilemma facing today's media: how to maintain a platform for free expression while not inadvertently sanitizing the actions of those it seeks to critique. Healy's acknowledgment of the "high bar" for satire and the hesitance to invoke Hitler comparisons highlights the precarious tightrope walked by publications in today's charged political climate.

The Intent vs. The Interpretation

Healy's assertion that David's piece is about "seeing someone for who they really are and not losing sight of that" is a crucial takeaway. It's a call to action, urging us not to be swayed by superficial changes in demeanor or isolated acts of diplomacy. The essence of a person's character, especially a public figure wielding significant power, is revealed through their consistent actions and policies, not momentary gestures of civility.

Conclusion: The Lesson Behind the Laughter

Larry David's op-ed, while wrapped in the trappings of satire, delivers a poignant message about the dangers of normalizing or attempting to rationalize the behavior of those who threaten the very foundations of democracy. It's a reminder that some figures, no matter how charismatic or seemingly reformed, should not be absolved of their transgressions against humanity and democratic ideals.

In essence, David and The New York Times have not just provided a piece of satire but a mirror to society, urging us to examine the reflections of our values and the leaders we choose to engage with. The real question is, will we heed the warning, or will we continue to dine with dictators, hoping they've changed their appetites?
