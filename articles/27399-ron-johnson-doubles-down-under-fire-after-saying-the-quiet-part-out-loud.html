Ron Johnson doubles down under fire after saying the quiet part out loud

Based on an article by Nancy J. Altman

Paulo SantosNovember 21, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Denial after repetitionThe shortfall presents a choice, not an inevitabilityA powerful official protects the wrong party from scrutiny

Sen. Ron Johnson did not merely insult Social Security from the sidelines. The Wisconsin Republican, who chairs the Senate Budget Committee and sits on the committees overseeing finance and aging, called the program “grotesque” and a “Ponzi scheme” in a Fox Business interview, then said, “you can’t fix it, you just shut it down and hope that you can recover some of your money.” Hours later, according to AlterNet, he used a Budget Committee hearing on Social Security’s future to repeat the “Ponzi scheme” attack.

The significance lies in Johnson’s power, not simply his language. He helps control the congressional machinery that can protect, restructure or weaken the retirement system. When an official in that position describes Social Security as inherently fraudulent and beyond repair, the remark reveals how at least one powerful Republican approaches a program tens of millions of Americans depend upon.

Denial after repetition

Johnson repeated the characterization when Alex Lawson of Social Security Works confronted him outside the hearing, the organization’s president, Nancy Altman, wrote for AlterNet. After a day of criticism online and from Senate Democrats, Johnson insisted that he had never said he wanted to eliminate Social Security.

That defense cannot erase his recorded formulation. Johnson paired “shut it down” with the claim that the system cannot be fixed. His later denial may dispute how listeners interpreted those words, but it does not make the words disappear.

Johnson then introduced legislation bearing the exquisitely evasive name “Stop Scaring Seniors Act.” The proposal would authorize the use of general federal revenue to finance Social Security. Naming the bill after the fear surrounding benefit cuts shifts attention from the officials empowered to impose those cuts to the people warning beneficiaries about them. Johnson generated the alarm by describing the system as irreparable; his bill’s title treats the resulting scrutiny as the offense.

Altman argues that moving Social Security toward general-revenue financing would weaken one of its central political defenses: the program is financed separately and does not add to the federal deficit. Once folded into ordinary budget politics, its benefits could become more exposed to deficit-cutting campaigns and recurring funding confrontations.

That concern is not detached from Johnson’s record. In 2022, he advocated moving Social Security spending into the discretionary budget, where Congress could reconsider it during annual appropriations battles. His new proposal is not identical, but it points toward the same political terrain: retirement benefits subjected more directly to lawmakers who routinely manufacture fiscal crises.

The shortfall presents a choice, not an inevitability

Social Security faces a funding shortfall around 2032 that, absent congressional action, would trigger an automatic benefit reduction of more than 20 percent, according to Altman. That outcome is not an unavoidable mechanical failure. Congress writes the financing rules and has the authority to change them.

The existing structure stops collecting Social Security contributions once a worker’s annual income exceeds $184,500. Altman points to a Democratic alternative that would require wealthy Americans to contribute on income above that threshold, including investment income. The Social Security Expansion Act, sponsored by Sens. Bernie Sanders of Vermont and Elizabeth Warren of Massachusetts, would use additional revenue not only to avert cuts but also to increase benefits by $200 a month, with further increases for some vulnerable groups.

No Republican senator has co-sponsored that legislation, although Altman notes that Ohio Republican Sen. Bernie Moreno has expressed openness to changing the contribution cap. That leaves the dispute in concrete terms: Congress can raise more revenue from affluent households, reduce benefits, expose the program to annual budget warfare, or produce another proposal. Calling Social Security a scam obscures those choices by portraying a politically constructed financing problem as proof that the institution itself is illegitimate.

Altman reports that only 2 percent of Americans support any benefit reduction. Whatever the precise electoral consequences, that opposition explains why Republican officials often deny plans to cut Social Security even as figures such as Johnson promote mechanisms that could make cuts easier. The public sales pitch is protection; the governing architecture creates vulnerability.

A powerful official protects the wrong party from scrutiny

Altman also reports that Johnson is worth about $40 million. His wealth does not by itself establish why he favors a particular policy. It does, however, sharpen the distributive question his rhetoric avoids. The Democratic proposal would collect more from people with high earnings and investment income. Johnson’s approach leaves that option untouched while changing how the program can be funded—and potentially how it can be attacked.

That is the misdirection at the center of the episode. Social Security beneficiaries did not create the statutory contribution ceiling. Seniors did not assign committee gavels or decide which revenues Congress would collect. Lawmakers did. Yet Johnson describes the program as the swindle, as though an institution built by Congress had somehow deceived the officials who control its rules.

Johnson’s fellow Republicans need not endorse the phrase “shut it down” to enable the policy direction behind it. They can establish their position through votes: support additional revenue, offer a workable alternative or block legislation and accept the resulting risk of benefit cuts. Silence and slogans preserve ambiguity, but legislative power eventually requires a choice.

Johnson briefly dispensed with that ambiguity. Then, facing the predictable consequences, he tried to relocate the scandal from what he said to the people who heard him clearly.
