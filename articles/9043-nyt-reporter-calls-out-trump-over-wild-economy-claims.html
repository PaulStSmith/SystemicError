NYT reporter calls out Trump over wild economy claims

U.S. President Donald Trump speaks to members of the media on board Air Force One en route to Joint Base Andrews, U.S., November 2, 2025. REUTERS/Elizabeth Frantz

New York Times reporter Peter Baker couldn’t help but notice in the recently released transcript of Donald Trump’s full “60 Minutes” interview that the president seemed very confused about inflation and the economy.

Among Trump’s claims on Sunday was that he inherited the highest inflation that the United States has ever seen.

“But my second term is blowing it away. It’s blowing it away when you look at the numbers, the stock market, the jobs. Look at the job numbers, how good they’ve been. And, again, I have costs down. Remember, Biden gave me the worst inflation rate in the history of our country--” Trump told Norah O’Donnell.

The highest inflation in U.S. history happened during World War I between 1917 to 1920, when inflation eclipsed 15%, Investopedia recalled.

Baker noted that inflation is a little closer to 3% as of September. One year ago, in September 2024, inflation was 2.4%.

Later in the interview, Baker noted that Trump claimed his tariffs “haven’t led to inflation.” In fact, according to Trump, “We have no inflation. We have no inflation. Biden had inflation, and he didn’t have tariffs. He didn’t use tariffs.”

“Trump to CBS: ‘I inherited the worst inflation in the history of our country.’ Inflation when Trump took office: 3 percent Trump: ‘We have no inflation.’ Inflation now: 3 percent,” Baker said.

Our Analysis:

A Masterclass in Misinformation: Trump’s Economic Fantasies

In a recent expose by New York Times reporter Peter Baker, the transcript from Donald Trump’s “60 Minutes” interview reveals a bewildering confusion—or perhaps a deliberate distortion—of economic facts by the former president. Trump’s claims about inheriting “the highest inflation that the United States has ever seen” and miraculously nullifying it in his imagined second term are not just false; they’re a fantastical revision of economic history.

Inflation Inaccuracies

First, let’s address the elephant in the room: the highest inflation in U.S. history occurred during World War I, peaking over 15%, a stark contrast to the relatively modest figures seen in recent years. Trump’s assertion that he inherited the worst inflation rate in U.S. history is not just inaccurate; it’s a flagrant disregard for easily verifiable facts. According to Investopedia, and as pointed out by Baker, inflation was around 3% when Trump took office—a far cry from the catastrophic levels he describes.

Trump’s Economic Wonderland

Trump’s claim that his administration “blew away” inflation during his second term—a term that, in our current reality, does not exist—ventures into the realm of the absurd. The stock market and job numbers he touts, while important economic indicators, do not directly correlate with inflation rates. Moreover, the notion that he has costs down is a mirage, especially when considering the inflation rate has hovered around 3%, hardly a significant deviation from when he took office.

Tariff Tales

The former president’s comments on tariffs and inflation are equally baffling. Trump’s assertion that his tariffs “haven’t led to inflation” flies in the face of economic consensus. Tariffs generally lead to higher prices for consumers, which can contribute to inflation—a basic economic principle that Trump seems to either misunderstand or ignore. His claim, “We have no inflation,” is patently false by any statistical measure, including those from his administration.

Baker’s Fact-Check

Peter Baker’s simple juxtaposition of Trump’s claims against the actual figures serves as a stark reality check. The tweet, “Trump to CBS: ‘I inherited the worst inflation in the history of our country.’ Inflation when Trump took office: 3 percent. Trump: ‘We have no inflation.’ Inflation now: 3 percent,” succinctly debunks the former president’s assertions with hard data.

Conclusion

Trump’s interview with “60 Minutes” is a harrowing reminder of the misinformation that can emanate from the highest levels of political power. It’s not just about being wrong on the numbers; it’s about creating an alternate reality that undermines public understanding of essential economic principles. Reality, as we know, is not a choose-your-own-adventure story where facts are malleable. In the real world, words have consequences, and so does the truth. Trump’s economic fantasies, as spotlighted by Peter Baker, are a stark testament to the former president’s disregard for factual accuracy in favor of his narrative. This isn’t just confused economics—it’s a systemic error in public discourse that demands rigorous fact-checking and public accountability.
