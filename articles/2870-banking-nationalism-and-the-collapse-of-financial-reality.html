Banking Nationalism and the Collapse of Financial Reality

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 19, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There is a particular kind of policy that looks small when written on paper and catastrophic when applied to the real world. The proposal to restrict access to U.S. banking services based on citizenship status falls squarely into that category. It presents itself as administrative housekeeping—verification, compliance, security—but in practice it functions as something far more dangerous: the politicization of financial infrastructure.

At its core, the idea is simple. Require banks to verify citizenship status for account holders. Restrict or revoke access for those who cannot prove they meet the definition of “American.” On its surface, this is framed as a tightening of standards. In reality, it is the introduction of ideological filtering into a system that only functions because it is predictable, stable, and broadly accessible.

That distinction is not philosophical. It is structural.

Banking Is Not a Domestic System

The first failure of this policy is conceptual. It treats banking as if it were a purely domestic service—like issuing a driver’s license or registering a vehicle. It is not.

Modern banking, especially at the level of major U.S. institutions, is global infrastructure. American banks do not simply serve American citizens. They serve:

  • Foreign nationals

  • Multinational corporations

  • Sovereign-linked entities

  • Cross-border investment vehicles

They are embedded in a web of international finance that spans trade, investment, and currency flows. The U.S. dollar’s dominance is not an accident—it is the product of decades of perceived reliability, legal stability, and openness.

Introduce a policy that conditions access on citizenship status, and that foundation begins to crack.

The Immediate Shock: Compliance Chaos

The moment such a policy is implemented, the system does not adjust smoothly. It fractures.

Banks would be forced to:

  • Re-verify millions of existing accounts

  • Demand documentation many customers do not readily possess

  • Freeze or close accounts during compliance reviews

This is not a marginal adjustment. It is a mass disruption event.

Foreign clients—who have long relied on U.S. institutions for stability—would suddenly face uncertainty. Accounts could be flagged, restricted, or terminated not because of suspicious activity, but because of a shifting definition of eligibility.

Contracts tied to those accounts would be jeopardized. Payments could be delayed or blocked. Entire transaction chains could stall.

The financial system is not designed for abrupt exclusion. It is designed for continuity.

Retaliation: The Inevitable Response

There is a persistent illusion in policy circles that other countries will simply absorb American decisions without consequence. That illusion does not survive contact with financial reality.

If U.S. banks begin excluding foreign nationals, other countries will respond—not out of outrage, but out of necessity.

Possible responses include:

  • Imposing restrictions on U.S. banks operating within their borders

  • Requiring local subsidiaries to ring-fence assets away from U.S. control

  • Forcing divestment or reducing operational permissions

These are not extreme measures. They are standard defensive reactions in international finance.

The result is fragmentation. U.S. banks, which currently operate as global entities, would find themselves increasingly constrained, their reach reduced not by market forces, but by regulatory pushback.

The Dollar’s Hidden Dependency

The global dominance of the U.S. dollar is often treated as an immutable fact. It is not. It is a system sustained by trust.

That trust rests on three pillars:

  1. Predictability of policy

  2. Accessibility of financial institutions

  3. Stability of legal frameworks

A citizenship-based banking restriction undermines all three.

It signals that access to the U.S. financial system is not governed solely by law or compliance standards, but by political identity. That message does not need to be shouted. It only needs to be understood.

And once understood, it changes behavior.

Countries and institutions begin to hedge:

  • Expanding non-dollar trade agreements

  • Increasing reliance on alternative settlement systems

  • Reducing exposure to U.S.-based financial intermediaries

This is not a dramatic collapse. It is a gradual rebalancing—one that erodes the centrality of the U.S. over time.

The Domestic Fallout: A Secondary Disaster

Even if one ignores the global implications, the domestic consequences alone are severe.

Millions of individuals lack easy access to the documentation required to prove citizenship under strict standards. This includes:

  • Elderly citizens without updated records

  • Individuals born outside formal hospital systems

  • Low-income populations with limited access to bureaucratic processes

The result is predictable. Accounts are closed. Access to financial services is lost. Individuals are pushed out of the formal economy.

This is often framed as collateral damage. It is more accurately described as systemic exclusion.

The Informal Economy Expansion

When people are removed from the banking system, they do not disappear. They adapt.

They move into:

  • Cash-based transactions

  • Informal lending networks

  • High-risk financial alternatives such as payday loans

This shift does not increase oversight. It eliminates it.

Ironically, a policy justified under the banner of financial security produces the opposite effect. It reduces visibility, weakens regulatory reach, and increases vulnerability to exploitation.

The Ideological Core

To understand why such a policy is pursued despite its obvious risks, one must look beyond economics and into ideology.

This is not a policy designed to optimize financial systems. It is a policy designed to enforce a boundary: who belongs and who does not.

Once that boundary becomes the organizing principle, other considerations—efficiency, stability, international coordination—become secondary.

This is where the danger lies.

Financial systems are not resilient to ideological distortion. They require consistency. They require predictability. They require rules that are applied uniformly, not selectively.

Introduce identity as a gating factor, and the system begins to behave unpredictably.

The Law of Unintended Consequences

Perhaps the most striking aspect of this proposal is not what is known, but what is unknown.

Policies of this scale interact with systems of immense complexity. They produce second-order and third-order effects that cannot be fully anticipated.

  • How will global markets react to sudden exclusion of foreign clients?

  • How quickly will alternative financial systems scale in response?

  • What cascading failures might emerge from disrupted transaction networks?

These are not rhetorical questions. They are unresolved variables in a high-risk experiment.

And the experiment is not being conducted in isolation. It is being conducted within the central node of the global financial system.

The Strategic Contradiction

There is a deep contradiction at the heart of this approach.

The same political framework that emphasizes national strength and global dominance is advancing a policy that weakens the very mechanisms through which that dominance is exercised.

Economic power is not maintained through exclusion. It is maintained through integration—through being the system others depend on.

By turning access into a conditional privilege rather than a reliable feature, the U.S. risks encouraging the world to build systems that do not depend on it.

That is not a short-term loss. It is a long-term strategic shift.

Conclusion: A System Under Strain

What is being proposed is not merely a regulatory adjustment. It is a transformation of how access to financial infrastructure is determined.

It replaces a system grounded in legal and procedural standards with one influenced by political identity. It introduces uncertainty into a network that depends on stability. It risks triggering international responses that could reshape the global financial landscape.

And it does all of this in pursuit of a goal that is fundamentally disconnected from the function of the system it seeks to control.

Banking is not a tool for defining national identity. It is a mechanism for enabling economic activity. When those roles are conflated, the result is not clarity. It is instability.

The consequences may not be immediate. They may not even be fully visible at first. But they will accumulate.

And once they do, the system will not fail dramatically.

It will simply stop being the center of the world.
