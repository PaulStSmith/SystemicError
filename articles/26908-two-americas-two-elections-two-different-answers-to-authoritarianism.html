Two Americas, Two Elections, Two Different Answers to Authoritarianism

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 26, 202612 min read

In 2024, Americans returned Donald Trump to the White House. In 2026, Brazilians faced a remarkably similar political choice—and, according to election-night projections, delivered a different verdict. The contrast exposes both the resilience and the fragility of modern democracy.

Two countries. Two presidential elections. Two candidates representing democratic continuity against two candidates associated with closely related political movements.

And two very different outcomes.

On November 5, 2024, the United States elected Donald Trump to a second, nonconsecutive presidential term. He defeated Vice President Kamala Harris, winning both the Electoral College and the national popular vote.

On October 25, 2026, Brazil held its presidential runoff between incumbent President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva and Senator Flávio Bolsonaro, son and political heir of former President Jair Bolsonaro.

As this article was being written, with approximately 99.7% of the count completed, Lula held 51.76% of the valid votes. His victory was mathematically assured, even though counting was not yet complete.

The numerical contrast is striking. But the political contrast matters far more.

In the United States, a political movement whose leader had attempted to overturn his previous electoral defeat returned to executive power.

In Brazil, voters faced a candidate carrying the name, political inheritance, and support of a former president convicted of plotting to overturn his own electoral defeat.

And the Brazilian electorate declined to return that political movement to the presidency.

The similarities deserve examination.

So do the differences.

The American election: A return to power

Donald Trump’s political history was hardly a mystery when Americans went to the polls in November 2024.

He had already occupied the White House for four years. His presidency had ended amid the January 6, 2021, attack on the United States Capitol, following his sustained efforts to challenge and overturn the results of the 2020 presidential election.

His refusal to accept defeat was not an obscure footnote to his administration. It was a defining event of his political career.

Yet four years later, American voters returned him to office.

Trump’s victory was decisive under the American electoral system, although his popular-vote margin was approximately 1.47 percentage points—not an overwhelming national consensus.

Nevertheless, the institutional consequences were enormous.

The American electorate knew Trump’s history. His previous presidency was public record. His efforts to remain in power after losing in 2020 had been extensively investigated.

The 2024 election demonstrated an uncomfortable reality: An electorate can be aware of a candidate’s attacks on democratic institutions and still decide to return that candidate to power.

Economic dissatisfaction, immigration, inflation, partisan loyalty, and hostility toward the incumbent administration all played roles in the campaign.

But explaining why people voted for Trump does not erase what they voted to return to office.

A vote has consequences independent of the voter’s stated intentions.

The Brazilian election: A political inheritance on the ballot

Two years later, Brazil confronted a remarkably familiar political scenario.

The candidate was different, but the family name was unmistakable.

Flávio Bolsonaro was not his father, Jair Bolsonaro. The distinction matters. The son must be judged by his own record, statements, alliances, and proposals, not simply assigned every action of his father.

But political inheritance was central to the campaign.

Jair Bolsonaro, imprisoned following his conviction for plotting a coup after his 2022 electoral defeat, had endorsed his son. Flávio campaigned as the representative of the political movement his father had built, with Donald Trump as an important international ally.

Brazil’s election also began with an ominous result for Lula.

In the first round on October 4, Flávio Bolsonaro received approximately 47.03% of valid votes, compared with Lula’s 45.16%.

That put Bolsonaro ahead by roughly 2.2 million votes.

More than 33 million eligible voters abstained.

In a contest separated by just over two million votes, the number of people who had not participated was more than enough to alter the outcome.

And the runoff did just that.

First-round figures reported by Folha; runoff figures are the election-night snapshot provided during counting. The second-round Bolsonaro share is calculated as the complement of Lula’s 51.76%.

The projected reversal was significant for another reason: since Brazil’s return to direct presidential elections in 1989, the first-round leader had always gone on to win the runoff.

Lula's comeback broke that historical pattern.

The campaign between rounds was intense. Lula’s organization increased its street mobilization and social media activity, while the candidates remained closely matched in polling. Several surveys published on the eve of the runoff even showed Flávio Bolsonaro numerically ahead, although the contests were within the margins of error.

The eventual result was not a sweeping repudiation of Bolsonarismo.

Nearly half of the voters who cast valid ballots still supported its candidate.

But the difference between nearly winning and actually winning is the difference between holding a political movement accountable from outside executive power and handing it the instruments of government.

That difference matters.

The similarities are not coincidental

Trumpism and Bolsonarismo developed in different countries, within different institutional and cultural environments.

They are not identical movements.

But their political approaches have substantial overlaps.

Both rely heavily on nationalist appeals, cultural conservatism, hostility toward established political opponents, and narratives presenting their movements as defenders of the nation against allegedly corrupt or dangerous elites.

Both have benefited from evangelical political networks, distrust of established news organizations, and social media ecosystems that allow political messaging to bypass traditional scrutiny.

And both have histories directly connected to attempts to overturn electoral defeats.

There is an important asymmetry here.

Trump personally participated in the effort to reverse his 2020 defeat. Flávio Bolsonaro was not the president who lost the 2022 Brazilian election; his father was. The younger Bolsonaro’s relationship to that history is political and familial, rather than identical personal responsibility.

Yet the relationship between the movements is hardly imaginary.

Flávio’s candidacy represented the continuation of a political project originally led by Jair Bolsonaro, while his alignment with Trump connected that project to an increasingly influential international right-wing network.

The differences between these movements should not obscure their common political interests.

Nor should their similarities erase individual responsibility.

Harris and Lula: Similar roles, very different candidates

There is another tempting comparison: Kamala Harris and Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva.

Both represented the political alternative to a Trump-aligned right-wing movement.

Both defended established constitutional institutions against political opponents whose movements were connected to attempts to reverse election results.

And both had to campaign while defending the record of an incumbent administration.

But the similarities largely end there.

Harris was the sitting vice president when she became the Democratic presidential nominee in 2024. She had to establish a distinct political identity while remaining tied to President Joe Biden’s administration.

Lula, by contrast, was already president. He had decades of national political recognition, an established party organization, and extensive experience running presidential campaigns.

Harris also entered the general election following Biden’s withdrawal from the race, compressing her time to introduce herself as the nominee.

Lula had been the expected standard-bearer of his political coalition from the beginning.

And although both campaigned on democratic institutions and social policies, their ideological backgrounds differ. Harris represents the American Democratic Party’s liberal tradition; Lula emerged from Brazil’s labor movement and the Workers’ Party.

Calling them interchangeable representatives of “the left” would conceal more than it reveals.

What makes the comparison valuable is not that Harris and Lula were the same kind of politician.

It is that both faced opponents associated with closely connected political movements—and the elections produced opposite outcomes.

The economy: The issue authoritarian movements exploit

There is another uncomfortable similarity between the campaigns.

Ordinary people were worried about money.

In the United States, voters had experienced significant inflation during the Biden administration. Although inflation had slowed before the 2024 election, many households remained frustrated with food, housing, and other everyday expenses.

In Brazil, the cost of living was also a central concern.

An August 2026 Quaest survey found that more than two-thirds of respondents believed their purchasing power had declined compared with the preceding year. Concerns about crime, household debt, public finances, and corruption also shaped the campaign.

There is nothing inherently authoritarian about voting against an administration because groceries are expensive.

Democratic accountability requires that voters be able to punish governments for poor economic performance.

But economic dissatisfaction can become politically dangerous when movements present the destruction or weakening of democratic constraints as an acceptable price for promised relief.

The argument is seductive in its simplicity:

The government has failed you. The institutions protecting it are corrupt. Give us power, remove the obstacles, and we will fix everything.

The institutions become the enemy.

Judicial oversight becomes persecution.

Independent journalism becomes propaganda.

Political opposition becomes sabotage.

The distinction is not between voters who care about democracy and voters who care about feeding their families.

People can reasonably care about both.

The question is whether economic frustration is allowed to excuse attacks on the mechanisms through which voters can later remove a government.

Because a government that cannot be peacefully removed is not an economic solution.

It is a different kind of problem.

Two electoral systems, two different political dynamics

The elections also operated under fundamentally different rules.

In the United States, the presidency is determined through the Electoral College. Voters participate in state-level contests whose results allocate electoral votes, generally on a winner-take-all basis.

A candidate can win the presidency without winning the national popular vote.

That did not happen in 2024: Trump won both.

In Brazil, the president is elected directly through a majority runoff system. If no candidate receives more than half of the valid votes in the first round, the two leading candidates contest a second election.

These differences are substantial.

Brazilian voters who initially supported a third candidate received another opportunity to choose between the two finalists.

First-round abstainers also had an additional opportunity to participate.

American voters in 2024 had no equivalent second national presidential vote after the field had narrowed to the major-party nominees.

But Brazil’s system does not automatically protect it against authoritarian candidates. Jair Bolsonaro won the presidency through that same two-round system in 2018.

Nor does the existence of a runoff guarantee that the second-round winner is more democratic.

Electoral design creates opportunities. It does not manufacture democratic judgment.

Brazilian voters had the opportunity to reconsider their options between October 4 and October 25.

The final outcome confirmed that enough voters supported Lula in the runoff to reverse the first-round ordering.

Exactly how much of that reversal came from turnout, changed preferences, or transfers from eliminated candidates requires detailed electoral analysis.

The outcome establishes the reversal. It does not, by itself, establish every reason behind it.

What Brazil did differently—and what remains unresolved

It would be comforting to conclude that Brazilians recognized a danger that Americans failed to recognize.

The reality is more complicated.

Brazil elected Jair Bolsonaro in 2018.

The United States rejected Donald Trump in 2020 before returning him to power four years later.

Neither country has a permanent democratic immunity certificate.

Indeed, the Brazilian runoff demonstrated just how competitive Bolsonarismo remains, even with its founder imprisoned.

Flávio Bolsonaro led the first round.

He attracted almost half the valid votes in the second.

His political movement remains capable of mobilizing tens of millions of people.

That is not the profile of a defeated ideology. It is the profile of an opposition movement that remains powerful enough to contest national leadership.

And the same lesson applies to the United States.

Trump’s 2024 victory did not prove that every American voter embraced every element of his political conduct.

But it did demonstrate that his established record, including his attempt to overturn an election, was insufficient to prevent his return to power.

In Brazil, the election demonstrated something different: a majority of valid runoff votes went to the incumbent rather than the candidate representing the Bolsonaro political inheritance.

That majority does not necessarily share a single motivation.

Some voters supported Lula enthusiastically.

Others may have preferred his economic policies.

Others may have been voting primarily against Bolsonarismo.

And others may have reached their decision for reasons unrelated to the democratic stakes.

Democracy does not require every voter to share the same reasoning.

It requires that their votes be counted, the result be respected, and political power remain subject to peaceful transfer.

The dangerous myth of the permanent victory

There is a recurring temptation after elections like these.

The winning side declares that democracy has prevailed, the threat has been defeated, and the country can finally return to normal.

But elections do not eliminate political movements.

They determine who exercises particular powers for a particular period.

Donald Trump’s defeat in 2020 did not eliminate Trumpism.

Jair Bolsonaro’s defeat in 2022 did not eliminate Bolsonarismo.

And Flávio Bolsonaro's defeat in 2026 does not eliminate the movement he represents.

Political organizations survive election losses. Their supporters continue voting. Their media ecosystems continue operating. Their donors continue contributing. Their politicians continue seeking office.

This is entirely normal in a democracy.

What deserves scrutiny is not the continued existence of an opposition movement, but whether that movement accepts democratic constraints when it loses.

That distinction is central.

Opposition to a government’s policies is legitimate.

Campaigning to replace its elected leadership is legitimate.

Questioning administrative decisions, criticizing judges, and demanding institutional accountability are legitimate.

Attempting to nullify an election because the wrong candidate won is something else.

A functioning democracy must preserve the first set of rights while preventing the second category of conduct from becoming a viable route to power.

Brazil’s conviction of Jair Bolsonaro over the post-2022 coup plot demonstrates one form of institutional accountability.

The 2026 runoff demonstrates a separate form: electoral accountability.

They should not be confused.

Judicial proceedings determine individual legal responsibility.

Elections determine political representation.

Neither can substitute for the other.

The verdict: Similar warnings, different responses

Consider the historical symmetry.

In 2024, Americans chose between Kamala Harris and Donald Trump, a former president who had attempted to overturn an election he lost.

They elected Trump.

In 2026, Brazilians chose between Lula and Flávio Bolsonaro, the political heir of a former president imprisoned for plotting a coup after losing an election.

They elected Lula.

The campaigns occurred in different economic circumstances, under different electoral systems, and with different candidates. No serious comparison should pretend otherwise.

But the political parallels remain striking.

Two countries confronted powerful right-wing movements connected to attempts to reverse democratic outcomes.

Two electorates made different choices.

And those choices have consequences.

The American result returned Trump to the executive branch of the world’s most powerful country.

The Brazilian result keeps the Bolsonaro political movement outside Brazil's presidency for another term.

That is a real difference.

But it should not be mistaken for a final victory over authoritarian politics.

Bolsonarismo remains powerful. Trumpism remains powerful. And neither political movement can be understood exclusively through its most prominent leader.

They are sustained by institutions, constituencies, incentives, political organizations, and systems of influence that do not disappear when one individual loses an election.

Which brings us to the lesson of these two elections.

Democracy is not protected merely because elections happen. It is protected when political institutions remain accountable, electoral defeat is accepted, and voters retain the practical ability to replace those who govern them.

In 2024, the United States returned to power a leader whose previous tenure ended with an attempt to overturn the electoral process.

In 2026, Brazil appears to have rejected the presidential candidate carrying the political inheritance of its own attempted coup.

Two Americas.

Two elections.

Different outcomes.

And one warning that applies to both:

The ballot box can keep an authoritarian movement out of office. It cannot, by itself, prevent that movement from trying again.
