Trump just broke the formula that kept Republicans in power: historian

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 6, 20263 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that historian Heather Cox Richardson interpreted the reported 2026 Democratic electoral landslide as a rejection not only of President Donald Trump, but of the Republican coalition built around cultural grievance, tax cuts, deregulation, and hostility toward government. Her answer to the headline’s implicit question is that Trump’s second-term policies exposed the material costs of that formula to voters who had previously sustained it.

Richardson pointed to Democratic gains from statewide offices to legislatures and courts, including victories in Texas and several governor’s races. Election scholar Michael McDonald estimated turnout at roughly 124.3 million—just under half of eligible voters and slightly below 2018. Trump reportedly maintained that Republicans would have won had he been on the ballot, while prospective Democratic committee leaders publicly signaled investigations and subpoenas.

The power was never merely cultural

The Republican formula Richardson describes did not survive for decades on rhetoric alone. Presidents, governors, legislators, judges, regulators, donors, and corporate beneficiaries converted that rhetoric into policy. Racial resentment and backlash against women’s rights helped assemble the coalition; tax cuts and deregulation directed the institutional rewards.

That distinction matters. Voters supplied ballots, but officeholders decided whose taxes to cut, which regulations to dismantle, and how government power would be deployed. Treating the arrangement as a collection of voter “anxieties” risks obscuring the officials and interests that cultivated those anxieties because they were politically useful.

Trump made the bargain harder to disguise

Richardson’s argument is that Trump did not invent this governing project. He stripped away enough of its sales pitch that its consequences became harder to separate from its targets.

Policies advertised as punishment for minorities, immigrants, and women allegedly produced broader harm that reached ordinary Americans, including voters in rural Republican strongholds. If that account is correct, the coalition fractured when symbolic dominance stopped compensating for material injury. The decisive shift was not a sudden outbreak of national enlightenment. It was recognition that a government organized around hierarchy and elite advantage does not reliably spare the people invited to cheer it on.

An election is evidence, not omniscience

AlterNet presents Richardson’s interpretation with confidence, but the article does not establish that every Democratic victory arose from the same motive. Large swings, broad gains, and high turnout demonstrate repudiation at the ballot box; they do not by themselves prove why each voter changed course.

The phrase “blue tsunami” captures scale while flattening causation. Candidate quality, local conditions, economic experience, corruption concerns, and reactions to specific administration policies may all have contributed. The reporting supplies outcomes and Richardson’s thesis, not enough voter-level evidence to convert that thesis into a settled explanation.

That weakness does not erase the result. It limits the claim. Republicans reportedly lost power across offices and regions they had relied upon, while Democrats gained the institutional capacity to govern and investigate.

Accountability is power, not a slogan

“LAWYER UP” and “Subpoenas” are satisfying posts. They are not accountability. Congressional committees possess investigative power, but that power matters only when lawmakers use it methodically: securing records, compelling testimony, documenting decisions, and distinguishing misconduct from political theater.

Democratic control would transfer more than prestige. Governors can veto legislation. Legislative margins determine whether those vetoes survive. Attorneys general can shape enforcement priorities. State supreme courts decide disputes with lasting consequences. The story’s most important development is therefore not the size of the electoral spectacle, but the redistribution of concrete authority.

The system rewards grievance until it sends the bill

The larger pattern is a familiar one: powerful actors turn social hierarchy into an electoral instrument, use the resulting mandate to distribute material benefits upward, and describe the damage as an unfortunate side effect. Weaker groups absorb the blame while officials, donors, and beneficiaries recede into abstraction.

Richardson argues that this machinery finally became visible to enough voters to break the coalition. The reporting cannot prove that the break is permanent, and one election cannot extinguish a political strategy refined over half a century. It can, however, show the strategy’s vulnerability: scapegoating works best when its audience believes the punishment will remain someone else’s problem.
