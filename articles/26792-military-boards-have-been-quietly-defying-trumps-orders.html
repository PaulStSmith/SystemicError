Military boards have been quietly defying Trump’s orders

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 24, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that military review boards in the Army, Navy, and Marine Corps have allowed some transgender service members to remain despite a Trump administration policy intended to remove them. Citing New York Times reporting, AlterNet says nine of 12 service members brought before the boards were retained. The Air Force, by contrast, was reportedly discharging transgender personnel more quickly.

The proceedings were closed, spouses were excluded, and the Pentagon would not disclose the total number of hearings or removals. One Marine staff sergeant won his case in August; his lawyer said early proceedings had produced dismissals before outcomes began to shift. Another service member, discharged after 18 years, was told that her commendable record was not the issue before the panel.

AlterNet describes these decisions as quiet defiance. But the reporting does not establish that board members disobeyed a specific lawful order. It shows that some panels, operating within an administrative process created by the Pentagon, declined to produce the result the administration apparently expected.

Power wrote the policy

The decisive institutional power belongs to President Donald Trump and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s Pentagon. The administration set the exclusionary policy, defined transgender troops as a burden, and created review proceedings whose instructions reportedly made removal the expected outcome.

Individual board members possess narrower authority: they can decide cases placed before them. Their retention votes matter enormously to the people involved, but they do not reverse the policy, open the secret proceedings, restore those already forced out, or protect future service members. Calling their choices “defiance” risks mistaking limited discretion for control.

The process was built to deliver removals

According to the reporting, the Pentagon established boards with parameters that heavily favored expulsion. Former Air Force lawyer Rachel VanLandingham characterized the process as an attempt to obtain a predetermined rubber stamp. The nine retention decisions suggest that some panel members refused to supply it.

That resistance exposes the policy’s weakness on its own terms. The administration invoked fitness and military effectiveness, yet the policy drove out doctors, lawyers, pilots, and nuclear technicians. Those were not abstract culture-war symbols. They were trained personnel whose qualifications had already been demonstrated through service.

Secrecy protects the institution

Closed hearings and undisclosed totals prevent the public from measuring the policy’s full operation. The Pentagon’s refusal to say how many boards convened or how many troops were removed leaves scattered victories more visible than the system-wide damage.

That asymmetry matters. A successful appeal can be presented as evidence that the process works, while discharged personnel, delayed careers, and months of uncertainty remain difficult to count. Secrecy does not prove illicit intent, but it plainly shields decision-makers from comprehensive scrutiny.

“Defiance” is the wrong center of gravity

AlterNet’s framing gives the most dramatic role to lower-level military panels. The more consequential act came from above: political leaders imposed a category-wide presumption against roughly 4,250 transgender troops, about 0.1 percent of the military, then required individuals to fight for careers they had already earned.

The reported board decisions are significant, but they are not the policy. Nor does the evidence show a coordinated revolt across the armed forces. The sharply different Air Force outcomes demonstrate that institutional implementation remains uneven—and that a service member’s future may depend less on performance than on which branch administers the exclusion.

Bureaucratic resistance cannot substitute for accountability

This episode reveals a recurring structure of discriminatory government: senior officials establish the harm, opaque machinery distributes it, and isolated acts of decency by subordinate officials are celebrated as resistance. The people targeted still absorb the cost through discharge, delay, legal expense, lost expertise, and prolonged uncertainty.

A system should not require anonymous board members to quietly frustrate its leaders before qualified people receive individualized treatment. The reported retention votes show that some officials retained agency. The larger fact is that presidential and Pentagon power forced them to use it against a process designed to turn political exclusion into routine administration.
