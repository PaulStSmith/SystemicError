GOP insiders say one official is about to quit and point the blame at Trump

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower came before distanceThe succession scrambleThe article’s convenient frameAccountability as career managementThe system protects the next candidate

What happened

Marco Rubio is the official Republican insiders expect may quit. AlterNet, citing reporting by the Independent, says party and UK government sources believe the secretary of state could leave President Donald Trump’s administration after Republicans lost both chambers of Congress in the midterms. No resignation had been announced; the reporting describes an expectation attributed to unnamed sources.

Those sources portrayed Rubio’s possible departure as an attempt to protect a prospective 2028 presidential campaign from the administration’s political collapse and the Iran war. An ally called Rubio the leading Republican contender and argued that remaining secretary of state would damage him. Vice President JD Vance separately blamed the midterm defeat on the war and said Trump had done the same.

The Independent’s sources also anticipated that four or five Cabinet members could leave as Democrats prepared to control Congress and pursue investigations or impeachment. They said Trump might shift responsibility toward Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, Rubio, or White House Chief of Staff Susie Wiles. That remains insider speculation, not an established White House plan.

Power came before distance

Rubio is not a bystander trapped beneath somebody else’s foreign policy. He is secretary of state, a Cabinet officer with access, status, and responsibility inside the administration conducting the war. The presidency holds the decisive executive power, but senior officials exercise agency through the advice they give, the policies they administer, and their choice to remain in office.

The reporting says Rubio maintained a quieter role while others led negotiations. Quiet is not opposition. Nor does a lower public profile erase institutional responsibility. If Rubio resigns only after voters punish the administration, the relevant distinction is not between loyalty and dissent. It is between participation while power was valuable and separation once association became costly.

The succession scramble

The reported maneuvering is less a policy reckoning than an allocation of electoral liability. Rubio’s allies want the Iran war attached to Trump. Vance has already made the war the central explanation for Republican losses. Trump, according to sources, may redirect blame toward Hegseth, Rubio, or Wiles.

Each faction is therefore preparing the same defense: the damaging outcome belongs to someone else. That argument may serve a presidential campaign, but it does not answer what these officials supported, enabled, opposed, or failed to stop while holding office. Ambition is driving the search for clean hands after collective governance produced dirty consequences.

The article’s convenient frame

The “blame at Trump” framing captures only part of the power structure. Trump, as president, sits at its center and cannot plausibly be treated as a spectator to his administration’s war. But presenting Rubio mainly as a politician seeking distance risks converting a powerful Cabinet official into another casualty of Trump’s unpopularity.

That is misdirection toward personality and away from institutional conduct. The essential questions are not merely whether Rubio quits or whether Trump scapegoats him. They are what Rubio did as secretary of state, what decisions he helped carry out, and why his objections—if he had any—did not produce a public break before the midterms. The supplied reporting does not establish those answers, and its reliance on unnamed insiders leaves the most consequential record largely unexamined.

Accountability as career management

The sources describe gas prices, public hostility to the war, Democratic victories in Republican strongholds, and the prospect of congressional scrutiny. Those are consequences of governing, not weather events. Yet the reported response inside the administration is flight, blame transfer, and positioning for 2028.

That pattern rewards officials who conceal disagreement until failure becomes undeniable, then market departure as independence. It also reduces accountability to whether a politician can escape the wreckage with a viable résumé. Resignation may end Rubio’s tenure; it would not settle responsibility for decisions made while he possessed power.

The system protects the next candidate

This story reveals a governing class trained to treat public office as a staging ground for the next contest. Policy failure becomes branding trouble. War becomes an inconvenient association. Electoral defeat triggers not disclosure or responsibility, but a race to identify the safest scapegoat.

Trump’s authority matters because presidential power shaped the administration’s course. Rubio’s agency matters because Cabinet rank is not decorative. Vance’s repositioning matters because succession politics is already rewriting shared responsibility into individual innocence. The system’s recurring error is to mistake strategic distance for accountability—and to let officials convert proximity to power into experience, then convert its consequences into somebody else’s fault.
