Trump's obsession with Saudi Arabia 'backfiring' on US oil companies: report

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 23, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:Trump’s “Corrosive Pact” with Big Oil: A Disastrous Deal for Everyone

U.S. President Donald Trump shakes hands with Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed Bin Salman during a welcoming ceremony in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, May 13, 2025. Bandar Algaloud/Courtesy of Saudi Royal Court/Handout via REUTERS

New York Times reporter Noah Shachtman says Trump made a “corrosive pact” with the U.S. oil industry, and today that pact is “wiping out jobs at home and strangling what little hope was left of avoiding a climate disaster.”

“Trump promised them he would do them so many favors that a billion dollars in donations would feel like a ‘deal.’ The executives gave him only a fraction of the money he sought. Mr. Trump, on the other hand, has given them more than they asked for,” said Shachtman.

As predicted, Trump scaled back regulations and encouraged more drilling. He’s also worked to crush the industry’s green competition, pulling the plug on the largest solar project in North America, which was on track to supply enough power for nearly two million homes. Trump also put a $5 billion wind farm project in New York temporarily on hold, threatening thousands of jobs until the state approved a new gas pipeline. And he’s killed incentives for electric vehicles, sales of which had more than doubled since his first term.

It’s all outdated political instinct from the 1970s, said Shachtman, back when Saudi Arabia and the rest of the OPEC cartel cut production and brought the world economy to its knees. For decades afterward, Trump expressed a mixture of admiration and resentment of the kingdom’s resource wealth.

But now, Trump is taking on damaging OPEC-like maneuvers, trying to dominate the international market, warning the world to buy more fossil fuels — or else. Now America is the planet’s leading producer of oil and natural gas, but his America First energy goals have their contradictions.

“In his total commitment to a hydrocarbon-heavy world, he has pushed not only domestic producers but also Saudi Arabia and the rest of the OPEC nations to keep pumping out more, more, more cheap oil,” said Shachtman. “That might be good news for consumers, offsetting some of these high electric rates. But with oil prices down to around $60 per barrel, American companies say they can’t afford to open up new wells, especially now that [Trump’s] tariffs have made drilling equipment so expensive. The total number of active rigs is down year over year.”

Meanwhile the combo of more data centers and fewer renewables is spiking average Americans’ electric bills. Natural gas prices are up, thanks to Trump’s export deals.

“If you gave me a piece of paper and asked me to think about the most creative way, the most effective way, to raise electricity prices in the United States, it would look a lot like what they’ve done,” said Ethan Zindler, a former climate counselor in the Biden Treasury Department who works on policy at Bloomberg New Energy Finance.

The U.S. oil and gas business would probably be thrilled were Trump’s “capricious attacks on renewables and the big swings in energy policy” not also a liability for an industry forced by the nature of its complex engineering projects to plan years, or even decades, ahead.

“Ever-changing policy, particularly as administrations change, is not good for business. It’s not good for the economy and ultimately, it’s not good for people,” said Exxon Mobil’s chief executive, Darren Woods.

Energy executives are unhappy, but they can’t be surprised, said Shachtman. Trump’s favor was always transactional and he wanted to use his oil industry favor to make their energy a tool to leverage his own personal power.

“If they drill themselves out of business, I don’t give a damn,” Trump told voters at a rally last year.

Our Analysis:

Trump’s “Corrosive Pact” with Big Oil: A Disastrous Deal for Everyone

In what can only be described as a Faustian bargain, former President Donald Trump’s alignment with the U.S. oil industry has proved to be more than just a handshake with the devil; it has morphed into a full-blown corrosive pact that, as New York Times reporter Noah Shachtman eloquently outlines, is not only “wiping out jobs at home” but also “strangling what little hope was left of avoiding a climate disaster.” This isn’t mere hyperbole; it’s a calculated observation of a grim reality we’re all part of.

Trump’s promises to the oil moguls were akin to selling the environmental future of the planet for a pittance, promising billions in favors for what amounts to loose change from the oil industry’s overstuffed pockets. This isn’t just a bad deal; it’s the epitome of reckless governance, where the scales of justice are not just tipped but completely toppled in favor of corporate greed over public good.

Trump’s War on Green Energy: A Retrograde Strategy

Shachtman hits the nail on the head when he describes Trump’s energy policies as “outdated political instinct from the 1970s.” However, let’s be clear: describing them as merely ‘outdated’ might be too kind. Trump’s aggressive rollback of green energy initiatives — from pulling the plug on North America’s largest solar project to putting a $5 billion wind farm project on ice — is not just retrograde; it’s an active declaration of war on the planet’s future.

And for what? To ensure the U.S. remains the “planet’s leading producer of oil and natural gas”? Trump’s “America First” energy goals are not just filled with contradictions; they are a testament to a deeply flawed understanding of what true energy leadership looks like in the 21st century. True leadership involves innovation and a forward-looking vision, not clinging to the past like a lifebuoy.

The Economic Backfire: A Lesson in Irony

In a twist that would be ironic if it weren’t so tragic, Trump’s policies have not only failed to secure the long-term prosperity of the oil and gas industry but have also inadvertently spiked the average American’s electric bills. Yes, the very constituency Trump claimed to champion is now bearing the brunt of his misguided policies. The natural gas price hikes, thanks to Trump’s export deals, are a direct hit to the pockets of American consumers.

Furthermore, the oil and gas industry, which Trump purported to save, finds itself in a precarious position, unable to afford new wells as oil prices plummet and the cost of drilling equipment skyrockets due to Trump’s tariffs. It’s a stark reminder that policies grounded in whim rather than wisdom lead to disaster.

The Verdict: A Transactional Tragedy

Shachtman’s analysis lays bare the truth: Trump’s alliance with the oil industry was never about securing America’s energy future but about leveraging power for personal gain. His transactional view of governance — where the ends justify the means, regardless of the collateral damage — has left the U.S. energy sector, and indeed the entire country, in a precarious position.

Trump’s parting message to the industry, as reported by Shachtman, encapsulates his tenure perfectly: “If they drill themselves out of business, I don’t give a damn.” This isn’t just indifference; it’s a glaring admission of guilt from a leader whose policies have not only endangered the planet but have also betrayed the very industry he vowed to protect.

In closing, Shachtman’s report isn’t just a critique; it’s a clarion call for urgent reevaluation and redirection. As the world teeters on the brink of irreversible climate disaster, it’s clear that the true cost of Trump’s corrosive pact with the oil industry is far greater than anyone could have imagined. It’s a sobering reminder that political alliances grounded in exploitation and short-term gain are not just unethical; they’re unsustainable.
