‘I’m Interviewing You’: The Moment Fox’s Questions Became an Argument.

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 6, 202611 min read

Abdul El-Sayed’s Fox News appearance was interesting for a reason much deeper than “Democrat clashes with conservative host.” He did something Democrats on hostile television too rarely do: he refused to accept the premise of the question.

There is a familiar ritual when Democrats appear on Fox News.

The host establishes the frame. The Democrat accepts it. Then the Democrat spends the next six minutes desperately explaining why, despite the assumptions embedded in the question, he is not in fact a terrorist-loving communist who wants Americans to pay $17 for gasoline while criminals loot their homes.

Abdul El-Sayed did something different.

During an October 5 appearance on Martha MacCallum’s The Story, the Democratic nominee for Michigan’s U.S. Senate seat repeatedly refused to play the defendant.

Instead, he kept asking a much more inconvenient question:

Does the policy actually work?

That sounds almost embarrassingly obvious.

It was apparently revolutionary television.

“Are You Saying It’s Our Fault?”

MacCallum opened with terrorism.

She referenced earlier statements in which El-Sayed had discussed why acts of political violence happen and how American policy can contribute to the conditions that produce radicalization.

Then came the framing:

“It almost sounds like somehow, even though this is a terrible thing, it’s actually our fault.”

This is an old trick.

Not necessarily a dishonest one — an interviewer is entitled to challenge a politician aggressively — but a very familiar one nonetheless.

Take an explanation of causation and translate it into an accusation of moral equivalence.

El-Sayed did not take the bait.

“The victim is never at fault,” he answered. Terrorism is wrong regardless of who commits it or who suffers from it.

And then he went right back to the question MacCallum’s framing attempted to make radioactive:

When the United States intervenes abroad, does it create a safer world or a more dangerous one?

That distinction matters.

Explaining why terrorism occurs is not the same thing as excusing terrorism.

We understand this perfectly well almost everywhere else.

Police departments study why gangs form.

Psychologists study why mass shooters become violent.

Governments study why insurgencies gain recruits.

Public-health researchers study which environmental factors make violence more likely.

Nobody seriously argues that examining the causes of gang recruitment means blaming murder victims for being murdered.

But terrorism inhabits a peculiar sanctuary from causal analysis in American politics. Ask why terrorists emerge and someone will eventually accuse you of sympathizing with them.

That is intellectually absurd.

If your actual objective is to produce fewer terrorists, understanding how terrorists are produced would seem like a useful place to start.

The Question MacCallum Couldn’t Quite Answer

From there, the discussion moved to Iran.

And this is where the interview changed.

El-Sayed’s argument was not particularly complicated:

Iran may be dangerous.

Iran may threaten American interests.

Iran may support violent organizations.

None of those facts, individually or collectively, automatically demonstrate that war is the best available response.

That requires another step.

You have to demonstrate that war leaves the United States safer than the alternatives.

El-Sayed kept returning to that question:

Are Americans safer?

Are there fewer threats?

What has happened to energy prices?

What has happened to food prices?

How many American servicemembers have died?

What exactly has the United States purchased with all that blood, money and instability?

At the time of the interview, El-Sayed cited 18 American servicemembers killed in the conflict. The toll has since reached 19, with more than 800 wounded, according to reporting this week.

Those are not abstractions.

Those are costs.

And if you advocate the policy that incurred them, you owe the public an explanation of what those costs bought.

MacCallum instead repeatedly returned to the threat posed by Iran.

Iran had killed Americans.

Iran threatened American interests.

Iran posed a nuclear threat.

American forces in the region were protecting the country.

All potentially relevant points.

None answers El-Sayed’s question.

Did this war make the United States safer?

There is a profound difference between demonstrating that an adversary is dangerous and demonstrating that a particular response to that adversary is wise.

Washington has spent decades pretending those are the same proposition.

They are not.

Then the Interviewer Became the Advocate

Eventually El-Sayed noticed something else.

MacCallum was no longer merely presenting questions.

She was making the counterargument herself.

When she insisted she was not arguing for “more war,” but rather emphasizing the need to defend American national security, El-Sayed turned the question around:

“You haven’t told me where you stand on the war in Iran. You seem to be a fan of it.”

MacCallum replied:

“I’m interviewing you.”

Fair enough.

Except that she had already been doing rather more than interviewing him.

She had argued that Iran created a grave threat.

She had argued that U.S. servicemembers were protecting Americans from those threats.

She had argued that Iran’s conduct helped explain the war.

And finally she explicitly stated that “there is an argument to be made that it will make the United States safer.”

El-Sayed immediately caught it.

“And you’re making that argument, right?”

That was the entire interview in miniature.

There is nothing wrong with a journalist presenting the strongest opposing case.

In fact, journalists should do that.

But there is a difference between saying:

“Supporters of the war argue that Iran represented an intolerable threat. Why are they wrong?”

and spending several minutes personally advancing the proposition before retreating behind:

“I’m just interviewing you.”

Once the interviewer begins making an argument, the interviewee is entitled to challenge the argument.

The press does not acquire diplomatic immunity from scrutiny merely because there is a television desk between the participants.

The Strait of Hormuz Problem

One of MacCallum’s stronger moments came when El-Sayed discussed disruption in the Strait of Hormuz.

He argued that the war had restricted the movement of oil and gas and contributed to higher energy costs.

MacCallum pushed back, citing reporting that shipments were recovering toward prewar levels.

There was substance behind that correction — but it did not prove what she seemed to think it proved.

Current Wall Street Journal reporting shows crude shipments through Hormuz have indeed recovered substantially, to around 76 percent of prewar levels.

But refined petroleum traffic remains badly impaired. Diesel and gasoline flows remain dramatically below their prewar share, and American diesel prices have remained painfully high.

In other words:

El-Sayed’s formulation was somewhat too sweeping.

His underlying argument was not.

War disrupted energy markets.

Consumers paid for it.

Traffic beginning to recover months later does not retroactively erase the disruption.

MacCallum herself essentially conceded the point when she said prices might take “a little while” to come down.

Yes.

Exactly.

That “little while” is the part where ordinary people get the bill.

“War Is Always Controversial”

Perhaps the most revealing sentence of the entire conversation came almost accidentally.

MacCallum said:

“War is always controversial.”

El-Sayed immediately objected.

And he was right to.

“War is always controversial” sounds profound until you examine it for approximately three seconds.

Vietnam was controversial.

Iraq was controversial.

Afghanistan became controversial.

The American Revolution was controversial.

World War II had opponents.

The existence of controversy tells us nothing about whether a war is necessary, lawful, strategically coherent or spectacularly stupid.

It substitutes sociology for analysis.

The question is not whether people disagree about a war.

The question is whether the war accomplishes its stated objectives at an acceptable cost.

El-Sayed refused to allow that distinction to disappear beneath television fog.

The Iraq Argument — Where El-Sayed Gets Sloppy

El-Sayed was not flawless.

At one point he characterized the Iraq War almost as an extension of the post-9/11 appetite for military action: America was already fighting in Afghanistan, Iraq was nearby, so Washington effectively decided to fight there too.

That is rhetorically satisfying.

It is also too glib.

The Bush administration constructed an elaborate justification for invading Iraq around weapons of mass destruction, UN resolutions, Saddam Hussein’s supposed connections to terrorism and the broader security environment following September 11.

Much of that case later collapsed, particularly the claims surrounding active WMD stockpiles.

But the lesson becomes stronger, not weaker, when described accurately.

After 9/11, the political system became extraordinarily receptive to worst-case threat assessments. Uncertainty was repeatedly converted into confidence. Skepticism was treated as weakness. Intelligence that supported military action received extraordinary political weight.

That is more disturbing than saying America simply invaded the country next door because it was already in the neighborhood.

El-Sayed does not need the caricature.

The history is bad enough.

And About That Nuclear Deal…

MacCallum also challenged El-Sayed’s claim that Iran’s nuclear threat had previously been contained under the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action.

Here the chronology matters.

Donald Trump withdrew the United States from the JCPOA in May 2018.

The International Atomic Energy Agency continued verifying Iran’s implementation of its nuclear commitments after the American withdrawal. Iran began reducing compliance roughly one year later. The IAEA itself records that Iran’s step-by-step abandonment of its JCPOA obligations began in May 2019.

That does not prove the JCPOA was perfect.

It does not prove Iran was trustworthy.

It does not prove diplomacy would have solved every problem forever.

It does establish something rather important, however:

The sequence was not:

Iran flagrantly abandoned the deal, therefore Trump withdrew.

The sequence was:

Trump withdrew, restored sanctions, and Iran subsequently began breaching the agreement.

That chronology has been repeatedly mauled in American political discourse because the actual sequence is inconvenient.

El-Sayed’s “contained” language may be stronger than I would use.

But MacCallum’s framing elides the part where the United States deliberately dismantled the containment mechanism being discussed.

The Muslim Brotherhood Trap That Didn’t Work

MacCallum then moved to terrorist designations involving Muslim Brotherhood organizations.

This section was almost comical.

She wanted El-Sayed to say the correct noun.

“Muslim Brotherhood.”

Preferably followed by “terrorist.”

El-Sayed instead gave her a principle.

Any organization engaged in political violence or advocating political violence, he said, should qualify for terrorist designation.

MacCallum pressed again.

Would he support designating these particular organizations?

Again:

Show him evidence that they engage in or advocate political violence and he will support the designation.

So MacCallum supplied examples.

El-Sayed answered that if organizations are engaging in or calling for political violence, yes, he supports those designations.

At that point, he had answered the question.

Just not in the form television wanted.

This is an important distinction.

The politically gratifying answer is:

“Yes! Muslim Brotherhood bad!”

The governing answer is:

“Show me the evidence establishing that this organization meets the legal and factual standard for designation, and I will apply that standard.”

That is not weakness.

That is how governmental authority is supposed to work.

We should probably want senators who ask to see evidence before placing organizations on government terrorism lists.

Apparently that is now considered evasive.

“I Can’t Let You Filibuster”

Eventually MacCallum cut El-Sayed off:

“I can’t let you filibuster.”

There are certainly politicians who deserve that sentence.

El-Sayed, however, had spent much of this exchange doing something slightly different.

He was trying to finish an argument.

There is a distinction.

A filibuster avoids the question.

El-Sayed kept returning to the same question so insistently that it became uncomfortable:

What did the war accomplish?

MacCallum would point to Iranian threats.

El-Sayed would return to outcomes.

MacCallum would invoke national security.

El-Sayed would return to outcomes.

MacCallum would point toward terrorism.

El-Sayed would return to outcomes.

That is not necessarily evasion.

Sometimes it is what answering a question actually looks like when the answer requires more than a slogan.

The Trump Argument Democrats Should Steal

Then El-Sayed made his most politically dangerous argument.

He stopped talking primarily to progressives.

He started talking to Trump voters.

He noted that many Michigan voters supported Donald Trump because Trump sold himself as “America First” — the president who would stop wasting American “blood and treasure” on foreign wars.

And yet, El-Sayed argued, months into Trump’s second administration, America was still at war.

That attack lands because Republicans themselves are now feeling the pressure.

Republican Senate candidates, including El-Sayed’s Michigan opponent Mike Rogers, have recently called for the Iran war to end as rising fuel costs and public dissatisfaction become increasingly dangerous electoral liabilities.

This gives Democrats a far more potent argument than the usual lecture about foreign-policy doctrine:

You were promised fewer wars.

You got another war.

You were promised cheaper living.

You got higher fuel costs.

You were promised America First.

You got another open-ended Middle Eastern conflict.

That does not require voters to become liberals.

It merely requires them to remember what they were promised.

The Most Important Thing El-Sayed Did

The Daily Beast described the encounter as El-Sayed “turning the tables.”

That is true, but not because of one devastating comeback.

He did something more important.

He rejected the assumption that appearing on hostile media requires accepting hostile premises.

When MacCallum implied that examining the causes of terrorism meant blaming its victims, he rejected the premise.

When she treated the existence of an Iranian threat as sufficient justification for war, he rejected the premise.

When she wanted a terrorist designation based on the name of an organization, he insisted on a standard.

When she argued that military force protects American national security, he asked whether the military action under discussion had actually done so.

And when she began personally articulating the case for the war, he asked her to defend it.

This is what political interviews should look like more often.

Not because El-Sayed was necessarily correct about every factual claim.

He wasn’t.

Not because MacCallum was wrong to challenge him.

She wasn’t.

But because interviews become much more useful when neither participant is granted ownership of the premises.

For years, Democrats appearing on conservative media have behaved as though Fox News were some neutral examination board before which they must demonstrate ideological fitness.

They are asked whether they love America enough.

Whether they condemn the correct person strongly enough.

Whether they can prove that social programs are not communism.

Whether understanding terrorism amounts to sympathizing with terrorists.

Whether opposing a war amounts to ignoring threats.

Then they obediently begin answering.

El-Sayed did something wonderfully disruptive.

He occasionally asked:

Wait. Why should I accept that question as constructed?

That should not be revolutionary.

It is merely argument.

And when the subject is war — when governments are spending trillions, destabilizing regions and sending young people into places where some will never come home — argument is not an inconvenience.

It is the minimum we should demand.

If someone proposes war, the burden is not on the skeptic to prove that the enemy is harmless.

The burden is on the advocate to demonstrate that war is necessary, that alternatives are inadequate, that the objectives are achievable and that the likely benefits justify the predictable costs.

“Because Iran is dangerous” is not enough.

“Because terrorism exists” is not enough.

“Because national security matters” is not enough.

Those are premises.

They are not conclusions.

Abdul El-Sayed’s most effective move on Fox News was simply refusing to pretend otherwise.
