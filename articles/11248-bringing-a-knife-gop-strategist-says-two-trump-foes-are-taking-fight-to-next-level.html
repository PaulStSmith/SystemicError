'Bringing a knife': GOP strategist says two Trump foes are taking fight to next level

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 25, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The "Fire with Fire" FallacyThe New Media LandscapeThe Memeification of PoliticsThe Harsh Reality

Donald Trump (Shutterstock)

Two of Donald Trump's political enemies are showing the Democrats how to "fight back," according to a conservative strategist Saturday.

Mike Madrid, who served as the Golden State's GOP political director before co-founding the group of current and former anti-Trump Republicans known as the Lincoln Project, has previously commented on Trump's political tactics.

This weekend, however, Madrid switched it up by talking about the Democrats.

Specifically, Madrid argued that polling proves two Democrats, California Governor Gavin Newsom and New York Democratic mayoral candidate Zohran Mamdani, are making headway.

Madrid started by declaring, "Let me state it up front: Democrats are finally learning to fight fire with fire (as much as I hate that line) and it’s working. From TikTok campaigns that topple political dynasties to governors who troll presidents with their own tactics, the party is discovering that authenticity beats propriety in the attention economy."

He continued:

"Something remarkable happened in New York City this summer, and it wasn’t just another political upset. When 33-year-old Zohran Mamdani surged from single-digit polling to victory in the Democratic mayoral primary, defeating Andrew Cuomo by 12 points, he didn’t just win an election Mamdani rewrote the playbook on how Democrats can compete in the new media landscape."

The strategist added, "Meanwhile, 3,000 miles away, California Governor Gavin Newsom was 'breaking the internet' with something even more audacious: out-Trumping Trump at his own social media game, complete with ALL-CAPS posts, playground nicknames, and memes that would make a Gen Z staffer blush…except that they’re written by Gen Z staffers."

Going even further, Madrid said Democrats were "bringing a knife to a meme fight." He also argued that these are "concrete signs" that the Democratic party is waking up, and that the Democrats may take Trump's tactics to the next level.

"These aren’t isolated incidents. They’re concrete signs of a party finally waking up to a harsh reality: in the attention economy, being right isn’t enough. You have to be seen. Quantity of message has now become more important than quality and if you have both you can become an internet sensation," he wrote, adding, "Yes, Donald Trump broke the mold and figure it out first but if there's any truism I’ve learned in over thirty years in politics its that the first adopter of innovation in the politics business is rarely the one who fully capitalizes on it."

Our Analysis:

The Democrats' New Playbook: A Scathing Analysis

In a world where political discourse seems to be perpetually spiraling into a cacophony of tweets, memes, and sound bites, an article emerges, touting the success of two Democrats in harnessing the very tactics that have, until now, seemed the exclusive purview of their opponents. Mike Madrid, a conservative strategist turned critic of Trumpian politics, extols the virtues of California Governor Gavin Newsom and New York Democratic mayoral candidate Zohran Mamdani, arguing that their success in the "attention economy" signals a seismic shift in Democratic strategy. But does this shift represent a genuine evolution in political engagement, or merely a descent into the mire of modern politics? Let's dissect.

The "Fire with Fire" Fallacy

Madrid begins with a bold proclamation: Democrats are fighting fire with fire—and it's working. This statement, while catchy, glosses over the inherent danger in adopting the tactics of those you oppose. The suggestion that authenticity beats propriety in the attention economy is a dangerous conflation of visibility with virtue. The reality is that the attention economy rewards not authenticity, but rather sensationalism, controversy, and simplicity over nuance. By celebrating the adoption of these tactics, Madrid inadvertently champions a political landscape where the loudest, not the most truthful or thoughtful, voices prevail.

The New Media Landscape

The victories of Newsom and Mamdani are presented as harbingers of a new era in Democratic politics, where engagement in the new media landscape is the key to success. Newsom's ability to "break the internet" and Mamdani's surge from obscurity to victory are indeed noteworthy. However, Madrid's analysis dangerously oversimplifies these successes. It's not merely their adept use of social media or their embracement of meme culture that propelled them to victory; it's also what they stand for. By focusing solely on their tactics, Madrid reduces their political successes to mere viral phenomena, ignoring the substance behind the style.

The Memeification of Politics

The assertion that Democrats were previously "bringing a knife to a meme fight" and are now waking up to the realities of the attention economy is both humorous and horrifying. The idea that political success might hinge more on meme virality than on policy effectiveness is a damning indictment of our current political climate. While Madrid views this as a positive development, it's worth questioning whether the memeification of politics is something to be celebrated. Politics is not a game; it's the mechanism by which societies make collective decisions about their futures. Reducing this process to who can generate the most likes or shares is a reduction of democracy itself.

The Harsh Reality

Madrid's commentary, though insightful, misses a critical point: the adoption of Trump's tactics by Democrats does not signify innovation; it signifies capitulation to a lower standard of political engagement. While he correctly identifies that being right isn't enough in the attention economy, the solution is not to dive headfirst into the shallow waters of internet sensationalism. The real innovation would be finding a way to elevate the political discourse, making it so that being right and being heard are not mutually exclusive.

In conclusion, while Madrid lauds the Democrats' new approach to battling in the attention economy, his analysis fails to address the deeper implications of this shift. The celebration of tactics over truth, style over substance, and virality over viability may lead to short-term gains but at what cost to the long-term health of our democracy? In our rush to adapt, we must not lose sight of what we are adapting to, lest we win the battle but lose the war.
