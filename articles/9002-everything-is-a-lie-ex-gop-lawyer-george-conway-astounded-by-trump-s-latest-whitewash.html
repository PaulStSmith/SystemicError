'Everything is a lie': Ex-GOP lawyer George Conway astounded by Trump's latest whitewash

A former Republican lawyer said on Monday that President Donald Trump appears to be using his pardon power to “whitewash” crimes committed by people in his administration.

George Conway, who left the Republican Party during the first Trump administration, discussed Trump’s recent pardons for people involved in the January 6 insurrection on a new episode of the “George Explains It All (To Sarah Longwell)” podcast that he co-hosts with the publisher of The Bulwark. Conway said he “gets worked up” over the pardons because they illustrate Trump’s efforts to create a reality where “everything is the opposite of what it truly is.”

“Everything is a lie,” Conway said. “Everything has to adhere to the fantasy in the mind of Donald Trump, or else you’re out.”

“He’s a criminal,” Conway added, referring to Trump. “So, of course, he’s going to rewrite history so that he didn’t commit crimes and the people who were working for him didn’t commit crimes, even though they were all criminals.”

Conway noted that Trump’s pardons drew a false equivalency between the January 6 insurrection, which Conway said fit the legal description of a “mob riot,” and the protests against his immigration policies.

“In Trump World, a mob riot is a bunch of people in frog costumes or whatnot singing at ICE officers,” Conway said.

Our Analysis:

Trump’s Pardon Power: A Tool for Historical Whitewashing?

In a recent revelation that shocks no one but should outrage everyone, George Conway, a former Republican lawyer and a vocal critic of Donald Trump, has articulated what many have observed but few in positions of power have dared to say: Trump appears to be using his pardon power not as a means of mercy, but as a tool to “whitewash” the criminal activities of his cronies. Conway, whose transformation from a Republican stalwart to a Trump critic encapsulates the journey of disillusionment of many, dissected Trump’s recent pardon spree on the “George Explains It All (To Sarah Longwell)” podcast with a precision that leaves little to the imagination.

The Audacity of Lies

Conway’s critique centers on a fundamental and disturbing premise: Trump is not merely pardoning individuals; he is attempting to rewrite history itself. According to Conway, Trump’s pardons serve to construct a parallel universe—one where his administration’s misdeeds are erased, its criminalities whitewashed, and its moral bankruptcies repainted as virtues. This is a world where “everything is the opposite of what it truly is,” where truth is not merely distorted but inverted. Conway’s assertion that “Everything is a lie” in Trump’s world is not hyperbole but a chilling diagnosis of a political strategy rooted in deception and denial.

The False Equivalency

Perhaps the most egregious aspect of Trump’s pardon strategy, as highlighted by Conway, is the false equivalency drawn between the January 6 insurrection and protests against Trump’s immigration policies. This is not just an insult to the intelligence of the American public; it is a dangerous rewriting of recent history. To equate a violent attempt to overturn a democratic election with the exercise of free speech in protest of inhumane policies is not only intellectually dishonest; it is morally repugnant. Conway rightly identifies the January 6 event as a “mob riot,” a term that barely scratches the surface of its attack on democracy.

A Criminal’s Gambit

Conway’s branding of Trump as a “criminal” attempting to rewrite history to exonerate himself and his followers from their crimes is a powerful indictment. This is not merely a political critique but an assertion that the very foundation of Trump’s post-presidency strategy is built on legal and moral quicksand. If Conway’s analysis holds water, and there’s little reason to doubt its veracity, Trump’s use of the pardon power transcends the boundaries of traditional political maneuvering and enters the realm of authoritarian revisionism.

Conclusion: A Democracy at Risk

The implications of Conway’s observations are profound. If a president can indeed use the pardon power to erase the crimes of his administration, to rewrite history in his image, and to construct a narrative devoid of truth, then the very foundations of democracy are at risk. This is not merely about the legal implications of Trump’s pardons; it’s about the existential threat to the American democratic experiment. Conway’s critique is a clarion call for vigilance, a reminder that the battle for truth is ongoing, and a stark warning that the tools of democracy can be weaponized to undermine it from within.

In summary, George Conway’s insights into Trump’s pardon strategy reveal not just the actions of a single man but a broader strategy of historical revisionism that poses a direct threat to the principles of truth, justice, and democracy itself. As Conway articulately points out, in Trump’s world, everything has to adhere to a fantastical narrative—a narrative dangerously detached from reality. It’s a scenario where criminals are recast as heroes, insurrections as protests, and authoritarian impulses as executive prerogatives. This isn’t just a systemic error; it’s a systemic failure—one that demands a robust and unyielding response from all who value democracy.
