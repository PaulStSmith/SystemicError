A Vietnamese company exposes the total failure of Trump’s tariffs

Based on an article by Terry H. Schwadron

Trump’s Tariff Theater Punishes Everyone Except the People Who Wrote It

The Real Judge Is the White House

The source article is about “transshipment,” but the real story is power. The White House is the one setting the tariff rates, changing the rules, and then denouncing everyone who routes around them. That is not a neutral enforcement regime. It is an executive branch using arbitrary trade penalties as leverage, then pretending the resulting evasions are proof of foreign villainy.

Peter Navarro’s report tries to dress this up as patriotism and anti-fraud vigilance. What it actually reveals is a government that wants total discretion for itself and total obedience from everyone else.

A Tax, Not a Victory Lap

Tariffs are not paid by China or Vietnam. They are paid by U.S. importers and passed on to U.S. consumers. Navarro’s rhetoric erases that basic fact because acknowledging it would break the spell. If the policy raises prices at home, then the “scam” is not only in the supply chain. It is in the sales pitch.

The article briefly admits that transshipment is, in business terms, part of global manufacturing and international supply lines. That is the inconvenient truth Navarro cannot absorb: companies respond to incentives, and Trump built a system that invites evasive routing by making the tariff itself the distortion.

Blame the Middleman, Protect the Boss

The report frames the issue as foreign actors “defrauding” America, but the stronger charge is against the administration that made this profitable in the first place. If a Chinese-made motor is finished in Vietnam and then shipped to the United States, that is a consequence of tariff arbitrage. It is what happens when policy is written as punishment rather than as coherent industrial strategy.

The misdirection is deliberate. The White House wants the public looking at shell companies, customs tricks, and “shadow networks” while ignoring the origin of the mess: a president imposing variable tariffs by personal whim, as if the Constitution were a customer service suggestion.

Surveillance as Policy Substitutes

The answer now being sold is more monitoring, more AI screening, more customs scrutiny. That is classic authoritarian reflex: break the system, then expand the apparatus that polices the damage. Customs and Border Protection gets a new machine called “Detective Border,” and the administration gets another excuse to present coercion as competence.

This does not solve the structural problem. It adds a layer of algorithmic suspicion over a tariff regime that was already built on arbitrariness. The state creates the incentive, then expands the machinery to punish the response.

The Manufacturing Fantasy

Trump and Navarro keep promising a manufacturing revival, but the article itself undercuts the fantasy. Where production does move, it relies heavily on robotics and AI, which means limited job creation. At the same time, the mass deportation campaign removes labor from the most routine manufacturing jobs. So the administration is tightening the screws on trade while hollowing out the workforce that would supposedly benefit from it.

That contradiction is the point. This is not an industrial strategy. It is a political performance that treats supply chains, labor markets, and consumer prices as props in a grievance campaign.

What This Story Really Shows

The larger pattern is simple: the White House wants the rules written so that it wins, and if that means everybody else loses, so be it. The tariffs are not just economic policy. They are a test of whether the president can impose personal will on markets, courts, agencies, and consumers while calling the result national strength.

This is how democratic institutions get degraded in practice. Not only through spectacle, but through a steady conversion of governance into punishment, and policy into loyalty test.
