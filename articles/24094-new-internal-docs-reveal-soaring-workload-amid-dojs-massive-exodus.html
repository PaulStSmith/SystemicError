New internal docs reveal soaring workload amid DOJ’s 'massive exodus'

The Justice Department’s Personnel Purge Is Becoming a Governing Crisis

The Damage Has an Author

Documents obtained by The Hill and NewsNation reportedly show a Justice Department civil division losing attorneys while workloads surge. Stacey Young of the DOJ alumni group Justice Connection called it a “massive exodus,” driven by firings and resignations since President Donald Trump returned to office in January 2025. The result is not an abstract morale problem: fewer lawyers are carrying substantially larger caseloads across offices responsible for commercial litigation, immigration cases, appeals, and federal programs.

Institutional Power Runs From the White House

The attorneys who remain do not control the Justice Department’s staffing, leadership, or legal priorities. The Trump administration does. It pushed out office leaders, fired career personnel, and created conditions that former DOJ attorneys told The Hill made continued service professionally difficult.

That distinction matters because “attrition” can make a political outcome sound like weather. People simply drift away; capacity mysteriously declines. But this reporting describes an administration exercising personnel power, followed by resignations from lawyers unwilling to repeat the legal and ethical pressures they experienced during Trump’s first term.

Jennifer Ricketts, a former DOJ attorney, said employees remembered being asked during that earlier administration to stretch the law while still trying to satisfy their professional obligations. Her account does not establish the reason for every departure. It does identify a foreseeable consequence of restoring the same political leadership: experienced attorneys expected renewed pressure and left rather than absorb it again.

The Numbers Describe Capacity, Not Inconvenience

Office of Personnel Management figures cited by The Hill show that DOJ lost roughly 10,000 workers after the administration began in January 2025. That total includes personnel in component agencies such as the FBI and Federal Bureau of Prisons; it is not a count limited to civil-division lawyers.

Within the civil division, the reported workload increases were severe. Commercial litigation work rose 84 percent per attorney. Caseloads increased 63 percent in the Office of Immigration Litigation, 54 percent for appeals attorneys, and 44 percent in the federal programs branch.

Those percentages measure what institutional degradation looks like from inside: less time per matter, more pressure on remaining staff, and greater dependence on lawyers already described as overworked and stressed. The government does not stop generating litigation merely because the administration has driven away the people responsible for handling it.

“Turmoil” Conceals the Chain of Responsibility

Calling this another episode of DOJ “turmoil” is inadequate. Turmoil is a condition. Firings are decisions. Leadership removals are decisions. Assigning administration priorities to a depleted workforce is a decision.

The passive framing also misdirects attention toward the remaining lawyers, as though their difficulty keeping pace were the central failure. They did not choose the staffing losses. They inherited them. The relevant question is not why a diminished workforce cannot perform as though it were intact. It is why those holding executive power diminished it while continuing to demand its labor.

Young interpreted the apparently recalled charts as a “desperate cry for help” and argued that the civil division needs funding to replace longstanding experts lost in what she called a purge. Whether the charts were intentionally circulated as a plea is her interpretation. The staffing and workload figures themselves point to the underlying contradiction: the administration weakened the institution and now confronts the predictable costs of that weakening.

A Weaker Department Can Still Serve a Stronger Executive

The federal programs branch defends executive orders and other administration priorities. Its reported workload rose 44 percent per lawyer. That detail exposes the political structure of the crisis: executive power continues producing actions that require legal defense while the professional apparatus assigned to defend them has fewer people.

This does not prove a deliberate plan to sabotage the civil division. It does reveal an administration willing to accept diminished institutional capacity as the price of removing personnel, experience, or resistance it does not want. Career expertise becomes expendable; political direction does not.

The danger is not merely that government performs less efficiently. A department stripped of veteran lawyers and office leaders becomes more dependent on political appointees, less capable of sustained internal resistance, and more vulnerable to errors under crushing workloads. Those are foreseeable institutional effects even where individual motives cannot be established.

The System Rewards Control and Disowns the Consequences

This is a recurring pattern of executive politics: leaders claim the authority to remake an institution, then describe the resulting dysfunction as an impersonal management problem. Responsibility disappears between the order and the outcome.

The civil division’s crisis is therefore larger than a staffing story. It shows how institutional power can be consolidated without formally abolishing anything. Remove expertise, exhaust the survivors, increase politically driven work, and preserve the department’s name while degrading its ability to act as a professional legal institution.

The bureaucracy remains on the organizational chart. What changes is whom it can resist, what it can competently defend, and how much damage its remaining employees are expected to absorb for decisions they did not make.
