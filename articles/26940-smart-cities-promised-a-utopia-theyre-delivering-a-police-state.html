Smart Cities Promised a Utopia. They’re Delivering a Police State.

The sales pitch was seductive.

Traffic lights would adapt to traffic. Sensors would detect water leaks before streets flooded. Public transit would respond intelligently to demand. Cameras would find missing children and stolen cars. Data would make government faster, cleaner, safer, more efficient.

The city would become smart.

Nobody put “automated suspicion generator” on the brochure.

Yet here we are.

In a recent video, a man described receiving an unpleasant lesson in what happens when the machinery of the smart city stops merely observing the world and starts drawing conclusions about the people living in it.

According to his account, police repeatedly called his wife because a black Chevrolet truck had reportedly been involved in a crime. His own black Chevy had been parked at home.

So why were police interested in him?

Because, he says, Flock cameras had recorded that his truck regularly traveled the road where the incident occurred.

Not because a camera photographed his truck committing the crime.

Not because somebody identified his license plate.

Not because a witness identified him.

The system knew that a vehicle resembling his regularly moved through the area.

He says that was enough for him to become a suspect.

That distinction should terrify us.

Because a camera that records a vehicle is evidence.

A database that remembers where vehicles travel is surveillance.

And a system that uses those histories to decide who might be suspicious is something else entirely.

It is automated suspicion.

The Machine Did Not Witness a Crime

There is an important factual limitation here: the video is one man’s account, and I have not independently verified the police department’s handling of the case.

But the technological capability he describes is not speculative.

Flock Safety operates a vast automated license-plate-reader network. As of August 2026, the ACLU reported that the company had more than 120,000 readers operating in at least 6,000 cities. The ACLU separately cited Flock as claiming roughly 20 billion plate scans per month.

These systems do much more than take a photograph of a license plate.

Flock itself markets cameras that use machine learning to categorize vehicles by details such as make, model, color, body type and identifying characteristics. Its current platform advertises access to a national LPR network and tools designed to uncover investigative leads.

That matters because traditional police work begins with a suspect and searches for evidence.

Mass surveillance reverses the process.

It collects evidence about everyone and waits for police to decide who among us should become a suspect.

That reversal is not semantic.

It is foundational.

“Objective Evidence” Is Doing a Lot of Work

Flock repeatedly describes the information captured by its cameras as “objective evidence.”

And at the most literal level, that can be true.

A photograph showing that license plate ABC-123 passed Camera X at 3:42 p.m. is an observation.

The camera does not hate you.

It does not have racial prejudices.

It did not wake up angry because its spouse left it.

But this is where the sales pitch commits a sleight of hand.

The observation may be objective. The inference is not.

Suppose police know only this:

A black Chevrolet pickup was reportedly connected to an incident.

A surveillance database contains thousands of black Chevrolet pickups.

Software identifies the ones frequently seen nearby.

What exactly has been established?

Nothing about guilt.

Perhaps almost nothing about involvement.

The system has merely transformed a description into a list.

But human beings are remarkably susceptible to believing that anything produced by a computer has acquired some additional epistemic authority.

The machine found him.

The database flagged him.

The algorithm identified him.

Those sentences sound considerably more impressive than:

“We searched a database for vehicles meeting criteria we selected and his happened to appear in the results.”

Yet the second sentence may be a much better description of what happened.

Computers do not remove assumptions from investigations.

They automate assumptions at enormous scale.

First the Police Have a Question. Then You Have to Answer It.

The man in the video says that after Flock supposedly associated his vehicle with the investigation, he was left having to establish that he hadn’t been involved — explaining that the truck had remained there while he and his family were working.

Formally, that does not reverse the burden of proof.

The government still must prove guilt in court.

But that answer belongs in a law-school classroom.

The lived reality is different.

When police call your family asking about a crime, you have entered the machinery of criminal investigation.

You may need an alibi.

You may need surveillance footage of your own.

You may need witnesses.

You may decide that you need a lawyer.

You may have to explain where you were, where your car was, who had access to it, and why the computer says whatever the computer says.

The government hasn’t convicted you.

It has merely created a problem that you now have to solve.

That is one of the ugliest characteristics of indiscriminate surveillance: it can redistribute the cost of police investigation from the state to innocent citizens.

Instead of:

“Show us evidence that Paulo committed the crime.”

we creep toward:

“The system returned Paulo. Let’s find out whether he can explain that.”

That is how suspicion metastasizes.

And Then Someone With a Gun Receives the Alert

The speaker raises another possibility.

What happens when the database doesn’t result in a telephone call?

What happens when it results in a traffic stop?

He imagines himself driving with his wife and children when police pull them over with weapons drawn because Flock has associated their truck with a serious crime.

The hypothetical matters because software doesn’t make arrests.

Police officers do.

The database produces an alert somewhere inside a technological stack.

That alert reaches a dispatcher, analyst or officer.

Somewhere along the way, probability becomes prose.

A weak match can become a “possible suspect vehicle.”

A possible suspect vehicle can become a “vehicle connected to an incident.”

And the officer approaching that vehicle has no idea how tenuous the original connection was.

He only knows what appeared on his screen.

If that screen suggests that the occupants may be dangerous, every subsequent decision occurs under that assumption.

The algorithm gets to be wrong in an air-conditioned data center.

The citizen experiences the consequences beside the road.

That distinction becomes rather important when one of the humans involved is carrying a Glock.

“What’s the Worst That Could Happen?”

Perhaps the most revealing part of the video comes when the speaker describes his original reaction to the cameras.

His city, he says, announced that Flock cameras would be installed to combat vehicle theft and help find missing people.

He supported it.

He hates car theft.

Who doesn’t?

And then he asks the question that has accompanied almost every expansion of surveillance technology in modern history:

“What’s the worst that could happen?”

Everything is hidden inside that sentence.

Nobody goes before a city council and says:

We would like to construct a continuously updated database recording the movements of nearly every driver in town so that police agencies can retrospectively investigate their whereabouts.

That sounds appropriately alarming.

So the argument is atomized.

Do you want us to catch car thieves?

Of course.

Would you like us to find kidnapped children?

Naturally.

Should we be able to locate an elderly Alzheimer’s patient who has wandered away?

Obviously.

Should murderers escape because police couldn’t identify their getaway vehicle?

Of course not.

Each question is carefully constructed so that opposing the surveillance sounds synonymous with supporting the crime.

Then the cameras go up.

And the database begins growing.

Infrastructure Has No Memory of the Sales Pitch

This is the point smart-city evangelists consistently miss — or choose not to discuss.

Technology remembers its capabilities, not the promises made when it was purchased.

A camera installed to find stolen cars does not contain a metaphysical limitation called Stolen Cars Only.

Once the infrastructure exists, someone discovers another use for it.

Then another.

Then another.

Flock’s own history illustrates that expansion beautifully.

The company began around license-plate readers. It now markets gunshot detection, video surveillance, mobile security trailers, investigative software, real-time crime-center technology and what it describes as a national LPR network, all integrated through FlockOS.

This is not merely a camera company anymore.

It is building infrastructure for policing.

The ACLU described Flock and competitor Axon in 2026 as pursuing something approaching an “operating system for police departments.”

Consider what that phrase means.

An operating system is not one application.

It is the environment in which everything else operates.

Mission Creep Isn’t a Bug. It’s the Default.

Perhaps the easiest mistake in surveillance debates is asking whether today’s administrators can be trusted.

Wrong question.

Assume they can.

Assume your police chief is honorable.

Assume the mayor is a civil-liberties absolutist.

Assume everyone currently authorized to use the database possesses judgment rivaling Solomon.

Wonderful.

What happens after the next election?

What happens after the next police chief?

What happens after another agency requests access?

What happens after the vendor changes its terms of service?

What happens when somebody discovers a use nobody considered when the city council approved the contract?

This is not paranoia. It is systems engineering.

If you create a capability, somebody will eventually discover it.

If the capability provides institutional advantage, somebody will eventually want to use it.

If using it is easier than obtaining the same information through traditional investigation, pressure will build to normalize that use.

And once normalized, yesterday’s extraordinary surveillance becomes tomorrow’s routine police procedure.

That is mission creep not as institutional failure, but as institutional gravity.

The Company Says Privacy Is Protected

To its credit, Flock does not pretend privacy concerns don’t exist.

The company’s current FAQ says its standard retention period is seven days, that customers own their data, and that Flock does not share or sell that data to third parties. It also emphasizes encryption, auditability and access controls.

Those safeguards matter.

But they do not answer the central objection.

The safest database of innocent people’s movements is the database that was never created.

Encryption protects information against unauthorized access.

It does nothing about authorized abuse.

Audit logs tell us who performed a search.

They do not establish that the search should have been possible.

Shorter retention limits reduce historical depth.

They do not change the nature of mass collection during the retention window.

And assurances about who controls the data deserve particular scrutiny. In April 2026, the ACLU warned municipalities that changes to Flock’s contractual language appeared to grant the company broader control and continuing rights over customer data than many local governments might assume.

This is exactly why civil liberties cannot depend upon corporate promises.

Terms of service are not constitutional amendments.

Now Add Artificial Intelligence

And this is where things become considerably worse.

The old surveillance model was:

Collect everything so investigators can search it later.

The emerging model is:

Collect everything and have software tell investigators what looks suspicious.

In 2025, the ACLU documented Flock’s development of tools capable of analyzing driving patterns for behavior characterized as suspicious.

That crosses an extraordinarily important line.

There is a difference between asking:

“Where was this specific vehicle after a crime occurred?”

and asking:

“Which vehicles are behaving like criminals?”

The first begins with an investigation.

The second manufactures suspects.

And what constitutes suspicious driving?

Visiting several parking lots?

Circling a neighborhood?

Driving late at night?

Taking an unusual route?

Stopping frequently?

Traveling between particular cities?

Perfectly innocent behavior becomes suspicious because the system does not understand why you did something.

It recognizes patterns.

Patterns are not motives.

Patterns are not probable cause.

Patterns are not guilt.

Yet attach a sufficiently sophisticated machine-learning model to them, put the results inside a polished police dashboard, and suddenly statistical weirdness begins dressing itself up as evidence.

This Is Why “If You Have Nothing to Hide…” Was Always Nonsense

The standard defense of surveillance has always been:

“If you haven’t done anything wrong, what do you have to worry about?”

This man’s story supplies the answer.

You have to worry about the system being wrong.

You have to worry about someone else driving a similar vehicle.

You have to worry about incomplete data.

You have to worry about mistaken plates.

You have to worry about incorrect assumptions.

You have to worry about innocent behavior fitting somebody’s model of suspicious behavior.

You have to worry about an officer treating an algorithmic suggestion as an established fact.

Privacy was never primarily about hiding wrongdoing.

Privacy protects innocent people from being required to continuously establish their innocence.

That distinction seems increasingly alien to a society enamored with collecting everything merely because storage is cheap and computers make searching easy.

Law Enforcement Is Supposed to Be Hard

We have said this before, but it bears repeating.

Law enforcement is supposed to be hard.

That is not a defect.

The state possesses powers no private individual possesses.

It can detain you.

Search you.

Confiscate your property.

Put you in a cage.

And, under sufficiently bad circumstances, kill you.

The friction placed between suspicion and those powers exists for a reason.

Warrants are friction.

Probable cause is friction.

Particularity is friction.

Judicial oversight is friction.

Requiring detectives to actually investigate is friction.

The history of civil liberties is, in enormous part, the history of deliberately making government power inconvenient to exercise.

Technology companies look at that inconvenience and see inefficiency.

Civil libertarians look at it and see the point.

A society in which police can instantly query the historical movements of everyone is unquestionably more efficient than one in which investigators must painstakingly establish leads.

So is a society in which police can enter every house without a warrant.

Efficiency is not the highest value of a free society.

Sometimes inefficiency is freedom’s implementation cost.

The Smart City Was Always Missing One Question

Smart-city advocates spent years asking:

What can we measure?

Traffic.

Energy consumption.

Pedestrian movements.

Vehicle movements.

Noise.

Air quality.

Public transportation.

Crime.

Every new sensor produced another stream of data.

Every stream of data promised another optimization.

But the important question was never:

What can we measure?

It was:

What should government be allowed to know about people who have done nothing wrong?

We skipped that conversation.

We built the infrastructure first.

Now we are discovering the politics embedded inside it.

And those politics are simple.

A city optimized entirely around visibility is a city in which anonymity becomes abnormal.

A city in which anonymity becomes abnormal is a city in which ordinary behavior becomes permanently investigable.

And a city in which every ordinary person’s movements are permanently investigable has acquired one of the defining capabilities of a police state — even if everyone involved insists that they built it only to catch car thieves.

First They Sold Us Convenience

The most effective surveillance systems do not arrive wearing jackboots.

They arrive with benefits.

They save time.

They solve crimes.

They recover stolen property.

They find missing people.

They make compelling presentations at city council meetings.

And many of those benefits are completely real.

That is precisely what makes the problem difficult.

A useless surveillance system is easy to reject.

A useful surveillance system presents society with a much harder proposition:

How much freedom are you willing to exchange for convenience and security?

And once the infrastructure exists, there is another problem.

You may discover that the exchange was not yours to make permanently.

Future governments inherit the cameras.

Future police departments inherit the databases.

Future prosecutors inherit the search tools.

Future authoritarians inherit everything.

The question therefore cannot be whether you trust today’s operator.

The question is whether you would build the same system if your worst political enemy controlled it tomorrow.

If the answer changes, you have already identified the problem.

The Camera Is Not the Dangerous Part

A camera is glass, silicon and software.

It has no ideology.

The danger emerges from what surrounds it:

the database,

the retention policy,

the search engine,

the sharing agreements,

the algorithms,

the institutional incentives,

and the armed authority waiting at the other end.

Taken individually, each component sounds manageable.

Combined, they produce something fundamentally different.

A system capable of watching nearly everyone, remembering where they went, searching those movements retrospectively, finding patterns among them and transforming those patterns into police leads.

We have a word for governments that possess that capability.

We just become uncomfortable using it when the cameras are solar-powered, the database lives in the cloud, and the interface has rounded corners.

The smart city promised to understand itself.

Instead, it increasingly understands us.

And now, in at least one man’s account, a machine that knew where his truck usually traveled helped make him answer questions about a crime he says he had nothing to do with.

That should settle one question.

We no longer have to ask:

“What’s the worst that could happen?”

We should start asking why we built the machinery before demanding an answer.
