General Tubman: The Revolutionary America Tried to Tame

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 12, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

For most Americans, Harriet Tubman exists in the tidy margins of grade school history books: a kind-eyed woman who helped slaves “escape to freedom” via the Underground Railroad. That’s the version they hand you—the sanitized, defanged, bite-sized tale that makes white America comfortable with its past. But here’s the truth: Harriet Tubman didn’t just help people escape. She waged war.

What the textbooks don’t teach—what they deliberately omit—is that Tubman was a military strategist, a Union spy, a saboteur, and the first woman in U.S. history to lead an armed military raid. She didn’t merely lead people to freedom; she led soldiers into battle and struck at the heart of the Confederacy. Her legacy isn’t one of passive defiance. It’s one of direct action, insurgency, and revolutionary fire. She deserves to be remembered not just as a hero, but as General Tubman.

The Myth We’re Told

The narrative we grow up with is all too neat: Harriet Tubman, born into slavery in Maryland in 1822, escapes to the North and returns multiple times to “guide” enslaved people to freedom through a clandestine network of allies and safehouses. That’s all true, but it’s only the surface.

What we’re not told is that she ran an intelligence-gathering and logistical operation that rivaled military campaigns. She didn’t just wait for people to come to her—she infiltrated plantations, organized extraction operations, coordinated with abolitionists, bribed informants, mapped troop movements, and navigated by the stars through hostile territory patrolled by slave catchers. This wasn’t charity. It was insurgency. She was one of the most successful guerrilla operators in U.S. history.

The Underground Resistance

The Underground Railroad wasn’t a railroad, and it wasn’t underground. It was an encrypted network of abolitionists, freedmen, and rebels working in defiance of federal law. Tubman was its most famous conductor—but her role was much more akin to a modern special forces operative.

Tubman made an estimated 13 missions into the South and rescued over 70 enslaved people directly. She trained others. She established routes and protocols. She was never caught, and she never lost a passenger. Every mission was a risk of death—not just for her, but for every person she helped. This wasn’t some charity project. It was a full-blown resistance network operating under the noses of one of the most brutal legal systems ever established on American soil.

And let’s not forget: this was during a time when simply existing as a free Black person in the U.S. was a risk. The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 made it legal to recapture escaped slaves even in free states. Tubman was constantly hunted, and a $40,000 bounty (over $1 million in today’s money) was placed on her head. She didn’t flinch.

Spy, Saboteur, Soldier

When the Civil War broke out, Tubman didn’t retire. She doubled down. She offered her services to the Union Army and was eventually recruited as a spy by the Union military. Working with Secretary of War Edwin Stanton and General David Hunter, Tubman gathered intelligence behind enemy lines. She recruited a network of informants among the enslaved populations and used their knowledge of Confederate supply lines, troop movements, and terrain to inform Union strategy.

Her most famous operation? The Combahee River Raid of June 2, 1863.

The Combahee River Raid: Tubman’s D-Day

With a team of Union soldiers from the 2nd South Carolina Volunteer Infantry—a regiment of Black troops led by white abolitionist Colonel James Montgomery—Tubman orchestrated a multi-boat raid along the Combahee River in South Carolina. Her intelligence identified plantations along the river that were poorly defended but housed large populations of enslaved people.

In the dead of night, Union gunboats steamed up the river, avoiding torpedoes (mines) thanks to Tubman’s detailed maps and knowledge of the area. When they reached their targets, they destroyed Confederate infrastructure, set fire to crops and warehouses, and, most importantly, freed over 750 enslaved people in a single strike.

Eyewitness accounts describe scenes of jubilation as men, women, and children rushed the boats. Tubman, standing on deck, called out to them herself. Many of the freedmen joined the Union Army that very week, swelling the ranks of the Black regiments fighting to crush the Confederacy.

This raid was the first time in U.S. history that a woman—Black or white—led a military assault. It was so effective that it crippled Confederate supply lines in the region. And yet, this fact is almost entirely absent from mainstream history education.

A Legacy Rewritten—Or Erased

Tubman spent the rest of her life fighting for justice: for the poor, for women, for the disabled, and for the elderly. She lobbied for women’s suffrage alongside Susan B. Anthony, though she often found herself sidelined by white feminists who saw race as a “secondary issue.” She struggled for decades to receive a military pension for her Civil War service, only to be granted a meager widow’s pension based on her late husband’s service—not her own.

She died in poverty, surrounded by those she had helped, unrecognized by the government she had served.

Why We Must Remember the Real Tubman

History is not neutral. It is curated, edited, and often weaponized to serve those in power. The reduction of Harriet Tubman to a saintly figure who merely “helped” enslaved people escape is not accidental—it’s a way to strip her of her radicalism, to turn a revolutionary into a mascot.

The truth is, Tubman was a tactical genius, a warrior, and a freedom fighter. She didn’t just dream of a better world—she fought for it, with fire and steel.

She should be studied in military academies. Her tactics analyzed in leadership seminars. Her legacy taught not just during Black History Month, but as part of the canon of American strategy and resistance.

And she should be remembered not just as Harriet Tubman, but as General Tubman.

Closing Thoughts

We don’t need another saint. We need more insurgents. We need people who understand that freedom isn’t granted—it’s seized. Harriet Tubman didn’t wait for the world to change. She changed it herself.

So the next time someone offers you the milquetoast version of history—the one where Tubman is a kindly conductor on a symbolic railroad—set them straight.

Tell them about the raid. Tell them about the gunboats. Tell them about the 750 souls she freed with a military plan so daring it shocked the Union brass.

And tell them: her name was General Tubman.

History remembers the bold. Let’s remember her boldly.
