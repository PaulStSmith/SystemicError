Haberman spills new details about Trump's behind-the-scenes meltdown

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

What happened

AlterNet reports that Maggie Haberman told CNN that President Donald Trump berated White House chief of staff Susie Wiles in the East Room on Election Night and blamed her for sweeping Republican midterm losses. Haberman said Trump had criticized Wiles privately, publicly within the administration, and to her face for at least six months before replacing her with Stephen Miller.

According to Haberman, Miller was not among those pressing for Wiles’s removal and reportedly had been comfortable with her remaining. She also said Trump had attacked Wiles over her failure to identify who leaked information about his travel to Turkey during a NATO summit, when officials allegedly misrepresented his whereabouts.

The power was always Trump’s

Wiles managed the White House, but Trump held the presidency, set its policy, and resisted being constrained. Haberman’s account therefore describes more than an angry boss firing a disappointing subordinate. It describes the country’s most powerful official assigning responsibility downward for political consequences produced under his own authority.

A wave extending, in Haberman’s description, from Mississippi to Maine cannot plausibly be reduced to one aide’s tactical mistakes. Voters were responding to a governing party and a president. Trump had the power to shape both. Wiles became useful precisely because she did not.

A scapegoat, not an explanation

Blaming Wiles converts a broad electoral rejection into a personnel failure. That misdirection protects Trump from the central implication of the results: Republican losses across disparate states may reflect opposition to the administration’s leadership and policies, not merely defective political management.

The distinction matters. Staff can influence scheduling, communications, and campaign execution. They cannot erase presidential responsibility for an administration whose policy, Haberman emphasized, was largely set by the president. Treating Wiles as the cause confuses proximity to power with possession of it.

Humiliation as management

Trump reportedly did not merely dismiss Wiles. He berated her before others and had spent months criticizing her while people inside the administration undermined her. That is a governing structure built around personal favor, internal rivalry, and the leader’s anger—not clear lines of accountability.

The fixation on finding the Turkey-travel leak sharpens the point. In Haberman’s account, Trump was furious that Wiles had not identified whoever exposed an alleged official deception about his whereabouts. The institutional priority was apparently not examining why officials misled people, but locating the person who revealed it. That is how secrecy becomes loyalty and truth-telling becomes betrayal.

Miller changes the personnel, not the structure

Stephen Miller’s elevation may alter who executes Trump’s agenda, but it does not relocate presidential agency. Haberman specifically said Miller was not driving Wiles’s ouster. The decisive actor was Trump, who had soured on his chief of staff and ultimately replaced her.

The danger in focusing chiefly on Miller’s reputation is that it can again shift attention away from the officeholder making the choice. Miller may wield substantial delegated influence, but Trump selected him, narrowed the decision-making circle, and rejected restraint. The structure is personal rule exercised through chosen loyalists.

Failure flows downward

This episode reveals a recurring pattern of personalized power: authority concentrates at the top while blame is distributed beneath it. Success validates the leader; failure incriminates advisers, disloyal officials, leakers, or insufficiently obedient managers.

That arrangement does more than produce a toxic workplace. It prevents electoral defeat from functioning as political feedback. When the person setting policy treats rejection as evidence that subordinates failed him, accountability becomes impossible by design—and the next response is likely to be greater loyalty, tighter secrecy, and fewer people empowered to say no.
