Megapastor Trump ally’s stunning backpedal just exposed 'bad' GOP candidate

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Robert Jeffress, a prominent evangelical pastor and longtime Donald Trump ally, said he would not ostracize congregants who vote for Democratic Senate nominee James Talarico over Republican nominee Ken Paxton in Texas. Jeffress told New York Times columnist David French that he would explain his objections to Talarico but ultimately tell such a congregant to follow their conscience and return to church the next Sunday.

The article presents that tolerance as a notable retreat from Jeffress’s harsher political rhetoric. It attributes Republican unease to Paxton’s scandals and says polls placed Talarico ahead as the 2026 midterms approached. But Jeffress did not endorse Talarico, predict the election, or renounce MAGA politics. His remark established only that voting against Paxton would not, in his view, justify religious exclusion.

The permission structure is cracking

Jeffress has spent years helping turn evangelical authority into political permission: a mechanism for excusing conduct that would ordinarily violate the moral standards pastors claim to enforce. AlterNet recalls his argument that evangelicals could disregard allegations surrounding Trump’s personal conduct because supporting him was politically “pragmatic.”

Now the permission runs in the opposite direction. Jeffress is giving church members room to reject a Trump-endorsed Republican without being treated as apostates. That shift matters because political power in this story does not belong primarily to conflicted congregants. It belongs to party leaders who selected and promoted Paxton, to Trump as the endorser, and to religious figures such as Jeffress who help define which political choices conservative Christians may regard as morally acceptable.

Paxton is the party’s decision

Calling Paxton merely a “bad candidate” risks laundering agency out of the story. According to AlterNet, he won the Republican nomination with Trump’s endorsement despite the scandals weighing on his general-election prospects. Republican voters and institutions produced this candidacy; Democrats did not impose it on them.

The resulting discomfort is therefore not an inexplicable electoral accident. It is the foreseeable cost of a political culture that treats scandal as disqualifying only when convenient. Jeffress’s softened posture does not correct that machinery. It manages the fallout after the machinery delivered a nominee some Republicans may find impossible to defend.

Conscience, selectively restored

Jeffress’s appeal to individual conscience sounds generous only because the political Christianity around him has so often made partisan loyalty a test of faith. “I’ll see you in church next Sunday” is ordinary pastoral conduct presented against an extraordinary backdrop: years of rhetoric that fused religious belonging with support for aggressive Republican power.

The asymmetry is revealing. AlterNet says Jeffress previously dismissed concerns about Trump’s alleged misconduct as secondary to political utility. Faced with Paxton, he now recognizes that a voter might be unable to “overlook” what the article describes only as “the other.” The reporting does not establish precisely which Paxton-related concern Jeffress meant, so it should not be converted into a confession or denunciation. What it does show is that moral discretion becomes available when partisan discipline becomes harder to sustain.

The headline overstates the evidence

Jeffress’s remark does not prove that Texas is turning Democratic, nor does it by itself “expose” Paxton’s quality as a candidate. The polling cited by AlterNet is stronger evidence of electoral weakness than one pastor’s refusal to excommunicate dissenters. Even a polling lead cannot establish the eventual result.

The article also compresses several distinct developments—reported declines in Trump’s evangelical approval, dissatisfaction over deportation and prices, conflict with the pope, war with Iran, economic strain, and Paxton’s candidacy—into a suggestive narrative of collapse. Those claims may describe a broader erosion, but the supplied reporting does not demonstrate how much each factor caused it. Political analysis becomes propaganda when correlation is dressed up as settled causation, even when the conclusion flatters the writer.

A movement meets the cost of its exemptions

The larger pattern is not that one pastor suddenly discovered tolerance. It is that a movement built on moral exemptions eventually produces candidates who expect those exemptions as an entitlement. Trump’s endorsement helped Paxton secure the nomination; religious allies helped normalize the idea that personal scandal could be subordinated to political usefulness. The same system now confronts the electoral consequences of that bargain.

Jeffress’s backpedal is significant because gatekeepers rarely loosen discipline without pressure. But it is not repentance, and it is not institutional reform. It is a limited release valve for voters trapped by choices their own political and religious authorities helped create.
