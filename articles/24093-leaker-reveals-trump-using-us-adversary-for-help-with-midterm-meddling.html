The China Scare Is a Tool, Not a Policy

An Allegation About Presidential Power

Miles Taylor, a former Department of Homeland Security official in Donald Trump’s first administration, alleges that Trump intends to use the possibility of Chinese election interference as a pretext for challenging the midterm elections. Taylor points to a contradiction: Trump publicly accused China of compromising the electoral system, yet, according to Taylor, imposed no sanctions, expulsions, or other consequences before welcoming Chinese leader Xi Jinping to Washington.

The Power Sits in the White House

China may possess the capacity to interfere in American politics, but Taylor’s allegation concerns the conduct of the American president. Trump holds the institutional power to direct intelligence priorities, pursue diplomatic penalties, shape federal election policy, and use the presidency’s platform to influence public confidence in electoral outcomes.

That distinction matters. A foreign threat can be real while also being politically exploited. Taylor says concerns about Chinese manipulation are legitimate; his accusation is that Trump treats those concerns selectively—urgent when they justify expanding his control, disposable when they interfere with diplomacy or spectacle.

Inaction Is the Central Evidence

The article’s strongest material is not the pageantry at Joint Base Andrews or the awkwardness of Trump’s praise for Xi. It is the gap Taylor identifies between accusation and action.

If an administration claims that a foreign government has attacked the electoral system, the relevant questions concern evidence, investigation, and official response. According to Taylor, Trump supplied none of the consequences his own rhetoric would ordinarily demand. Taylor interprets that inconsistency as evidence that the allegation serves a domestic political purpose.

That remains Taylor’s inference, not an established account of Trump’s plans. The reporting provides no documents, directives, or corroborating officials demonstrating a scheme to manipulate the midterms. Calling it proven would replace analysis with credulity.

Spectacle Is Not the Scandal

The source spends considerable energy on Trump personally greeting Xi, service members smoothing a red carpet, and billionaires attending a lavish White House gathering. Those images may illustrate deference and elite access, but they do not establish election misconduct.

Fixating on decorum also weakens the political critique. The serious issue is not whether Trump behaved too warmly toward an adversary. It is whether the president invokes foreign interference without pursuing a proportionate response, leaving the allegation available as a future justification for disputing an unfavorable result. Manners are incidental; control over the federal government’s response to elections is not.

A Convenient Enemy Has Many Uses

Taylor describes China as a recurring foil for Trump’s projects involving elections, data centers, trade, and economic management. The source does not independently establish all of those asserted motives, but the pattern Taylor alleges is recognizable within the reporting: Beijing is presented as an existential villain when fear expands presidential leverage, then treated as a valued partner when diplomacy and elite ceremony become useful.

That is propaganda’s practical function. It does not require consistent belief. It requires an enemy flexible enough to explain domestic problems, justify concentrated authority, and disappear temporarily when inconvenient. The contradiction is not proof of a secret plan, but it is a reason to scrutinize who benefits each time the threat is activated.

Elections Cannot Run on Presidential Convenience

The systemic failure exposed here is an accountability structure that treats incendiary presidential claims as political content instead of demands for evidence and action. When a president alleges foreign interference, institutions should not permit the charge to remain suspended between national-security warning and campaign slogan.

Taylor has made a serious allegation, not proved a case. But the burden now belongs where the power resides: with the administration making claims about compromised elections and controlling the machinery capable of answering them. Without disclosed evidence, a defined response, and limits on how such claims can be used after voting, “foreign interference” becomes more than a security concern. It becomes an instrument of presidential discretion over which election results are allowed to count.
