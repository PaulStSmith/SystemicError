SEALs flatten 'weak' Hegseth as elite captain's career ruined because she's female: report

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 12, 20255 min read

Pete Hegseth speaks to senior military leaders at Marine Corps Base Quantico, Virginia. Andrew Harnik/REUTERS

The first woman nominated to lead the Naval Special Warfare command overseeing Navy SEALs had her promotion abruptly canceled — and military sources blame Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s hostility to women in combat roles.

CNN reported the Navy captain, a troop commander with Navy SEAL Team Six and a Purple Heart recipient, had been ranked the top officer for promotion in her cohort and was in line to take charge over the elite unit until the plans were scrapped just two weeks before a formal ceremony was scheduled, and thanks to the Navy’s “up or out” policy, her military career was effectively ended.

“They want to keep it the brotherhood and don’t like that she’s coming in and challenging the status quo,” a Navy special operations source familiar with the situation told CNN.

One defense official familiar with the matter said there had been discussions about whether the role was necessary, and a Pentagon source said Hegseth was not involved and the captain’s nomination was pulled because she was not a SEAL, but multiple sources told CNN they didn’t buy those explanations.

“They can justify it by saying she’s not qualified because she’s not a SEAL,” said one retired SEAL. “But the SEALs thought she was qualified.”

CNN is not naming the captain, who did not respond to requests for comment, but the retired SEAL said the move clearly reflected Hegseth’s views about women serving in the military and stated the Pentagon chief was a misogynist.

“I’m sure they would repeal the whole women in combat thing [if they could], but this is what they can do,” the retired SEAL said.

Hegseth has already removed several women from top leadership roles, and active-duty women – who make up about 18 percent of the U.S. military – and military veterans told CNN the self-styled “secretary of war” had made service less safe and more difficult for them.

“To be quite honest, I am fearful for women in uniform right now,” said Patti J. Tutalo, a retired Coast Guard commander who served on an advisory group for women in the military before it was shut down this year.

“I definitely think there will be a retention issue for women,” Tutalo added. “I also think that you’re going to see an increase in assaults, increase in harassment, increase in bullying, hazing, and I think there’ll be a lack of accountability for those things.”

The Navy captain’s former SEAL Team Six comrades are furious about her command getting yanked.

“That’s horse s--t,” the former senior enlisted SEAL said.

The former SEAL voted for Trump and still supports him, but he said Hegseth’s personal views were interfering with his ability to lead the military.

“I think my job is to protect women and children but occasionally there’s bada-- [women] out there, and we should capitalize and not limit ourselves,” he said.

The Navy special operations source pointed out the captain had been effective at recruiting women to special operations roles, and that person offered withering criticism of Hegseth.

“It p---es me off because it is clearly someone who is capable and has done extraordinary things and is being punished because of — and I hate that I have to say it this way — weak-a-- men,” that source said.

Our Analysis:

Analysis of Naval Special Warfare Command Leadership Controversy

In a move that has sparked controversy and drawn sharp criticism, the first woman nominated to lead the Naval Special Warfare command, overseeing the Navy SEALs, had her promotion unceremoniously canceled. This decision has laid bare the undercurrents of discrimination and the systemic hurdles women face in military roles, particularly in combat and leadership positions.

The Facts:

The woman in question, a Navy captain with a distinguished record including service with Navy SEAL Team Six and a recipient of the Purple Heart, was poised to break a significant glass ceiling. She was not only ranked the top officer for promotion in her cohort but was also on the cusp of leading one of the most elite units in the United States military. However, this groundbreaking moment was halted abruptly - just two weeks before a formal ceremony - effectively ending her military career due to the Navy’s “up or out” policy.

The Allegations:

Multiple military sources have attributed this sudden reversal to Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s alleged hostility towards women in combat roles. Despite official explanations questioning the necessity of the role and the captain’s qualifications, given her non-SEAL status, these justifications are seen by many, including a retired SEAL and other insiders, as thin veils for underlying misogyny. The consensus among these voices is clear: this was not about qualifications but about preserving a status quo that excludes women from certain echelons of military leadership.

The Implications:

Misogyny in Military Leadership: If the allegations hold true, this represents a stark reminder of the systemic barriers women face in the military. The notion that Hegseth, described by a retired SEAL as a misogynist, could influence such decisions paints a grim picture of the leadership culture within the Pentagon.

Impact on Women in the Military: The ramifications of this decision extend beyond a single promotion. As Patti J. Tutalo, a retired Coast Guard commander, noted, there is a palpable fear for the safety and career progression of women in uniform. This incident could exacerbate issues of retention, assault, harassment, and a general environment of hostility towards women in the military.

A Blow to Diversity and Inclusion: The captain was reportedly effective at recruiting women to special operations roles. Her removal sends a disheartening message about the military’s commitment to diversity and inclusion, suggesting that even exemplary service and qualifications might not shield one from discrimination.

Conclusion:

The cancellation of the Navy captain’s promotion is not just about one individual’s thwarted career progression; it symbolizes a broader struggle against institutionalized sexism within the military. It raises serious questions about the commitment of military leadership to foster an environment where individuals are judged solely on their merits and capabilities, regardless of gender.

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s alleged involvement in this decision is particularly troubling. Leadership at the highest levels should strive to dismantle, not reinforce, barriers to equality. The military, as an institution, must reflect on this incident and take concrete steps toward creating a more inclusive environment where every service member has the opportunity to succeed based on merit, not gender.

In light of these developments, one cannot help but feel a sense of outrage and disappointment. The narrative surrounding the Navy captain’s halted promotion is not just a tale of personal loss but a damning indictment of the systemic failings that continue to plague our military institutions. It’s high time for a systemic overhaul, one that ensures equality and fairness for all who serve, regardless of gender.
