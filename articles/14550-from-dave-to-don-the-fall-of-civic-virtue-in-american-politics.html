From Dave to Don: The Fall of Civic Virtue in American Politics

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 22, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Legacy vs. LegitimacyPublic Service vs. Public CorruptionHumility vs. NarcissismThe Grift Industrial Complex

There’s a moment at the end of the 1993 film Dave that feels like it came from a parallel reality—one where decency and civic responsibility are the default settings for public office, not the punchline of a cruel joke. Dave Kovic, an affable, earnest man who stumbles into impersonating the President of the United States, chooses to fake a stroke so that the real, incapacitated president can quietly die with dignity. Dave then steps down from the spotlight and returns to obscurity, not with bitterness, but with purpose. He starts anew as a city council candidate, campaigning under his own name, walking the streets, talking to neighbors, doing the gritty, unglamorous work of democracy from the ground up—not because he craves power, but because he’s discovered what it means to serve.

Now step out of that hopeful dream and back into the grungy reality show that is American politics in 2025, where Donald Trump Jr.—a man whose only credential is being the angriest echo of his father—is reportedly toying with the idea of a presidential run. The idea is both laughable and dangerous. Where Dave gave us a vision of humility, growth, and earned legitimacy, Don Jr. serves us a grotesque inversion: an entitled legacy kid wielding inherited grievance like a branding iron, angling not to uplift a nation, but to further monetize its divisions.

He doesn’t want to serve. He wants to sell.

Don Jr. isn’t trying to understand governance, let alone practice it. He’s not out meeting constituents, listening to needs, or crafting policies. He’s not knocking on doors unless there’s a photo op or a donor dinner behind one. His political identity isn’t built on ideals; it’s a side hustle dressed in red, white, and rage. His platform is the same as his podcast: outrage, victimhood, and a relentless call to arms against imaginary enemies.

In that sense, his political ambitions are perfectly in line with the Trump family’s evolution. The old family business—hotels, golf resorts, licensing deals—has been eclipsed by something far more profitable: a media-political grift machine so shameless it would make a televangelist blush. The presidency wasn’t a pinnacle of achievement for his father—it was a brand expansion. And for Junior, it’s the next logical step in the same marketing campaign.

The Trump clan didn’t just blur the line between public office and personal gain—they bulldozed it, paved it over with gold-plated tiles, and put their names on every inch. Inauguration grifts, emoluments scandals, monetized patriotism—all of it was a masterclass in industrialized corruption. Don Jr. doesn’t even bother with the thin veneer of civic duty. He Instagrams the con, hashtags the outrage, and livestreams the pitch.

Let’s break down just how far we’ve fallen:

Legacy vs. Legitimacy

Dave walks away from ill-gotten power and decides to earn his place the right way. It’s a quiet revolution of principle. Don Jr., by contrast, believes that his last name alone entitles him to the keys to the kingdom. He doesn’t need to prove competence or compassion—he just needs to keep the base angry and the merch moving. His claim isn’t rooted in public trust but in inherited grievance.

Public Service vs. Public Corruption

Dave willingly embraces the long, slow work of change at the local level. He’s willing to start small and build upward. Don Jr. wants to skydive into the Oval Office with a MAGA parachute and a Fox News camera crew. He’s not interested in policy—only in performance. He represents a system that doesn’t just tolerate corruption anymore. It rewards it. Public corruption isn’t a bug in his campaign; it’s the business model.

Humility vs. Narcissism

Dave’s humility is the engine of his transformation. He learns, adapts, and ultimately steps aside for the good of the country. Don Jr., by contrast, is a creature of pure narcissism—soaked in ego, addicted to attention, and incapable of self-reflection. He doesn’t want to govern; he wants to perform governance for a crowd of angry followers. He doesn’t fade into the background; he lights the background on fire to stay visible.

The Grift Industrial Complex

For Don Jr., America isn’t a republic—it’s a storefront. The newsletters, the social media monetization, the speaking fees, the signature merch, the pay-to-play access—it’s all product. The presidency, should he ever touch it, won’t be a solemn oath to the Constitution. It’ll be a rebranding opportunity. Instead of “Ask what you can do for your country,” it’ll be “Click the link in bio to save America—10% off with code TRUMPJUNIOR.”

The tragedy here isn’t just Don Jr.'s potential candidacy. It’s the fact that no one’s really surprised. In an era where mediocrity is marketed as authenticity and cruelty is sold as strength, a man like Don Jr. can credibly campaign for the highest office in the land while contributing nothing of substance and promising even less. We’ve come so far from Dave that the idea of stepping down to earn the public’s trust feels almost quaint—like something out of a bedtime story.

Dave ends with a spark of hope—a citizen reinvigorated, taking the long road toward public good. If Don Jr.'s tale continues, it’ll end with subpoenas, infomercials, and a limited-edition gold-plated bobblehead.

One man walked away from power to build something real. The other will never stop screaming into a ring light until someone hands it back.

And that, sadly, is the state of American politics today.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
