The Ouroboros of Anti-Vax Logic: How Going Full Circle Means Biting Your Own Tail

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 16, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Vaccines 101: For the Terminally ConfusedReactionary Logic: The Science of ContradictionThe Cost of Rebranding Science as HeresyWhen the Snake Eats Its Brain

There are moments in the cultural zeitgeist when irony isn’t just thick—it's positively biblical. An old tweet, which I just saw on Quora, by notorious far-right propagandist Jack Posobiec delivered one such moment on a silver platter. In a burst of performative contrarianism, he tweeted:

"What if instead of a vaccine we just were able to get exposed to a weak version of the virus that enabled us to build the antibodies we need to fight the real thing?"

To which the Internet responded with a collective facepalm.

That is literally how vaccines work.

It wasn’t just a scientific misstep—it was a full-scale ideological belly-flop. The tweet unwittingly described the process of immunization so precisely, it could have been ghostwritten by Edward Jenner himself. But instead of being hailed as a moment of clarity, it stood as a glittering example of how intellectual negligence has become a political performance.

Enter @davejorgenson, who captured the absurdity with laser precision:

"The anti-vaxxers went so far right, they looped around and invented vaccinations."

This is where we introduce the ouroboros—an ancient symbol, originating in Egyptian and later Greek tradition, that depicts a serpent eating its own tail. It stands for eternal cycles, self-destruction, and rebirth. In modern parlance, it’s also a metaphor for recursive folly: when a movement becomes so obsessed with rejecting its perceived enemies that it ends up adopting the very principles it claimed to hate.

In this case, it’s anti-vaxxers reinventing vaccines and presenting them as if they just cracked the biomedical Enigma code.

Vaccines 101: For the Terminally Confused

Let’s be crystal clear: vaccines are not witchcraft, they are not “experimental government mind control,” and they are most certainly not new. The idea dates back centuries. The earliest documented practice of inoculation dates back to at least the 10th century in China. In the West, Edward Jenner’s smallpox vaccine in 1796 kicked off the modern era of immunology.

The core idea is simple. You introduce a weakened or inactivated version of a pathogen—or sometimes just a fragment of it, like a protein spike—into the body. This exposure doesn’t cause illness, but it does train your immune system to recognize and neutralize the real deal if it ever shows up. Your body then builds antibodies, the immune system’s snipers, ready to take the shot.

Jack Posobiec didn’t describe an innovation. He described basic immunological training wheels. The same science that’s protected millions from polio, measles, hepatitis, and influenza.

And yet, here we are—watching ideologues stumble through the fog of their own misinformation, only to arrive at the gates of science dressed like conquering heroes.

Reactionary Logic: The Science of Contradiction

This isn’t a one-off slip—it’s symptomatic of a culture that has made kneejerk opposition into a lifestyle brand. In the world of far-right commentary, rejecting “mainstream narratives” is more than a pastime—it’s a sacrament. If the CDC supports it, it must be part of the globalist plot. If Anthony Fauci says it, it’s obviously wrong.

The irony, of course, is that this kind of thinking isn’t rebellious. It’s infantile. It doesn’t question authority in a meaningful way; it just does the opposite of whatever authority says. It’s not skepticism—it’s ideological Pavlovianism.

The problem with this approach is that it’s not built on principles or facts. It’s built on reaction. Eventually, it loops so hard on itself that you begin to contradict your own beliefs. You’re against vaccines until you accidentally invent them. You scream about tyranny while demanding the government enforce your worldview. You talk about freedom while championing fascism.

That’s the ouroboros in motion—a closed circuit of unexamined contradictions.

The Cost of Rebranding Science as Heresy

Let’s not be lulled by the comedy here. The consequences are anything but funny. Anti-vax rhetoric kills. Full stop. It kills by spreading disinformation, by sowing distrust in science, and by politicizing public health. It convinces people to skip life-saving interventions, leaving them and others vulnerable to entirely preventable diseases.

We saw this during the COVID-19 pandemic in brutal detail: packed ICUs, overwhelmed hospitals, young and old alike on ventilators. And still, some proudly refused to take a vaccine—some based on misinformation, others based on political identity, and many based on the nonsense fed to them by talking heads like Posobiec.

The irony reaches grotesque levels when those same voices accidentally endorse vaccination under a different name. It’s like rebranding oxygen as “freedom air” and pretending you’ve invented breathing.

And let’s not forget the grift. Many of these self-styled health rebels aren’t just wrong—they’re monetizing their wrongness. Selling supplements, pushing miracle cures, hawking books and Patreon subscriptions to audiences groomed to distrust science and embrace pseudoscientific rebellion.

When the Snake Eats Its Brain

Jack Posobiec didn’t just invent vaccination—he reinvented it for people too stubborn to accept they were wrong. His tweet is a monument to how deep the anti-science rabbit hole goes. It’s also a grim reminder that in this ouroboros of disinformation, the serpent isn’t just eating its tail anymore.

It’s gnawing on its own brain.

And it’s proud of it.

This kind of epistemological collapse isn’t just embarrassing—it’s dangerous. We are witnessing a growing subset of the population that believes reality is optional, that facts are negotiable, and that truth is whatever makes them feel vindicated.

So the next time someone asks, “What if we just exposed ourselves to a weaker version of the virus to build immunity?”—tell them they just described vaccination. Then give them a pat on the back for accidentally catching up to the 18th century. And finally, hand them a biology textbook. Or better yet, a mirror.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
