'Basically never happens': Legal expert singles out Alito slight in overnight Trump ruling

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 20, 20254 min read

Supreme Court Justice Clarence Thomas and Justice Samuel Alito attend a private ceremony for retired Supreme Court Justice Sandra Day O’Connor before public repose in the Great Hall at the Supreme Court in Washington, Monday, Dec. 18, 2023. Jacquelyn Martin/Pool via REUTERS/

In a series of posts on BlueSky, Slate legal analyst Mark Joseph Stern suggested the majority of justices on the Supreme Court appear to be angry with Donald Trump's administration's attempt to swiftly deport more immigrants in Texas while there are still questions about its legality.

Noting that seven of the nine justices on the conservative-majority court stepped in to stop the Alien Enemies Act rendition flight with an order issued at 1:30 AM, Stern pointed out that Justices Sam Alito and Clarence Thomas dissented but were not given time to publish their reasoning.

As he wrote on BlueSky, "Obviously, a huge victory for the migrants here and a major defeat for Trump’s lawless effort to rush out a rendition flight before the courts could act. But also—potentially a massive signal from the Supreme Court that it is finally prepared to go toe to toe with Trump to halt AEA deportations."

Having written that, he elaborated, "I think the majority that lifted Boasberg’s restraining order truly believed Trump would heed its warning about due process and pause further AEA renditions until SCOTUS ruled on their legality. Instead Trump tried to sneak out migrants before courts could act. And now I think SCOTUS is pissed."

In particular, he noted Alito would be publishing his dissent at a later time after that the rest of the court, led by Chief Justice John Roberts, rushed out their ruling without waiting for him.

"It is SO unusual for the Supreme Court to issue an order this late at night and honestly incredible only Thomas and Alito noted their dissents," he wrote before adding, "Also fascinating that SCOTUS rushed out the order before Alito could finish writing his dissent. That basically never happens! Again—majority seems pissed."

Our Analysis:

Supreme Court's Late-Night Rebellion: A Sign of Judicial Discontent?

In a striking display of judicial urgency, the Supreme Court's decision to halt the Trump administration's attempt to expedite the deportation of immigrants in Texas—under the shadowy auspices of the Alien Enemies Act (AEA)—signals a remarkable moment of defiance against what has been perceived as executive overreach. As reported by Slate legal analyst Mark Joseph Stern via a series of posts on BlueSky, the court's actions not only underscore a significant victory for migrant rights but also illuminate a potentially seismic shift in the court's patience with the Trump administration's legal maneuvers.

The Nighttime Order: An Uncommon Judicial Gesture

It's not every day—or rather, every night—that the Supreme Court issues a decision at the witching hour of 1:30 AM. This unusual timing is our first indicator that we're not in Kansas anymore when it comes to the standard operating procedures of the highest court in the land. The majority's decision to issue an emergency order to stop a rendition flight, slated to deport immigrants under the controversial AEA, speaks volumes about the urgency and gravity with which the court viewed the situation.

The Dissenting Voices in the Dark

The fact that Justices Sam Alito and Clarence Thomas were the only dissenters is notable, yet even more intriguing is the court's decision to proceed without waiting for Alito to publish his dissent. This is an extraordinary deviation from normative practice, suggesting a level of expediency and perhaps frustration not typically associated with the Supreme Court's modus operandi. Stern cleverly highlights this anomaly, underlining the rarity and significance of the court's decision to "rush out" its ruling.

A Signal of Judicial Independence?

Stern posits that the court's actions may signify a broader willingness to confront and counter the Trump administration's attempts to sidestep legal scrutiny and due process, especially in matters of immigration and deportation. This interpretation suggests that the Supreme Court, or at least a majority thereof, is not only aware of the administration's tactics but increasingly prepared to intervene.

The Implications for Trump and Beyond

The implications of this episode extend far beyond the immediate halt of a deportation flight. This could very well be a watershed moment in the court's relationship with the Trump administration, marking a newfound assertiveness in preserving the judiciary's role in the balance of power. Furthermore, the majority's decision to act with such urgency—effectively sidelining Alito's dissent in the process—could be interpreted as a strong rebuke of the administration's disregard for due process and legal norms.

A Victory for Migrants, A Defeat for Lawlessness

At the heart of this narrative is the human element—the migrants whose lives hang in the balance as legal and political titans clash. Stern's analysis rightly frames the court's decision as a substantial victory for these individuals, emphasizing the broader fight against what he terms Trump's "lawless effort" to expedite deportations without adequate legal scrutiny.

In conclusion, the Supreme Court's middle-of-the-night intervention is a remarkable deviation from business as usual, driven by a majority seemingly at its wit's end with the Trump administration's legal strategies. Stern's insights provide a compelling narrative of judicial independence and integrity in the face of executive overreach—a narrative that will undoubtedly continue to unfold as the court and the administration navigate the treacherous waters of legal and constitutional precedent. The message, it seems, is clear: even in the darkest hours, the law remains a beacon of accountability.
