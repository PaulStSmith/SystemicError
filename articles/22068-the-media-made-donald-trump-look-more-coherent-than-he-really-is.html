The media made Donald Trump look more coherent than he really is

Based on an article by The Conversation

Paulo SantosAugust 20, 20262 min read

Sanewashing Is Not the Main Crime

The Real Object of the Story

The source article is about how journalists described Trump’s incoherent September 2024 remarks, and whether that coverage softened the absurdity of what he actually said. It finds some evidence that mainstream coverage tended to emphasize the tidy parts of his speeches while treating the rambling parts as background noise. That is real enough. But the deeper problem is not diction. It is power.

Who Was Actually in Charge

Trump was not some confused spectator being misunderstood by the press. He was the candidate for the presidency, speaking from a position of prospective state power, and the reporting described in the source shows how easily that power can be laundered through normalizing language. Editors and reporters made choices about what counted as the “story,” but Trump and his political apparatus were the ones setting the terms. The press did not create the danger. It decided, too often, to talk around it.

The Comfort of Normalization

The article’s strongest example is also its most embarrassing one: a failed answer about childcare gets buried while the “efficient” sounding policy fragments get the headline treatment. That is not neutral filtering. It is a professional reflex to preserve coherence where none exists. Calling this “sanewashing” is apt, but incomplete. The press is not merely making Trump sound sane. It is helping convert incoherence into admissible politics, as if a rambling authoritarian can be processed like a conventional candidate if the grammar is smoothed out enough.

Biden Scrutiny, Trump Leniency

The source correctly notes the contrast with Biden, whose decline received intense scrutiny and helped push him out of the race. That comparison matters, but not in the lazy way pundits usually use it. The issue is not that one man was treated harshly and another gently. The issue is that the press could identify weakness in a Democrat and turn it into a governing crisis, while treating Trump’s instability as a style issue, a “jumbled and meandering answer,” a bit of campaign texture. One candidate’s frailty became a decisive narrative. The other candidate’s incoherence became a formatting problem.

What the Article Misses

The source eventually gestures toward the real stakes when it mentions the efficiency commission that became DOGE and the dismantling of USAID, with its forecast of 14 million excess deaths worldwide. That is the right direction, but it arrives late, almost apologetically, as if the press should first be forgiven for obsessing over oddities before being reminded that the oddities sit next to machinery of state destruction. This is the recurring failure: journalism treats the spectacle as the headline and the apparatus as a footnote.

The Larger Pattern

The pattern is not just sanewashing. It is normalization as a governing ideology of elite media. Institutions that are supposed to name power often settle for making it legible, and in the process they reduce deliberate political harm to personality quirks, communication glitches, or “meandering” speech. That is how authoritarian politics advances in plain sight: not only through lies, but through a press corps trained to translate danger into something that sounds manageably conventional.
