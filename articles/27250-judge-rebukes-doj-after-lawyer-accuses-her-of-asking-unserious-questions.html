Judge rebukes DOJ after lawyer accuses her of asking 'unserious questions'

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 14, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A presidential deadline becomes an official decisionThe safety emergency arrived lateThe court refused the administration’s preferred framing

The Justice Department conceded that President Donald Trump’s public demand helped determine when the federal government would close a Washington, D.C., golf course he wants renovated—after first portraying the matching date as coincidence. The admission came during a contentious hearing in which Principal Deputy Assistant Attorney General Adam R.F. Gustafson dismissed one of U.S. District Judge Ana Reyes’s questions as potentially unserious, earning an immediate rebuke.

The exchange mattered for more than courtroom etiquette. Reyes was testing whether the administration had manufactured the conditions it now cited to justify urgent action, then wrapped a presidential directive in the language of public safety.

A presidential deadline becomes an official decision

Trump posted on Truth Social that the course had to close on Nov. 15. The government subsequently selected Nov. 15, while Gustafson initially maintained that the alignment was coincidental.

According to AlterNet’s account of live reporting by Lawfare’s Molly Roberts, the Justice Department eventually identified three connected considerations behind the date: alleged safety hazards, the impending absence of a course operator, and Trump’s statement calling for closure that day.

That concession stripped away the most convenient version of the government’s story. The date was not simply the neutral product of an administrative safety review. By the department’s own account, the president’s pronouncement was one of the factors shaping the decision.

The administration offered two principal justifications for shutting the course: the property was dangerous to the public, and nobody would remain to manage it because the government had terminated the operator’s contract. Reyes focused on the circularity of the second argument. The administration created the management problem and then invoked that problem as a reason for closure.

Her analogy was blunt: a person cannot set their own house on fire and then claim entitlement to the insurance proceeds. Gustafson said the situation was more complicated, but complexity does not erase agency. If the government ends a contract and thereby removes the operator, it cannot honestly present the resulting vacancy as an external emergency that simply befell it.

The safety emergency arrived late

The department’s safety rationale drew similar scrutiny. It argued that conditions around the island and its seawall posed risks, including the possibility that children could be injured. Yet, as Reyes noted, roughly 30,000 people had recently come through the area for a marathon.

That did not establish that the property was safe. It did expose a basic inconsistency in the government’s claimed urgency: officials allowed a mass public event near the alleged hazard, had identified concerns months earlier, and still waited until Trump announced a Nov. 15 closure to treat those concerns as grounds for emergency action.

Gustafson compared the situation to unsafe roads that governments improve as money becomes available. Reyes tested that logic with a hypothetical about a government building at risk of collapsing and killing somebody. Would officials leave such a building open despite the danger?

“Your Honor, I’m not sure that’s a serious question,” Gustafson replied.

“You can be assured it’s a serious question because I asked it, and I don’t ask unserious questions,” Reyes answered.

The rebuke was deserved, but the underlying exchange was more revealing than the discourtesy. Reyes was asking the Justice Department to define the risk standard it wanted the court to accept. Gustafson responded by questioning the legitimacy of the inquiry instead of resolving the contradiction.

When Reyes asked whether officials ordinarily address critical hazards in places used by thousands of people, the department answered that they do so “gradually.” She then observed that nothing had been addressed since May, when the case reportedly did not feature claims of imminent danger. The government’s position therefore depended on two descriptions at once: the hazards were serious enough to require a sudden closure, but not serious enough to prompt remedial work over the preceding months.

The court refused the administration’s preferred framing

The administration holds the relevant executive power here. It terminated the management contract, advanced the safety justification, selected the closure date and defended those choices in court. Trump supplied the public deadline. Treating the outcome as bureaucratic happenstance would obscure the chain of responsibility Reyes forced the government to acknowledge.

The available reporting does not establish that the safety concerns themselves are fabricated. It does show that the Justice Department struggled to explain why those concerns became urgent only when they aligned with Trump’s renovation plans and stated deadline. That is the central issue: not whether hazards can exist, but whether officials are using them consistently or opportunistically.

By the lunch break, the government had moved from insisting on coincidence to admitting that Trump’s Nov. 15 declaration helped determine the Nov. 15 closure. Reyes’s questions did not create that contradiction. They prevented the Justice Department from talking around it.
