Terror as red states wage quiet war against divorce: 'Most dangerous time'

Based on an article by Anna Claire Vollers, Stateline

The State Calls It Marriage. The Exit Looks Like a Trap.

A Killing at the End of a Legal Ordeal

Cerina Fairfax spent nearly two years sharing a home with the husband she was trying to divorce. Virginia required parents to wait a year before filing, and Justin Fairfax used additional legal tactics to prolong the case. After a judge awarded Cerina sole custody and ordered Justin out, police say he fatally shot her and then himself, days before his deadline to leave.

That sequence does not prove the waiting period caused her death. It does expose what divorce restrictions do: they extend the time during which a person seeking escape remains legally, financially, and sometimes physically entangled with someone who does not consent to the separation.

The Power Belongs to the Person Who Can Prevent an Exit

Opponents of no-fault divorce market obstruction as “accountability.” The word conceals the allocation of power. If one spouse wants to leave and the other can force a trial over adultery, abandonment or abuse, the law has not preserved mutual commitment. It has given the unwilling spouse leverage over the unwilling captive.

Mississippi already demonstrates the mechanism: one spouse’s objection can help turn divorce into years of litigation. Waiting periods, fault hearings and covenant marriages all impose different versions of the same rule—the state may require continued attachment after consent has been withdrawn.

That burden is especially dangerous in abusive relationships. Evidence rarely arrives in the courtroom-ready form lawmakers pretend it does. There may be no police report, medical record, photograph or cooperative witness. Requiring proof does not separate genuine abuse from false claims with scientific precision. It rewards whoever can better survive, finance and manipulate prolonged legal conflict.

“Family Values” Is the Misdirection

Republican lawmakers and party organizations in Iowa, Oklahoma, Texas, Nebraska, South Carolina and South Dakota have advanced proposals or platforms restricting no-fault divorce. Oklahoma state Sen. Dusty Deevers went further, pairing an abolition proposal with an expressed desire for public shaming of people deemed responsible for divorce.

This is not confusion about how to strengthen families. The available research cited in the reporting says state divorce rules are not an effective lever for controlling divorce rates. Divorce has declined nationally for decades, while several Republican-led states remain among those with the highest rates. The policy persists because its practical function is not to repair relationships. It is to make departure costlier.

Iowa state Sen. Jesse Green blamed the “circus” of no-fault divorce for marital breakdown and declining marriage. That framing erases the actor who abuses, intimidates or simply refuses to release a spouse, then assigns social disorder to the person seeking an exit. The trapped party becomes the offender; state coercion becomes moral support.

Legislators Are Choosing the Risk

The institutional power here sits with state lawmakers, judges and party organizations—not with the women describing their divorces on TikTok. Influencers such as Hadley Vlahos and Jen Hamilton can attract millions of followers and tens of thousands of petition signatures. They still cannot shorten a separation period, eliminate a fault requirement or prevent an abusive spouse from weaponizing procedure. Legislatures can.

Domestic-violence advocates told Iowa lawmakers exactly what additional barriers would mean: leaving is often the most dangerous stage, and forcing survivors to prove abuse can keep them exposed longer. Once lawmakers receive that warning, restrictions cannot honestly be described as an innocent effort to honor marriage. They are a decision to impose foreseeable danger on people whose safety depends on leaving.

Some states have chosen differently. Maryland adopted a purely no-fault system. Virginia created a narrow route to accelerate divorce and began studying whether to eliminate fault-based divorce. Missouri clarified that pregnancy cannot prevent a divorce from being finalized. These changes are limited, but they establish the central point: delay is not an unavoidable feature of marriage law. It is a policy choice.

Domestic Rule Is the Larger Project

The renewed campaign against no-fault divorce belongs beside state restrictions on abortion, contraception, fertility treatment and pregnancy outcomes. Each places intimate decisions under institutional supervision while presenting diminished autonomy as protection. The pattern is clearest where women’s freedom depends on permission—from a spouse, a judge, a prosecutor or a legislature.

No-fault divorce does not prevent counseling, reconciliation or durable marriage. It prevents one spouse from using the state to compel the other to litigate for release. That is why its opponents target it.

The political project is not merely to praise marriage. It is to redefine marriage as a status the government can make difficult to escape, even after safety and consent have collapsed. “Family stability” is the sales pitch. Enforceable dependency is the power underneath it.
