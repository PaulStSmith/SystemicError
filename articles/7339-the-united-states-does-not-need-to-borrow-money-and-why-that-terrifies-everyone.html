The United States Does Not Need to Borrow Money — And Why That Terrifies Everyone

A sentence that sounds like heresy

Let’s begin with a statement that sounds outrageous only because we have been trained, very carefully and for a very long time, to believe it must be false:

The United States does not need to borrow money in order to spend, service its debt, or keep its economy functioning.

That sentence alone is enough to short-circuit most political conversations. It immediately triggers images of wheelbarrows of cash, Weimar Germany, Zimbabwe, and reckless politicians drunk on the power of the printing press. And yet, stripped of ideology and examined mechanically, the statement is not radical at all. It is simply a description of how the U.S. monetary system actually works.

To understand this, we have to unlearn the single most damaging metaphor in modern economics: the idea that the federal government operates like a household.

Households must earn before they spend. Governments that issue their own currency do not.

This is the starting point of what I will refer to here as Murphy’s explanations, and once you see it clearly, the rest follows with uncomfortable logic.

Monetary sovereignty and how spending actually happens

The United States is monetarily sovereign. It issues the U.S. dollar. Its debts are denominated in that dollar. It has a central bank that clears all federal payments. No external entity can force it into insolvency in its own currency. These are not opinions. They are operational facts.

When Congress authorizes spending, the Treasury does not go looking for tax dollars in a vault somewhere. It instructs payments. The Federal Reserve marks up bank accounts. Money is created at the moment of spending. This is not metaphorical. It is literally how the system settles transactions.

Taxes, therefore, cannot be the source of that money. They occur after spending, not before. There would be no dollars available to pay taxes unless the government had already spent them into existence.

If taxes don’t fund spending, what do they do?

This is where Murphy’s explanations begin to collide head-on with popular intuition. If taxes do not fund federal spending, what do they do?

They do several things, all of them essential. They withdraw purchasing power from the economy, helping to control inflation. They create mandatory demand for the currency, giving the dollar its value. They shape behavior through incentives and penalties. And they redistribute economic power in ways that either stabilize or destabilize society, depending on how they are designed.

But what taxes do not do, at the federal level, is provide the government with money it needs in order to spend.

Why the U.S. issues debt at all

At this point, an obvious question arises: if the government can create dollars, why does it borrow them at all?

The answer is subtle, and it is where much of the confusion originates.

U.S. government borrowing does not exist to finance spending. It exists to manage the macroeconomy. Treasury bonds convert liquid dollars into interest-bearing assets. They remove spending power from circulation without requiring tax increases. They provide safe assets for pensions, banks, and foreign governments. And they help the Federal Reserve maintain control over interest rates.

In other words, government debt is not a sign of fiscal weakness. It is a policy tool. It is closer to a thermostat than a credit card.

Could the United States simply pay off its debt?

Once this is understood, another provocative conclusion becomes unavoidable: yes, the United States could technically pay off all of its outstanding debt without borrowing a single cent.

Treasury securities mature. The government instructs the Fed to credit accounts. The debt disappears.

So why doesn’t it do this?

Because this is where your intuition about inflation is exactly right.

If trillions of dollars currently locked away in Treasury securities were suddenly released as liquid cash, the result would not be neutral. Demand would surge. Asset prices would spike. Inflationary pressure would ripple through the economy. Financial markets would destabilize.

The problem would not be the government’s ability to do it. The problem would be the consequences of doing it without offsetting measures.

Inflation is about capacity, not keystrokes

This is why the phrase “just print money” is both technically correct and profoundly misleading.

Inflation is not caused by money creation alone. It is caused when demand outstrips real productive capacity. That capacity includes labor, energy, housing, food, infrastructure, and supply chains. When those resources are scarce, additional demand bids up prices.

If the government creates money to mobilize unused capacity — idle workers, underutilized factories, neglected infrastructure — inflationary pressure can be limited or even nonexistent. If it creates money into an economy already operating at its limits, inflation follows.

Same mechanism. Different context. Radically different outcome.

This is the part of Murphy’s explanations that most directly undermines austerity politics. The real constraint on government spending is not money. It is resources.

Taxation as a stabilizer, not a punishment

Which brings us back to taxation.

If government spending injects demand into the economy, something must remove demand to keep prices stable. Taxes are the primary democratic mechanism for doing so. They are not punishment for success. They are instruments of macroeconomic regulation.

This also explains why chronic under-taxation of the wealthy is not just unjust but dangerous. When large concentrations of purchasing power are allowed to accumulate unchecked, demand becomes distorted. Asset bubbles inflate. Housing, healthcare, and education become unaffordable. Political instability follows.

The problem is not tax. The problem is tax design.

Why this understanding is so threatening

So why does this understanding provoke such resistance?

Because if the public grasps that the United States cannot run out of money, that federal debt is not a solvency risk, and that taxes do not fund spending, then austerity collapses as a moral argument. What remains is an honest political debate about priorities.

Who gets resources? Who bears inflation risk? Who benefits from public investment?

Those are questions of power, not accounting. And that is precisely why decades of political effort have been spent teaching people the wrong story.

The lie that refuses to die

The household-budget analogy survives because it is useful. It justifies cuts to public services, blocks universal healthcare, and frames inequality as unavoidable. All while the government quietly rescues banks, backstops markets, and subsidizes corporations whenever the system wobbles.

This is not fiscal responsibility. It is selective constraint.

The only question that actually matters

The United States does not face a financial limit on what it can do. It faces physical limits, ecological limits, human limits, and political limits.

Money is a tool. Taxes and bonds are control mechanisms. Inflation is a signal, not a punishment.

Murphy’s explanations strip away the mysticism and leave us with an uncomfortable but clarifying truth:

The real question is not whether we can afford something.

The real question is whether we have the resources — and who we choose to serve.
