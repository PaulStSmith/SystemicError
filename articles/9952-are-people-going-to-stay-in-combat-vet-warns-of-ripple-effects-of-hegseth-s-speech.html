'Are people going to stay in?' Combat vet warns of 'ripple effects' of Hegseth's speech

A combat veteran had a sharp warning after Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth‘s comments about women in the military and suggested “ripple effects” would come after his big speech, asking, “Are people going to stay in?”

Major Kyleanne, a retired combat veteran and CEO of the Iraq and Afghanistan Veterans of America, told CNN anchors Brianna Keilar and Boris Sanchez that using a “culture war” is “distracting from real military readiness.” Hegseth’s comments could also impact recruiting — a top priority.

“There’s real concerns about recruiting, these big recruiting booms that we’ve seen are largely driven by women joining the military,” she said. “But there’s also a lot of concerns about retention. Are people going to stay in if that’s what’s going to happen? But also what it does to our veteran population.”

Hunter also pointed to veteran mental health as a major concern. She suggested that Hegseth’s comments could have a negative message for struggling veterans.

“We are hearing from veterans, men and women alike, that remarks like this have a real impact on veteran mental health and how they feel about their own services, you know, their own service. And were they actually worthy to be in the service? And so, you know, while there’s a focus on the active duty right now, we absolutely need to be focused on that aspect,” she added. “We also need to be looking at what are the ripple effects of this rhetoric due to the mental health of our veteran population, as well.”

She pointed to current Pentagon changes as concerning, especially for women in the military.

“I have deep concerns about the shutting down of the Defense Advisory Committee on Women in the Services,” or DACOWITS, she said.

“I had the honor of serving on DACOWITS twice. And what I can say is that the recommendations that we made for women in the services benefited everyone, and increased military readiness increased the authority and increased unit cohesion, whether it was on ensuring there was properly fitting gear and equipment for everyone who goes downrange into harm’s way, or ensuring that there is adequate familial support so people retain and stay in the military. These are our actions that impact everyone. And I think with the loss of the Defense Advisory Committee on Women’s Services and with these statements that, you know, show that women may not be adhering to the same standards, which is just blatantly not true.”

She also argued that merit-based, skill level and achievement are important in the military, not implicit or explicit bias.

“I think it’s unfortunate when statements like this get made because it leads to division,” she said.

It should be a meritocracy, she explained, and initiatives under DACOWITS removed pronouns from fitness reports or officer qualification records so people would be promoted based on their skills, achievements, and merit.

“And I will say from my time in the military, I had the opportunity that I was judged objectively against my peers. And where I succeeded, I succeeded all my merits and where I fell short, I fell short of my own shortcomings.”

The military has tried to make it more common for women to enter the military. But Hegseth’s words were the opposite.

“And so the very claims that they’re wanting, if they want a better meritocracy system, they are dismantling the offices and the programs that made it a meritocracy to begin with, which is a little counterintuitive in my mind,” Hunter said.

Our Analysis:

Dissecting the Discord: Hegseth’s Comments and Their Fallout

In a recent flashpoint of controversy, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s comments have ignited a firestorm of debate over the role of women in the military, with potential repercussions that ripple through recruitment, retention, and the mental health of veterans. Major Kyleanne Hunter, a retired combat veteran and CEO of the Iraq and Afghanistan Veterans of America, has emerged as a vocal critic, highlighting the multifaceted impact of Hegseth’s remarks. Let’s peel back the layers of this complex issue, examining the implications of Hegseth’s comments with the precision of a scalpel.

The Culture War Cannon

Hunter’s accusation that Hegseth is deploying a “culture war” as a smokescreen for issues impacting military readiness is a damning indictment. This tactic, while not new in the political playbook, is especially insidious when applied to the armed forces—a domain where cohesion and meritocracy should be sacrosanct. The real kicker? The potential erosion of military readiness due to these divisive comments. It’s as if the Defense Secretary has forgotten the cardinal rule of leadership: divide et impera only works if you’re the one outside the gates, not within.

Recruitment and Retention Repercussions

Hunter shines a spotlight on a critical concern: the impact on recruitment and retention, particularly among women, who have been a driving force behind recent “recruiting booms.” The irony is as rich as it is tragic. At a time when the military faces challenges in filling its ranks, biting the hand that volunteers could not be more counterproductive. It begs the question: Is Hegseth’s stance a strategic misfire in the battle for the heart and soul of the military?

Veteran Mental Health: The Hidden Casualty

Perhaps the most poignant of Hunter’s points is the potential impact on veteran mental health. The suggestion that Hegseth’s comments could exacerbate feelings of unworthiness among veterans is a sobering reminder of the invisible wounds of war. It’s a stark warning that the battleground is not just physical but psychological, extending far beyond active duty into the very essence of those who have served.

The DACOWITS Dismantling: A Step Backwards

Hunter’s defense of the Defense Advisory Committee on Women in the Services (DACOWITS) highlights a crucial mechanism for positive change within the military. Her firsthand experience underscores the committee’s role in enhancing military readiness through initiatives that benefit all service members, not just women. The devaluation of such a committee, according to Hunter, reflects a broader trend of undermining progress towards a more inclusive and effective military.

Meritocracy or Myth?

The crux of Hunter’s argument is the call for a merit-based military, free from the shackles of bias—implicit or explicit. Hegseth’s actions, she argues, are at odds with this ideal, dismantling the very frameworks that promote meritocracy. This paradoxical stance undermines efforts to judge service members by their abilities and achievements rather than their gender. Hunter’s experience, judged on her merits and falling short on her own shortcomings, exemplifies the meritocratic ideal that should be the bedrock of military assessment.

Conclusion: A Call to Arms for Reason

In analyzing Major Kyleanne Hunter’s impassioned critique of Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s comments, it’s clear that the stakes are high. This is not just about the role of women in the military but about the foundational principles of military cohesion, readiness, and the health and well-being of those who serve. Hegseth’s remarks, as dissected by Hunter, serve as a clarion call for a return to reason, where meritocracy trumps division, and inclusivity strengthens rather than weakens our armed forces. The battle for the soul of the military is not won through divisive rhetoric but through unity, respect, and the recognition of merit—principles that should guide our armed forces into the future.
