'Victimhood!' GOP lawmaker loses it at disabled veterans in profane tirade

Based on an article by David Edwards

Paulo SantosAugust 18, 20262 min read

Veterans as Budget Furniture

The Power on the Floor

House Republicans controlled the bill, the floor, and the final decision to pull it. The measure was not some neutral act of gratitude toward veterans; it was a trade: expand one benefit by cutting disability ratings for tinnitus and sleep apnea, with House Speaker Mike Johnson later saying the whole thing would be delayed for weeks. The bill collapsed after opposition from the Veterans of Foreign Wars and a near loss on a Democratic effort to force revisions.

The Real Financing Scheme

The core story is not “a veterans bill got stuck.” It is that Republicans tried to pay for political praise with a cut to existing benefits. The Major Richard Star Act would have increased retirement pay for about 50,000 combat-injured veterans, but the offset would have hit up to 1.5 million veterans and saved about $57 billion over a decade. That is not fiscal restraint. It is an engineered transfer: take from a broader class of disabled veterans to flatter a narrower, more politically useful one.

Crenshaw’s Contempt

Rep. Dan Crenshaw did not just defend the bill. He attacked the people and organizations resisting it, calling the VFW “self-serving” and claiming disability benefits were “encouraging victimhood.” That is the rhetoric of discipline, not representation. Crenshaw was not describing a policy disagreement; he was trying to shame wounded veterans into accepting a cut. His complaint about conditions like sleep apnea and tinnitus is the giveaway. The target is not waste. The target is legitimacy itself: who gets to count as injured, and who gets told to stop asking.

The Misdirection Game

The blame-shifting here is lazy and deliberate. Crenshaw framed the VFW as the obstacle, as if a veterans’ organization forcing a bad bill into the open were the problem. It was not. The problem was a Republican bill that depended on clawing back benefits from disabled veterans to finance its headline item. When that bargain got exposed, Republicans did what they always do when the invoice becomes visible: they blamed the people who refused to sign it.

Veterans as Political Cover

This is the familiar pattern. Politicians wrap themselves in military reverence, then treat actual veterans as a budget category to be squeezed, sorted, and moralized. The language of “victimhood” is especially revealing because it recasts injury as weakness and public obligation as indulgence. That is how austerity sells itself: by turning beneficiaries into suspects and cruelty into seriousness.

The Larger Lesson

The story is not about one bill or one profane post. It is about a governing style that wants the prestige of supporting veterans without paying the cost of supporting them. It wants applause for honoring service while cutting the people who bore the consequences. That contradiction is not accidental. It is the structure.
