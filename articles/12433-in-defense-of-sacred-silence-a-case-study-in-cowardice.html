In Defense of Sacred Silence: A Case Study in Cowardice

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 24, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There are moments in public discourse when you come face-to-face with something so grotesque, so deeply unjust, that it shakes you. Not because it's new—but because it has been accepted for so long that people now defend it without flinching. One such moment came during a public debate about Washington State's Senate Bill 5375, a law that sought to make clergy mandatory reporters of child abuse, even when the abuse was confessed in the sanctity of a confessional booth.

The law was clear. It added members of the clergy to the list of mandatory reporters under Washington law, removing the long-held religious exemption that allowed priests and other religious leaders to remain silent if a child rapist confessed their crime during a religious rite. And for the first time, Washington dared to say that no ritual—no matter how sacred—should shield a predator.

Predictably, the backlash came swiftly. Catholic bishops sued. The Trump-aligned Department of Justice intervened, cloaking their defense of silence in the language of "religious liberty." And soon after, a federal judge blocked the law, issuing an injunction that argued the state had failed to prove why clergy were treated differently from others, like attorneys and supervised employees.

But the real rot wasn't just in the legal challenge. It was in the rhetoric of those defending the confessional loophole.

They insisted that the law was unfairly targeting religious practice. That priests, unlike teachers or nurses, should not be forced to betray sacred confidences. That the state was overstepping. And that, somehow, asking a religious leader to report a child rape was a violation of religious freedom.

The justifications were polished. The tone was condescending. But the message, stripped of its ceremonial robes, was simple: protect the institution, not the child.

Let’s be absolutely clear: An abuser who admits guilt in a religious setting is still describing a crime. And when that confession is met with silence—when it ends with nothing more than a whispered prayer and a pat on the head—it allows that crime to continue. It protects the predator. It dooms future victims to the same fate.

And if a child confesses that they are being abused, the tragedy deepens. Because unlike a therapist or a mandated reporter, a priest—bound by the seal of confession—can do nothing. Not even to protect that child from further harm. The ritual demands silence, even in the face of a child's suffering.

This isn’t theoretical. The Catholic Church alone has paid out billions of dollars in settlements to abuse survivors. Entire dioceses have filed for bankruptcy. And still, there are people who think the problem is the law that demands accountability—not the culture of silence that enabled generations of systemic abuse.

What was most galling in this debate wasn’t the disagreement itself. Reasonable people can differ on policy. But this wasn’t policy. This was denial. Gaslighting. A refusal to acknowledge what the law actually said, what the Church actually fought for, and what children actually endure.

Over and over, defenders insisted the law didn't really target confession. They misquoted it, mischaracterized it, and ignored the parts that plainly stated clergy were required to report regardless of how they learned about the abuse. They pointed fingers at other countries. They deflected with irrelevant statistics. They called outrage "irrational."

But outrage is rational when the subject is institutional complicity in child rape.

And what these defenders revealed, again and again, was not a commitment to faith—but a commitment to power. To tradition. To a system that views clerical authority as something more important than a child's safety.

Let’s strip it down:

  • If a therapist hears about abuse, they report it.

  • If a teacher hears about abuse, they report it.

  • If a doctor hears about abuse, they report it.

  • But if a priest hears about it in a wooden box with a cross on top? Suddenly, silence is sacred.

This isn’t just a legal inconsistency. It's a moral absurdity.

Privilege in secular law exists to serve secular needs:

  • A therapist keeps things private to foster treatment, but can break confidentiality to prevent harm.

  • A lawyer protects communication to enable a fair trial, but loses that shield if the client is committing a future crime.

  • A priest? He offers imaginary absolution. No justice. No treatment. No defense. Just a spiritual blank check signed in incense.

The clergy-penitent privilege serves no secular utility. It does not help the victim. It does not help the accused navigate due process. It does not prevent future harm. It is purely theological—a ritualized loophole designed to comfort the predator and protect the institution.

It exists not to serve society, but to serve the Church.

The purpose of SB 5375 was not to attack religion. It was to end an exemption that has shielded monsters for generations. The law didn’t single out the Church—it held it to the same standard we expect from everyone else.

And the reaction to it? The legal wrangling, the rhetorical gymnastics, the smug dismissals of moral outrage? They weren’t just a defense of tradition. They were a defense of silence.

Let this be said plainly, for the record, and for history:

Religious freedom does not mean freedom to cover up rape. The confessional seal is not more sacred than a child's life. And any institution, doctrine, or defender that says otherwise is standing on the wrong side of justice.

Sacred silence has protected predators long enough.

We're done whispering.
