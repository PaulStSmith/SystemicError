Gold Star family ‘devastated’ after Hegseth gives hope – then goes 'ghost': report

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 10, 20263 min read

The Advocate Who Vanished

A Familiar Performance

A Gold Star family asked for answers about Cmdr. Job Price’s 2012 death. Pete Hegseth, then a Fox News host and now Defense Secretary, briefly sounded like a champion for them. He called the evidence “damning,” rejected the suicide story as implausible, and suggested the Navy had failed the case. Then he stopped answering.

That is the whole political shape of the story: outrage without obligation, sympathy without accountability, and a public persona that evaporates the moment it would require institutional action.

Who Had Power

The family did not. The dead officer did not. A friend with text messages did not. The person with leverage was Hegseth, first as a media figure with access and later as the civilian head of the Pentagon. He was the one who could elevate the case, demand a review, or simply keep pressure on the institution he now runs.

That matters because this was never just a private exchange of condolences. Hegseth was not an outside observer discovering a tragedy. He was building a political identity around military loyalty, then inheriting the very institution he had criticized.

The Convenient Silence

The Post’s account shows the pattern clearly: Hegseth expressed interest, promised help, then let the issue die in the inbox. The family says they have seen no indication the case will be reopened, even after he became Defense Secretary in an administration that advertises itself as pro-military.

That is not confusion. It is drift in one direction only: toward inaction. When a powerful official loudly validates a grievance and then disappears, the effect is not neutral. It leaves the people harmed holding the emotional bill while the official keeps the political benefit.

Misdirection as a Career Strategy

This story also exposes how easy it is for elite actors to weaponize suspicion without ever carrying it through. Hegseth could signal distrust of the Navy’s findings, gain credibility with military families, and position himself as a truth-teller. Once the spotlight moved on, the obligation dissolved.

That is a common political scam: perform indignation against a bureaucracy, collect the reputation for courage, and avoid the work of actual oversight. The family gets hope. The institution gets a theatrically skeptical critic who never has to prove anything. The performer gets to move up.

The Real Lesson

The larger pattern here is institutional cruelty wrapped in patriotic branding. A family searching for answers about a service member’s death was met first by a troubled investigation, then by a powerful man who used their grief as a credential and later abandoned them.

This is how political legitimacy gets laundered in plain sight. The same figures who advertise themselves as defenders of troops and families often treat those people as useful props until real responsibility arrives. Then the messages stop, the promises thin out, and the machinery of power protects itself as usual.
