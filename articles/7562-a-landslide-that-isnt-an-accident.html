A Landslide That Isn’t an Accident

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 17, 20254 min read

When Newsweek reports that a Democrat just won a Kentucky state senate race by nearly fifty points, the reflex reaction is predictable. Either it’s framed as a meaningless fluke—“deep‑blue district, low turnout, move along”—or as breathless evidence that Kentucky is about to flip en masse. Both takes miss the point. Badly.

What happened in Kentucky’s Senate District 37 is neither magic nor trivia. It’s a warning flare.

Gary Clemons, a union leader and U.S. Army veteran, didn’t just win. He annihilated his Republican opponent, Calvin Leach, 72.6 percent to 25.1 percent, with a Libertarian scraping together the leftovers. That’s roughly a 47‑point margin in a state Republicans like to treat as permanently theirs.

Yes, context matters. This was a December special election. Turnout was anemic—about seven percent. And the district, in south Louisville, has leaned Democratic for years. No serious analyst should pretend otherwise.

But context doesn’t absolve Republicans here. It indicts them.

Low turnout cuts both ways

Low‑turnout elections are often waved away as statistical weirdness. That’s lazy thinking. Low turnout doesn’t erase political meaning; it concentrates it.

When only the most motivated voters show up, what you’re measuring isn’t persuasion—it’s enthusiasm, discipline, and organizational muscle. And once again, Democrats had it. Republicans didn’t.

Consider the benchmarks. The outgoing Democrat, David Yates, beat the same Republican by about 20 points in 2024. Kamala Harris carried the area by only six points in the presidential election that same year. Clemons didn’t just exceed those results. He obliterated them.

That gap matters. It tells us something important about who still believes voting is worth the effort—and who’s drifting into political apathy or internal exhaustion.

A party that dominates culturally but can’t mobilize locally is not a healthy party. It’s a hollow one.

Candidate quality still matters—Republicans forgot that

Gary Clemons isn’t a TikTok celebrity or a national brand. He’s a local union president. A veteran. Someone whose biography makes sense to the voters he represents.

That shouldn’t be remarkable. It used to be normal.

But modern Republican strategy has increasingly replaced local credibility with national grievance. Culture‑war performance substitutes for policy. Loyalty tests replace competence. And candidates are chosen for their ability to provoke rather than persuade.

Labor politics—real labor, not podcast rhetoric—still works in working‑class districts. Democrats, imperfect as they are, continue to field candidates who can talk about jobs, wages, and dignity without sounding like they’re reading from a donor memo.

Republicans abandoned that terrain years ago. They traded shop floors for outrage cycles. This is the result.

Andy Beshear isn’t bragging—he’s stating a trend

Kentucky Governor Andy Beshear’s reaction to the race was telling. He didn’t frame Clemons’ win as a one‑off. He called it part of a broader pattern: Democrats consistently outperforming expectations in special elections.

That pattern didn’t start yesterday. It’s been visible since 2022, across states, offices, and issues. Democrats keep winning contests that models say should be closer—or even Republican‑leaning—especially when abortion, labor, or basic governance competence is anywhere near the ballot.

Republicans know this. Which is why they’re not responding with better candidates or broader appeals.

They’re responding with maps.

Mid‑decade redistricting is not confidence—it’s fear

The most revealing line in the Newsweek piece isn’t about Kentucky at all. It’s the warning that Republican pushes for mid‑decade redistricting could create “real tension” for incumbents through the rest of the 2020s.

Redistricting is supposed to follow censuses, not panic attacks.

When a party redraws maps mid‑cycle, it’s admitting something fundamental: it no longer trusts voters to endorse its agenda. Gerrymandering is a defensive crouch, not a show of strength. It’s the political equivalent of boarding up windows before the storm hits.

And storms are coming.

Low‑turnout elections, special elections, and off‑year contests are stress tests. They reveal which coalition is expanding and which is shrinking. Right now, Republicans are failing those tests with disturbing consistency.

This isn’t about Kentucky turning blue

Let’s be precise. This race does not mean Kentucky is suddenly a Democratic stronghold. Anyone selling that story is chasing clicks, not truth.

What it does mean is more uncomfortable for Republicans: their coalition is aging, their enthusiasm is fragile, and their strategy increasingly relies on voter suppression and structural advantage rather than persuasion.

Democrats don’t need to convert every voter. They don’t even need a sweeping ideological shift. They just need to show up, nominate adults, and let Republicans keep over‑performing on television while under‑performing at the polls.

That’s not a revolution. It’s attrition.

The real lesson

The Kentucky landslide isn’t an accident, and it isn’t a fluke. It’s a symptom of a deeper imbalance.

Republicans have built a movement optimized for outrage, not turnout. For loyalty, not legitimacy. For cultural dominance, not democratic consent.

Democrats, flawed and frustrating as they often are, are winning not because they’re radical—but because they’re still capable of functioning as a political party.

Maps can delay that reckoning. They can’t prevent it.

And when a party has to keep redrawing the lines instead of widening the tent, the problem isn’t geography. It’s decay.
