America Doesn’t Need Five More Joe Manchins

Elect more Joe Manchins.

Not literally, of course. There is, thankfully, only one Joe Manchin. But the former West Virginia senator has enthusiastically endorsed Matthew McConaughey’s proposal to elect a small group of independents to Congress, where they could hold the balance of power between Democrats and Republicans.

McConaughey imagines three to five independent lawmakers compelling the two major parties to negotiate, compromise, and rediscover the sensible political center.

Manchin thinks this is wonderful.

Of course he does.

He spent much of Joe Biden’s presidency demonstrating exactly how much political power one obstinate senator could accumulate when his vote became indispensable.

And apparently, after watching that experiment play out, his conclusion is that America needs more of it.

You really couldn’t invent a better political satire.

Meet the Man Who Made Himself Indispensable

A little history is in order.

Joe Manchin was elected to the United States Senate as a Democrat in 2010 and reelected in 2012 and 2018.

He wasn’t elected alongside Joe Biden in 2020. But when Biden entered the White House in January 2021, Manchin found himself occupying an extraordinarily advantageous position.

The Senate was divided 50–50.

Democrats controlled the chamber through Vice President Kamala Harris’s tie-breaking vote.

And Republicans were generally united in their opposition to Biden’s major legislative priorities.

That meant any Democratic senator could potentially derail legislation requiring every member of the caucus to vote yes.

Manchin understood his position.

He understood it very well.

Over the following two years, he became one of the most powerful figures in Washington—not because he commanded a large coalition of lawmakers, but because the governing coalition could not afford to lose him.

Consider what that means.

Millions of Americans had voted for Biden’s agenda. Democrats controlled the presidency and both chambers of Congress. Yet entire portions of that agenda could be stopped by a single senator from a state with roughly 1.8 million residents.

To be clear, Manchin had every legal right to disagree with his party.

A senator isn’t a presidential employee. Political parties aren’t supposed to operate like military organizations demanding unquestioning obedience.

But that isn’t the criticism.

The criticism is that a system designed to represent millions of people allowed one politician to acquire an effective veto over policies supported by far larger constituencies.

And rather than recognizing that concentration of power as a democratic deficiency, Manchin now seems to regard it as a governing model worthy of replication.

Exhibit A: The Build Back Better Debacle

Remember Build Back Better?

Biden’s signature domestic policy initiative proposed major investments in childcare, healthcare, education, climate policy, and assistance for working families.

For months, Democrats negotiated the legislation.

They reduced its proposed spending. They removed provisions. They adjusted benefits, eligibility requirements, and implementation timelines.

And throughout those negotiations, Joe Manchin occupied the most valuable position in Washington.

He was the indispensable vote.

He could demand concessions that other senators could only dream of obtaining. He could insist that legislation be rewritten to satisfy his concerns. And because Republicans were united against the proposal, Democrats had little choice but to negotiate with him.

Now, there is nothing inherently improper about a senator negotiating legislation.

That’s part of the job.

But negotiations are supposed to produce agreements—or, at the very least, clear explanations of why agreements cannot be reached.

They’re not supposed to become an endless obstacle course in which the finish line keeps moving whenever the participants get close.

And then came December 2021.

On December 14, Manchin personally presented President Biden with a written legislative proposal.

Two days later, on December 16, Biden publicly indicated that Manchin had reaffirmed support for spending at approximately the level of the president’s revised framework. Negotiations were continuing, although a Senate vote had already been postponed until the following year.

Then, on December 19, Manchin appeared on Fox News Sunday and announced that he could not support the legislation.

The White House reportedly received only about 30 minutes’ notice before his television appearance.

The administration’s response was extraordinary.

Press Secretary Jen Psaki accused Manchin of a “sudden and inexplicable reversal,” publicly challenging his account of the negotiations.

To be precise, Manchin had never unconditionally promised to vote for the final bill. He had also voiced concerns about inflation, spending, and the legislation’s structure long before December.

But that doesn’t erase the sequence.

Tuesday: present the president with a legislative proposal.

Thursday: the president publicly expresses confidence that negotiations can continue.

Sunday: announce on Fox News that the legislation is unacceptable.

Months of negotiations. Extensive concessions. A written proposal delivered directly to the president.

And the decisive announcement comes on national television, catching the administration off guard.

Manchin had every right to reject legislation he considered unacceptable.

But the manner of his rejection raises a legitimate question about accountability.

When one senator’s approval is indispensable, and that senator can negotiate for months before abruptly terminating the process, what incentive does the system provide for reaching an agreement rather than prolonging the negotiations?

We cannot establish from that sequence alone that Manchin deliberately prolonged negotiations because he enjoyed exercising power.

What we can establish is that his pivotal position allowed him to dominate the process, demand extensive changes, and ultimately reject the legislation anyway.

The original Build Back Better proposal collapsed.

Some of its provisions later survived in the much smaller Inflation Reduction Act.

Others, including major elements of Biden’s childcare and social policy agenda, did not.

Millions of Americans never received the proposed benefits in their original form.

And this is the part that should concern anyone listening to Manchin’s current enthusiasm for independent congressional kingmakers.

He didn’t merely demonstrate how one senator could negotiate a better bill. He demonstrated how one senator could become an obstacle that an entire governing majority could not bypass.

Now imagine five of them.

Five sets of demands.

Five competing definitions of fiscal responsibility.

Five opportunities to announce that, despite months of negotiations, the latest compromise still isn’t good enough.

And five politicians insisting that their ability to stop legislation is precisely what makes them indispensable to democracy.

The problem isn’t that politicians disagree.

The problem is designing a legislature in which withholding agreement can become a more powerful political instrument than building it.Exhibit B: Democracy Can Wait. The Filibuster Cannot.

Then there was voting rights.

During Biden’s presidency, Democrats attempted to advance federal legislation addressing voting access, election administration, and protections weakened by previous Supreme Court decisions.

Republicans opposed the proposals.

The Senate filibuster, with its effective 60-vote threshold for most legislation, created a formidable procedural obstacle.

Democrats considered changing Senate rules to allow voting-rights legislation to advance with a simple majority.

Manchin refused.

So did Senator Kyrsten Sinema.

On January 19, 2022, Manchin and Sinema joined all 50 Republican senators in a 52–48 vote preserving the existing procedural obstacle.

The proposed rules change failed.

The voting-rights legislation consequently remained blocked.

Manchin defended the filibuster as an institutional protection for minority-party rights and a mechanism encouraging bipartisan cooperation.

But there is an obvious weakness in that argument.

A procedural rule cannot force bipartisan cooperation when one party has decided that obstruction is more advantageous than negotiation.

It can merely give that party the procedural means to obstruct.

A minority’s legitimate right to participate in legislation is not necessarily a right to prevent the majority from legislating at all.

And protecting congressional procedure is not the same thing as protecting democracy.

Democratic institutions are supposed to serve the public.

The public does not exist to preserve every procedural convention adopted by the institutions.

The irony is especially striking because the legislation concerned voting rights—the mechanisms through which citizens exercise political power in the first place.

Manchin insisted that voting was fundamental to democracy.

But when Democrats attempted to overcome Republican opposition to voting-rights legislation, he prioritized preserving the filibuster.

He was entitled to that position.

Americans are equally entitled to judge its consequences.

Exhibit C: He Didn’t Block Everything. That’s Part of the Problem.

Now, intellectual honesty requires acknowledging something important.

Manchin did not spend Biden’s entire presidency voting against everything Democrats proposed.

He supported the American Rescue Plan in 2021.

He helped negotiate the bipartisan Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act, which passed the Senate with substantial Republican support.

And in July 2022, he reached an agreement with Senate Majority Leader Chuck Schumer that eventually produced the Inflation Reduction Act.

That legislation included approximately $370 billion in climate and energy investments, provisions permitting Medicare to negotiate prices for certain prescription drugs, and significant changes to corporate taxation.

These were meaningful achievements.

Manchin deserves credit for his role in making them possible.

But here’s what those achievements also demonstrate.

The legislative agenda advanced when Joe Manchin decided its terms were acceptable.

The original Build Back Better package could not pass.

The smaller Inflation Reduction Act could.

Not because an entirely different electorate had suddenly been consulted.

Not because voters had conducted another national referendum on childcare, climate policy, or corporate taxation.

But because the legislative package had been negotiated into a form Manchin was willing to support.

That is how the Senate’s arithmetic worked.

His vote was indispensable, and therefore his preferences carried extraordinary weight.

His successful negotiations were real achievements. They also illustrate how much control a pivotal legislator can exercise over the final content of national policy.

And now he wants America to embrace a political arrangement in which several additional lawmakers might enjoy similar leverage.

What could possibly go wrong?

The Great Independent Rebranding

In May 2024, Manchin announced that he was leaving the Democratic Party and registering as an independent.

He complained that the country’s political system had become dysfunctional and that both major parties were failing to find common ground.

He nevertheless continued caucusing with Democrats for the remainder of his Senate service.

There is something revealing about this transition.

For years, Manchin benefited from the institutional infrastructure of the Democratic Party.

He won elections as a Democrat.

He received committee assignments and influence through his place in the Senate Democratic Caucus.

He exercised decisive leverage precisely because Democrats needed his vote to advance legislation.

And when his Senate career was approaching its conclusion, he left the party and embraced the language of political independence.

That was his right.

But independence is not a cleansing ceremony.

Changing the letter next to a politician’s name doesn’t erase their record.

It doesn’t retroactively transform obstruction into statesmanship.

It doesn’t prove that their judgment is uniquely objective.

And it certainly doesn’t establish that they have discovered some enlightened political philosophy inaccessible to ordinary partisans.

Joe Manchin didn’t suddenly become independent of political power. He changed the political identity through which he proposed exercising it.

That distinction matters.

Enter Matthew McConaughey

Fast-forward to October 2026.

Matthew McConaughey releases a video encouraging Americans to consider independent candidates in the upcoming midterm elections.

He argues that independents are underrepresented and that a small number of independent lawmakers could force Democrats and Republicans to negotiate.

He references Manchin’s Independent Leadership Council while saying he isn’t formally endorsing an organization.

Then Manchin appears on NewsNation and enthusiastically praises McConaughey’s argument.

On October 20, The Hill reported Manchin describing McConaughey as “absolutely so accurate.”

Manchin explained that the major parties would have to work with independent lawmakers rather than simply overpower them.

And there it is.

The entire proposal.

A few lawmakers positioned between the major parties, possessing enough leverage to demand concessions before legislation can advance.

In other words:

The Joe Manchin Experience, now available in a congressional multipack.

McConaughey sees independence as a way to make government more responsive.

Manchin sees independents as essential negotiating partners.

But we’ve already witnessed what happens when a politician holds the decisive vote in a narrowly divided legislature.

Sometimes that politician helps broker important legislation.

Sometimes they stop important legislation.

Sometimes they spend months determining which provisions survive, which disappear, and which are rewritten to satisfy their personal priorities.

The system does not guarantee that the resulting compromises reflect what most Americans want.

It guarantees that the politicians holding the pivotal votes can demand extraordinary influence.

That may be ordinary legislative bargaining.

But it isn’t inherently better government.

And multiplying the number of potential veto players does not automatically solve the underlying problem.

The Tollbooth Theory of Government

Imagine a highway.

Millions of people need to travel along it.

The government builds the road, maintains the bridges, establishes the traffic rules, and ensures everyone has access.

Then someone proposes installing five privately operated tollbooths along the route.

The operators aren’t responsible for the whole highway.

They don’t have to agree on where people should travel.

They don’t even have to share the same priorities.

But they can decide whether traffic gets through.

The proposal’s supporters announce that this arrangement will improve transportation because everyone will have to negotiate with the tollbooth operators.

Ridiculous?

Perhaps.

But the metaphor captures the problem with treating a handful of pivotal legislators as a cure for political dysfunction.

Their leverage can be useful when it produces reasonable compromise.

It can be destructive when it enables obstruction or disproportionate demands.

And if five independent lawmakers disagree with one another, the number of possible obstacles may increase rather than decrease.

The crucial question is not whether these politicians identify as independent.

It’s whether the institutional arrangement produces accountable, effective, representative government.

An independent politician is not automatically a neutral referee. They’re another political actor with interests, priorities, and ambitions.

They may be principled.

They may be cynical.

They may be competent.

They may be spectacularly unqualified.

Their party registration tells us remarkably little about which possibility we’re dealing with.

The Middle Is Not a Moral Destination

Both McConaughey and Manchin appeal to a familiar American fantasy.

Somewhere between the two major parties, supposedly, lies a politically enlightened center where common sense reigns and everyone can finally get along.

It’s a comforting story.

It’s also a poor substitute for evaluating actual policy.

Suppose one group proposes expanding access to healthcare.

Another opposes the expansion entirely.

A compromise might be possible. It might even be desirable.

But that depends on what the proposal costs, how it works, what benefits it provides, and which alternatives are available.

It doesn’t become good policy simply because it occupies the midpoint between two initial negotiating positions.

Now consider a question involving constitutional protections.

One faction supports a particular civil liberty.

Another wants to curtail it.

Does compromise automatically improve the result?

Of course not.

The correct answer depends on constitutional law, evidence, competing rights, and the consequences of the proposed restriction.

The midpoint between two political positions has no special claim to truth or justice.

Sometimes compromise is the best available solution.

Sometimes it produces a disastrous half-measure.

Sometimes refusing to compromise is the responsible decision.

And sometimes politicians use the language of moderation to present their own preferences as inherently more rational than everyone else’s.

Manchin’s career provides ample reason to examine that last possibility.

He frequently cast himself as a defender of common sense and bipartisan cooperation.

Yet his positions repeatedly determined whether major legislation could proceed.

That wasn’t simply moderation.

It was power.

The Republicans Got Fifty Votes. Manchin Supplied the Fifty-First Obstacle.

One point deserves particular emphasis.

Manchin did not personally create Republican opposition to Biden’s agenda.

Fifty Republican senators made their own decisions.

They voted against legislation.

They defended the filibuster.

They pursued their party’s priorities.

Those choices belong to them.

But in a 50–50 Senate, Republican unanimity and Manchin’s refusal to support particular Democratic proposals combined to make some legislation impossible.

This is why focusing exclusively on Manchin would be misleading.

He wasn’t the only source of obstruction.

He was the indispensable additional obstacle in certain votes.

And that made his cooperation—or refusal to cooperate—extraordinarily consequential.

The irony is that McConaughey now proposes expanding precisely the kind of pivotal leverage that enabled this dynamic.

A few independents might become indispensable to Democrats.

Or indispensable to Republicans.

Or alternate between the two depending on the legislation.

Their political power would not necessarily derive from representing a large proportion of the electorate.

It could arise simply from where their seats happened to fall in a closely divided Congress.

That’s arithmetic, not political enlightenment.

And it certainly isn’t a guarantee of democratic accountability.

Spoiler Candidates First. Kingmakers Later.

There is another problem.

Before McConaughey’s envisioned independent lawmakers can negotiate in Congress, they have to get elected.

And American congressional elections are not designed to distribute seats proportionally among competing political movements.

Most use single-member districts and plurality voting.

The candidate with the most votes wins.

A third-party or independent candidate can therefore attract significant support without winning a seat.

Worse, an independent candidate can draw enough votes from a competitive major-party candidate to change the winner.

That possibility is not inevitable in every race.

It depends on voter preferences, candidate viability, turnout, and the distribution of support.

But it is real.

Consider an illustrative election:

CandidateVote shareRepublican46%Democrat45%Independent9%

The Republican wins.

If most independent voters would otherwise have chosen the Democrat, the independent candidacy may have helped produce an outcome contrary to those voters’ preferences.

If the independent instead attracts mostly Republican voters, the effect may be reversed.

And if the independent has a genuine path to victory, the calculations change again.

This is why blanket appeals to support independent candidates are so irresponsible.

A candidate can be personally admirable and electorally counterproductive at the same time.

The existence of a third option does not guarantee that choosing it produces a better outcome.

In October 2026, this is not some obscure theoretical issue.

Control of Congress will determine legislative priorities, committee leadership, oversight powers, and the capacity of lawmakers to scrutinize executive actions.

Those consequences are real.

McConaughey’s rhetoric about political independence doesn’t make them disappear.

And Manchin’s enthusiasm doesn’t provide a solution.

The Reform They Keep Skipping

There is a serious argument for reducing America’s dependence on two major political parties.

We made that argument in our previous article, Alright, Alright, Alright… But Learn How Elections Work, Matthew.

Genuine electoral reform could give voters more meaningful options while reducing the danger of spoiler outcomes.

Ranked-choice voting could help voters express preferences without necessarily abandoning their next-best viable candidate.

Multi-member districts with proportional representation could give smaller political movements opportunities to win legislative seats roughly reflecting their public support.

Fairer ballot-access rules could reduce unnecessary barriers facing new political organizations.

These reforms would not automatically produce enlightened lawmakers or harmonious government.

No voting system can guarantee political wisdom.

But they would change the incentives that currently discourage voters from supporting candidates outside the two major parties.

And unlike a strategy centered on a few pivotal personalities, structural reform would benefit future voters regardless of which politicians happened to occupy the seats.

That is the difference between reforming a political institution and merely changing who gets to exploit its weaknesses.

If your solution to excessive political leverage is to create more politicians with excessive political leverage, you haven’t solved the problem. You’ve franchised it.

The Manchin Paradox

Here is what makes Manchin’s endorsement of McConaughey’s proposal so spectacularly ironic.

He spent much of Biden’s presidency arguing that his resistance to Democratic legislative priorities was necessary to ensure responsible government.

He presented himself as the voice of fiscal restraint, moderation, and common sense in a Congress supposedly dominated by ideological extremes.

And because Democrats could not afford to lose his vote, they had to listen.

They negotiated.

They compromised.

They reduced their ambitions.

They rewrote legislation to accommodate his objections.

And when they thought they were finally approaching an agreement, Manchin could simply announce that it wasn’t good enough.

His handling of Build Back Better illustrated this dynamic with almost theatrical precision.

After months of negotiations, extensive concessions, and a written proposal delivered personally to President Biden, Manchin appeared on Fox News Sunday and declared that he could not support the legislation.

The White House had reportedly received barely half an hour’s warning.

Biden had publicly expressed confidence that negotiations could continue just three days earlier.

Manchin had never unconditionally promised his vote, and his concerns about spending and inflation were hardly secret. But the extraordinary sequence exposed something much more troubling than an ordinary policy disagreement.

Democrats had spent months negotiating with a senator whose approval was indispensable, only to discover that securing concessions did not necessarily mean securing an agreement.

And there was nothing they could do about it.

They couldn’t bypass him.

They couldn’t replace his vote.

They couldn’t simply proceed with the legislation that millions of Americans had elected them to pursue.

They could negotiate some more, abandon the proposal, or accept whatever alternative Manchin might eventually support.

That was the extent of their practical options.

Consider the sheer imbalance of power.

One senator, representing roughly 1.8 million West Virginians, possessed sufficient leverage to determine the fate of legislation affecting hundreds of millions of Americans.

Not because he had persuaded a congressional majority that his position was correct.

Not because the American electorate had specifically endorsed his preferred compromises.

But because the Senate’s arithmetic had placed him at the precise point where the governing majority could no longer function without his consent.

And Manchin knew it.

Whether he personally enjoyed that position is something we cannot establish. But his repeated willingness to exercise that leverage is a matter of public record.

He could demand changes, reject compromises, and remain the indispensable negotiating partner.

The system rewarded his ability to withhold agreement far more than it rewarded anyone else’s ability to build it.

Now, to be fair, Manchin did eventually help deliver important legislation.

His negotiations contributed to the Inflation Reduction Act, including substantial clean-energy investments and prescription-drug reforms.

But that accomplishment doesn’t invalidate the criticism.

It reinforces the central point.

The legislation that passed was legislation Manchin found acceptable.

The provisions he rejected either disappeared, were substantially altered, or remained stalled.

The difference between those outcomes was not some new expression of the national electorate’s will.

It was the approval of one pivotal senator.

That is an extraordinary amount of political influence for one individual to wield.

And it raises an obvious institutional question:

Should a democratic government depend so heavily on the preferences, temperament, and negotiating decisions of a single legislator?

Joe Manchin apparently believes the answer is yes.

In fact, he seems to believe the arrangement would improve if we multiplied the number of pivotal legislators.

Because that is precisely what makes his enthusiastic support for McConaughey’s independent-voting proposal so difficult to take seriously.

Elect three to five independents, we’re told.

Give them enough seats to hold the balance of power.

Make Democrats and Republicans negotiate with them before anything can pass.

Let them serve as the indispensable intermediaries between the two major parties.

Sound familiar?

That isn’t a new theory of government. It’s Joe Manchin’s Senate career repackaged as an electoral reform movement.

And the contradiction is glaring.

If Manchin’s extraordinary leverage during Biden’s presidency represented effective governance, why did so many major legislative priorities become hostage to protracted negotiations?

If that concentration of power was merely an unfortunate consequence of a narrowly divided Senate, why deliberately attempt to reproduce it?

And if the argument is that future independents would exercise their leverage more wisely, fairly, or constructively, what exactly guarantees that?

Their independence?

Their supposed moderation?

Their willingness to describe themselves as common-sense problem solvers?

None of those is an institutional safeguard.

Hope is not a governing mechanism. Political independence is not evidence of superior judgment. And possessing the power to obstruct legislation does not magically confer the wisdom to use that power responsibly.

Manchin’s proposal doesn’t solve the underlying democratic problem.

It elevates that problem into a political philosophy.

It asks Americans to believe that government will function better if more legislators acquire the ability to stop it.

Perhaps some would use their leverage constructively.

Perhaps others would extract concessions, prolong negotiations, or obstruct legislation they personally disliked.

The point is that the system would remain dependent on the choices of a handful of politicians whose extraordinary influence arose from their strategic position rather than any broad national mandate.

That’s not structural reform.

That’s institutionalizing the tollbooth.

And after watching Joe Manchin spend years operating one of the most consequential tollbooths in American politics, we are now being told that the solution is to build four more.

Apparently, Washington’s problem wasn’t that Joe Manchin had too much power.

It was that there weren’t enough Joe Manchins.

No, We Don’t Need Another Joe Manchin

Let’s be clear about what this criticism does—and does not—mean.

Joe Manchin was entitled to vote according to his convictions.

He was entitled to oppose Biden.

He was entitled to leave the Democratic Party.

And he is entitled to advocate independent political representation today.

Similarly, Matthew McConaughey is entitled to encourage Americans to consider alternatives to the two major parties.

Neither man needs our permission to participate in politics.

But neither deserves exemption from scrutiny.

Manchin’s Senate record is public.

The consequences of his pivotal votes are documented.

And the electoral mechanics governing independent candidacies are well understood.

We don’t need conspiracy theories or allegations of secret Republican coordination to criticize the proposal.

We don’t need to establish that Manchin was secretly working for the GOP.

We don’t even need to establish that McConaughey has Republican sympathies.

Their actual argument is flawed enough without inventing hidden motives.

Manchin helped demonstrate what happens when individual lawmakers gain disproportionate leverage over a legislative majority.

He now supports a movement whose proposed influence depends on recreating similar leverage.

That’s a perfectly legitimate political objective.

It is also a remarkably questionable answer to the problem of congressional dysfunction.

America needs better mechanisms for representation.

Better safeguards for voting rights.

More accountable institutions.

A legislative process that can function without requiring every major proposal to survive the personal veto of a handful of pivotal politicians.

And an electoral system capable of accommodating genuine political diversity without routinely punishing voters for expressing it.

What it doesn’t need is another collection of self-appointed guardians of common sense, standing between elected majorities and legislative action, insisting that nothing can happen until everyone satisfies them.

Because we’ve seen that movie.

We know the plot.

We know the character.

And we’ve already spent years watching him negotiate the ending.

Joe Manchin wants to bring more independents to Washington so they can force everyone else to negotiate.

Fine.

But he should answer one question first.

If having one Joe Manchin holding the balance of power was so good for American governance, why does America need five more?

Perhaps the problem with Washington was never that there were too few politicians capable of holding legislation hostage.

Perhaps the problem was that the system allowed them to do it in the first place.

And perhaps—just perhaps—we should fix that system instead of recruiting additional tollbooth operators.

America doesn’t need five more Joe Manchins.

It needs a government that doesn’t have to depend on one.
