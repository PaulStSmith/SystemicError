Furious Trump eviscerates this low-level lackey — for copying his signature move

Based on an article by Jason Miciak

When the Small Fish Gets Thrown Back

The story here is not a teleprompter technician making $100,000 on a betting market. The story is a presidency and a political machine that have turned conflicts of interest into ambient weather, then pretended to discover moral clarity only when a disposable employee can be sacrificed for it.

The Real Power Sits Higher

Gabriel Perez is the visible target because he is reachable. Donald Trump is not. That matters.

According to the reporting, Perez was put on unpaid administrative leave after Kalshi flagged suspicious activity and alerted the Commodity Futures Trading Commission. White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt said Trump called it a “disgrace” and personally approved the leave. That is not accountability. It is hierarchy performing hygiene.

The institution with actual power is the White House itself, and the person who decides what counts as corruption is the president whose operation normalizes much larger ones.

A Convenient Scandal

This is the oldest trick in elite politics: isolate the least powerful participant, make a spectacle of discipline, and call it enforcement.

Perez’s alleged misconduct may be real. But the administration’s outrage is selective to the point of parody. A low-level technician allegedly profiting from what he could hear in advance becomes the scandal. The president’s own brokerage activity, including reported purchases of Intel and other tech stocks before market-moving announcements on China and tariffs, is treated as a separate universe, not a conflict to be investigated but a fact to be rationalized away.

That is the misdirection. A petty operator gets branded a disgrace while the people with the authority to move markets and shape policy are granted the fiction of independence.

Corruption Is Not a Glitch

The article also points to a deeper pattern: the Trump family’s money trail runs through government power, and then somehow lands in the right places.

The Washington Post reporting cited here says Donald Trump Jr. and Eric Trump invested in defense-technology companies after Trump’s reelection, and those companies later received billions in Pentagon contracts, options, or eligibility for future awards. That is not a side note. That is the operating model: proximity to state power creates private upside, and private upside is then dressed up as normal business.

If the public is meant to be outraged, it should not be at the existence of corruption in the abstract. It should be at how efficiently this administration sorts corruption into acceptable and disposable categories.

The Scapegoat Has a Name

The source itself notes the obvious subtext: the administration is always ready to make an example of the person easiest to discard. That usually means someone lower-status, more replaceable, and less protected than the people who actually set the rules.

So when the White House declares Perez a disgrace, it is not cleaning house. It is protecting the house by burning one room.

That is why the spectacle feels so insulting. Not because the misconduct is trivial, but because the punishment is calibrated to hide the real scale of the racket.

What This Story Really Shows

This is what authoritarian-adjacent corruption looks like in practice: the top behaves as if rules are for underlings, then stages a moral performance when an underling can be blamed. The powerful keep the brokerage accounts, the contract pipelines, and the market-moving authority. The subordinate gets the humiliation.

The larger lesson is simple. In this political order, corruption is not an exception to the system. It is one of the system’s organizing principles, with discipline reserved for the expendable and immunity reserved for the people who can most profit from the damage.
