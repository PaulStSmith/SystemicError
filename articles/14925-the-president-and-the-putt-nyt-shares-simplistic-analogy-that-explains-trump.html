'The president and the putt': NYT shares 'simplistic' analogy that explains Trump

By Paulo Santos

U.S. President Donald Trump gestures as he attends the National Republican Congressional Committee (NRCC) dinner at the National Building Museum in Washington, D.C., U.S., April 8, 2025. REUTERS/Nathan Howard

President Donald Trump gave the game away with a "simplistic" analogy when pressed by reporters on why he is taking a $400 million luxury plane from the Qatari government, wrote Shawn McCreesh for The New York Times on Monday — a gift far above and beyond anything a president has accepted from a foreign nation in modern history, and that even has some of Trump's most ardent supporters complaining smells rotten.

Specifically, he said, Trump offered up a parable that might be called "the president and the putt."

"This luxurious gift from the Qataris presented all sorts of concerns — ethical, legal, logistical, mechanical. There was also the fact that Mr. Trump had once described Qatar as a 'funder of terrorism at a very high level,'" wrote McCreesh. But how Trump chose to defend his actions spoke volumes.

“There was an old golfer named Sam Snead. Did you ever hear of him?” said Trump.

Snead, he continued, had a way of doing things.

“When they give you a putt, you say, ‘Thank you very much.’ You pick up your ball, and you walk to the next hole. A lot of people are stupid. They say, ‘No, no, I insist on putting it.’ Then they putt it, they miss it, and their partner gets angry at them.”

In Trump's analogy, graciously accepting when your golf opponent lets you skip the last putt, McCreesh continued, is equivalent to "accepting a luxury jet from a foreign government."

But it puts a lot of Trump's ethics-challenged decisions into a mindset that makes more sense, McCreesh wrote.

"His family has six pending deals with a majority Saudi-owned real estate firm; Qatar is backing another Trump project; and the United Arab Emirates is getting in on the Trump family’s cryptocurrency ventures. His two oldest sons are currently hopscotching the globe, striking deals that directly benefit their father. He is also now selling access to himself to top buyers of the digital coin his family is marketing. Even the first lady is pushing crypto these days."

As far as Trump is concerned, McCreesh concluded, "all the world is a golf course, and only a fool would turn down a putt."

Our Analysis:

Trump's "Presidential Putt": A Masterclass in Ethical Bankruptcy

In a move that starkly showcases the ethical chasm at the heart of his presidency, Donald Trump's acceptance of a $400 million luxury plane from the Qatari government is a brazen display of disregard for legal and ethical standards. The audacity of this act is not just in its unprecedented nature in modern political history, but also in the sheer defiance of previously espoused positions by Trump himself, notably his labeling of Qatar as a "funder of terrorism at a very high level."

"The President and the Putt" - A Simplistic Analogy for Complex Corruption

Trump's defense, invoking a golfing analogy about accepting easy victories, encapsulates his presidency's ethos: take what you can, when you can, consequences be damned. Through the lens of Trump's comparison, we're not just observing a man out of depth in the complex sea of ethics; we're witnessing a leader who actively chooses to ignore the lifelines of integrity and legality thrown his way.

"This luxurious gift from the Qatari government presents all sorts of concerns — ethical, legal, logistical, mechanical." Yet, Trump's primary concern seems to be how best to spin this into a scenario where his actions are not just defensible but commendable. His analogy, "the president and the putt," is not just simplistic; it's insultingly reductive, equating critical ethical decisions with a leisurely game of golf.

The Global Golf Course: Trump's Worldview

What's more damning is the backdrop against which this decision was made. Trump's family is deeply entangled with foreign investors, with six pending deals with a majority Saudi-owned real estate firm, Qatar backing another Trump project, and the United Arab Emirates involved in the Trump family's cryptocurrency ventures. This presents a quagmire of potential conflicts of interest and ethical breaches so vast that it should set off alarm bells in any functioning democracy.

Trump's analogy of the world being a golf course where only fools refuse a given putt is a telling insight into his worldview. It's a world where ethical considerations are not just secondary but irrelevant, where the end justifies the means, and where personal gain trumps (pun intended) any semblance of public service or moral responsibility.

A Call to Action

As we dissect Trump's actions and his justification for them, it becomes clear that this is not just about a luxury plane. It's about a systemic failure of ethical leadership at the highest levels of power. Trump's presidency and post-presidency maneuvers serve as a stark reminder of the dangers of conflating personal interest with public service.

The acceptance of this plane is not an isolated incident but a symptom of a much larger problem. It's a call to action for stricter regulations, more transparent dealings, and a reevaluation of what we, as a society, are willing to accept from our leaders.

In sum, Trump's golf analogy, intended to simplify and justify his actions, instead reveals the complexity and depth of his ethical failings. As the saying goes, "You can tell a lot about a man by the way he plays golf." If that's the case, Trump's game is one of recklessness, privilege, and a profound disregard for the rules — a dangerous combination not just on the golf course but in the highest office of the land.
