Trump’s border czar makes remarkable admission as questions swirl about killer ICE agent

ICE Does Not Fail at Vetting. It Fails at Being ICE.

A killing, then a confession

ICE agent David Brouillette fatally shot Colombian man Johan Sebastián Guerrero during a traffic stop in Maine on July 13. After The Washington Post reported allegations from court filings that Brouillette had a violent past, Trump’s border czar Tom Homan was forced into an unusual admission: if those allegations are true, Brouillette should never have passed vetting. ICE then suspended traffic stops after two people were killed by the agency in one week, and Trump promptly pushed to reverse that suspension.

Who actually holds power

The power here sits with the administration that made ICE what it is: a federal enforcement machine built to detain, intimidate, and kill with legal cover. Tom Homan is not a detached investigator discovering a flaw from the outside. He is Trump’s enforcer, defending the apparatus after it has already done its work. When he talks about “vetting failure,” he is describing a symptom of a deeper design problem while pretending it is the whole problem.

The real decision was upstream

The decisive choices were made long before Guerrero was shot. They were made in recruitment, authorization, and political encouragement of violent enforcement. If an agent with alleged abuse and gun threats could still be cleared, that is not a clerical mishap. It is institutional negligence under authoritarian conditions, where force is treated as qualification and cruelty as professionalism.

The blame game is already underway

The framing here tries to shrink a structural crime into an administrative error. That is misdirection. The issue is not just that one man allegedly should not have been hired. The issue is that ICE repeatedly sends armed agents into civilian life and then treats the resulting harm as an exception. Homan’s comments convert a political choice into a paperwork question, which is exactly how institutions launder responsibility.

Violence is the operating logic

Lucinda Brouillette’s statement matters because it names what institutions often erase: violence was not a surprise to the people who lived near it. That history did not become disqualifying until after a Colombian man was killed and the press forced the issue into daylight. This is how state violence sustains itself: it depends on private suffering being ignored until it becomes publicly inconvenient.

Abolish the machine

The larger pattern is not a bad hire. It is a federal deportation regime that keeps reproducing danger, then asks the public to believe the danger is accidental. ICE should not be repaired, retrained, or gently overseen. It should be abolished. An agency built to normalize armed racialized enforcement will keep producing the same outcome: people harmed, accountability delayed, and the state calling it a process problem.
