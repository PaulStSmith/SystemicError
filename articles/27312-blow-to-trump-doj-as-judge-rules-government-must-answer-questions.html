Blow to Trump DOJ as judge rules government must answer questions

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 17, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The department tried to choose the court’s witnessTransparency is not the same as speculation

A federal judge rejected the Justice Department’s attempt to keep career prosecutors beyond the court’s reach, ruling that the government could not broadly invoke privilege to prevent questions about alleged misstatements in a pending case. The decision matters because the department was not merely disputing which questions were relevant; it was asserting the power to decide which of its officials a judge could hear from while warning that it could appeal if she refused to accept those terms.

U.S. District Judge April Perry addressed the dispute after the Justice Department made a last-minute request that she refrain from directly questioning the frontline career prosecutors who handled the case, according to Jose Pagliery of The Washington Sun. The department wanted Perry to rely instead on Andrew Boutros, the Trump-appointed U.S. attorney and political head of the office.

Perry refused to treat much of the information as privileged and therefore unavailable to the court, The Washington Sun reported, citing two people in the courtroom. The government must now answer questions it had tried to restrict.

The department tried to choose the court’s witness

The Justice Department’s proposed arrangement would have placed Boutros between the judge and the career employees with firsthand involvement. Henrique Carneiro, counsel to the associate attorney general, argued that “there is plainly no need for anyone but the U.S. Attorney to provide information,” according to Pagliery.

That was not a neutral dispute over courtroom efficiency. The department sought to substitute a political appointee for the prosecutors who worked directly on the case, limiting Perry’s ability to question the people closest to the conduct under examination. It also signaled that it would challenge her on appeal if she rejected that restriction.

The department had previewed its position in an earlier filing seeking a protective order ahead of a hearing scheduled for December 2, 2026. It asked Perry to narrow the testimony to preserve “all applicable governmental privileges” and confine questioning to the “purported misstatements” she had identified.

Courts routinely adjudicate privilege claims and limits on testimony. What made this confrontation significant was the department’s broader institutional demand: it wanted the executive branch to control how the judicial branch investigated representations made in a case before it. Perry’s ruling rejected that attempted insulation.

Transparency is not the same as speculation

Former Democratic congressional candidate Kat Abughazaleh, who was among those charged in the case, told The Washington Sun that transparency “should be in the government’s interest” if it had done nothing wrong. She then asked what could be “so horrible” in the communications that the department would work so hard to conceal them.

The first point follows from the government’s conduct; the second remains speculation. The reporting does not establish what the communications contain or why the department resisted their disclosure beyond its stated privilege arguments. Perry’s ruling is not proof of hidden misconduct, and Abughazaleh’s suspicion should not be converted into a finding.

But the department’s procedural maneuver is consequential on its own. Government lawyers answer to courts when their conduct or representations become material to judicial proceedings. By trying to channel all questioning through a Trump-appointed officeholder, the department sought control over both the witness and the account the court would receive.

Perry’s decision does not resolve the underlying case or establish what the prosecutors will say. It does establish that the Justice Department cannot simply announce that its preferred official is sufficient and expect the court to abandon its inquiry. The immediate setback for the Trump administration is therefore concrete: the judge, not the department, retains authority over the questions needed to examine what happened in her courtroom.
