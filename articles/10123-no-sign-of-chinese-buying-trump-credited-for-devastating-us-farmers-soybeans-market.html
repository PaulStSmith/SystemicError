'No sign of Chinese buying': Trump credited for 'devastating' US farmers' soybeans market​

By Paulo Santos

President Donald Trump’s tariffs have decimated U.S. farmers’ soybean market and there is “no sign of Chinese buying.”

The fall harvest has started without any orders from China, the world’s biggest buyer, according to a new report published on agriculture.com’s Successful Farming.

American farmers are reporting record yields for crops this fall but it’s unclear who will buy them. The USDA estimates that American farmers are harvesting 4.3 billion bushels, however there is no indication if or when shipments to China will continue. Most years, China buys more than half of U.S. soybean exports — but not this year.

Brazil, however, has had record demand from China from January to August 2025 for Brazilian soybeans. And in the new report, experts “consider the possible consequences if a trade deal is not reached this fall.”

“From the 2017/18 to the 2024/25 crop season, Brazil jumped its soybean production from 4.5 billion bushels to 6.3 billion bushels, according to the National Supply Company (Conab) – Brazil’s food supply and statistics agency.”

There is speculation that Chinese buyers importing soybeans could be “accelerating shipments to avoid sourcing from the United States.”

The ongoing trade war between China and the U.S. is testing farmers’ faith in the Trump administration. During the first trade war in 2018 during Trump’s first term, farmers took a hit and during President Joe Biden’s term had begun to recover.

Now under Trump’s second term, farmers have called for more assistance during the prolonged economic uncertainty.

“Some relief might come from federal government payments to producers, as happened during the first round of trade war, but in many cases, that assistance may not be enough to prevent an uptick in financial stress,” according to the report.

Chinese buyers are expected to continue shifting soybean purchases to other South American countries, including Argentina, Paraguay and Bolivia. These countries are planning to expand planting acreage for their crops and focusing on planting soon for the 2025 and 2026 crop in the Southern Hemisphere.

Critics are pointing to Trump’s trade policies as a major disruption to the U.S. agriculture industry.

“Until Trump’s first term, the US was by far the world’s largest exporter of soybeans. Now Brazil dominates,” David Frum, writer at The Atlantic, wrote via X.

“Has Iowa thanked Trump/Vance for devastating their soybeans market?” Former U.S. Representative Barbara Comstock (R-VA) wrote on X.

Our Analysis:

Trump’s Tariffs: A Masterclass in Market Disruption

In an era where political decisions have immediate and far-reaching effects on global markets, the impact of President Donald Trump’s tariffs on the U.S. farmers’ soybean market stands as a stark testament to the consequences of erratic economic policymaking. A new report from agriculture.com’s Successful Farming paints a desolate picture: U.S. soybean farmers, despite boasting record yields, find their largest market – China – virtually closed to them. Here, we dissect the fallout, the response, and the sheer shortsightedness of the Trump administration’s trade policies.

The Soybean Saga: From Boom to Gloom

Under normal circumstances, a record harvest should be a cause for celebration. Yet, for American soybean farmers, this year’s 4.3 billion bushels harvest is met with trepidation rather than triumph. The crux of the issue lies in the absence of Chinese orders, a direct fallout from Trump’s trade war. Historically, China has been the biggest buyer of U.S. soybeans, but now, there’s “no sign of Chinese buying.”

Brazil, on the other hand, has seen a surge in demand from China, with soybean production jumping significantly from 4.5 billion bushels in the 2017/18 crop season to 6.3 billion bushels by 2024/25. This shift not only underscores the adaptability and opportunism of Brazilian agriculture but also highlights a significant realignment in global trade patterns, leaving American farmers in the lurch.

A Trade War’s Collateral Damage

The ongoing trade war, reignited under Trump’s second term, has tested farmers’ faith in an administration that seems all too willing to sacrifice agricultural stability for political posturing. The article notes that during Trump’s first term, the initial trade war already inflicted considerable damage on farmers, a situation only marginally mitigated during President Joe Biden’s tenure, thanks to a slight recovery. Yet, here we are again, witnessing a repeat of the same disastrous policies without any meaningful resolution in sight.

The call for more government assistance is a telling sign of the desperation facing the agricultural sector. While federal payments may provide temporary relief, they are but a Band-Aid over a gaping wound. The expectation that Chinese buyers will divert their attention (and funds) to South American nations like Argentina, Paraguay, and Bolivia further compounds the issue, signaling a potential long-term shift away from U.S. soybeans.

Criticism and Consequences

Critics, including David Frum and former U.S. Representative Barbara Comstock, have been quick to point out the folly of Trump’s trade policies. The U.S. agriculture industry, once a global leader in soybean exports, now watches as Brazil claims the title, a shift facilitated by the very hand supposed to protect it. The rhetorical question posed by Frum, “Has Iowa thanked Trump/Vance for devastating their soybeans market?” encapsulates the absurdity and tragedy of the situation.

In Conclusion: A Policy-Induced Quagmire

What we’re witnessing is a policy-induced quagmire, one that has not only destabilized a critical sector of the American economy but also eroded the U.S.’s standing in the global agricultural market. The repercussions of Trump’s tariffs, far from being confined to the realm of international trade, have seeped into the very fabric of rural America, sowing seeds of economic uncertainty and resentment.

As the fall harvest commences, U.S. soybean farmers are left to ponder a grim future – one marked by bumper crops with nowhere to go, thanks to a trade war that serves no one’s interests but perhaps those of a president more concerned with appearances than real-world impacts. The stark reality is this: the Trump administration’s trade policies have not only decimated the U.S. soybean market but have also fundamentally undermined the economic well-being of American farmers.
