Bari Weiss 'joke' hires leave '60 Minutes' insiders laughing as  ratings plummet: report

CBS Is Mistaking Branding for Journalism

A Power Grab in a News Suit

CBS News staff are objecting to Bari Weiss’s latest round of hires at 60 Minutes, with Ross Douthat, Sebastian Junger, Gianna Toboni, Sir Trevor Phillips, and Norah O’Donnell now part of a reshaped on-air roster. That is the surface story. The real story is that Paramount Skydance chief David Ellison installed Weiss, and Weiss is now using a storied news program to stage a personnel and identity overhaul that insiders say has little to do with reporting competence and everything to do with direction.

Who Actually Holds the Levers

The institutional power is not with the veteran correspondents rolling their eyes in private. It sits with Ellison, who appointed Weiss, and with Weiss, who is executing the shift. Nick Bilton, the new executive producer, is helping sell the makeover with internal memos and grandiose language about “The Avengers of news.” That phrase is not a newsroom philosophy. It is a confession that management is treating journalism like franchise branding.

Competence Is Not the Point

The criticism of Douthat is not that he is ideologically inconvenient. It is that he has no reporting background and minimal on-camera experience, yet is being placed in one of television’s most coveted news jobs. That matters because CBS is not hiring for raw opinion columns or prestige-name recognition. It is choosing whether 60 Minutes remains a reporting institution or becomes a stage for managerial vanity and ideological curation dressed up as “rigor.”

The Framing Is Already Doing the Damage

The network line is that these are strong additions, that the hires bring depth, that the memo language proves seriousness. The staff reaction tells a different story: insiders see a mismatch between the prestige of the program and the qualifications of the people being installed into it. When management frames obvious internal resistance as a misunderstanding, it obscures the actual issue: authority is being used to override professional standards, and the public is supposed to accept that as innovation.

Rightward Drift Is a Choice

Critics inside CBS say the network has lurched rightward under Weiss and grown reluctant to air anything critical of the Trump administration. That is not a neutral calibration. It is an editorial posture with consequences. If a major news operation becomes more cautious around a president and more enthusiastic about conservative personalities with no newsroom track record, the result is not balance. It is managed deference.

The Larger Pattern

This is what institutional decline looks like when it is dressed as renewal: wealthy owners install ideological operators, operators import prestige names, and the brand gets used as cover for a political shift that staff are expected to absorb quietly. The ratings declines cited in the source may be real, but they do not justify replacing journalism with theater. They expose a deeper problem: when media executives confuse audience capture with authority, they stop defending the newsroom and start hollowing it out.
