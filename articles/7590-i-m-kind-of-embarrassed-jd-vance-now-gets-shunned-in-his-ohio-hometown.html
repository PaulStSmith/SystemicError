'I'm kind of embarrassed': JD Vance now gets shunned in his Ohio hometown

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 17, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:

Vice President JD Vance attends the annual Thanksgiving turkey pardoning ceremony in the Rose Garden at the White House in Washington, D.C. on Nov. 25, 2025. REUTERS/Jonathan Ernst

Residents of Vice President JD Vance’s hometown in Ohio have mixed feelings about the city’s most famous native son.

The 41-year-old Vance detailed his troubled upbringing in Middletown, where globalization hollowed out its industrial core and chased off good-paying jobs, in his 2016 memoir, “Hillbilly Elegy,” and Washington Post columnist Carine Hajjar found the folks back home never quite embraced him as one of their own.

“I’m kind of embarrassed sometimes to tell people that I’m from Middletown,” said Bethany Tompkins, a mother and seamstress. “I don’t want the world to judge us by him and how he behaves.”

Hajjar was surprised how little fanfare about the local product she spotted around town during a recent visit, just over a year after he was elected to serve as President Donald Trump’s second in command.

“There were no banners, no plaques, not even a weather-beaten Trump-Vance ’24 sign as I drove around town,” she wrote. “[A] portrait of Vance [in an art studio] was the most visible display of admiration I found. Though Middletown, like surrounding Butler County, went 62 percent for the Republican ticket, turnout in Middletown was nine points lower than the county as a whole. And in the 2022 Senate race, without Trump on the ticket, Vance carried Butler by a smaller margin than the rest of the statewide Republican winners.”

The painter of that Vance portrait, David Bailey, offered as much praise for him as anyone Hajjar encountered.

“I thought it was pretty neat, we got a local guy from Middletown, Ohio,” Bailey told her. “That’s something I think is worthy of notoriety.”

Middletown’s city council placed a handful of signs acknowledging Vance around town after his mother asked a month after his election, reminding elected officials that he had graduated from the local high school and still returns sometimes to take her out to dinner, but Hajjar said those displays were fairly inconspicuous.

“We don’t name a lot of things after people around here,” said Butler County treasurer Michael McNamara, but he added that doesn’t mean locals aren’t proud. “I mean, to be able to produce a vice president or potential future president is a pretty incredible feather in your cap.”

Trump promised on the campaign trail that his trade policies and immigration crackdown would boost fortunes in places like Middletown, but manufacturing jobs remain down and prices are still high 11 months into his second term, and Vance is preaching patience while promising an economic “boom” is just around the corner.

“Middletown residents are just as conflicted about the prospect of a boom as they are about the local man delivering the message,” Hajjar wrote.

Fred Couch, a bartender in a local pizza parlor, told the columnist he backed Vance as a senator but had since lost faith in his leadership due to Trump’s trade war.

“I think tariffs are a tax, myself, and it’s done nothing but hurt people so far,” Couch said.

The city’s economic challenges are older than Vance and will most likely outlive his tenure as vice president, but locals like 60-year-old Wilma Smith recall a golden age back in the 1970s, before the factories closed down and online shopping upended retail shopping.

“I met a lot of residents like Smith who could remember better days, but didn’t see it as Vance’s job alone to save Middletown from decades of decline,” Hajjar wrote. “Besides, Smith said, it ‘made my day’ when she found out that Vance was a local kid. ‘It gives us a boost, too,’ she said. ‘We need that boost, you know.’”

Our Analysis:

JD Vance: Middletown’s Reluctant Hero or Opportunistic Politico?

In the heart of Ohio, Middletown stands as a testament to the American industrial decline, a narrative deeply ingrained within the fibers of its community. Enter JD Vance, the city’s most famous (or infamous, depending on who you ask) native son, whose ascent to the Vice Presidency under Donald Trump has left his hometown in a state of ambivalence. The Washington Post’s Carine Hajjar’s exploration into this mixed reception reveals a complex portrait of local pride entangled with political disillusionment.

Middletown’s Mixed Emotions

Bethany Tompkins, a local resident, encapsulates the community’s sentiment when she expresses embarrassment over Vance’s representation of their town. This is a striking indictment of a man who, through his memoir “Hillbilly Elegy,” positioned himself as the voice of the rust-belt’s forgotten. Yet, the tangible manifestations of pride one might expect for such a high-profile figure are conspicuously absent in Middletown. No banners, no plaques, and barely a whisper of his vice-presidential tenure. This lack of fanfare speaks volumes, highlighting a rift between Vance’s political persona and the community that ostensibly shaped him.

The Election Numbers Don’t Lie

Hajjar’s observation that Vance’s electoral performance in Middletown lagged behind wider Republican outcomes is telling. Despite the town’s Republican leanings, the diminished enthusiasm for Vance suggests a skepticism of his ability to authentically represent their interests. David Bailey’s portrait of Vance might stand as the most visible display of admiration in the town, but it’s a solitary beacon in a sea of indifference.

Economic Promises vs. Reality

Trump and Vance’s promises of an economic revival through trade policies and immigration crackdowns have yet to materialize, leaving residents like Fred Couch disillusioned. The narrative of a looming economic “boom” does little to assuage the immediate challenges faced by the community. Middletown’s economic woes are deep-rooted, predating and likely to outlive Vance’s political career. Yet, the expectation for Vance to play a pivotal role in the town’s resurgence persists among some, like Wilma Smith, who sees his success as a source of local pride.

The Verdict

JD Vance’s story is a microcosm of the broader American political and social landscape. His journey from a troubled upbringing in a declining industrial town to the Vice Presidency underlines the potent mix of personal ambition and the exploitation of a populist narrative. However, the response from Middletown reveals the limitations of such a narrative when confronted with the realities of those it purports to represent.

Middletown’s lukewarm reception of Vance is not just a reflection on him as an individual but a commentary on the wider disillusionment with political figures who claim to represent the “forgotten” Americans yet fail to deliver tangible improvements to their lives. The absence of overt displays of pride in Vance’s achievements suggests a community still waiting for a reason to celebrate, caught between hope for a better future and skepticism of promises unfulfilled.

In essence, Middletown and its residents find themselves at a crossroads, symbolizing the broader American struggle between the allure of populist rhetoric and the harsh realities of economic and social decline. JD Vance’s story, while unique, is not unlike the countless other narratives of political figures who have risen to prominence on the back of promises to uplift the very communities that remain ambivalent towards their success.
