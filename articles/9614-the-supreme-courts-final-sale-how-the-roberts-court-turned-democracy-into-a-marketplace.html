The Supreme Court’s Final Sale: How the Roberts Court Turned Democracy into a Marketplace

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 14, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There was a time when corruption at least had the decency to hide. Politicians whispered favors behind closed doors, corporate executives passed envelopes in the shadows, and everyone understood there were lines not to be crossed. But now, the corruption is open, codified, and robed in black. It doesn’t need to hide because it’s been declared legal. And that, in essence, is the Roberts Court’s legacy.

This is the culmination of a project that began half a century ago, when Lewis Powell — before he ever donned a Supreme Court robe — penned a memo warning that corporate America needed to seize control of politics before democracy itself turned on them. The corporate class listened. They built the think tanks, funded the propaganda, and bought the politicians. But the true stroke of genius came when they bought the referees — the judiciary. And now, fifty years later, they’re cashing in.

Citizens United: The Legalization of Bribery

When Citizens United v. FEC dropped in 2010, it didn’t just bend the law; it broke it over a billionaire’s knee. The ruling that spending money was the same as speech and that corporations were entitled to personhood wasn’t jurisprudence — it was a heist in broad daylight. It transformed elections into auctions where the richest bidder gets to rewrite the rules.

The numbers are staggering: a 28-fold increase in election spending since Citizens United. Twenty-eight times more money buying twenty-eight times more influence. That’s not democracy. That’s market capture. The Roberts Court didn’t just open the floodgates — it installed a revolving door between boardrooms and ballot boxes.

Now, Vice President J.D. Vance, that manufactured populist who swapped his Appalachian memoir for a billionaire-funded megaphone, is fronting a new case to finish what Citizens United started. His case seeks to destroy the last limits on campaign contributions by eliminating restrictions on coordinated party spending. If successful, political parties could become money laundromats for oligarchs — sluicing unlimited cash directly into candidates’ campaigns. In plain English: bribery, but make it legal.

And the argument? That pooling money from multiple donors somehow “dilutes corruption.” It’s a line so absurd it would make Orwell choke. By that logic, a cartel isn’t dangerous because its shareholders are diversified.

Redefining Bribery: The Sittenfeld Gambit

But dismantling campaign finance law is only half the operation. The other half is the systematic weakening of anti-corruption laws themselves. The Supreme Court’s recent history is a monument to this effort. From Bob McDonnell’s overturned bribery conviction to the Chris Christie aides and Cuomo cronies who walked free, the Roberts Court has been busy redefining corruption out of existence.

Their guiding philosophy is simple: unless there’s a notarized contract reading “quid pro quo for legislative favor,” it’s not bribery — it’s gratitude. Roberts himself made that perfectly clear when he waved away the McDonnell case, declaring that the Court’s concern wasn’t with “tawdry tales of Ferraris and Rolexes.” No, heaven forbid the law touch the delicate wrists of power. Their concern was preserving the ability of politicians to sell influence without consequence.

So when Cincinnati’s former councilman P.G. Sittenfeld — caught on tape accepting $20,000 for a development project — takes his bribery case to the Supreme Court, he isn’t just pleading innocence. He’s carrying water for a broader agenda: to make it legally impossible to prosecute pay-to-play corruption. His lawyers argue that corruption has become so common, so normalized, that it can’t be prosecuted without criminalizing politics itself. Translation: the system is so dirty, it must stay dirty to survive.

The Self-Dealing Court

It would be laughable if it weren’t so obscene. The same justices making these rulings — who have quietly accepted luxury vacations, private jet rides, and lavish gifts from billionaire patrons — are the ones declaring that such gifts aren’t corrupting. They’re not interpreting law; they’re laundering their own reputations.

The Roberts Court has become the most effective protection racket in American history. The message is clear: if you’re rich enough, if your corruption is gilded with enough zeros, you can’t be touched. The law isn’t blind — it’s just bought.

The States’ Revolt: A Path Out of the Abyss

And yet, as David Sirota and Jared Jacang Maher point out, there are off-ramps from this highway to plutocracy. The federal system may be captured, but states still hold power over corporate law. Montana, of all places, is leading the rebellion. Thirteen years after the Supreme Court gutted its century-old anti-corruption statute, Montanans are fighting back — using the same legal structure that Citizens United exploited.

The plan is simple and brilliant: change state incorporation laws so that corporations are no longer endowed with the right to spend on elections. A corporation only exists because a state grants it that privilege — and a state can take it back. If Montana succeeds, corporations could still conduct business, but their wallets would be locked out of politics. Other states could follow, eroding the corporate power base from below while Congress dithers above.

Meanwhile, public financing of elections offers another way forward. Fourteen states and twenty-six cities already provide small-dollar matching funds or grants to candidates who refuse private money. It’s not perfect — billionaires can still drown them in cash — but it’s proof of concept. For a fraction of what we spend on wars or corporate tax cuts, we could fund democracy itself.

The True Cost of Corruption

As Senator Jon Ossoff said, corruption isn’t an abstract issue — it’s the reason everything else is broken. It’s why your insulin costs a fortune, why hedge funds own your neighborhood, and why an ambulance ride costs more than a month’s rent. Every inequity, every injustice, every “market inefficiency” traces back to legalized bribery.

And that’s the genius — and the horror — of the conservative legal movement. They’ve managed to make corruption sound like freedom. They’ve weaponized the First Amendment into a license for oligarchy. And while Democrats tweet and posture, the billionaires are busy etching their ownership deeds into constitutional law.

The Verdict

This isn’t about campaign finance. It’s about whether democracy can survive when the Supreme Court itself is on the take. The Roberts Court has redefined corruption so narrowly that it can’t see its own reflection. It has built a legal architecture where bribery is free speech, and accountability is tyranny.

The American experiment was never meant to be run by billionaires and adjudicated by their butlers in robes. Yet here we are — a republic hollowed out from within, its Constitution twisted into a business plan. If the states fail to fight back, if citizens don’t make anti-corruption the defining cause of our time, then the last chapter will be written not in legislation, but in ledgers.

The Supreme Court doesn’t need to burn the Constitution. It’s already sold it — one opinion, one loophole, one luxury vacation at a time.
