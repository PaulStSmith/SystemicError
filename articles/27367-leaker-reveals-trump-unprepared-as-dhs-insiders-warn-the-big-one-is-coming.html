Leaker reveals Trump unprepared as DHS insiders warn 'the big one is coming'

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 20, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A warning from the staff left behindElection retaliation became a security liabilityThe secretary who “wasn’t there”

A former senior Homeland Security official says the remaining cyber specialists inside the department fear a major digital crisis is approaching while the Trump administration has left the federal cyber-defense apparatus badly understaffed. Miles Taylor, who served as a DHS deputy chief of staff during Donald Trump’s first term and leaked information from that White House, said agency experts are staying in government despite lucrative private-sector prospects because they worry no one will be at their posts when a large-scale disruption hits.

The warning followed a week of outages affecting aviation, banking, the National Security Agency and other systems. Those failures disrupted air travel, damaged economic activity and raised national-security concerns, according to AlterNet. The reporting does not establish that the outages shared a cause or resulted from a cyberattack. But the government’s failure to give a clear, consistent account has left room for concerns about emerging threats, including attacks enhanced by artificial intelligence.

A warning from the staff left behind

Taylor said he had spoken with AI and cybersecurity specialists still working inside DHS. In his account, they could leave immediately for private-sector salaries of roughly $2 million a year but have chosen to remain because they believe “a major cyber upheaval is coming.”

That assessment is not a formal government forecast, and “the big one” is not identified as a particular operation, adversary or target. Its significance lies elsewhere: the people Taylor describes as closest to the government’s cyber defenses reportedly believe the institution may soon face a severe test after losing substantial capacity.

Trump, traveling abroad as the outages unsettled critical systems at home, told Americans to “relax.” Reassurance is not preparedness, particularly when the administration offering it cannot explain what happened and has reduced the workforce responsible for preventing, diagnosing and containing digital emergencies.

Election retaliation became a security liability

Taylor traces the damage at the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency to Trump’s grievance over the 2020 election. CISA’s previous director refused to support Trump’s false claim that the election had been rigged, and Trump fired him by tweet.

That episode matters because it shows political loyalty being imposed on an agency whose usefulness depends on technical competence and factual reporting. Trump punished an official for declining to validate his preferred election narrative. Taylor’s contention is that the retaliation did not end with one dismissal: the agency now operates with a depleted staff after years of hostility rooted in that dispute.

Under former DHS Secretary Kristi Noem, thousands of CISA employees were pushed out, according to the supplied reporting. Politico has also reported significant cuts at the agency during Trump’s second administration. Whatever justification the administration attached to those staffing decisions, the consequence described here is concrete: fewer specialists are available to protect systems on which transportation, finance and national security depend.

This is how an authoritarian demand for personal affirmation can become an operational vulnerability. An agency created to assess threats cannot function safely if telling the president an unwelcome truth is treated as disloyalty.

The secretary who “wasn’t there”

Congress has begun pressing DHS for answers. At a Wednesday hearing, even Republican lawmakers sought more information about CISA’s staffing decisions. DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin could not provide specific answers about rehiring.

Asked whether forcing out technical experts had been a mistake, Mullin replied that he “wasn’t there” when it happened. The response tries to convert institutional responsibility into personal absence. Mullin may not have made the earlier decisions, but he now leads the department living with their consequences. His obligation is to know what capacity was lost, what risks followed and whether the government intends to rebuild it.

The White House itself reportedly has so little confidence in Mullin’s public performance that it has put him on a “timeout” from television appearances. That is a communications judgment, not a cybersecurity plan. Keeping an ineffective messenger away from cameras does nothing to restore technical personnel or answer lawmakers’ questions.

Taylor said the situation was not “confidence inspiring.” The phrase is restrained. The administration dismantled expertise, failed to explain a series of consequential outages, and sent an unprepared secretary to Congress without firm answers. Its public message, meanwhile, amounted to an instruction not to worry.

The strongest evidence that concern is warranted may be the people who have not left. According to Taylor, highly marketable specialists are accepting government pay because they fear what could happen if their posts are abandoned. Their presence offers some protection. The fact that they believe they must personally hold together a weakened federal defense system is the warning.
