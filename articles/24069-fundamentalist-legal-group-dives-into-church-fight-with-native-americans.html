Fundamentalist legal group dives into church fight with Native Americans

Based on an article by Alex Henderson

Religious Liberty Is Not a License to Override Tribal Authority

The Dispute Beneath the Slogan

Foundations for Nations came under pressure after Pastor Sarah Lucas urged Native congregants to renounce Northern Arapaho traditions. The Food Bank of Wyoming subsequently ended its partnership with the church, according to Lucas, after receiving a complaint that her message was discriminatory. Liberty Counsel then filed a discrimination complaint for the church and challenged the Northern Arapaho Business Council’s response.

Power Is Split, Not Absent

The Northern Arapaho Business Council holds governmental authority on tribal lands. But the church reportedly owns private fee-simple property, and the council acknowledged in an August 31 resolution that it does not regulate activities on that property. The same resolution said neither the church nor any individual had been formally designated a “bad man.”

That distinction matters. Some tribal members called for using treaty provisions to remove the church, and the council initially told church officials to leave. Yet the reporting does not establish that the council ultimately asserted legal power over the church’s private parcel.

The Food Bank of Wyoming holds a different kind of power: control over its partnerships and resources. Whether ending this particular relationship violated discrimination law is the subject of the church’s complaint, not a settled fact.

Liberty Counsel Changes the Balance

Liberty Counsel is not merely defending an isolated congregation. The Florida-based legal organization is providing free representation, filing complaints with state and federal agencies, and asserting that the church needs no tribal authorization to conduct religious activity on its property.

That intervention shifts the conflict. A tribal government and Native community confronting a religious message hostile to their traditions now face an experienced ideological litigation group capable of turning local resistance into an expensive constitutional dispute.

Calling that arrangement a simple free-speech battle erases the unequal legal machinery now in motion. The church has acquired institutional reinforcement far beyond the reservation. The people targeted by its sermon have not been shown to possess comparable resources.

“Religious Freedom” Conceals the Direction of Pressure

Liberty Counsel’s framing centers the church as the injured party. That framing pushes the sermon itself—and the community against which it was directed—to the margins.

The reported message did not merely present Christian doctrine. It urged Native congregants to abandon Northern Arapaho traditions. The predictable consequence is conflict with a community whose cultural practices were placed in opposition to the pastor’s religion.

The evidence does not establish that Lucas intended a broader campaign of cultural suppression. It does establish the direction of the demand: Native people were told to renounce their traditions, while the institution delivering that demand now portrays resistance as discrimination against itself.

The Reporting Needs a Firmer Distinction

The story’s language risks collapsing political rejection, community outrage, loss of a food-bank partnership, and legal eviction into one generalized claim that the church is being forced out.

Those are not interchangeable consequences. The council’s August 31 resolution expressly disclaimed regulatory authority over the church’s private property and denied making a “bad men” designation. Meanwhile, the legality of the food bank’s decision remained contested through an administrative complaint.

Blurring those facts strengthens Liberty Counsel’s preferred narrative: that an embattled church faces coordinated government persecution. The reporting supports a serious dispute, but not that complete story.

A Familiar Conversion of Power Into Victimhood

The larger pattern is institutional inversion. A church challenges Indigenous traditions; community members object; a national legal organization arrives; and the institution with outside counsel recasts itself as the vulnerable dissenter.

The Northern Arapaho council’s authority is real, but the reported resolution also shows its limits. Liberty Counsel’s power lies elsewhere: litigation, federal and state complaint systems, and a political vocabulary that treats consequences imposed on religious organizations as presumptively suspect while treating pressure on Native culture as background.

That is the systemic maneuver. The actor demanding cultural surrender becomes the victim, the community resisting that demand becomes the aggressor, and legal firepower sanitizes the reversal as a neutral defense of liberty.
