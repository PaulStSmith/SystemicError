The Siege of Peace: How the Trump Administration Invaded the U.S. Institute of Peace and the Supreme Court Helped Pave the Way

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 23, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

In a nation increasingly disoriented by legal chaos, executive overreach, and judicial manipulation, few events so chillingly encapsulate the collapse of institutional norms as the armed seizure of the United States Institute of Peace (USIP). For most Americans, this Orwellian incursion into a federally chartered but independent nonprofit likely slipped through the cracks—eclipsed by scandal fatigue and information overload. But what occurred wasn't just an overreach. It was an autocratic test balloon, a blueprint for dismantling the architecture of democracy under the smokescreen of "executive efficiency."

What is the U.S. Institute of Peace?

Established in 1984 by an act of Congress, the U.S. Institute of Peace was conceived in the aftermath of Vietnam and amidst Cold War tensions. Its mandate: promote international peace, support conflict resolution without violence, and serve as a hub for research, education, and on-the-ground peacebuilding efforts. The USIP is an independent, nonpartisan, nonprofit entity. It is chartered by Congress but is not a federal executive agency.

The structure of USIP reflects a deliberate choice to shield it from partisan swings. Its 15-member board includes three ex officio members (the Secretary of State, Secretary of Defense, and the President of the National Defense University), and twelve individuals nominated by the president and confirmed by the Senate. To preserve political neutrality, no more than eight members may belong to the same political party, and all appointees serve fixed terms. They can only be removed for cause.

Over the decades, USIP has functioned as America’s think tank for diplomacy. It has supported field operations and mediation in conflict zones from Iraq and Afghanistan to Colombia and the Philippines. It has trained leaders, conducted interfaith dialogues, brokered fragile ceasefires, and provided evidence-based strategies for preventing violence before it starts. The Institute holds no coercive power, no enforcement authority—its influence is grounded in expertise, not force.

The Hostile Takeover

That didn't stop Donald Trump and his inner circle from trying to dismantle it by fiat.

On February 19, 2025, President Trump signed Executive Order 14217, titled Commencing the Reduction of Federal Bureaucracy. Buried within the bureaucratic jargon was a not-so-subtle directive: eliminate the non-statutory functions of the USIP. Yet the executive branch has no authority over an entity like the USIP. It would be like the president attempting to shut down the Red Cross because he didn't like their disaster response.

Instead of going through Congress—which holds the power of the purse and legislative oversight—Trump's Department of Government Execution (Doge) deployed armed agents and private security contractors to physically seize the USIP headquarters in D.C. They arrived with no court order, no legal warrant, and no statutory mandate. What followed was the administrative equivalent of a coup d'état.

Computers were confiscated. Website servers were shut down. Senior leadership was escorted out. Federal officials announced via email that the Institute's board members had been "terminated," ignoring statutory protections. This wasn’t politics. It was raw, authoritarian muscle masquerading as governance.

The Institute filed an emergency lawsuit. Initially, Judge Beryl Howell denied a temporary restraining order due to the steep legal threshold required—emergency relief demands a clear showing of irreparable harm and likelihood of success. But later, Howell issued a sweeping injunction. She declared the administration's actions unlawful, describing them as a "gross usurpation of power" and an act that traumatized a staff dedicated to peace.

The Supreme Court's Quiet Coup: Weaponizing the Shadow Docket

While the judiciary briefly served as a bulwark against executive excess, the Supreme Court soon undercut that foundation through its now-infamous shadow docket. Originally intended for urgent, non-controversial rulings, the shadow docket has become the Roberts Court’s tool for pushing major ideological shifts without full hearings or transparency.

Enter Trump v. Wilcox. The administration unilaterally removed Democratic appointees to the National Labor Relations Board and the Merit Systems Protection Board, citing the same theory used in the USIP takeover: the president can fire anyone, at will, regardless of statutory protections. The lower courts ruled against Trump, but the Supreme Court issued a shadow docket order staying that decision. Just like that, 90 years of precedent under Humphrey's Executor was mortally wounded.

Worse still, the Court created a mystical exception for the Federal Reserve—declaring it immune from presidential interference, without any legal basis. The justices conjured a footnote from thin air, citing historical tradition as if this were a Hamilton musical and not a judicial proceeding. Justice Elena Kagan dissented in blistering terms, accusing the majority of prioritizing ideology over law.

The result? A new precedent, created in the dark, allowing presidents to decapitate independent agencies on a whim while shielding only those sacred enough to keep Wall Street from having a stroke.

Why This Should Terrify You

What happened to the USIP is not some arcane jurisdictional squabble. It is a demonstration of how easily the safeguards of democracy can be circumvented when all three branches abandon their duty.

The executive branch initiated an armed assault on a congressionally chartered institution. The judicial branch—at its highest level—reversed long-settled protections in silence. The legislative branch barely registered a pulse.

We are not witnessing isolated incidents. We are watching a methodical erosion of democratic checks and balances. Trump’s playbook, drafted in the form of Project 2025, is not hypothetical. It is live, it is organized, and it is now legally viable thanks to the Court’s willingness to bypass procedure in favor of ideology.

The shadow docket has become a weapon of autocracy. It allows sweeping changes to constitutional norms without deliberation, transparency, or public accountability. The Court no longer acts as an impartial guardian of the law but as a partisan instrument of executive will.

The implications reach far beyond the USIP. If independent agencies are no longer independent, if statutory protections can be ignored, and if constitutional precedent can be erased in unsigned two-page orders, then we are no longer governed by laws, but by the whims of those in power.

And if a president can send armed federal agents to overthrow the board of an institute dedicated to peace, what else—or who else—can he silence next?
