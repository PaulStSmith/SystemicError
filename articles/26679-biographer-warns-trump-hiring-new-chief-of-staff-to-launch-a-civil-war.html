SYSTEMIC ERROR / COMMENTARY

Biographer warns Trump hiring new Chief of Staff to 'launch a civil war'

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 17, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Trump biographer Seth Abramson interprets rumors of a possible post-midterm White House chief-of-staff change as preparation for an effort to “launch a civil war.” That is Abramson’s warning, not an established plan. The article provides no direct evidence that President Donald Trump has decided to replace Susie Wiles for that purpose.

According to the report, Trump has asked allies about replacing Wiles after the midterms, while Stephen Miller, Bill Pulte, and Lee Zeldin are among the rumored contenders. Separately, Axios reportedly found that MAGA activists—including Trump’s childhood friend and attorney Peter Ticktin—were urging extraordinary measures, including martial law and the seizure of ballots and election equipment, if they deemed the elections “rigged,” despite no evidence cited here that they would be.

The claim outruns the evidence

The headline converts one biographer’s inference into something resembling an identified White House objective. But AlterNet does not report an order, operational plan, named collaborator or direct statement from Trump establishing that a staffing decision is intended to produce civil conflict.

Abramson may see a dangerous convergence: election denial, demands for martial law and competition to replace an official viewed as insufficiently useful. That convergence merits scrutiny. It does not make his conclusion a confirmed explanation. When covering extraordinary allegations, precision is not timidity; it is the difference between documenting authoritarian politics and merely predicting them.

Power sits in the White House

The weaker actors in this story are not the people lobbying for presidential favor. Trump holds the institutional power to choose his chief of staff and to determine which advisers receive access and influence. The reported contenders are senior administration officials, not spectators shouting from outside the gates.

Likewise, activists can propose seizing election materials, but only public officials can attempt to convert that demand into state action. The central political question is therefore not whether MAGA personalities are saying reckless things. It is whether the president and his administration adopt, encourage or reject those demands. The reporting supplied here does not establish that answer.

Election denial is the mechanism

Ticktin’s quoted proposal reveals the operative structure: allege a “rigged election” without evidence, then use the allegation to justify commandeering ballots and equipment before normal processes can run their course. The accusation becomes permission for intervention. Proof is treated as optional because control is the objective of the proposal, whatever its advocates claim their objective to be.

Axios reportedly noted that some suggested measures would exceed Trump’s legal authority. That distinction matters. Martial law is not a magic phrase that dissolves statutory limits, state authority or election law. Presenting unlawful or unsupported proposals as one more factional option risks laundering coercion into ordinary political strategy.

Palace intrigue is a distraction

The “Wild Wild West” atmosphere, Trump’s reported “freakout” and speculation that Wiles may become a post-election scapegoat offer vivid court gossip. They do not answer the consequential question: what conduct would a replacement be expected to enable?

That is where the article’s strongest material lies, but its framing weakens it. “Civil war” is an explosive label supplied by Abramson; the concrete reported threat is narrower and already serious—political allies urging the use of presidential power and coercive measures against election administration on the basis of an unsubstantiated fraud claim.

The systemic error

Authoritarian politics often arrives through personnel decisions, legal pretexts and bureaucratic obedience rather than a formally announced rupture. A chief of staff controls access, information and execution. Choosing that official can shape which impulses are contained and which are translated into government action.

But alarmism can obscure the same power structure it seeks to expose. The defensible indictment here is not that a civil war has been proven to be in preparation. It is that influential Trump allies reportedly proposed overriding election processes through extraordinary state power, while senior officials maneuvered for proximity to the president who could try to act on such demands. That documented pattern is grave enough without converting inference into fact.
