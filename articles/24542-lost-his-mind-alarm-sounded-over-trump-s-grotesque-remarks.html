'Lost his mind': Alarm sounded over Trump's grotesque remarks

Based on an article by D. Earl Stephens

Paulo SantosOctober 8, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power is in the officeThe speaker owns the remarkThe media claim outruns the evidenceExpendability as politicsThe system rewards degraded standards

What happened

AlterNet columnist D. Earl Stephens reported that Donald Trump, speaking at a campaign rally in Grand Island, Nebraska, said Iran should be allowed to “take out Los Angeles” and San Diego because it would be “a very small price to pay.” Stephens described the remarks as disqualifying and said Trump had “lost whatever was left of his mind.”

The column says the speech occurred on a Monday night roughly four weeks before the midterm elections, but it supplies no calendar date, transcript, video, or surrounding remarks. It also does not explain what Trump meant by “price,” what scenario he was discussing, or whether “take out” was literal or rhetorical. That missing context does not make the quoted language defensible; it does limit what can responsibly be concluded from the column alone.

Stephens also reported finding no mention of the remarks on the front pages of The New York Times, the Associated Press, or NPR the following morning. That observation establishes what he saw during those checks—not that the organizations never covered the speech.

The power is in the office

The central fact, if the quotation is accurate and representative, is not that Trump sounded deranged. It is that a president spoke casually about foreign attacks on two American cities. Psychiatric speculation turns an exercise of presidential power into a story about personal instability.

“Lost his mind” is the headline’s answer, but it is not an established diagnosis. More importantly, it is an analytical escape hatch. A powerful official does not need to be irrational to treat millions of people as expendable. He needs only to believe that doing so carries no meaningful political cost.

The office gives the remark its significance. A random provocateur can posture about sacrificing cities. A president speaks from an institution entrusted with national security, and audiences at home and abroad must account for the possibility that his words reflect policy, tolerance, or intent.

The speaker owns the remark

Trump chose the language attributed to him. His supporters did not put those words in his mouth, and residents of Los Angeles and San Diego did not create the political conditions being discussed. The agency begins with the president who spoke and extends to every powerful institution deciding how seriously to treat it.

Stephens is right to focus on normalization, but his column weakens that case with unsupported escalation. It calls the statement illegal and treasonous without identifying a law or presenting evidence sufficient to establish treason. It demands imprisonment while supplying no legal proceeding, charge, or adjudicated offense. Outrage does not convert accusation into proof.

The cleaner indictment is already severe: the reported words reduce mass civilian harm to an acceptable cost. No amateur diagnosis or invented legal conclusion is needed.

The media claim outruns the evidence

Stephens found no front-page treatment at three outlets on one morning. That is evidence of editorial placement at a particular moment, not proof of a coordinated blackout, permanent silence, or institutional “submission.”

Still, placement is an exercise of power. Editors decide which presidential statements become national emergencies and which disappear into the daily flood. Repetition can dull judgment: conduct once treated as extraordinary becomes merely another item competing for attention. That is not neutrality. It is an allocation of scrutiny.

The column should have documented that failure more carefully. A systematic claim about media normalization requires more than three front-page checks. By overstating what he established, Stephens gives the institutions he criticizes an easy way to dismiss the harder question: why language contemplating attacks on American cities did not, in his account, command immediate prominence.

Expendability as politics

The deeper pattern is the conversion of human beings into bargaining chips. The reported formulation does not discuss Californians as people the government is obligated to protect. It presents their destruction as a tolerable transaction.

That logic is politically useful because the cities named are abstractions to much of Trump’s audience—and frequent targets in partisan narratives. The source does not establish why he selected them, so motive cannot be asserted. But the foreseeable effect is clear: it invites listeners to imagine that some Americans may be sacrificed without ending the leader’s claim to represent the nation.

Authoritarian politics does not begin only with formal repression. It also depends on sorting the population into those whose safety counts and those whose suffering can be minimized, mocked, or traded away.

The system rewards degraded standards

This episode reveals more than one inflammatory quotation. It shows how accountability can fail through imprecision on every side: a president uses grotesquely reckless language; media institutions allegedly give it little prominence; and a furious critic substitutes diagnosis and unsupported criminal conclusions for disciplined evidence.

Those failures are not equivalent. The president holds the institutional power and made the reported remark. But weak criticism still serves powerful actors when it blurs what is documented with what is merely asserted.

The systemic danger is a political culture trained to process contempt as spectacle. Once talk of sacrificing cities becomes another outrage in the feed, power has learned that even the most dehumanizing language may produce attention without consequence.
