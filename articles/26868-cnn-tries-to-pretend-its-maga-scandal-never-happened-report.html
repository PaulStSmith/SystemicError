CNN tries to pretend its MAGA scandal 'never happened': report

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Hasan Piker sued CNN contributor Scott Jennings in federal court in Washington, D.C., alleging defamation and seeking $72.5 million in damages. The complaint concerns an August broadcast on Jennings’s Salem radio show, where Jennings said Piker was “calling for Black voters to be lynched.”

According to the lawsuit, the underlying clip combined excerpts from three streams and removed context showing that Piker was describing and mocking someone else’s remark. AlterNet says the clip circulated through the National Republican Senatorial Committee before Jennings’s program used it. These remain allegations in a pending civil case; the report supplies no ruling on whether Jennings defamed Piker.

Journalist Colby Hall argued that CNN had responded conspicuously: 64 words aggregating Variety in its Reliable Sources newsletter and, at the time of his writing, no on-air coverage. Hall said CNN did not answer his request for comment.

The power is institutional

Piker can sue, deny the accusation and show the omitted context. But Jennings, Salem, CNN and the NRSC occupy the institutions that can convert a manipulated social-media clip into a nationally distributed political claim.

That hierarchy matters. CNN pays Jennings and repeatedly places him inside its news product. Hall’s point is not merely that Jennings appears on the network; it is that CNN has made him a central conservative voice across debates, conventions and major speeches. The network supplies reach, prestige and an implicit judgment that his commentary belongs within legitimate political discussion.

The decisions have names

The lawsuit alleges that Jennings broadcast a grave accusation based on a deceptively edited clip. Salem aired it. The NRSC reportedly circulated the clip. CNN did not make the disputed statement, but it chose to build programming around Jennings and then, according to Hall, largely avoided covering the resulting lawsuit.

Those are separate decisions with different degrees of responsibility. CNN is not automatically liable for what a contributor says on another company’s program. But editorial responsibility is broader than legal liability. A network cannot make a pundit valuable through constant exposure, benefit from his provocations and then treat scrutiny of his conduct as somebody else’s departmental problem.

The scapegoat machine

The political function of the disputed clip was straightforward: turn a left-wing streamer into evidence that Democratic politics harbors extremism. AlterNet describes Republicans using Piker as a boogeyman against Democratic midterm candidates. If the complaint’s account of the editing is accurate, the maneuver was not criticism of what Piker said. It was the manufacture of a more useful thing for him to have said.

That is how scapegoating works in modern campaign media. A weaker actor’s words are stripped of context, recirculated by a party committee and repeated by a credentialed commentator. The accusation then becomes the story, while the institutions that produced and amplified it fade into the background.

Silence is also framing

CNN’s scant treatment, as described by Hall, redirects attention away from its own role. The issue is not that the network must declare Jennings guilty before adjudication. It can report the complaint, describe the allegation, seek responses and state clearly that the case is unresolved. That is routine journalism.

Ignoring the case on air instead creates an asymmetry. Piker can be extensively covered as a political liability for Democrats, while a serious allegation against the CNN personality helping construct that image receives minimal institutional scrutiny. The network’s apparent standard is therefore not simply newsworthiness. It is newsworthiness constrained by self-interest.

The pundit loophole

The larger pattern is a media business model built to harvest provocation while dispersing accountability. Networks hire personalities whose value comes from conflict across television, radio and social platforms. When those personalities generate attention, the network claims the energy. When their outside conduct becomes damaging, it invokes the corporate boundary.

That boundary may matter in court. Politically, it is a loophole. Institutions manufacture authority through repetition and visibility, then pretend that authority disappears whenever responsibility arrives. The result is a system in which inflammatory claims travel at full institutional speed, while corrections, context and accountability are forced to proceed one lawsuit—and one conveniently neglected story—at a time.
