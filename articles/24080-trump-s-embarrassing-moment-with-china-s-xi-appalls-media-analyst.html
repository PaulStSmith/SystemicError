Trump's 'embarrassing' moment with China's Xi appalls media analyst

Based on an article by Daniel Hampton

The White House Wanted Obedience, Not Journalism

The Context

During Xi Jinping’s reported state visit on Thursday, Donald Trump joked that China had the “friendliest press corps” and observed that Chinese reporters ask Xi for permission to speak. That same morning, Trump’s White House continued denying entry to journalists from CNN, MS NOW, and Politico for hours after U.S. District Judge Timothy Kelly ordered their access restored.

Power Was Never in Doubt

The excluded reporters did not control White House credentials. The administration did. Trump had banned the three organizations the previous week after labeling them “FAKE NEWS,” and White House personnel enforced that decision by refusing entry and, in some cases, confiscating passes.

Judge Kelly—himself appointed by Trump—found that the passes had been revoked without “constitutionally adequate due process.” Yet CNN reporter Betsy Klein was reportedly rejected at least four times before gaining entry around noon. The outlets returned to court for an emergency hearing before full access was restored around lunchtime.

Calling this a dispute between the president and the press would flatten the power structure. One side possessed the state-controlled access system. The other was trying to report on the state.

The Joke Revealed the Standard

China’s press corps appeared “friendly” because, as Trump reportedly acknowledged, its reporters seek Xi’s permission to speak. In an authoritarian system that jails journalists and requires media loyalty, that friendliness is not evidence of a healthy relationship. It is the visible product of political control.

The report does not establish that Trump was announcing a plan to reproduce China’s media system. It does show him praising—or at minimum admiring as a joke—the outward deference produced by that system while his administration was punishing American outlets he had denounced.

The contrast is not subtle: permitted speech was treated as pleasant; independent scrutiny was treated as grounds for exclusion.

“Embarrassment” Is Too Gentle

Axios correspondent Sara Fischer called the episode a national embarrassment and emphasized the contradiction between America’s stated commitment to press freedom and the administration’s conduct. Her criticism was justified, but embarrassment describes the country’s damaged image, not the government action that caused it.

The sharper fact is institutional retaliation followed by delayed compliance with a judicial order. The administration used access to a public institution against disfavored news organizations, then reportedly kept blocking reporters after a federal judge ordered restoration. Whether officials intended outright defiance is not established by the account; the practical consequence was still that the court’s protection meant little for several consequential hours.

Professionalism Is Not the Counterweight

Fischer praised the White House press corps for remaining professional. Professional reporting matters, but it should not be mistaken for an institutional remedy.

Journalists cannot enforce their own credentials, compel executive compliance, or neutralize the president’s power to brand critical coverage “fake.” Their restraint may preserve credibility. It does not redistribute authority. Framing professionalism as proof that American press freedom endures risks turning reporters’ composure into a substitute for government obedience to constitutional process.

Authoritarianism Arrives as Administration

The larger pattern is not merely hostile rhetoric. It is the conversion of personal grievance into official procedure: the president condemns particular outlets, the executive apparatus withdraws access, and a court order must intervene to restore what should not have been revoked without due process.

Authoritarian political culture is often recognizable before it becomes comprehensive. It appears when loyalty is praised as friendliness, scrutiny is recast as illegitimacy, and legal restraints are treated as obstacles to be honored only after pressure mounts. The danger lies in the machinery: once state access can be conditioned on favorable treatment, censorship no longer requires a formal ban. It needs only officials willing to turn the president’s resentments into administrative fact.
