Changpeng Zhao, the billionaire founder of crypto exchange Binance who recently received a pardon after months of boosting a crypto venture owned by President Donald Trump’s family, reportedly asked Warren to retract a social media post about him. Specifically, the post claimed Zhao had pleaded guilty to a money laundering charge, which Warren says is objectively true.

Writer Brendan Pedersen flagged a letter sent by Warren’s attorneys.

“We obtained a letter sent by lawyers for Sen. Elizabeth Warren (D-Mass.) today in response to a defamation threat from Binance founder Changpeng Zhao. CZ wants Warren to retract a tweet saying he pleaded guilty to a ‘criminal money laundering charge,’” he wrote before quoting the response statement.

“Your client asserts that Senator Warren has published a ‘defamatory’ statement about him in an October 23, 2025, post on X. Specifically, we understand your client to maintain that it is objectively false that he ‘pleaded guilty to a criminal money laundering charge.’ In relevant part, Senator Warren’s post stated that ‘CZ pleaded guilty to a criminal money laundering charge and was sentenced to prison.’ Senator Warren’s post is true in all respects and therefore cannot be defamatory,” the letter says. “Senator Warren accurately represented publicly available and widely reported facts. The ‘charge’ referenced in Senator Warren’s X post refers to the ‘charge’ to which Mr. Zhao pled guilty and as to which President Trump had just pardoned him.”

The letter adds, “The law Mr. Zhao pled guilty to violating is an anti-money laundering law. All of this is public record, and is plainly what Senator Warren’s X post concerned. Simply put, any threatened defamation claim would be without merit. We provide further context below, which we trust resolves any misunderstanding as to what Senator Warren’s statement means.”

Our Analysis:

The Billionaire’s Bluff vs. The Senator’s Stand

In an era where the truth is often stranger than fiction, the recent spat between Changpeng Zhao, a pardoned billionaire, and Senator Elizabeth Warren unfolds like a modern political thriller, rife with intrigue, power plays, and a fierce battle over the interpretation of facts.

The Heart of the Matter

At the core of this dispute is Zhao’s attempt to silence criticism through legal intimidation, a tactic as old as time but freshly emboldened in the age of social media and rapid news cycles. Zhao, having been recently pardoned by Donald Trump—a detail dripping with its own set of controversies—found himself at the sharp end of Warren’s pen, or more accurately, her social media presence.

Warren’s post, which Zhao labels as defamatory, stated plainly that Zhao had “pleaded guilty to a criminal money laundering charge” and was sentenced to prison—a statement rooted in the unyielding ground of fact, as her lawyers were quick to point out.

The Legal Dance

The legal retort from Warren’s camp is a masterclass in the art of the legal rebuke. It’s sharp, concise, and unyieldingly grounded in the public record. The letter essentially boils down to a simple premise: You can’t defame someone with the truth.

Zhao’s charge and subsequent pardon are not whispered rumors in the halls of power but well-documented facts, widely reported and easily accessible to anyone with an internet connection. Warren’s reference to these facts, therefore, cannot be construed as defamatory.

The Bigger Picture

This incident is emblematic of a larger, more concerning trend: the use of legal threats to stifle criticism and suppress free speech. It’s a maneuver that seeks to weaponize the legal system against factual discourse, particularly discourse that is critical of individuals in positions of power.

Senator Warren, a long-time advocate for transparency and accountability, especially in the financial sector, finds herself once again defending these principles, not just on her behalf but as a matter of public interest. This isn’t merely a squabble between a senator and a billionaire; it’s a litmus test for the state of free speech and the robustness of truth in a post-factual era.

Conclusion

As we dissect this incident, it’s essential to recognize the broader implications at play. This isn’t just about whether Zhao feels aggrieved by Warren’s words. It’s about the ongoing battle for truth in a time when facts are often treated as malleable and the truth as negotiable.

In this instance, Elizabeth Warren stands not just as a senator from Massachusetts but as a bulwark against the erosion of factual discourse. Her response, through her attorneys, is more than a legal rebuttal; it’s a declaration that the truth, no matter how inconvenient, will not be bullied into silence.
