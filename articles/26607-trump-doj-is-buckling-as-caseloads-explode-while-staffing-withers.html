Trump DOJ is buckling as caseloads explode while staffing withers

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 13, 20263 min read

What happened

AlterNet, citing The Hill’s review of internal Justice Department metrics, reports that the Trump administration’s immigration policies have sharply increased the department’s legal workload while staffing has fallen. The Civil Division’s Office of Immigration Litigation lost nearly 80 attorneys as its workload rose 63 percent.

The office reportedly shrank from about 320 attorneys at the start of Trump’s second term to 243 by August, while its caseload climbed from roughly 7,700 to nearly 9,500. Former DOJ official Margy O’Herron attributed the surge partly to historically low asylum approvals and the administration’s decision to deny bond hearings, which generated more habeas petitions from detained migrants.

The pressure could increase further. The DOJ said it filed 40 denaturalization cases from late August through early October, while an anonymous former staffer said attorneys had been given a target of 300. The administration has also fired and replaced immigration judges, including within the Board of Immigration Appeals.

Power created this crisis

This is not an unexplained collision between rising demand and scarce personnel. The administration controls the policies producing the cases, the detention practices provoking legal challenges, and the staffing decisions weakening the offices assigned to defend those policies.

Its choices expanded detention to people whom O’Herron described as longtime residents without criminal histories. Cutting off bond hearings then forced detainees to seek judicial review through habeas petitions. The government created more confinement, restricted an ordinary avenue for challenging it, and produced the litigation that followed.

Cruelty has paperwork

Administrative capacity is often treated as separate from policy, as though staff shortages merely interfere with an otherwise coherent agenda. Here, capacity is part of the political story. Aggressive enforcement generates legal obligations, even when the administration would prefer immigrants to disappear into detention without meaningful resistance.

Each additional case represents more than an inconvenient file. Asylum denials must be defended. Detention must be justified. Attempts to revoke citizenship require litigation. The caseload is the institutional residue of decisions imposed on people with far less power than the officials making them.

The buckling frame misdirects

Saying the DOJ is “buckling” accurately describes strain but obscures agency. The department did not simply encounter a natural disaster of immigration litigation. Trump officials intensified enforcement, reduced avenues for release, pursued more denaturalizations, removed personnel, and remade the immigration adjudication system.

The weaker actors are migrants responding to detention, rejected asylum claims, or threatened loss of citizenship. Their petitions did not originate the crisis. They are attempts to make the government account for its conduct. Treating those cases as the burden reverses the power relationship: the state acts, the targeted person challenges it, and the challenge is blamed for exhausting the state.

Institutional depletion serves executive power

The reporting does not establish that staffing losses were deliberately engineered to obstruct legal review. But the foreseeable effect is clear. Fewer government lawyers facing more cases means less time for careful legal work, more pressure on remaining staff, and greater risk that volume will substitute for judgment.

Firing and replacing immigration judges compounds that danger. An administration that simultaneously changes adjudicators, expands enforcement, and overwhelms the lawyers handling the resulting disputes is not merely managing immigration more aggressively. It is concentrating policy control while degrading the institutions expected to process objections.

Dysfunction as a governing pattern

The larger pattern is government by escalation: impose harsher rules, generate predictable resistance, hollow out the machinery responsible for handling it, and describe the resulting breakdown as an external burden.

That framing protects the officials with authority and shifts attention toward those trapped inside the system. The DOJ’s strain is real, but it is not politically mysterious. It is what happens when executive power treats legal process as an obstacle, human beings as enforcement inventory, and institutional competence as expendable.
