Trump's own appointee holds his admin in contempt for defying 3 court orders

Based on an article by Erik De La Garza

Three Court Orders, Two Deportations, One Government That Would Not Stop

The Government Knew the Boundary

According to Politico legal affairs reporter Kyle Cheney, the Trump administration deported 19-year-old asylum seeker E.L.A. to El Salvador on September 1, 2026, despite federal court orders issued in November 2024, April 2025, and July 2026. U.S. District Judge Stephanie Gallagher held the government in contempt and ordered officials to return him, restore belongings taken during his removal, and provide five business days’ notice and a legal justification before any future deportation attempt.

“Communication Errors” Do Not Explain Repetition

Justice Department lawyers attributed the removal to communication errors. That explanation does not account for the institutional record described in the ruling.

E.L.A. had already been deported in March and returned on August 7. The government then removed him again while he awaited a psychological evaluation connected to his asylum case. Gallagher had already held the administration in contempt in July. An error can explain a missed message; invoking error after repeated removals and repeated orders functions as an attempt to dissolve agency inside the bureaucracy.

The responsible actor here was not the teenager navigating an asylum system. It was the federal government, whose officials controlled his custody, transportation, possessions, and access to the legal process.

Due Process Was Treated as an Obstacle

The Maryland case protects asylum seekers who arrived as unaccompanied minors. Gallagher’s orders did not grant E.L.A. asylum or immunize him permanently from removal. They required the government to wait for a final immigration decision—or the expiration of the applicable settlement—before acting.

That distinction matters. The administration did not merely lose a dispute over immigration policy. According to Gallagher’s contempt finding, it exercised removal power in violation of binding judicial restrictions governing when that power could be used.

The foreseeable consequence was severe: a young asylum seeker was expelled from the country while still pursuing the process the government was legally required to respect. The court’s order to return him is therefore not procedural fussiness. It is an attempt to restore a legal position federal officials had already disrupted twice.

The Court Is Being Forced to Micromanage Obedience

Gallagher ordered the government to return E.L.A., give back his belongings, refrain from executing a final removal order for the protected period, and provide advance notice before trying again. Those details expose the depth of the institutional failure.

Courts ordinarily issue legal commands; they should not have to design an operational checklist to prevent the executive branch from repeating the same prohibited act. When a judge must specify notice periods, demand legal justifications in advance, and order the restoration of seized property, the dispute is no longer about an ambiguous policy boundary. It is about whether executive agencies can be trusted to obey orders without continuous supervision.

Gallagher was appointed by Trump. That fact does not make her ruling more valid, but it strips away the administration’s easiest partisan misdirection. The conflict described here is not between Trump and an opposing party’s judge. It is between executive power and judicial enforcement.

Accountability Cannot End at “Someone Miscommunicated”

The reporting does not establish which officials authorized the September 1 removal or whether any individual intentionally defied the court. It therefore does not prove a coordinated plan to sabotage E.L.A.’s case. But the absence of a named official is itself politically consequential: state power acted decisively against a vulnerable person, while responsibility retreated into an unspecified communication failure.

That asymmetry is familiar. The government’s coercive machinery can identify, detain, dispossess, and deport an individual with precision. Asked who caused an unlawful outcome, the same machinery suddenly becomes fog.

A contempt finding matters only if it penetrates that fog. Institutional accountability requires determining who received the orders, who controlled the removal, who approved it, and why prior judicial intervention failed to stop a second deportation.

Executive Defiance Becomes a System Before It Becomes a Doctrine

This case sits alongside another unresolved contempt dispute identified in the reporting: in June 2026, the full D.C. Circuit agreed to reconsider whether a criminal contempt inquiry concerning the administration’s 2025 deportation flights could proceed. The article also notes a separate access confrontation involving the White House and another Trump-appointed judge. These proceedings are distinct, and none alone establishes a single coordinated strategy.

Together, however, they reveal the institutional pattern that deserves scrutiny: executive action happens first, judicial review follows, and remediation must chase consequences already imposed on people with far less power than the officials responsible.

The danger in that arrangement is concrete. If the government can violate an order, describe the violation as confusion, and leave courts to retrieve the person afterward, obedience becomes optional at the moment it matters most. A legal system cannot restrain executive power when officials treat compliance as an administrative preference and contempt as merely another stage of litigation.
