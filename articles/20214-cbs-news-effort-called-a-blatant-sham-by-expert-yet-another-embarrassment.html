CBS News effort called a 'blatant sham' by expert: 'Yet another embarrassment'

CBS’s Ombudsman Was Never Meant to Answer to the Public

Accountability as Stage Prop

CBS News has wrapped a simple reality in the language of reform: the people with power chose a reviewer who answers upward, not outward, and then sold that arrangement as public accountability. Margaret Sullivan’s complaint is not that the ombudsman has been imperfect. It is that the role was built to be performative from the start.

Kenneth R. Weinstein, tapped by the network’s Trump-aligned new owners, is not presented here as an independent check on editorial power. He is described as a right-leaning think tank veteran, a Republican donor, and a man with no background supervising news coverage. That is not a neutral résumé. It is the profile of someone selected for compliance, then dressed up as oversight.

Power Sits Upstairs

The real decision-makers are obvious. CBS’s new owners installed the arrangement. Editor-in-chief Bari Weiss sits inside the editorial chain. Corporate leadership controls the structure. Weinstein, by Sullivan’s account, is not a watchdog facing the audience; he is a filter reporting to corporate bosses.

That distinction matters. If an ombudsman cannot speak publicly, cannot answer complaints in any visible way, and works only one day a month, then the job is not accountability. It is containment. It exists to absorb criticism without letting criticism travel anywhere useful.

The Complaints Are the Point

Sullivan’s examples show what this setup is designed to avoid. She points to the delayed airing of a report on an El Salvador prison while a merger was pending, the departure of major talent, the collapse of ratings, and questions about whether Weiss’s pro-Israel views compromise Gaza coverage. These are not random internal nuisances. They are exactly the kinds of pressures that reveal how editorial independence gets traded away.

The ombudsman’s silence is itself the story. When the public asks whether coverage is being bent, delayed, or managed for business reasons, the institution responds with a functionary who appears to do almost nothing. That is not transparency. It is a procedural shrug.

Blame Gets Reassigned Downward

The larger trick is familiar: institutional decisions get translated into ambient “tumult.” Networks, owners, and executives make choices; then the resulting damage is framed as a weather system. Ratings fall, staff leave, trust erodes, and suddenly the problem is the “chaos” rather than the people who engineered it.

Sullivan refuses that laundering. She names the ownership change, the political alignment of the new power bloc, and the fact that the ombudsman role was built to serve the company, not the audience. That is the core misdirection in the CBS story. The network wants the optics of self-scrutiny without any of the exposure.

What This Reveals

This is not just about CBS News. It is about a corporate media pattern in which “independence” survives as branding after its substance has been stripped out. A newsroom can keep the vocabulary of public service while handing actual control to owners, donors, and politically convenient appointees. It can stage oversight while ensuring nobody with authority is ever forced to answer in public.

That is the system at work here: power centralizes, accountability gets privatized, and the audience is asked to mistake silence for process.
