Americans can’t afford to just 'move on' again

Based on an article by John Stoehr

Paulo SantosNovember 27, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Affordability and corruption are not competing agendasThe lesson Washington chose not to learnA case must come before a prosecutionThe damage is not confined to Washington

Americans cannot afford another official return to “normal” that leaves abuses of power intact. The argument advanced in AlterNet is that Democrats’ next turn in power should link economic relief to accountability: investigate corruption, establish facts, and pursue criminal cases where evidence supports them. Political scientist Joshua Zingher’s warning is blunt: Donald Trump learned from escaping substantial consequences after his first term, and future officials will study the same lesson unless misconduct carries a real cost.

That is the article’s strongest case. Its weakest is the attempt to turn a strategic dispute into a hunt for a Democratic “fifth column.” Skepticism about investigations can reflect cowardice, opportunism or bad judgment without proving that critics secretly want authoritarianism—or that they want Democrats to fail. Accountability requires evidence and due process. So does an accusation of betrayal.

Affordability and corruption are not competing agendas

The supposed choice facing congressional Democrats is between lowering household costs and investigating wrongdoing. Incoming House Speaker Hakeem Jeffries has rejected that separation in his rhetoric about “the cost of corruption,” a phrase that treats corruption as material governance rather than an abstract offense against etiquette.

That connection matters. Officials who exploit public authority for private benefit do not operate in a sealed moral compartment. Their decisions determine who receives government favors, who bears regulatory costs and whether public resources serve citizens or insiders. Investigating those arrangements can therefore expose economic harms as well as legal ones.

Zingher told AlterNet’s John Stoehr that Democrats should begin by pursuing corruption “on all fronts.” He pointed to alleged misconduct involving Trump’s cryptocurrency activities, Nvidia and congressional stock trading, though the interview does not establish criminal liability in any of those matters. His proposal is to investigate first, explain the evidence publicly and prosecute only conduct that can be shown to violate the law.

That distinction is essential. A government that manufactures charges against opponents is practicing political persecution. A government that declines to enforce the law against powerful people is practicing selective impunity. Treating those two abuses as equivalent protects officeholders by making any attempt at accountability appear vindictive.

The lesson Washington chose not to learn

Zingher expects pressure on Democrats to “move on” if they win the presidency in 2028. He sees a precedent in 2021, when many Democrats wanted relief from the first Trump administration and a restoration of ordinary government. In his account, the pursuit of normalcy did not secure normal government. It left intact the expectation that Republicans could regain power without facing durable institutional restraints.

“Normalcy is a privilege,” he told AlterNet. His point is less nostalgic than procedural: stable government depends on officials believing that constitutional and legal limits will be enforced even after the immediate crisis has passed.

Zingher argues that Trump interpreted the limited punishment following his first term as permission. That is an inference about Trump’s lesson, not a documented admission from him, but the institutional incentive is plain. When misconduct produces political turbulence but few lasting consequences, the next officeholder has little reason to regard restraint as mandatory.

Accountability cannot guarantee deterrence. It can, however, change the calculation. Investigations can expose mechanisms of abuse; legislation can close opportunities revealed by those inquiries; prosecutions supported by admissible evidence can demonstrate that public office does not confer personal immunity. Zingher also invokes the Supreme Court’s reasoning in Trump v. United States as an obstacle to restoring that principle.

A case must come before a prosecution

The interview becomes more troubling when it shifts from investigating reported corruption to “building the case for prosecutions in 2029 against people like Hegseth.” No specific offense by Pete Hegseth is identified in the supplied account. Naming a future defendant before presenting the alleged crime reverses the standard Zingher otherwise says Democrats must uphold.

He correctly notes that the public must be convinced officials committed actual crimes and are not being punished for their politics. But that obligation begins with the investigators themselves. Congress can uncover facts, refer evidence and write laws. Prosecutors must still make independent charging decisions based on conduct and proof, not a party’s promised timetable.

The same discipline applies to the article’s denunciation of left-wing critics. Stoehr describes some self-identified progressives as bad actors who use affordability politics to help Democrats fail and advance themselves. Zingher calls a small authoritarian-left faction “fellow travelers with Trump.” The report establishes that both men hold those views; it does not establish the motives they assign to an undefined group.

“Fifth column” is not analysis unless the alleged coordination or disloyalty is demonstrated. Used loosely, it turns dissent into subversion and reproduces the authoritarian habit the article condemns: replacing an argument about conduct with a declaration that inconvenient people are internal enemies.

The damage is not confined to Washington

AlterNet also places immigration at the center of the consequences. Drawing on an essay by Cory Doctorow, Stoehr argues that two years of enforcement and fear drove documented and undocumented immigrants from the United States, depriving an aging country of workers, families and future citizens. He contends that many will build their lives elsewhere rather than return.

That harm, as described, cannot be repaired by a congressional hearing or a change in tone. People who have fled, relocated their families or abandoned plans do not automatically reverse those decisions when an administration changes. The state can halt an abusive policy faster than it can restore the trust the policy destroyed.

This is where the argument against “moving on” becomes concrete. The issue is not whether Democrats should spend their time punishing enemies instead of lowering prices. It is whether they will identify how public power was used, repair what remains repairable and impose lawful consequences where evidence warrants them.

Democrats will eventually lose power again; that is not failure but the ordinary condition of electoral government. What they leave behind will matter more than the duration of their control. If they substitute revenge for law, they validate the machinery of persecution. If they substitute relief and ceremony for enforcement, they preserve a different privilege: the right of powerful officials to wait out public outrage and try again.
