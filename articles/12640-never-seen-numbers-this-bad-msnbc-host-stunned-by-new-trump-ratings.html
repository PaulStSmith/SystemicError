Political strategist Cornell Belcher said the polling done by his organization, Brilliant Corners Research & Strategies, shows Donald Trump's job approval and favorability are at an all-time low with the Black community.

"I've been around doing polling for 30 years; I've never seen an official's numbers as bad as they are among Black voters, as Donald Trump's is right now," Belcher said, causing Jansing to interrupt.

"Really? So, you just broke some news here! What's behind it?" Jansing asked.

"What's behind it is a lot of the economic angst, but also, quite frankly, look the racism, right? What Donald Trump has done in the first moves in office is go after DEI, go after affirmative action programs...take Civil Rights leaders' names off of ships, right? If he was as focused on bringing down prices as he is focused on on diversity and equity, and stripping away diversity and equity in our government, we'd have really low, low prices right now. "

Jansing asked if that poll included young Black men, "many of whom voted in higher numbers for Donald Trump because of economic issues?"

"Of course it does," Belcher said, before clarifying, "If you look at the aggregate percentage of Black voters overall who voted for Donald Trump, it's nothing historic."

Belcher added, "I'm less worried about the number of Blacks who voted for Donald Trump and more worried about the horrendous turnout among among younger voters of both Black and Brown that we saw in the last election, and it means death for the Democrats and progressive movement moving forward if we cannot...find a better way to engage and meet these younger voters."

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Disconnect: Trump's Unfavorables Among Black Voters

In a recent segment that could only be described as an eye-opener for those insulated in a bubble of political naivety, MSNBC's Chris Jansing and political strategist Cornell Belcher laid bare the stark unpopularity Donald Trump faces among Black voters. According to polling done by Belcher's organization, Brilliant Corners Research & Strategies, Trump's approval and favorability within the Black community have plummeted to what Belcher describes as unprecedented lows. This revelation, while not shocking to those who have paid even a modicum of attention to Trump's policies and rhetoric, serves as a critical pulse check on the Black community's reception of Trump's political maneuverings.

The Root of the Rejection

Economic Angst and Racism: The reasons behind this massive disapproval are hardly a conundrum. Belcher points to "economic angst" and, more blatantly, "racism" as the colossal elephants in the room. Trump's tenure in office was marked by a series of actions that could only be interpreted as hostile to the principles of diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI). From attacking affirmative action programs to the symbolic erasure of Civil Rights leaders' names from federal properties, Trump's policies have been perceived as a direct affront to the Black community. Belcher quips rather pointedly that if Trump had been as focused on reducing prices as he was on dismantling DEI efforts, the economic landscape might look markedly different.

The Young Black Voter Dilemma

An interesting facet of Belcher's analysis, however, shifts focus from the Black community's overall disdain towards Trump to the worrying trend of decreased turnout among younger Black and Brown voters. This demographic's disillusionment or disengagement poses a significant threat to the Democratic Party and, by extension, the progressive movement. It's a clarion call for a strategic overhaul in how these groups are engaged and mobilized.

Between the Lines

While the segment does an admirable job of highlighting the dire approval ratings of Trump among Black voters, it also inadvertently shines a light on a larger, more systemic issue. The Democratic Party's reliance on the Black vote without adequately addressing Black voters' needs and concerns, or taking their support for granted, could be a ticking time bomb for future elections.

Furthermore, the conversation about the higher numbers of young Black men who voted for Trump due to economic reasons should not be overlooked. It underscores a critical failure in messaging and policy prioritization by Trump's opponents, who have yet to find a resonant economic narrative that appeals to this demographic.

Conclusion

The dissection of Trump's unpopularity among Black voters by Jansing and Belcher offers a glimpse into the broader political landscape and the shifting allegiances within the Black community. However, the real takeaway here should be the urgent need for genuine, sustained engagement with Black and Brown voters by those who claim to represent their interests. Ignoring the root causes of their disillusionment or failing to mobilize this crucial demographic could indeed spell "death for the Democrats and progressive movement moving forward," as Belcher rightly warns. Let this be a wake-up call to re-evaluate not just how political campaigns are run, but how genuinely inclusive and responsive political platforms are to the needs of those they seek to serve.
