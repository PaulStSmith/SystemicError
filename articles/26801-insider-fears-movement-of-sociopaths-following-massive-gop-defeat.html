Insider fears 'movement of sociopaths' following massive GOP defeat

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 24, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that conservative writer Nick Catoggio fears a major Republican electoral defeat would not moderate the party but drive it toward even more extreme candidates. The article does not establish that the “massive GOP defeat” in its headline has occurred; Catoggio is discussing what Republicans might do after a prospective November drubbing.

Catoggio cites former New Jersey Gov. Chris Christie’s condemnation of a Republican attack ad in Ohio and Vice President J.D. Vance’s defense of it, though AlterNet does not describe the ad’s contents. He also points to a Wednesday Truth Social post in which, according to AlterNet, Donald Trump called Democrat Amy Acton a liar over her account of childhood sexual abuse. The outlet reports that her stepfather was indicted by a grand jury in connection with the alleged crime and later fled.

Catoggio predicts that Republican activists would explain electoral losses as a failure of turnout or ideological purity, then promote Vance-style isolationists and similarly aligned congressional candidates. That forecast—not a documented party decision—is the basis for his description of postliberalism as a “movement of sociopaths.”

Power is not an infection

Christie says the Republican Party has been “infected” by a corrupted moral center. That metaphor conceals agency. A party does not contract attack ads, smears, or political cruelty like a virus. Candidates authorize them. Officials defend them. Influential media figures rationalize them. Voters and donors reward them.

The institutional power here belongs to Trump, Vance, party officials, major funders, and the media personalities capable of defining Republican orthodoxy. Christie can diagnose the decay, but he no longer controls the organization. His objection illustrates the balance of power precisely because the people he condemns possess the offices, audience, and machinery that he lacks.

Cruelty with a distribution network

Trump’s reported attack on Acton matters beyond one social-media post. Calling a political opponent a liar about alleged childhood sexual abuse turns personal trauma into campaign ammunition. The foreseeable effect is to punish disclosure and signal that no vulnerability is protected from partisan exploitation.

But precision matters. AlterNet supplies no evidence that every postliberal conservative shares one motive or psychological condition. “Sociopaths” is Catoggio’s condemnation, not a clinical finding. The substantiated political point is narrower and stronger: prominent Republicans are willing to use or defend degrading attacks, and their movement’s power structure provides mechanisms for amplifying them.

The alibi of irrational voters

Catoggio places considerable weight on what the right-wing base may do after defeat. That risks shifting responsibility downward. Ordinary voters influence primaries, but they do not independently manufacture candidate fields, finance campaigns, commission advertising, or give commentators national platforms.

Party elites may claim that “real Trumpism has never been tried” or blame an unreliable working-class electorate. If they do, those explanations will serve an institutional purpose: protecting the people who selected the strategy from accountability for its consequences. An electoral loss does not compel leaders to learn. It merely creates a struggle over which faction gets to define the lesson.

Defeat is not reform

The comforting theory is that sufficient electoral punishment forces a party back toward restraint. Catoggio’s forecast exposes the missing mechanism. Losing can weaken officeholders without dislodging the donors, propagandists, primary incentives, and safe districts that reward escalation.

In heavily Republican seats, ideological actors can nominate harsher candidates while facing limited danger from the opposing party. That structure allows national rejection and internal radicalization to occur simultaneously. A smaller caucus can become more extreme because defeat removes marginal members while leaving the safest—and often least accountable—political terrain intact.

The system reproduces the conduct

This story is ultimately not about whether Republicans can “regain their soul.” That language turns organized choices into spiritual weather. The relevant question is whether cruelty continues to bring attention, loyalty, money, nominations, and protection from consequences.

AlterNet’s thin description of the Ohio ad leaves an important evidentiary gap, and its headline overstates a defeat the article presents as prospective. Yet the structural warning survives that weak framing: elections can remove officials, but they do not automatically dismantle the machinery that selected them. When humiliation is rewarded and accountability is redirected toward weaker actors, electoral defeat can become fuel for the next round rather than a cure.
