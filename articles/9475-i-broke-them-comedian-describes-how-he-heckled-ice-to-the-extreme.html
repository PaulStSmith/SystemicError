'I broke them': Comedian describes how he heckled ICE to the extreme

Comedian and activist Robby Roadsteamer dressed as a giraffe (Photo: Screen capture)

Robby Roadsteamer believes that he broke the officers of the U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) in Portland, Oregon, after dressing up like a giraffe and singing his own version of Rod Stewart’s “Da Ya Think I’m Sexy?”

President Donald Trump has sent in National Guard soldiers and federal agents into Washington, D.C., Portland, and Chicago, Illinois, under the guise that there is too much crime in so-called “blue cities.”

Using alternative lyrics and occasionally shouting heckles at “glorified mall cops” with “super-soakers,” Roadsteamer told The Bulwark’s Tim Miller that he thinks he finally “broke them.”

“It’s a really fun scene to a certain degree,” Roadsteamer said of the protest/gatherings in Portland made up of tents with food and performance artists.

“So I’m doing the Rod Stewart and I’m making fun of them left and right. They’re getting pissed. They’re getting pissed. There’s no question. And you see the video today, they’re teeing off on me with pepperballs. Like you can see the dust. it looks like Rambo!” he recalled.

“So, it’s like, come on, come on, what’s the name of that plane? I broke them. You could see the guys on the top of the roof very — completely frustrated,” he continued. “And so they send out the guys, and you can see the video and stuff, and they grab me. And yeah they pull me into the detention center.”

Miller asked what he was charged with, and Roadsteamer pulled out a long sheet of paper that he said resembled a CVS receipt.

“It’s a citation,” he said. “And it said, basically, failure to comply with the directions of a law enforcement officer.”

They said he kept crossing the “blue line” where the protest area was blocked off. The problem, however, is that Roadsteamer brought his own people to film the ordeal. The videos don’t show him crossing the line until the officers drag him over it.

“I was like, you guys got nothin on me! I’m from New England! We got six rings! Don’t tell me how to do this thing,” he quipped, citing the six Super Bowl rings for the New England Patriots.

Roadsteamer and Miller met at the post-prison press conference with Steve Bannon where Roadsteamer heckled Bannon. He was able to “infiltrate” the press, he said, by wearing an all-red suit. Roadsteamer asked when the next insurrection would be.

“But they want me to come back to Portland, that’s the inconvenience,” said Roadsteamer. “They thought they could humiliate me by — I have to go back to Boston. You know, when they realized I wasn’t getting scared by their ‘federal charges’ bulls----. They were like, you’re gonna have to come back to Portland. Well, it’s gonna suck! Well, I’m gonna come back with my dream team of lawyers, cause you guys are getting sued for $20 million. Because those pepper pills are toxic and I got all the footage of and stuff. You guys unlawfully detaining me here.”

Miller noted that while in Portland, Roadsteamer dressed as a unicorn, a frog, a hippo, and a giraffe. He was “smoking a joint” and “eating vegan macaroni and cheese.”

Our Analysis:

The Art of Political Satire: A Closer Look at Robby Roadsteamer’s Protest

In an era where political discourse often feels like navigating a minefield, the antics of Robby Roadsteamer offer a peculiar yet instructive case study. His method of protest—donning animal costumes and belting out a parody of Rod Stewart’s hit—against the U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) in Portland, Oregon, is not just a sideshow. It’s a mirror to the absurdity and tension woven into the fabric of American politics. Let’s dissect the layers of this bizarre saga and what it reveals about protest, authority, and the state of democracy.

The Spectacle vs. The Serious

At face value, Roadsteamer’s approach is ludicrous. A man dressed as a giraffe, taunting federal agents with a song, might seem like fodder for late-night comedy rather than a serious act of protest. Yet, beneath the surface humor, there’s a sharp critique of the militarization of police forces and the aggressive tactics used against protestors. The absurdity of Roadsteamer’s performance art underscores the surreal nature of federal agents, behaving more like an occupying force than protectors of peace, in cities they were sent to under the dubious pretext of combating crime.

The Response: Proving the Point

The reaction Roadsteamer elicited from ICE officers—being pelted with pepper balls—demonstrates a disproportionate use of force that activists have decried for years. The imagery he invokes, comparing the scene to Rambo, is a damning commentary on how law enforcement has turned urban protests into war zones. This escalation not only validates Roadsteamer’s critique but also highlights a concerning trend where the right to peaceful assembly is met with violence.

The Legal Entanglement: A Farce within a Farce

The charges against Roadsteamer, which he mockingly compared to a CVS receipt, epitomize the Kafkaesque legal battles many protestors find themselves in. Accused of failing to comply with law enforcement, despite evidence to the contrary, paints a picture of an authority more interested in silencing dissent than upholding justice. His quip about returning to Portland with a “dream team of lawyers” and suing for $20 million over the toxic effects of the pepper balls further satirizes the absurdity of the situation. It’s a bold declaration of fighting absurdity with absurdity, where the judicial system becomes another arena for protest.

Beyond the Laughter: A Serious Undertone

While Roadsteamer’s antics provide comic relief, they also serve as a critical commentary on the state of American democracy. His ability to “infiltrate” a press conference and directly question figures like Steve Bannon on the next insurrection is more than just a stunt; it’s a pointed critique of the vulnerabilities in our political and media ecosystems. The humor masks a deeper, more unsettling inquiry into how farcical elements have seeped into the very core of our political discourse.

Conclusion: The Clown as the Critic

Robby Roadsteamer, in his giraffe costume and with his parody song, emerges not just as a protestor but as a modern-day jester critiquing the king’s court. His methods, while unorthodox, cut through the noise, offering a unique lens through which to examine the excesses of authority and the state of protest in America. As we navigate these turbulent times, perhaps there’s room to appreciate the role of satire in sparking conversation and, hopefully, inspiring change.

In a landscape where traditional forms of protest are often met with heavy-handed responses, the clown, it seems, might just be the most effective critic we have.
