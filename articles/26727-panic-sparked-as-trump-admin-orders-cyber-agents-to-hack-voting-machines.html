Panic sparked as Trump admin orders cyber agents to hack voting machines

Based on an article by Andy Mannix And Doug Bock Clark, Propublica

Paulo SantosOctober 20, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power came from WashingtonSabotage disguised as securityTrust was part of the infrastructureThe panic had a rational objectThe systemic error

What happened

AlterNet reports that, early in President Donald Trump’s second term, political appointees at the Department of Homeland Security directed the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency to secretly obtain and hack a voting machine, then send its findings to the White House. CISA staff were also reportedly told not to notify the manufacturer about discovered vulnerabilities. Career employees resisted, fearing the work could be used to challenge election results.

The operation was never carried out. According to former officials, CISA no longer had the necessary personnel because employees capable of performing the work had been dismissed through Elon Musk’s Department of Government Efficiency initiative. The appointees eventually stopped pressing the request.

Meanwhile, the administration cut CISA’s workforce by more than 1,000 employees, ended or reduced election-security services, and released previously confidential information supplied by state partners. CISA published a 2026 election-security plan on September 24, after states had completed most preparations for the midterms.

The power came from Washington

The central actors were not unnamed “hackers” or anxious election administrators. They were presidential appointees exercising authority over the federal agency responsible for protecting election infrastructure.

That distinction matters. CISA had conducted authorized penetration testing before, but as part of a cooperative process designed to identify and repair vulnerabilities. The reported request inverted that model: secretly acquire a machine, conceal flaws from its manufacturer and deliver the results to the White House. Career staff did not mistake this for routine security work.

State officials possess substantial responsibility for administering elections, but they do not control federal intelligence, CISA staffing or DHS policy. The Trump administration does. When states were forced to purchase their own monitoring equipment or proceed without intelligence about foreign actors, that was not a vague coordination failure. It was the foreseeable result of federal decisions.

Sabotage disguised as security

Trump has repeatedly portrayed election systems as dangerously vulnerable, including through claims the reporting describes as disproven. His administration simultaneously dismantled the federal capacity built to find and fix genuine vulnerabilities.

That contradiction is politically useful. Remove personnel, monitoring and trusted communication; then point to the weakened system as evidence that elections cannot be trusted. The article does not establish that every cut was made to manufacture such a crisis. It does establish that the administration weakened CISA while seeking secret technical findings that staff feared could later be used against unwanted results.

The consequence is a government less able to detect real attacks and better positioned to circulate suspicion. Security becomes theater, while institutional damage supplies the scenery.

Trust was part of the infrastructure

CISA’s election work depended on states and vendors sharing sensitive information under assurances of confidentiality. That trust took years to build and could not be replaced by publishing a 13-page plan weeks before voting.

The administration’s release of raw state-supplied records fractured that arrangement. The White House denied breaking any agreement and defended the disclosure as transparency. But state authorities quoted in the reporting said they no longer trusted CISA with sensitive information. In cybersecurity, that refusal is not an interpersonal dispute. It deprives defenders of the information needed to recognize threats across jurisdictions.

The damage therefore extends beyond one election cycle. Personnel can eventually be rehired and sensors repurchased. A federal agency regarded as a partisan collection point cannot easily persuade states to expose their systems again.

The panic had a rational object

The headline’s “panic” was not primarily fear that CISA technicians might test a machine. It arose from the possibility that federal power could be used to seize election equipment, extract vulnerabilities and build a pretext for discrediting results.

Officials in one large jurisdiction reportedly planned with vendors for the possibility that the federal government might seize machines or declare them unusable. That contingency planning followed reported federal efforts to obtain access to machines in Missouri and seize machines in Puerto Rico, although the administration offered different explanations for the latter operation and the contractor involved found no evidence supporting claims about the 2020 election.

Calling this atmosphere mere polarization would misdirect responsibility toward frightened state officials. Their distrust followed concrete federal conduct: staffing cuts, withdrawn support, disclosure of sensitive material and an extraordinary hacking request originating from political appointees.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is the conversion of a protective institution into an instrument of political uncertainty. CISA once helped establish facts that constrained presidential falsehoods. Trump fired its director after the agency defended the 2020 election; in his second term, his administration stripped away staff, services and relationships that gave the agency independent credibility.

Institutional guardrails do not disappear only through formal abolition. They can be emptied, subordinated and then reintroduced too late to perform their stated function. What remains still carries the government’s name, but no longer commands the cooperation on which its mission depends.

That is the systemic danger exposed here: federal officials weakening election security while claiming exceptional concern about election security. The resulting vulnerability is not proof of their allegations. It is the consequence of their governance.
