You Can Admire the Machine Without Admiring the Man

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 16, 20268 min read

Elon Musk is not Tesla. And recognizing extraordinary engineering is not an endorsement of extraordinary egos.

At a SpaceX testing facility in Texas, the long-awaited second-generation Tesla Roadster demonstrated capabilities that would have seemed absurd in a production-oriented automobile just a few years ago.

It drove upside down using a powerful suction-fan system. It launched itself over other vehicles using cold-gas thrusters. It performed aerial maneuvers and landed back on its wheels. Tesla also demonstrated a claimed acceleration from zero to sixty miles per hour in approximately 0.81 seconds.

Think about that for a moment.

A car accelerating to highway speed in less than a second. A machine capable of driving upside down and jumping over a truck.

This isn’t quite the flying DeLorean from Back to the Future, but someone at Tesla clearly decided that the laws of physics were less a limitation than an engineering challenge.

And the engineers rose to it.

Now comes the uncomfortable part.

I think Elon Musk has become one of the most politically objectionable public figures in modern American life.

I find his political interventions troubling, his public behavior frequently reprehensible, and his increasingly close association with authoritarian politics deeply disturbing.

And I think what Tesla demonstrated was absolutely extraordinary.

Those two statements are not contradictory.

The fact that I disagree profoundly with the man at the top does not obligate me to pretend that the people working beneath him are incapable of brilliance.

Unfortunately, that distinction seems increasingly difficult for people to make.

When Everything Becomes a Loyalty Test

One of the more exhausting characteristics of modern political polarization is the expectation that our opinions must come in complete ideological packages.

If you support someone, you are expected to support everything associated with them.

If you oppose someone, you are expected to reject everything they touch.

It’s tribalism masquerading as moral consistency.

For some of Elon Musk’s most devoted admirers, every Tesla achievement is further evidence that the man is a once-in-a-generation genius whose judgment deserves deference in virtually every field.

For some of his fiercest critics, acknowledging a Tesla accomplishment feels uncomfortably close to surrendering political ground.

Both positions are ridiculous.

A remarkable engineering achievement does not become less remarkable because you dislike the company’s CEO.

Nor does successfully building a remarkable machine confer wisdom about immigration, elections, constitutional law, or the administration of government.

Engineering competence is not moral authority.

And moral objection is not a substitute for evaluating evidence.

It should be possible to say, “That is an incredible car,” without someone interpreting the statement as “Therefore, I endorse Elon Musk’s political worldview.”

Yet somehow, we’ve constructed a political culture in which even acknowledging reality can feel like betraying your team.

Elon Musk Did Not Build That Car Alone

There is another problem with treating every Tesla accomplishment as a referendum on Musk.

It erases the people who actually do the work.

Behind that Roadster are engineers who understand fluid dynamics, mechanical design, control systems, aerodynamics, materials science, software, and countless other disciplines.

There are technicians building prototypes, researchers testing components, programmers implementing control algorithms, and manufacturing specialists figuring out whether an impressive demonstration can become something reproducible.

These people have names, careers, expertise, and accomplishments of their own.

They are not interchangeable extensions of Elon Musk’s personality.

Yes, Musk occupies a leadership position. Yes, he has helped establish ambitious technical goals and has influence over the direction of the companies he leads. Pretending that leadership contributes nothing would be just as intellectually dishonest as attributing everything to it.

But there is a substantial difference between setting an objective and solving the thousands of technical problems required to achieve it.

The same principle applies to SpaceX.

The extraordinary accomplishment of routinely landing orbital-class rocket boosters deserves recognition. So do the engineers, scientists, and technicians who turned that once-implausible ambition into an operational capability.

We should be able to celebrate their work without treating their employer as a prophet.

Ironically, Musk’s most enthusiastic admirers and his most reflexive detractors sometimes make the same mistake.

They both make the story entirely about Elon Musk.

One group attributes every success to him.

The other finds it difficult to acknowledge success because of him.

Both approaches reduce thousands of talented professionals to supporting characters in one billionaire’s biography.

That is a disservice to the people whose work actually makes the technology possible.

Admiration Is Not Absolution

Of course, separating a person’s accomplishments from their conduct does not mean ignoring that conduct.

There is a very important difference between recognizing achievement and granting absolution.

A brilliant scientist can hold appalling political beliefs.

A gifted artist can behave reprehensibly.

A successful entrepreneur can demonstrate extraordinary business judgment in one area and astonishingly poor judgment in another.

Human beings are complicated, frequently inconsistent creatures.

Unfortunately, our political discourse often refuses to tolerate that complexity.

We prefer heroes and villains.

Heroes must be right about everything. Villains must be wrong about everything.

Once someone has been placed into one of those categories, every subsequent fact is expected to conform to the classification.

But reality does not cooperate.

Elon Musk can lead companies that produce impressive technology while simultaneously engaging in political behavior deserving serious criticism.

His engineering organizations’ successes do not excuse his politics.

His politics do not erase their engineering successes.

Neither fact cancels the other.

And we should be suspicious of anyone insisting that one must.

The Difference Between Admiring and Buying

There is, however, an important ethical distinction here.

Acknowledging that Tesla makes impressive vehicles is not the same as deciding to purchase one.

Buying a product is an economic decision with consequences. Depending on the company’s ownership and financial structure, your money can help support its business, its leadership, and ultimately the activities of those who benefit financially from its success.

A consumer may reasonably decide that they do not want their money supporting a company whose leadership they find politically objectionable.

That’s a legitimate ethical choice.

Someone else may decide that the product’s merits outweigh those concerns.

That, too, is a decision that deserves evaluation on its actual reasoning rather than automatic condemnation.

What neither consumer should need to do is falsify reality to justify the decision.

You do not have to pretend a Tesla is a terrible automobile simply because you refuse to buy one.

You do not have to pretend Elon Musk is a wonderful human being simply because you own one.

Your purchasing decisions can reflect your values without requiring you to distort your assessment of the product.

This distinction becomes particularly important in a global economy where almost every major corporation is connected, directly or indirectly, to people whose politics, conduct, or priorities we might find objectionable.

If moral purity requires approving of every individual who profits from a product, ethical consumption becomes nearly impossible.

That does not make ethical choices meaningless.

It means those choices require thought rather than slogans.

Skepticism Is Not Sabotage

None of this means Tesla should receive a free pass on its claims.

Quite the opposite.

The Roadster demonstration was impressive, but it was still a controlled demonstration conducted by Tesla.

The extraordinary acceleration figure has not yet been independently verified. A car capable of impressive aerial stunts is not necessarily a practical flying automobile. And a successful prototype does not guarantee a safe, affordable, regulatory-compliant production vehicle.

Tesla originally revealed the second-generation Roadster in 2017, with deliveries initially promised for 2020.

It’s now October 2026, and production is being targeted for 2028.

That history matters.

It would be foolish to treat another production promise as a certainty simply because the latest demonstration was spectacular.

But notice what we’re doing here.

We’re evaluating specific claims against available evidence.

We’re distinguishing what was demonstrated from what was promised.

We’re acknowledging legitimate engineering accomplishments while maintaining skepticism about marketing and deadlines.

That is what critical thinking is supposed to look like.

It is neither blind admiration nor reflexive dismissal.

And it works regardless of whether the person making the claim is someone we admire or someone we detest.

Indeed, the test of intellectual honesty is not whether we can identify weaknesses in the arguments of people we dislike.

That’s easy.

The test is whether we can recognize when those people are right, just as readily as we can recognize when our own preferred figures are wrong.

Reality Does Not Have a Political Affiliation

There is something particularly absurd about politicizing technological achievement.

Physics does not vote Republican or Democrat.

Mathematics does not care whether you are conservative, liberal, socialist, or libertarian.

A propulsion system generates thrust according to physical principles, not according to the political convictions of the person signing the engineers’ paychecks.

The same scientific methods that allow us to evaluate a vaccine’s effectiveness allow us to evaluate an automobile’s acceleration or a rocket’s performance.

Evidence is evidence.

We should not become selectively skeptical whenever the evidence is politically inconvenient.

And this is where the issue extends far beyond Tesla.

If we teach ourselves to reject facts merely because they benefit someone we dislike, we surrender one of the most important tools we have for understanding the world.

We begin replacing the question “Is this true?” with “Whose side does this help?”

Once that happens, objective reality becomes subordinate to political loyalty.

And that is dangerous.

It is precisely the kind of thinking that makes propaganda effective, allows misinformation to flourish, and turns disagreements over verifiable facts into endless tribal warfare.

For those of us who spend considerable time criticizing political movements for rejecting inconvenient evidence, the obligation is especially important.

We cannot demand intellectual honesty from our opponents while granting ourselves permission to ignore it.

Our standards either apply consistently, or they are not standards at all.

They are merely weapons.

Give Credit Where Credit Is Due

I have no intention of becoming an Elon Musk admirer.

His political conduct deserves scrutiny. His public statements deserve fact-checking. His business promises deserve skepticism, especially given the history of missed deadlines.

And if Tesla ultimately produces a Roadster capable of everything demonstrated this week, I will happily acknowledge the achievement.

Because somewhere behind that machine are people who spent years solving extraordinarily difficult problems.

Their work matters.

Their expertise matters.

Their accomplishments deserve recognition regardless of what I think of the man whose name dominates the headlines.

I don’t have to admire their boss to admire what they’ve built.

And neither do you.

The real systemic error is our growing inability to distinguish between the truth of an accomplishment and our feelings about the person associated with it.

We should be able to recognize brilliance without worshiping its beneficiaries, criticize misconduct without denying accomplishments, and make ethical decisions without pretending the facts are different from what they are.

That shouldn’t be a controversial position.

It should be the bare minimum of intellectual maturity.

After all, the laws of physics don’t care who you voted for.

Neither should our recognition of the people who successfully put those laws to work.
