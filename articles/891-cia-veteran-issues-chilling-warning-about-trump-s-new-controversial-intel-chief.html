Loyalty Over Analysis: What the Pulte Appointment Says About Power

Bill Pulte’s elevation to acting director of national intelligence is not just a personnel choice. According to former CIA officer John Sipher, it is a signal that the administration does not want intelligence to inform power. It wants intelligence to serve power. That distinction is the whole story.

The Office Was Built for Truth, Not Patronage

The DNI role exists to deliver facts, warnings, and hard judgments to decision-makers, including the ones they would rather not hear. The law requires extensive national security expertise for a reason: intelligence is not gossip with access badges. It is a discipline bounded by law, method, and institutional restraint.

Pulte, by Sipher’s account, does not fit that standard. Installing an unqualified loyalist in a post designed for professional independence is not a harmless break from tradition. It is a refusal of the office’s purpose.

The Real Qualification Is Loyalty

Sipher’s more damaging point is not that Pulte lacks experience. It is that his record shows how this administration rewards people who treat public institutions as political weapons. At the Federal Housing Finance Agency, Pulte reportedly referred Trump critics, including prominent Democrats, for mortgage fraud investigations. That is not neutral enforcement. It is the familiar corruption of regulatory power into selective intimidation.

The message is blunt: if you can target enemies, qualifications become optional.

This Is Not Chaos. It Is Method.

The usual defense of these moves is incompetence, as if the country were being governed by a series of accidents. That framing is too generous. A politically obedient intelligence chief does not need to fabricate reports to do damage. The abuse is cleaner than that. It can happen through selective declassification, cherry-picked raw reporting, buried caveats, and the laundering of uncertainty into accusation.

That is the point. You do not have to destroy intelligence to ruin it. You only have to reward the people willing to distort it.

“Deep State” as Pretext for Capture

The administration’s rhetoric about the “deep state” is not a critique of bureaucracy. It is a loyalty test disguised as anti-elite theater. Professional judgment gets recast as sabotage. Independent analysis gets treated as disloyalty. Once that logic takes hold, the institution starts training itself to survive rather than to tell the truth.

The result is predictable: top people leave, cautious analysts self-censor, ambitious officials learn the wrong lesson, and loyalists move up. That is how a professional culture is hollowed out without a formal purge.

The Pattern Beneath the Personnel

Post-Watergate intelligence reforms were built to stop exactly this kind of political abuse. The Church Committee exposed what happens when secret agencies answer to personal or partisan agendas instead of democratic oversight. Sipher is right to treat those reforms as a guardrail, not a historical ornament.

The deeper pattern here is institutional degradation through managed disrespect. Once expertise is treated as expendable and truth as valuable only when it serves the ruler’s interest, every agency becomes vulnerable to the same conversion. The intelligence community is only the most dangerous place to test that theory.
