The Exorcism America Cannot Outsource

By Paulo Santos

An absurd AI meme accidentally gets something important right—and something even more important terribly wrong.

The image is ridiculous.

America, rendered as a terrified redheaded anime woman, is strapped to a chair while Canada and NATO hold her down. Denmark—because apparently Copenhagen has acquired jurisdiction over supernatural affairs—brandishes a crucifix and screams:

“Begone, MAGA! Begone, Trump! Leave her now!”

Behind them, a demonic caricature of Donald Trump disappears into a cloud of HATE, LIES, DIVISION, FEAR, AUTHORITARIANISM, SELFISHNESS, CHAOS, GREED.

Subtle, it is not.

But beneath the AI slop, melodrama and political wish fulfillment, there is a surprisingly powerful idea:

America wants herself back.

And there is also a comforting lie.

Because America was not possessed.

America voted.

Nobody Summoned Trump

It would be pleasant to imagine Trumpism as something foreign that crawled into American politics one night, possessed the Republican Party, and turned an otherwise healthy democracy into something grotesque.

That isn’t what happened.

Donald Trump was elected president in 2016 through the Electoral College. Then Americans spent four years watching him govern.

They knew him afterward.

They knew January 6.

They knew his attempts to overturn the 2020 election.

They knew the criminal cases, civil judgments, impeachments, election lies, attacks on institutions, treatment of allies, admiration for strongmen and the extraordinary degree to which the Republican Party had reorganized itself around one man’s personality.

And then, in 2024, 77,302,580 Americans voted for him.

This time he also won the national popular vote, receiving 49.8% of votes cast for president.

There was no demon.

There were ballots.

That distinction matters because blaming everything on Trump lets America avoid the considerably more uncomfortable question:

Why were so many Americans willing to put him back in the White House?

Trump did not create every resentment, grievance, prejudice, conspiracy theory or authoritarian impulse that eventually assembled beneath the MAGA banner. He discovered an audience for them, exploited them extraordinarily effectively and became their focal point.

The man matters enormously.

But the audience was already there.

And Yet the Woman in That Chair Is Real

This is where the meme gets something right.

There is another America.

It is the America that looks at what has happened to its relationships with Canada and Europe and barely recognizes the country doing it.

The choice of characters in this cartoon is almost accidentally perfect.

Trump’s second administration has repeatedly clashed with Denmark over Greenland. As recently as July, Trump publicly reiterated that Greenland should be controlled by the United States rather than Denmark, while Denmark insisted that Greenland’s future belongs to Greenlanders and that Danish sovereignty isn’t something Washington gets to appropriate.

Meanwhile, uncertainty about American reliability has been changing European defense planning. Reuters reported this week that Trump’s pressure on NATO has eroded confidence in America’s commitment and encouraged European governments to diversify away from dependence on American military equipment.

Canada has been doing something similar. It recently applied to join the UK-led Joint Expeditionary Force and has been deepening European defense relationships as part of a strategy to reduce its reliance on the United States.

So when cartoon-NATO says:

“You are not America! We want our leader back!”

there is a recognizable geopolitical anxiety beneath the melodrama.

Not because America’s allies historically obeyed Washington. They didn’t. Friends disagree. Allies sometimes disagree spectacularly.

But there is an enormous difference between arguing with the leader of an alliance and wondering whether that leader still believes in the alliance.

That is the rupture the picture is trying to represent.

“I Want My Old Self Back”

That is the best line in the entire image.

Not Canada’s.

Not Denmark’s magnificent Begone, foul MAGA spirit!

America’s:

“I want my old self back!”

But which old self?

That is the uncomfortable part.

The old America was never the immaculate champion of democracy imagined by nostalgic political art. Vietnam happened. Iraq happened. Jim Crow happened. Japanese internment happened. The CIA has a résumé considerably longer than anyone wants to read before bedtime.

There was no pristine democratic Eden from which America suddenly fell in 2016.

But countries can still possess political norms without having perfectly honored them.

Peaceful transfers of power mattered.

The independence of courts mattered.

Facts were at least supposed to matter.

Presidents were supposed to regard themselves as temporarily occupying an office rather than personally owning the machinery of the state.

Allies could reasonably expect that a treaty signed with the United States would outlive whichever individual happened to occupy the Oval Office.

The argument represented by the woman in that chair isn’t necessarily “America used to be good.”

It is:

“America used to have boundaries.”

And a large part of the current political struggle concerns whether those boundaries still exist.

The Exorcism Problem

There is another reason the image works.

Trumpism has become extraordinarily personalized.

A June 2026 Brookings analysis found that 62% of rank-and-file Republicans identified as MAGA. More importantly, the authors described MAGA as increasingly resembling a personal constituency devoted to Trump rather than an ideological movement governed by a coherent set of principles. They pointed to policy disputes in which MAGA voters continued following Trump despite positions that conflicted with supposedly foundational elements of the movement. Brookings

That creates a fascinating succession problem.

Because eventually, Donald Trump will no longer dominate American politics.

Every politician leaves the stage eventually.

And then MAGA faces a question that ordinary political movements can answer much more easily:

Who replaces him?

There can be another conservative.

There can be another nationalist.

There can be another populist.

There can certainly be another authoritarian politician.

But there cannot be another Donald Trump.

Someone can inherit his voters. Someone can imitate his mannerisms. Someone can repeat his slogans, attack the same enemies, wear the same uniform and promise to continue the same policies.

But a personality cult has one inconvenient structural weakness: the personality is not transferable.

The contradiction inside MAGA has frequently been resolved by Trump himself. If two supposed MAGA principles collide, the movement doesn’t necessarily require a philosophical debate.

Trump chooses.

Whatever Trump chooses becomes the position.

Remove that central authority and suddenly the contradictions matter. Isolationists and interventionists have to argue. Corporate Republicans and economic populists have to argue. Libertarians and Christian nationalists have to argue. People who genuinely believe in limited government have to reconcile themselves with people who want enormous state power deployed against their cultural enemies.

Trump has been the gravitational force keeping very different objects in the same political system.

Remove the mass at the center, and the orbital mechanics change.

That doesn’t guarantee that what follows will be benign. It could fracture. It could reorganize. Another movement could absorb much of it. Another politician could successfully build a different coalition from the pieces.

But Trump’s departure would matter enormously precisely because Trumpism has been so thoroughly organized around Trump.

America Cannot Be Exorcised

And this is where our ridiculous meme finally fails.

Canada cannot hold America’s shoulders.

NATO cannot grab her arm.

Denmark cannot wave a crucifix and drive Trumpism screaming back into Hell, however entertaining the mental picture may be.

America’s allies cannot solve America’s democratic problems.

Neither can a single election magically cleanse the country of the political conditions that produced Trump.

Because Trump was not the demon that possessed America.

Trump was something America produced.

That distinction makes the problem harder—but it also makes the path forward clearer.

Getting Trump out of the center of American political life would remove the organizing personality around which MAGA has been built. What happens afterward depends on institutions, political leaders, media, civil society and—most importantly—the choices millions of Americans continue to make.

There will be no holy water.

No crucifix.

No convenient cloud of black smoke carrying authoritarianism out through the window.

America does not need an exorcism.

It needs to decide what America is going to be after Donald Trump.

And unlike the woman strapped helplessly into that chair, Americans themselves have their hands on the restraints.
