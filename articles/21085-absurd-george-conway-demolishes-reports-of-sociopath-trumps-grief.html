'Absurd': George Conway demolishes reports of 'sociopath' Trump’s 'grief'

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosAugust 12, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Times Mistook Calculation for Grief

A New York Times headline described Trump as “grieving” and “uncharacteristically emotional” over Lindsey Graham’s death, prompting George Conway and others to call the framing absurd. The pushback is not about Trump’s inner life in the abstract. It is about what the reporting chooses to make visible: emotion, personality, and weirdness on one side; power, calculation, and loyalty on the other.

Power Was the Point

The only institutional actor that matters here is Trump. He was the president, and the article itself says the endorsement was a decision. That is the fact with political weight. Whether Trump felt anything is beside the point unless it explains action. The relevant question is not whether he grieved. It is why he used the moment to back a candidate and what that endorsement buys him.

The framing does the usual softening trick: it turns an exercise of power into a story about mood. That is not analysis. It is narrative laundering.

Grief Is Not the Story

The source text gives the game away. Even the critics quoted in it say Trump was not acting from grief but from political calculation and sycophancy. Marcy Wheeler’s point is the sharpest one: the article itself places the evidence of calculation before the language of emotion. In other words, the headline announces a feeling the reporting does not actually establish.

That matters because feeling is the least important thing Trump offers politics. His real currency is leverage. Endorsements, loyalty tests, and public signals are how he disciplines institutions and rewards obedience. Calling that “grief” is not just imprecise. It drains the act of its political content.

The Media’s Favorite Escape Hatch

This is how prestige journalism often covers authoritarian politics: it dresses strategy up as psychology. A manipulator becomes a sad man. A calculated endorsement becomes an emotional reaction. Cruelty becomes confusion. The result is a safer story for readers and a weaker one for everyone trying to understand how power is actually used.

The Times does not need to prove Trump is a sociopath, psychopath, or any other clinical label. Those claims in the source are rhetorical noise, even when they come from people hostile to him. What the paper does need to do is distinguish observation from inference. If the evidence points to tactical endorsement, then that is the story. Anything else is theater with a newsroom logo on it.

Sycophancy, Not Sentiment

The source also highlights the deeper structure beneath the headline: Trump’s orbit is built on submission. Commentators in the piece argue that he chose Graham’s sister because she would be another reliable yes-person. That is the governing logic of Trump-world, and it is far more important than any sentimental gloss.

This is what authoritarian politics looks like in practice: not grand speeches about domination, but routine personnel choices and loyalty enforcement. The point is not persuasion. The point is control. When media outlets translate that into emotional color, they help normalize the machinery.

Conclusion: Narrative as Cover for Power

The larger pattern is simple. Elite political coverage often prefers a psychologically tidy Trump to a strategically legible one. That preference helps no one except the people benefiting from confusion. Trump is not interesting because he may or may not grieve. He is consequential because he uses institutional power to reward deference and extract obedience, while major outlets still reach for language that makes his actions seem human, accidental, or merely odd.

That is the problem. Not a president with feelings. A press corps that keeps mistaking power for personality.
