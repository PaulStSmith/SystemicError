CIA officials sound alarm as Trump chief pushes agency to breaking point

The CIA’s “Adult in the Room” Is Serving the Man Who Owns the Room

The Decision-Maker Is John Ratcliffe

The relevant fact is not that the CIA is approaching its 79th anniversary. It is that CIA Director John Ratcliffe controls the authority, personnel, and public credibility of an institution built to give presidents information they may not want. Former officers John Sipher and Julia Curlee argue that Ratcliffe has instead used that position to reinforce President Donald Trump’s preferred political narratives.

That is not bureaucratic drift. Ratcliffe has agency. According to the former officials, he selectively released questionable intelligence, supported disputed declassifications, tolerated retaliation against intelligence personnel, and permitted old analysis to be withdrawn when its subjects displeased the administration.

Politicization Is a Method, Not a Mishap

Ratcliffe’s conduct predates his current post. As director of national intelligence in 2020, he released unverified Russian intelligence concerning Hillary Clinton while acknowledging that it might contain exaggeration or fabrication. The disclaimer did not neutralize the act. It merely documented that he knew the material was unreliable before lending it institutional legitimacy.

The political method is straightforward: take an allegation useful to the president, attach the prestige of the intelligence apparatus, and release it into public debate. The government’s seal supplies credibility that the underlying claim has not earned. Uncertainty becomes a shield for propaganda rather than a reason for restraint.

Silence Is Also an Exercise of Power

Sipher and Curlee describe a serving CIA officer being publicly identified and forced out, other intelligence officials being fired after delivering unwelcome judgments, and established analysis being retracted. They also point to Ratcliffe’s silence when Director of National Intelligence Tulsi Gabbard stood with FBI agents during the seizure of 2020 ballots in Fulton County, Georgia.

That silence should not be mistaken for passivity. A director who speaks when declassification serves the administration and disappears when intelligence power intrudes into domestic electoral politics is making a choice about what the institution will permit. Selective silence is how officials preserve plausible deniability while subordinates learn which truths carry professional costs.

The “Adult” Label Conceals Complicity

Calling Ratcliffe the “adult in the room” lowers the standard from independence to comparative composure. The phrase asks whether an official appears steadier than other Trump appointees, not whether he is performing his constitutional role.

This is a familiar piece of Washington misdirection. Institutional surrender becomes moderation when conducted without shouting. An official receives credit for tone while using his office to validate conspiracy theories, punish dissent, and weaken the distinction between intelligence work and presidential grievance. Professional polish does not mitigate that conduct. It makes the conduct easier to normalize.

Congress Chose Spectatorship

The source identifies another center of power: Congress. The post-Church Committee safeguards separating the CIA from domestic politics did not create or enforce themselves. Legislators possess oversight authority, investigative machinery, appropriations power, and the ability to impose consequences. According to Sipher and Curlee, Congress has mostly watched.

That is institutional cowardice, not helplessness. When lawmakers decline to defend boundaries they are empowered to enforce, those boundaries become optional. Ratcliffe may be carrying out the politicization, but congressional inaction enables it.

An Intelligence Service Recast as Political Property

The larger pattern is the conversion of supposedly independent institutions into instruments of personal rule. The objective need not be total control of every analyst or every report. It is enough to punish inconvenient findings, reward useful ones, and teach the bureaucracy that loyalty determines which facts survive.

The damage extends beyond any single declassification or dismissed officer. Intelligence loses its value when analysis is filtered through the leader’s grievances, while official secrecy and prestige remain available to authenticate those grievances. The institution still looks like an intelligence service. Its authority, however, is increasingly spent as political currency—and the officials capable of stopping that conversion have found observation more comfortable than resistance.
