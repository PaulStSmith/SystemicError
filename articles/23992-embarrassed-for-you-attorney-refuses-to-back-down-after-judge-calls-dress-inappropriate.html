'Embarrassed for you': Attorney refuses to back down after judge calls dress inappropriate

Based on an article by David Edwards

The Dress Code Wasn’t the Point. Judicial Power Was.

A Public Rebuke From the Bench

Texas attorney Mariah Medina said an unnamed judge summoned her around the bench, inspected her sleeveless gray dress, laughed, questioned whether she considered it appropriate for a jury trial, and asked whether she had a blazer. According to Medina, her case was not going to trial, and the outfit violated neither the posted courtroom dress code nor the judge’s separate list of prohibited clothing.

The Judge Held the Power

This was not a disagreement between social equals. A judge controls the courtroom. An attorney appears there on behalf of a client and must operate under that authority.

If Medina’s account is accurate, the judge did not merely communicate an expectation. She staged the correction in front of defendants and other lawyers, invoking the prestige of the bench to turn an uncovered shoulder into a professional deficiency. The dress may be trivial; the power used to police it is not.

Judges can enforce courtroom standards. But discretion without clear rules easily becomes personal rule. Medina says the dress breached no published restriction. That makes the central question less about clothing than about whether an officeholder imposed an unwritten preference through public embarrassment.

“Professionalism” as a Moving Target

Professional dress codes often present subjective judgments as neutral standards. Here, the supposed violation was not identified in advance, written on the courtroom door, or tied to any disruption described in the reporting. It emerged when the judge decided Medina’s shoulders were objectionable.

That ambiguity protects the authority issuing the rebuke. The target must defend herself against a rule she could not consult beforehand, while critics can insist that any punishment proves the rule must have existed. “Professionalism” becomes whatever the more powerful person declares after the fact.

The comparison to a man wearing a tank top sharpened the misdirection. Medina was not wearing a tank top. Replacing the actual dress with a more casual imaginary garment allowed critics to condemn a costume of their own invention.

The Internet Supplied the Misogyny

Once Medina posted about the exchange, online critics escalated from dress-code scolding to gendered degradation. One called her a “hooker.” Another said she should have been jailed for ten days and described contempt as a starting point for dealing with “loud modern women.”

Those comments expose the punitive politics underneath the etiquette argument. The grievance was not simply that an attorney’s clothing allegedly fell short of courtroom decorum. It was that a woman publicly challenged an authority figure, rejected anonymous lectures, and answered insults rather than absorbing them quietly.

Medina responded with insults of her own. That may explain why the thread became a spectacle; it does not equalize the underlying power. Anonymous accounts could provoke her and log off. The judge, as described by Medina, exercised institutional authority in a courtroom.

Virality Obscures Accountability

The post drew more than 4.8 million views by Tuesday, according to X’s public counter. That scale encourages coverage to center the feud: the dress, the replies, the profanity, the engagement.

But the judge remained unnamed, while Medina’s photograph, profession, words, and appearance became the public material. The official power at the center of the allegation receded from view; the person subjected to it became the controversy.

That imbalance does not prove misconduct by the judge. The account is Medina’s, and the reporting provides no response from the judge or independent confirmation of the exchange. It does, however, reveal the weakness in framing this chiefly as an attorney “hitting back” at critics. The consequential allegation concerns how judicial discretion was exercised. The online pile-on is evidence of the social enforcement surrounding that power, not a substitute for examining it.

Petty Rules, Serious Hierarchies

Institutions rarely advertise arbitrary power as arbitrary. They call it decorum, tradition, standards, or professionalism. Sometimes those rules serve legitimate purposes. Sometimes their vagueness permits officials to convert personal preference into an order and humiliation into instruction.

The broader pattern is familiar: authority acts, the target objects, and public attention shifts from the exercise of power to whether the target objected acceptably. The courtroom dispute then becomes a referendum on Medina’s temperament and wardrobe rather than on the judge’s judgment.

That is how hierarchy protects itself. The official’s discretion is treated as natural. The weaker actor’s resistance is treated as the disruption.
