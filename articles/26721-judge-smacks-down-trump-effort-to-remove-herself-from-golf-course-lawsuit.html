Judge smacks down Trump effort to remove herself from golf course lawsuit

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 20, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Judge Ana Reyes denied President Donald Trump’s motion to remove her from litigation over the East Potomac Golf Course. The administration alleged that Reyes was biased, hostile and overly emotional; she answered that her criticism followed the government’s abrupt removal of hundreds of trees after months in which she had credited its lawyers’ representations and declined repeated requests to restrict its work.

According to Reyes’s filing, the government gave notice at 4:55 p.m. on the Friday of a holiday weekend that removals would begin within 15 hours. It then cut down nearly 430 trees in one day and more than 500 overall; rain prevented further removals before Reyes granted the plaintiffs a temporary restraining order. AlterNet reports that Trump called the trees diseased, a characterization the outlet disputes, and later attacked Reyes on Truth Social while asserting that Interior had an “absolute right” to proceed.

The power was never with the judge’s critics

The federal government controlled the land operation, the contractors, the timetable and the information supplied to the court. It possessed the practical power to transform the site before opponents could obtain meaningful judicial review.

That imbalance is the essential fact. The plaintiffs could file motions. Reyes could issue orders. But Interior could deploy crews and turn a disputed plan into hundreds of felled trees within hours. Once a tree is gone, later legal vindication cannot restore the proceeding to its prior state.

The administration made the choice

The removal schedule was not an atmospheric misunderstanding. Government officials chose to provide notice late on a holiday-weekend Friday, chose a start time 15 hours later and carried out nearly 430 removals in a day. Reyes described the trees as healthy and non-invasive; AlterNet reports that Trump called them diseased.

The government then sought Reyes’s disqualification after she responded forcefully. Its filing, as described by AlterNet, treated her anger as proof of bias while minimizing the conduct that produced it. That is not a neutral objection to judicial temperament. It is an attempt to make scrutiny itself the controversy.

Anger became the decoy

The recusal motion redirected attention from executive conduct to the judge’s tone. Reyes answered with the record: she had praised government counsel’s writing and analysis, repeatedly expressed respect for their work and resisted intervening despite nearly a dozen requests from the plaintiffs.

That history matters because it punctures the administration’s account of preexisting hostility. On the facts quoted by AlterNet, the sharper judicial language appeared after the government’s accelerated tree removal—not before it. Former federal prosecutor Joyce Vance made the same point: the recusal filing omitted the context for Reyes’s anger.

Trump’s response pushed the diversion further. He called Reyes a “crazed lunatic,” questioned her sanity and declared that Interior needed no approval. Those insults did not answer the chronology Reyes documented or the legal claims before her. They substituted presidential assertion and personal degradation for legal argument.

The damage creates leverage

Rapid executive action can manufacture its own defense: by the time a court intervenes, much of the contested outcome already exists. The institution with the machinery gains an advantage from speed; everyone else is left arguing over remnants and remedies.

AlterNet also reports allegations of noncompliance with court orders in several jurisdictions, while noting that no administration or Justice Department official had been held in civil or criminal contempt. The article does not establish every underlying case in detail, but the pattern it identifies is politically significant: judicial authority weakens when adverse orders produce delay, evasion or attack without personal consequence for the officials involved.

Impunity changes the constitutional balance

The broader issue is not whether a judge sounded furious. It is whether executive officials can exploit timing, complete irreversible acts and then portray judicial resistance as partisan misconduct.

Courts depend heavily on government lawyers telling the truth, preserving disputed conditions and obeying orders before enforcement becomes necessary. If an administration can benefit from violating that trust and answer scrutiny by targeting the judge, restraint becomes a vulnerability. The resulting system still has hearings, filings and rulings—but executive power sets the facts on the ground first and litigates accountability afterward.
