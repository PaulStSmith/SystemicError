Red state governor tears apart 'wrong headed' Trump as backlash grows on key issue

Based on an article by Joshua Haiar, South Dakota Searchlight

Paulo SantosSeptember 7, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

South Dakota Is Not Negotiating. It Is Pleading.

The Setup

At the South Dakota State Fair, Gov. Larry Rhoden and Democratic challenger Dan Ahlers both took shots at Donald Trump over imported beef, and both wrapped their criticism in the language of advocacy, information, and better channels to Washington. That is the surface story. The real story is who actually controls the lever and who is left asking for a softer landing.

The Center of Power

Trump made the decision that mattered. He announced a pause on tariffs on imported beef, claimed it would lower consumer prices, and triggered backlash from ranchers. That is not a policy disagreement between equals. It is a reminder that state officials can object, lobby, and posture, but the decisive authority sits in the federal executive branch, where one man can swing an industry and force an entire state to react.

Rhoden’s response exposes the imbalance. He said he would press Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins for an advisory committee so Trump has better information before he makes decisions. That is a plea for a better filter around arbitrary power, not a defense of ranchers. It treats impulsiveness as a communications problem instead of what it is: governing by whim.

Rhoden’s Managed Dissent

Rhoden is trying to occupy two incompatible positions at once. Trump endorsed him, and Rhoden still wants to sound like the adult in the room. He says Trump was “wrongheaded,” “impulsive,” and defended by weak voices, but he stops at procedural scolding. No rupture. No accountability. Just a request that the machinery surrounding Trump be slightly less incompetent.

That is the Republican method in miniature: absorb the damage, preserve the coalition, and call it seriousness. The problem is not just Trump’s decision. It is the elected officials who keep treating his behavior as an isolated lapse rather than the operating logic of the movement they still stand beside.

Ahlers Names the Dependency

Ahlers at least points to the structure. He says the state needs to be a stronger advocate for farmers and ranchers, and that means pressuring Trump through South Dakota’s congressional delegation. That is still limited, but it identifies the actual relationship: the state is dependent on a federal authority it does not control.

He also ties the issue to consequences, not optics. When federal decisions whiplash an agricultural market, the people who pay are the ranchers, the consumers, and everyone whose livelihood depends on stability. The story is not that Trump surprised the state. The story is that the state remains vulnerable to a federal system where loyalty matters more than competence.

The Familiar Dodge

The article’s framing lets the political class hide behind the language of “advocacy” and “better information.” That is a neat way to avoid naming responsibility. Trump made the call. Rhoden manages the fallout. Ahlers describes the damage. And the ranchers, once again, are expected to absorb the cost of a national spectacle dressed up as economic policy.

This is not unique to beef. It is the same pattern that runs through the Medicaid fight and the department merger in the same debate: power is centralized upward, then local leaders are asked to defend the results or request revisions after the fact. When Noem merged agriculture and environment by executive order, when Republicans moved to roll back Medicaid expansion, and when Trump decided to play tariff games with beef, the common thread was the same. Decisions were made by people with leverage. Everyone else was told to adapt.

The Bigger Pattern

South Dakota’s debate shows how authoritarian politics works in practice: not always through dramatic repression, but through casual domination, arbitrary decisions, and a political class trained to translate damage into process language. The state does not need more ceremonial committees to understand what is happening. It needs leaders willing to say that power concentrated in one impulsive federal hand is the problem, not merely the tone.

The Democratic argument here is not that Democrats are perfect or that federal institutions are sacred. It is that democracy requires actual accountability over the people and offices making the decisions. Rhoden wants a committee. Ahlers wants pressure. Neither is enough if the system keeps rewarding leaders who can break things first and invite everyone else to clean up the mess later.
