Amazon’s “Buy From Me”: When Exposure Becomes a Weapon

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 30, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Amazon has rolled out a new initiative branded as “Buy From Me.” The name is soft, friendly, almost intimate — the linguistic equivalent of a corporate hand on your shoulder. But strip away the marketing varnish and what remains is something far more aggressive: a system designed to capture off‑platform commerce, appropriate creator labor, and collapse independent businesses under the weight of algorithmic demand.

This is not innovation. It’s enclosure.

What “Buy From Me” Actually Does

At its core, the program uses AI to surface products that are not sold on Amazon, pulling images, descriptions, and branding from independent storefronts. These products are then presented to Amazon users inside Amazon’s interface, mediated by Amazon’s recommendation and checkout logic.

The creator does not opt in. The creator does not control presentation. The creator does not own the customer relationship.

Amazon positions itself as a neutral conduit — a helpful bridge between shoppers and small businesses. In reality, it is inserting itself between the buyer and seller, absorbing data, shaping behavior, and redirecting loyalty back into its ecosystem.

The product may not be Amazon’s.
The customer always will be.

The Real Asset Isn’t the Sale — It’s the Relationship

Defenders of the program often reach for the same tired line: “They’re not taking a cut, so what’s the harm?”

That argument misunderstands modern platform economics.

Amazon doesn’t need margin today if it can extract behavioral data forever. Every click, hesitation, comparison, and purchase trains its models. Every interaction teaches Amazon how to sell better, bundle better, and eventually replace the original seller entirely.

This is why the program exists at all. Independent AI shopping agents threaten Amazon’s role as gatekeeper of discovery. “Buy From Me” is a preemptive strike — a way to ensure that even when commerce happens elsewhere, Amazon still owns the funnel.

Virality as a Denial‑of‑Service Attack

Now to the part most people miss — and the part that turns this from exploitative to outright destructive.

Small shops are not built for global virality.

A one‑ or two‑person operation has:

  • Finite inventory

  • Limited supplier throughput

  • Manual fulfillment processes

  • Honest lead times based on human labor

Their survival depends on controlled demand.

Amazon obliterates that balance.

When an algorithm pushes a product to a global audience overnight, demand doesn’t rise — it detonates. Inventory vanishes. Backorders stretch from days into months. Customers grow angry. Refunds spike. Chargebacks follow. Reputation collapses.

And here’s the crucial asymmetry: Amazon bears none of the fallout.

The customer doesn’t blame the platform. They blame the shop.
The algorithm doesn’t absorb the hit. The creator does.

This is growth without consent and exposure without capacity — effectively an algorithmic denial‑of‑service attack against human‑scale businesses.

Reputation Laundering and Brand Erasure

Even when orders are fulfilled, the damage continues.

Customers who buy through Amazon rarely remember the original seller. The purchase is mentally logged as “something I got on Amazon.” Repeat business returns to the platform, not the shop. Branding, storytelling, and community — the things small creators actually compete on — are stripped away.

The seller becomes a background asset. Replaceable. Interchangeable.

This isn’t accidental. It’s the same pattern Amazon has followed for decades:

  1. Observe what sells

  2. Learn how it sells

  3. Replicate it

  4. Undercut it

“Buy From Me” simply moves step one upstream.

The Myth of Benevolent Exposure

There is a persistent fantasy in tech culture that exposure is always good, that visibility is inherently empowering. This fantasy only survives because it is usually told by people with scale.

For small businesses, exposure without agency is a liability.

Real growth is paced. It is negotiated. It respects capacity. What Amazon offers instead is forced acceleration — growth on the platform’s terms, at the platform’s speed, with the platform holding the parachute.

When things go wrong — and they inevitably do — Amazon shrugs and routes demand elsewhere.

Enclosure Disguised as Convenience

“Buy From Me” is not about helping creators. It is about preventing escape.

As AI threatens to disintermediate traditional marketplaces, Amazon is racing to ensure that no matter where a product originates, commerce still passes through its hands. The walled garden grows taller. The exits get narrower.

Convenience is the bait.
Dependency is the product.

The Endgame

If this model succeeds, the future of small commerce looks bleak:

  • Creators reduced to upstream inventory providers

  • Platforms owning discovery, data, and loyalty

  • Failure externalized, profit centralized

This is not a market. It’s a supply chain with delusions of neutrality.

Amazon’s “Buy From Me” isn’t a feature.
It’s a warning.

Unchecked, it accelerates a world where creativity survives only at the pleasure of platforms — and where going viral is no longer a dream, but a death sentence.
