The 'reckoning': Trump capitulators spooked as blowback looms

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 27, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet, citing reporting from The Washington Post, says technology, defense, manufacturing, and mining companies with close Trump administration ties are preparing for congressional investigations if Democrats win House and Senate majorities in the midterm elections. The investigations remain prospective: the supplied reporting establishes neither Democratic control of Congress nor any resulting findings.

According to the Post, possible targets include firms connected to Donald Trump Jr. that received Pentagon funding; companies tied to Elon Musk and the U.S. DOGE Service; and military contractors awarded noncompetitive contracts or equity investments. Congressional staffers reportedly want to examine whether some firms benefited financially from government information or relationships, but the article supplies no adjudicated findings of wrongdoing.

Jamie Bair, a congressional-investigations lawyer, predicted that Democrats would move faster than they did after the 2018 midterms. Staffers also reportedly see the proposed inquiries as a way to develop policy arguments and political messaging for the 2028 elections.

The power was never in the apology

The companies described here were not helpless institutions swept along by Washington weather. They possessed money, technical infrastructure, government access, and—in some cases—valuable federal contracts. The Trump administration possessed the greater coercive prize: control over procurement, regulation, executive agencies, and privileged information.

“Capitulation” can obscure that exchange. The relevant question is not whether executives acted cravenly in public. It is whether government decisions steered public money, access, or competitive advantage toward politically connected firms. Corporate flattery is embarrassing; the use of state power for favored relationships would be the consequential issue. That is what subpoenas, records, and sworn testimony would need to establish.

Suspicion is not a verdict

AlterNet calls the second term’s dealings corrupt, but the evidence summarized here does not prove that conclusion. It identifies investigative priorities and alleged risk areas: Pentagon funding, noncompetitive awards, military equity stakes, DOGE access, and possible private benefit from government relationships.

Those are legitimate subjects for oversight precisely because they involve concentrated public power. But a target list is not a set of findings. Donald Trump Jr.’s or Elon Musk’s connection to a company may justify examining how an award was made; it does not, standing alone, establish that the award was improper. Serious accountability requires more than reversing the partisan direction of insinuation.

Congress chose delay before

Bair’s account exposes an institutional failure with consequences. After Democrats took the House in 2018, he said, investigations lost time to “polite” protocols. Courtesy was not an external constraint. Congressional leaders chose procedures and pacing while the executive branch retained every incentive to resist scrutiny.

If Democrats gain subpoena and committee power, speed will matter—but speed is not the same as rigor. Hearings designed chiefly for viral confrontation can produce spectacle without durable findings. Investigators would need to trace contracts, communications, access, recusals, and financial gains. The test is whether Congress can convert suspicion into a documented public record.

Accountability or campaign production

The reported link to 2028 messaging is politically revealing. Oversight can inform legislation and expose misconduct. It can also become opposition research conducted with public authority. The difference lies in the work product: verified findings, enforceable reforms, procurement safeguards, and consequences where evidence supports them.

Companies are reportedly “bracing” because congressional control determines whether relationships with the executive branch receive serious examination. That is itself an indictment of episodic oversight. Public contracting should not become transparent only when the opposition wins an election.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is a government in which access, contracts, and scrutiny can all turn on partisan control. Executives cultivate the administration holding procurement power; the opposition promises investigation if it later captures Congress; both accountability and impunity become electoral contingencies.

The alleged “reckoning” therefore remains conditional and incomplete. The systemic failure is not that powerful firms may finally face uncomfortable hearings. It is that institutions allowed politically connected companies to operate amid unresolved conflicts until a possible change in congressional power made examination plausible. A functioning oversight system would inspect those arrangements when they occur—not save accountability for the next campaign cycle.
