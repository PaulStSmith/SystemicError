​FCC official breaks rank as Brendan Carr's bullying rebuked in new legal battle

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

The FCC Is Not Regulating Disney. It Is Extorting It.

Power, Not Principle

The reporting here is simple enough to fit in one breath: the FCC forced Disney’s ABC stations into an early license-renewal process while the agency was already probing The View and Disney’s DEI practices, and Disney responded by suing. The trigger was not a neutral enforcement action. It followed Trump’s public attacks on ABC and Jimmy Kimmel, and ABC says the administration is punishing the network for what it broadcasts.

Who Actually Decided

The decision-making chain is not mysterious. Trump set the tone, Brendan Carr operationalized it, and the FCC turned that political hostility into administrative pressure. That is the point of the machinery: power can look bureaucratic while acting as retaliation. Carr’s invocation of “public interest” and broadcast doctrines does not cleanse the motive. It is the legal costume worn by a political threat.

The Point of the Probe

The agency is not merely “reviewing” Disney. It is using licensing leverage, investigations, and regulatory delay as pressure points. That is coercion with paperwork. The targets are revealing: a talk show, a late-night host, and corporate DEI practices. This is not governance aimed at public harm. It is discipline aimed at media compliance.

The Misdirection

The weak framing would be to treat this as a dispute between a corporation and a regulator, as if both sides were just maneuvering inside a normal system. That misses the power imbalance. Disney is not the central actor here; the federal government is. The corporation may be self-interested, cautious, and long overdue for backbone, but it is still defending itself against a state apparatus using broadcast regulation as punishment for speech.

A Rare Crack in the Corporate Silence

Anna Gomez’s response matters because it is so unusual: a federal regulator publicly naming the campaign as intimidation and control. That clarity is the exception, which tells you how normalized the cowardice has become. Corporate America generally absorbs this kind of pressure, then calls it “risk management.” When a company finally resists, it should not be mistaken for moral purity. It is often just the moment the cost of submission becomes higher than the cost of fighting back.

The Pattern

This is the larger pattern: authoritarian politics does not always announce itself with bans and uniforms. Sometimes it comes through regulators, licensing, investigations, and selective enforcement wrapped in the language of public interest. The substance is the same. The state punishes disfavored speech, then expects institutions to pretend the motive is procedural. The real test is not whether Disney deserves sympathy. It is whether anyone still has the nerve to call retaliation by its name.
