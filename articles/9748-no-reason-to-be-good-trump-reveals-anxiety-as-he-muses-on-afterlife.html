'No reason to be good': Trump reveals anxiety as he muses on afterlife

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 8, 20255 min read

A child yawns next to U.S. President Donald Trump in the Oval Office, on the day Trump signs an executive order on AI and pediatric cancer research, at the White House, Washington, D.C., U.S., September 30, 2025. REUTERS/Nathan Howard

President Donald Trump mused about his eternal fate again during a news conference at the White House.

The 79-year-old president was asked about a new “America Prays” initiative by the White House that will mark next year’s semiquincentennial by “inviting America’s great religious communities to pray for our nation and for our people.”

His comments probed the great beyond, reported The Daily Beast.

“You know, there’s no reason to be good,” Trump told reporters. “I want to be good because you want to prove to God you’re good so you go to that next step, right?”

“That’s very important to me,” he added. “I think it’s really, very important.”

The Trump administration’s Religious Liberty Commission and other initiatives have raised concerns about the influence of Christian Nationalist organizations on the government and public schools, but the president seemed to argue that might be good.

“I’ve felt for a long time that if a country doesn’t have religion, doesn’t have faith, doesn’t have God, it’s going to be very hard to be a good country,” Trump told reporters.

Trump has seemed to be preoccupied with the afterlife in recent months, and one fundraising email – with the subject line “I want to try and get to Heaven” – cited his apparent brush with death last summer in Butler, Pennsylvania, as the reason.

“Last year I came millimeters from death when that bullet pierced through my skin. My triumphant return to the White House was never supposed to happen!” the email read. “But I believe that God saved me for one reason: TO MAKE AMERICA GREAT AGAIN!”

The president also ruminated on Fox News this summer about his anxiety about gaining entry to heaven upon his demise.

“I’m hearing I’m not doing well,” Trump told the hosts. “I hear I’m really at the bottom of the totem pole.”

He spoke on the topic again in an interview with conservative talk radio host Todd Starnes.

“You know, [people] get punished if they’re not good, right?” Trump said. “If you don’t think about that, if you’re not a believer, and you believe you go nowhere, what’s the reason to be good, really? There has to be some kind of a report card up there someplace, you know, like, let’s go to heaven, let’s get into heaven, and it’s sort of a beautiful thing.”

Our Analysis:

The Eternal Musings of Trump: A Reflection on Faith, Morality, and Political Maneuvering

In a recent news conference that feels more like a theological seminar, President Donald Trump has once again blurred the lines between state and spirituality, invoking notions of divine judgment and the eternal quest for goodness. His comments, as reported by The Daily Beast, delve into the murky waters of morality, religion, and governance, revealing a complex, if not confounding, perspective on what drives one to be “good.”

The Divine Justification for Governance

Trump’s remarks on the new “America Prays” initiative, designed to commemorate next year’s semiquincentennial, underscore a troubling conflation of religious fervor with national identity. “You know, there’s no reason to be good,” Trump stated, before paradoxically adding, “I want to be good because you want to prove to God you’re good so you go to that next step, right?” This dichotomy between inherent goodness and the desire for divine approval encapsulates Trump’s transactional view of morality — a viewpoint that is as simplistic as it is problematic.

A Country Without God?

Trump’s assertion, “I’ve felt for a long time that if a country doesn’t have religion, doesn’t have faith, doesn’t have God, it’s going to be very hard to be a good country,” is a stark manifestation of Christian Nationalist rhetoric seeping into the fabric of governmental discourse. This notion not only alienates a significant portion of the American populace that may not subscribe to traditional religious beliefs but also raises red flags about the erosion of the secular principles upon which the United States was founded.

Trump’s Preoccupation with the Afterlife

The article also highlights Trump’s growing preoccupation with the afterlife, a theme that has surfaced in his fundraising emails and media appearances. The narrative of his “triumphant return” following a near-death experience and his belief that “God saved me for one reason: TO MAKE AMERICA GREAT AGAIN!” veers dangerously close to messianic delusion. It’s a rhetoric that not only feeds into the cult of personality surrounding Trump but also manipulates spiritual beliefs for political gain.

The Misguided Quest for Heavenly Approval

Trump’s candid admissions of anxiety about gaining entry into heaven and his repeated references to a celestial “report card” reveal a fundamental misunderstanding of the principles of faith and goodness. The implication that morality is merely a means to an end — a ticket to heaven — rather than an inherent value, speaks volumes about the moral compass guiding the current administration.

The Political Exploitation of Religious Beliefs

This article lays bare the extent to which religious beliefs and the language of faith are being exploited for political ends. The intertwining of Trump’s personal spiritual musings with policy initiatives and public statements is not just unorthodox; it’s a deliberate strategy aimed at bolstering support among religious voters. However, it’s a strategy fraught with peril, as it threatens to further divide an already polarized nation along religious lines.

In conclusion, Trump’s reflections on faith, morality, and the afterlife are more than just idle musings. They are indicative of a deeper, more concerning trend of religious exploitation in politics. As we stand on the precipice of the semiquincentennial, one must wonder whether the soul of America can withstand the weight of such divisive rhetoric. The path to national unity and goodness, contrary to Trump’s assertions, lies not in the imposition of religious conformity, but in the celebration of our diverse beliefs and the upholding of secular democracy.
