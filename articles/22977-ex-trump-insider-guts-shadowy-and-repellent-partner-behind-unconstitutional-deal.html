Ex-Trump insider guts 'shadowy and repellent' partner behind 'unconstitutional' deal

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

Paulo SantosSeptember 3, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Oil Deal Is Not Policy. It Is Plunder With a Flag on It.

The Shape of the Scheme

Elliott Abrams, no enemy of imperial damage himself, says the Trump White House is trying to package Venezuelan oil as a strategic bargain while leaning on a businessman he describes as corrupt and radioactive. The setup is simple enough: the administration wants access to 65 billion barrels of oil, and it is willing to treat constitutional limits, Venezuelan sovereignty, and basic political decency as obstacles to be routed around.

That is the story. Everything else is branding.

Who Actually Holds Power

The decisive power here sits with the White House and the U.S. officials making the arrangement possible, not with Venezuelan drivers, not with American consumers, and not with the people whose resources are being treated as negotiable inventory. Abrams says the plan depends on a Pentagon-to-oil-trade maneuver that likely violates U.S. law and on a deal that ignores Venezuelan law, which says those deposits belong to the people.

So the relevant question is not whether this is “creative diplomacy.” It is who has the authority to try to turn public wealth into a private bargaining chip, and who gets excluded from the room when that happens. The answer is plain: the powerful act, and everyone else is asked to accept the outcome as if it were economics.

The Useful Middleman

Betancourt is not incidental to the story. He is the point. Abrams describes him as the “most shadowy and repellent” Venezuelan businessman the administration could find, and notes a Swiss arrest warrant tied to alleged money laundering involving Venezuela’s state oil company. That is not a side detail. It is the operating method: when legitimate capital will not touch the scheme, the government reaches for a figure with enough grime to move money through the cracks.

This is how corruption survives public language. The administration cannot sell naked theft, so it needs intermediaries with the right mix of money, access, and deniability. Betancourt is not a flaw in the process. He is what the process requires.

Gas Pump Theater

The oil deal is being sold against the backdrop of pain at the gas pump, but Abrams himself says it will do nothing to ease that pain. That matters because the public justification is doing ideological work the policy cannot do on its own. If a plan is unconstitutional, legally dubious, and built around a suspect partner, then “helping drivers” becomes the moral costume for a much uglier transaction.

This is a familiar trick: use consumer anxiety to soften a resource grab. The result is a narrative in which ordinary people are invited to identify with the administration’s supposed pragmatism while the real beneficiaries are grifters, favored companies, and the president himself. The article’s strongest line is also its most revealing: the announced investments are likely to come from people seeking special advantages, oil companies seeking favor, and Trump seeking his own cut of the spectacle.

The Real Betrayal

The deeper offense is not merely that the deal is ugly. It is that it normalizes a relationship with an unelected strongman while treating Venezuelans as an afterthought in the disposition of their own resources. Abrams calls it a sellout, and that is exactly right. The U.S. is not being positioned as a stabilizing partner or democratic counterweight. It is being positioned as the enabler of a resource transfer dressed up as statecraft.

What gets exposed here is not one bad transaction but a durable political pattern: authoritarian power does not always arrive with tanks. Sometimes it arrives with contracts, legal contortions, and a chorus of people pretending that plunder is just complicated governance.
