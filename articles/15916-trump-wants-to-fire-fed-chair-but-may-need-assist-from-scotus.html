Trump wants to fire Fed chair — but may need 'assist from' SCOTUS

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 18, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:Trump's Misunderstanding of Federal Reserve Independence: A Dangerous Precedent

U.S. Federal Reserve Chairman Jerome Powell on on March 22, 2023 (Federal Reserve/Flickr)

Early Thursday morning, April 17, President Donald Trump angrily railed against U.S. Federal Reserve Chairman Jerome Powell on his Truth Social platform.

Trump posted, "The ECB is expected to cut interest rates for the 7th time, and yet, 'Too Late' Jerome Powell of the Fed, who is always TOO LATE AND WRONG, yesterday issued a report which was another, and typical, complete 'mess!' Oil prices are down, groceries (even eggs!) are down, and the USA is getting RICH ON TARIFFS. Too Late should have lowered Interest Rates, like the ECB, long ago, but he should certainly lower them now. Powell's termination cannot come fast enough!"

In an article published later that morning, Axios' Avery Lotz poses a question: Does Trump have the authority to fire Powell?

Trump, of course, has the power to fire members of his administration. During his first presidency, he fired everyone from a national security adviser (John Bolton) to a secretary of state (Rex Tillerson). But Fed chair is not a Cabinet position, and the U.S. Federal Reserve is independent from the White House.

"President Trump's latest social media tirade against Fed Chair Jerome Powell is raising the possibility of an unprecedented — and experts say prohibited — use of executive power," Lotz reports. "Why it matters: Trump may want to remove the country's most powerful economic policymaker, but it won't be as easy as telling him, 'You're fired!' He'd likely need an assist from the Supreme Court…. Some of its conservative justices have already signaled they're open to providing.

The U.S. Federal Reserve was created 112 years ago when Democratic President Woodrow Wilson signed into law the Federal Reserve Act of 1913.

"The Federal Reserve Act, which established the Fed more than a century ago, spells out that the central bank's governors are to serve 14-year terms," Lotz explains. "They can only be dismissed for cause, which courts have generally interpreted to mean malfeasance, not policy disputes. Powell, whose term runs through May 2026, has said removal of top officials from the independent central bank is 'not permitted under the law."

Lotz continues, "Here is what to know about removing a Fed chair…. Scholars often point to a 1935 Supreme Court decision they say backs the Federal Reserve Act's cause for termination clause, the Wall Street Journal reported. In the case, Humphrey's Executor v. United States, the High Court blocked then-President Franklin D. Roosevelt from booting a commissioner off the Federal Trade Commission. That precedent prohibits commissioners at independent agencies from being fired at will…. If a legal battle ensues, the Supreme Court would likely have to clarify whether Humphrey's Executor applies to the chair of the Fed's Board of Governors."

Our Analysis:

Trump's Misunderstanding of Federal Reserve Independence: A Dangerous Precedent

In an early morning tirade on April 17, former President Donald Trump launched an attack on U.S. Federal Reserve Chairman Jerome Powell via his Truth Social platform. Trump's frustration was palpable, as he criticized Powell for not lowering interest rates in tandem with the European Central Bank (ECB), calling him "Too Late" Jerome Powell and lambasting his decisions as a "complete 'mess!'". Trump's call for Powell's termination underscores a fundamental misunderstanding of the Federal Reserve's role and independence, a cornerstone of the U.S. economic system that seems lost on him.

Trump's Authority: A Question of Limits

Axios' Avery Lotz raises a pertinent question: does Trump even have the authority to fire Powell? This question isn't just academic; it hits at the core of the U.S. governance system's checks and balances. Trump, notorious for his "You're fired!" catchphrase from his reality TV days, seems to believe that his executive power extends across all branches of government, including independent agencies like the Federal Reserve. This belief is not only misguided but dangerous.

The Federal Reserve, established over a century ago, operates independently of the White House for a reason—to ensure that the country's monetary policy is insulated from political interference. The Fed Chair, appointed for 14-year terms, can only be dismissed for cause (read: malfeasance, not policy disagreements). This independence is crucial for maintaining economic stability and credibility. Trump's inability to grasp this concept, or his willful ignorance of it, is alarming.

The Supreme Court's Role: A Potential Constitutional Crisis?

The article points out that any attempt by Trump to remove Powell could spark a legal battle that lands at the Supreme Court's doorstep. Herein lies a potential constitutional crisis. Some conservative justices have signaled openness to expanding executive power, which could set a dangerous precedent if they decide in favor of Trump. The reference to the 1935 Supreme Court decision in Humphrey's Executor v. United States highlights the historical precedent for protecting officials at independent agencies from arbitrary dismissal.

The Real Issue at Hand

Trump's rant against Powell is emblematic of a larger issue: his apparent belief that the economy should serve his political interests. By demanding Powell lower interest rates to align with the ECB, Trump is advocating for short-term gains that could jeopardize long-term economic stability. His fixation on tariffs and immediate economic indicators misses the forest for the trees, ignoring the nuanced balance required to manage the country's monetary policy.

In summary, Trump's latest outburst against Jerome Powell isn't just another social media tirade; it's a stark reminder of his naked economic incompetence. His misunderstanding of the Federal Reserve's role, combined with his apparent desire to bend it to his will, represents a clear and present danger to the U.S. economic system. This isn't just about Trump's inability to fire Powell—it's about the imperative to protect the independence of the Federal Reserve, without which the U.S. economy's stability is at risk.
