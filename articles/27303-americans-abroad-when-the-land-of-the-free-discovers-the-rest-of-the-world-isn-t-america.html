Americans Abroad: When the Land of the Free Discovers the Rest of the World Isn't America

Some Americans move to Europe seeking universal healthcare, better work-life balance, and a more humane society. Then they discover that enjoying those benefits might require living like Europeans.

There is a peculiar kind of American exceptionalism that apparently survives even the decision to leave America.

It is the belief that the United States is simultaneously a deeply dysfunctional society from which one must escape and the universal standard against which every other society must be judged.

The healthcare is broken. The work culture is exhausting. The politics are increasingly authoritarian. Public transportation is inadequate. Social protections are embarrassingly weak for a wealthy nation.

Enough is enough. Time to move to Europe!

And then, upon arrival:

What do you mean people don't do things the American way?

That contradiction is at the heart of a wonderfully revealing video by American expatriate YouTuber Evan Edinger, who examines an October 2026 WIRED article titled “They Fled Trump’s America. Now They Regret It.”

WIRED interviewed Americans who moved to Europe, discovered that their new homes weren't quite the paradise they'd imagined, and began contemplating a return to the United States.

There are legitimate stories to tell about immigration, homesickness, bureaucracy, employment difficulties, cultural exclusion, and disappointment.

Unfortunately, some of the complaints presented offer an entirely different story.

Not one of European failure, but of American expectations colliding with the inconvenient existence of other cultures.

The Dishwasher Crisis: A Tragedy in Two Continents

Consider Annie.

Annie and her husband were in their twenties when they moved from the American South to a town outside London in late 2024. Her husband had received a job offer, and the couple decided to take an adventure.

Before moving, Annie researched British life through TikTok, Instagram, and Reddit.

This is already an interesting choice.

Moving to another country is a consequential decision involving taxation, employment, housing, healthcare, immigration law, transportation, and the fundamental question of how people actually live.

Annie apparently supplemented her preparations with the international-relocation equivalent of reading restaurant reviews.

She arrived expecting charming British suburbs, affordable transportation, excellent healthcare, and the sort of politeness she'd seen in movies.

Instead, she encountered reality.

Among her complaints was that dishwashers and clothes dryers, which she considered commonplace in America, seemed like luxuries in Britain.

Now, Britain certainly has housing problems. Smaller homes, expensive energy, and older buildings can make appliance ownership less convenient than in the United States.

But dishwashers and dryers are not exotic European inventions that have yet to cross the English Channel. They exist. People own them.

Some Europeans also choose not to use dryers because hanging clothes is cheaper, consumes less energy, and can be gentler on fabrics.

Different priorities. Different habits.

But apparently the discovery that British people might dry their underwear without consuming electricity constituted a measurable decline in civilization.

Then came the complaint about having less disposable income.

That's a perfectly legitimate concern. British salaries are often lower than comparable American professional salaries, and housing costs can be punishing.

But the underlying cultural contrast is worth examining.

If your definition of a better life primarily involves purchasing more things, a country that emphasizes different social and economic priorities might indeed disappoint you.

The question is whether the country has failed to deliver quality of life—or whether you've defined quality of life so narrowly that you cannot recognize it when it takes another form.

A society's success cannot be measured solely by how much disposable income remains for the next Amazon delivery.

The Mystery of America's “Smooth” Healthcare

Annie's most interesting complaint concerns healthcare.

She reported difficulties with British National Health Service maternity care and contrasted those experiences with the smoothness of her private health insurance in America.

Evan responded with understandable incredulity.

Smooth?!

American health insurance?!

The system that has turned deductibles, copayments, prior authorizations, network restrictions, and insurance denials into a specialized vocabulary of financial anxiety?

The system in which having health insurance does not necessarily mean you can afford healthcare?

However, there is another possible explanation for Annie's experience, and it deserves consideration.

Perhaps American healthcare was genuinely smooth for Annie because someone else was doing the work.

Under the Affordable Care Act, Americans can generally remain covered under their parents' health insurance until age 26.

Remember, Annie and her husband were in their twenties when they moved.

We don't know her precise age, previous coverage arrangement, or family's financial circumstances. But it is entirely possible that much of her adult healthcare experience occurred while she was still covered by her parents' plan.

Imagine that arrangement.

She visits a doctor. Presents an insurance card. Receives treatment. Goes home.

Meanwhile, her parents might be paying the premiums, handling deductibles, receiving provider bills, reviewing explanations of benefits, and dealing with whatever administrative nonsense the insurer produces.

Depending on the arrangement, she might never have seen most of that paperwork.

To Annie, healthcare would indeed have been remarkably smooth.

Not because the system itself was efficient.

Because her parents may have been absorbing the friction.

Then she moves to Britain, becomes responsible for navigating a different healthcare system, and requires maternity care—potentially more complex than the routine services she previously needed.

Suddenly she encounters delays, bureaucracy, and expenses she cannot ignore.

Her conclusion?

American healthcare was better.

But that conclusion might be confusing a change in the healthcare system with a change in her own circumstances.

There's an important distinction between a system that works well and a system whose failures are invisible to you.

Naturally, this remains a hypothesis. We have no evidence that Annie was on her parents' insurance or that they handled her bills.

And her reported difficulties with maternity care should not be dismissed. Healthcare failures matter regardless of which country commits them.

But the possibility illustrates a much broader phenomenon:

Sometimes what people remember as a system that worked perfectly was simply a system whose failures someone else was quietly handling for them.

But I Pay Taxes!

Annie also objected that British healthcare wasn't actually free because she paid taxes and an immigration health surcharge.

Well, yes.

Public healthcare costs money.

Doctors receive salaries. Hospitals require equipment. Ambulances consume fuel. Medicine needs manufacturing. Someone must pay for all of it.

The distinction is not between healthcare that costs money and healthcare that magically materializes through benevolent government sorcery.

It is between different methods of financing and distributing those costs.

In Britain, NHS services are largely financed collectively, with many services available without a charge at the point of use. Most eligible immigrants also pay an immigration health surcharge, and certain services still carry charges.

In America, healthcare financing combines public programs, private insurance, employer contributions, household premiums, deductibles, and out-of-pocket payments.

The relevant comparison is not simply how much tax someone pays.

It is how much healthcare costs them overall, what care they can obtain, how quickly they can obtain it, and what happens when they become seriously ill or lose their income.

Annie is right about one thing: British healthcare isn't literally free.

But pointing out that taxpayers fund public services is not the devastating revelation she apparently imagined.

It's how public services work.

American Work Culture Meets People Who Have Lives

Then there is Sarah.

After living in Spain and Edinburgh, Sarah returned to New York, explaining that she missed American outgoingness and the country's characteristic readiness to act.

She described American professional culture as faster-moving, particularly when it came to collaboration and decision-making.

Now, this complaint is not inherently unreasonable.

Slow bureaucracy, indecisive management, and unresponsive colleagues can be genuinely frustrating. Respecting work-life balance is not an excuse for professional incompetence.

But Evan identifies a fascinating contradiction.

Americans frequently praise Europe for its supposedly healthier relationship with work.

More vacation. Greater employment protections. Stronger boundaries between professional and personal life.

Then some arrive and discover that these differences are not decorative features of a European lifestyle brochure.

They influence how people actually behave.

People may not treat every email as an emergency.

They may not organize their identities around their employers.

They may not consider working late an admirable demonstration of personal commitment.

They may even—brace yourself—have plans after work.

There is an interesting cultural assumption buried in the notion that faster automatically means better.

Faster for whom?

The employer? The customer? The employee waiting for a decision? The shareholder expecting quarterly growth?

Speed can be valuable.

But speed is not the only measure of productivity, and productivity is not the only measure of a worthwhile life.

An American employee might interpret a delayed response as insufficient ambition.

A European colleague might interpret the expectation of immediate responsiveness as an inability to respect personal boundaries.

Neither interpretation automatically proves the other culture superior.

But if you deliberately move to a country because you admire its work-life balance, becoming irritated when people actually maintain that balance suggests you haven't fully examined what attracted you there.

You cannot simultaneously demand a society that refuses to live for work and complain that its people refuse to live for work.

Germany: The Terrible Discovery That Freedom Has Boundaries

Then we meet Josh.

Josh moved to Berlin in 2023, partly because of concerns about American politics and the possibility of another Trump presidency.

Unlike Annie, he wasn't entirely unfamiliar with his destination.

He had lived in Germany before. He spoke German. He had friends in Berlin.

Nevertheless, he struggled with finding employment and navigating German bureaucracy.

Those are real problems, and Germany's reputation for administrative complexity is hardly undeserved.

Josh also described feeling like an outsider, arguing that people who weren't born and raised in Germany, and who didn't look German, would never be fully accepted.

That allegation deserves to be taken seriously.

Xenophobia and discrimination do not disappear simply because a country has universal healthcare or better labor protections.

But Josh made another complaint.

He missed American freedom of speech.

And that opens an entirely different discussion.

Freedom of Speech Is Not Freedom From Every Restriction

Germany protects freedom of expression under Article 5 of its Basic Law.

It also places legal limitations on certain forms of expression, including Holocaust denial, particular forms of Nazi propaganda, and unlawful incitement to hatred.

These restrictions exist within the historical context of a country whose previous democratic institutions were destroyed by a movement that used political freedoms to establish a murderous dictatorship.

Germany learned, at an almost incomprehensible human cost, that democratic rights can be exploited by movements seeking to abolish democracy itself.

The principle is straightforward:

Tolerance cannot survive if it is required to grant unlimited protection to organized intolerance.

That does not mean every German speech restriction is wise, proportionate, or beyond criticism.

Nor does Josh's complaint establish that he wanted to express Nazi sympathies or engage in hate speech. He never explained precisely what expression he felt unable to exercise.

But his criticism raises a meaningful question.

What specific freedom did he lose, and why did that limitation matter more to him than the protections Germany provides?

Without an answer, “I miss freedom of speech” is too vague to sustain a serious comparison between two democracies.

The United States and Germany have different constitutional traditions.

One cannot establish that Americans are freer merely by treating the American approach as the only legitimate definition of liberty.

There is another distinction that Americans frequently misunderstand: freedom of speech is not freedom from consequences.

The First Amendment protects your right to express opinions without unconstitutional government interference. It does not require other people to agree with you, respect your opinions, associate with you, or remain silent when you say something objectionable.

You have the right to speak. Others have the right to respond. You can criticize their beliefs, and they can criticize yours. That is not censorship. That is freedom of speech working exactly as intended.

What some people appear to want is not freedom of speech but freedom from accountability: the ability to say whatever they please while demanding that everyone else quietly tolerate it.

And when other people exercise their own freedom to object, suddenly the self-appointed champions of free speech discover that freedom is considerably less appealing when everyone gets to use it.

Escaping the American Right by Moving to… Poland?

And then comes the crown jewel.

Nick, an art director from New York, relocated to Kraków with his Polish wife and their young child.

One of his motivations for leaving the United States was the growth of right-wing populism.

His destination?

Poland.

Poland!

A country with a substantial history of powerful nationalist-conservative movements, bitter disputes over democratic institutions, and deeply contested questions involving abortion, religion, minority rights, and national identity.

Of all the European countries someone might select to escape the political influence of the authoritarian right, Poland is certainly an interesting choice.

Now, to be fair, Nick wasn't choosing a random destination from a map.

His wife was Polish. They had family connections and personal reasons to relocate there.

Those factors matter.

But his subsequent disappointment that European politics were not significantly better illustrates a fundamental misconception about the continent.

Europe is not a country. And it certainly isn't a political ideology.

Sweden is not Hungary.

Portugal is not Poland.

The Netherlands is not Italy.

Different countries have different political histories, institutions, economies, religious traditions, and social conflicts.

Moving to Europe to escape the right without researching the politics of your particular destination is rather like moving from New York to Texas to escape conservative governance because someone told you Austin has excellent coffee shops.

And there's an even larger problem.

Authoritarianism is not an exclusively American export.

Nationalist movements, anti-immigrant agitation, attacks on minority rights, and political efforts to weaken democratic safeguards have their own histories and domestic constituencies throughout Europe.

The American far right may admire, cooperate with, and borrow tactics from European counterparts, but those movements do not exist merely because the United States influences them.

Escaping a political movement requires considerably more research than crossing the Atlantic.

Geography is not an ideology.

Even the Critic Has His Blind Spots

Evan's video is entertaining and often incisive, but not every criticism lands equally well.

For example, he dismisses the employment difficulties of an American influencer who moved to Britain, questioning why she expected to support herself through sponsored content.

There is a reasonable point about researching employment prospects before immigrating.

But content creation is work. Building an audience, securing sponsorships, producing material, and maintaining a business are legitimate economic activities.

One can criticize a poorly researched business decision without pretending the underlying profession isn't real.

Similarly, Annie's experiences with accent-mocking should not be waved away as harmless British banter.

An occasional joke between friends is one thing.

Repeatedly mocking a foreign colleague's accent in a workplace is another.

And an immigrant who feels excluded is not necessarily refusing to assimilate. Sometimes the host society is doing the excluding.

Europe has real problems with xenophobia, racism, class prejudice, and cultural gatekeeping.

Acknowledging those problems does not invalidate the broader criticism of entitlement.

It makes the criticism more precise.

There is a difference between expecting equal dignity and expecting universal conformity to your preferences.

Immigrants deserve respect. They are not entitled to cultural obedience.

The Fundamental Misunderstanding: Comfort Is Not Quality of Life

The most revealing part of this entire discussion is how frequently American expatriates seem to confuse material convenience with overall quality of life.

The United States offers extraordinary conveniences to people who can afford them.

Large houses. Big appliances. Consumer choice. Fast commercial services. Higher salaries in certain professions.

There is nothing inherently wrong with enjoying these things.

But a society's quality of life also depends on factors less easily photographed for Instagram.

Can people afford to become sick?

Can they take time off work without financial catastrophe?

Can they raise children without sacrificing their careers?

Can they move around their communities without owning a car?

Can they maintain friendships, participate in civic life, and spend meaningful time with their families?

Can they retire with dignity?

And perhaps most importantly:

Does a person need to be relatively affluent before those possibilities become realistic?

European countries have not solved all these problems. Some have severe housing crises, strained healthcare systems, low wages, and deteriorating public services.

But their social arrangements often reflect different priorities.

An American accustomed to a large suburban home might feel deprived in a smaller European apartment.

A European accustomed to walking to shops, using public transit, and taking several weeks of paid vacation might regard that same American suburban lifestyle as isolating and exhausting.

Neither perception is imaginary.

The mistake is assuming that one person's accustomed lifestyle represents an objective universal standard.

Moving Abroad Is Not Ordering a Different Version of America

There is another distinction worth making.

Most immigrants do not enjoy the luxury of choosing their destinations based on an idealized lifestyle.

Many move because of war, persecution, economic necessity, family obligations, or the simple absence of viable opportunities at home.

They often arrive knowing perfectly well that life will be difficult.

They learn unfamiliar languages, navigate bureaucracies, accept professional setbacks, and endure separation from family because the alternative is worse.

That doesn't mean wealthier or more privileged migrants are forbidden from complaining.

It does mean there is something particularly revealing about treating cultural adjustment as an unexpected injustice.

When you immigrate, you become the foreigner.

Your accent is different.

Your assumptions are unfamiliar.

Your professional credentials may not translate neatly.

Your habits may be considered peculiar.

Your expectations may not be shared.

And sometimes, despite your best efforts, you won't fit in.

That experience can be uncomfortable, humbling, and occasionally painful.

It can also teach you something valuable.

For perhaps the first time, the culture that shaped you is no longer the default.

You are no longer surrounded by people who instinctively understand your references, preferences, and social rules.

You must confront the possibility that things you assumed were universal are merely American.

And that other people have built perfectly functional lives around different assumptions.

This is not evidence that your new country has failed.

It is evidence that you've moved to another country.

Nostalgia Is Not a Comparative Study

Near the end of his video, Evan makes an important observation.

After more than fourteen years in Britain, he still misses aspects of his American life.

Certain roads in New Jersey.

Familiar restaurants.

Foods he cannot easily obtain in Britain.

Places that carry memories of an earlier life.

And that's entirely normal.

You can miss your childhood home without wanting to move back into it.

You can remember an old workplace fondly without wanting your old job back.

You can miss American conveniences while preferring European social protections.

There is no contradiction.

Nostalgia is an emotion, not an economic indicator.

And immigration is not a referendum requiring you to declare one country superior in every conceivable category.

The most mature perspective recognizes that societies are complicated.

You can admire Britain's NHS while criticizing its waiting lists.

You can appreciate German labor protections while despising its bureaucracy.

You can enjoy American friendliness while condemning the country's healthcare financing.

You can appreciate European public transportation while demanding that rail operators improve service and pricing.

You can even decide that you were happier in America and return home.

None of that is inherently hypocritical.

The hypocrisy begins when you seek the advantages of another society while rejecting the conditions and cultural differences that help produce them.

The Failure Was Designed

What makes these stories fascinating isn't that some Americans regret moving abroad.

People regret major life decisions all the time.

What makes them revealing is the expectation that another society should deliver all the advantages of American life, eliminate its disadvantages, and require no meaningful adjustment in return.

Better healthcare, but without acknowledging how collective financing works.

Better work-life balance, but without accepting that colleagues may prioritize their lives over immediate professional responsiveness.

Better transportation, but without researching actual routes, service quality, or ticket prices.

Greater political security, but without investigating the politics of the destination.

A different culture, but preferably one that behaves exactly like home.

And, naturally, a dishwasher.

The deeper problem is a particular kind of American exceptionalism that persists even among Americans who reject American exceptionalist politics.

They may recognize that their country has serious institutional failures.

They may admire other countries for making different social choices.

But they still approach those countries as consumers accustomed to having their preferences accommodated.

They want to purchase a better society without learning how that society works.

And when reality fails to match the advertisement, they complain that the product was defective.

Yet countries aren't products. Cultures aren't customer-service departments. And immigration is not an all-inclusive vacation package.

There is a world of difference between moving somewhere because you want to experience another way of life and moving somewhere because you expect another way of life to accommodate yours.

One requires curiosity.

The other requires entitlement.

And sometimes the most difficult part of leaving America isn't crossing the ocean.

It's accepting that America was never the center of the universe.
