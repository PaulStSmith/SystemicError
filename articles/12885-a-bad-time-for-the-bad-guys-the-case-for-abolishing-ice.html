A Bad Time for the Bad Guys — The Case for Abolishing ICE

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 10, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

It begins with a uniform. A badge. A job title sterilized by bureaucracy: "Immigration and Customs Enforcement Officer." But peel away the veneer and what you find is not law enforcement. Not public service. What you find is a modern-day enforcer of xenophobic policy, a machine built to terrorize, detain, and dehumanize in the name of national security.

This is ICE—an agency so fundamentally broken, so morally bankrupt, that its continued existence is not just indefensible, it is a stain on the very idea of a democratic society. A recent video commentary—equal parts biting satire and righteous fury—reminds us why it's long past time to stop talking about "reform" and start demanding abolition.

The speaker in the video wastes no time drawing the line: ICE isn’t misunderstood. It isn’t a few bad apples. It’s a systemic apparatus of cruelty. These are not “good guys doing a hard job.” These are people coming home to their neighborhoods after spending their days separating families and throwing terrified human beings into cages. And yes, we see them. We know who they are. The neighbor in camo, the guy with the tactical bumper stickers, the one who thinks due process is optional if you have the wrong accent.

The proposal is simple, yet devastatingly effective: let these people feel the heat. Social pressure works. Mock them. Shame them. Remind them—publicly and persistently—that their job is an ethical disgrace. Because ICE isn’t just built on funding and policy. It’s built on people who willingly choose to carry it out.

No one is conscripted into ICE. They apply. They sit through interviews. They train. They know exactly what they’re signing up for. And for every quota they enforce, every screaming child they help vanish into the system, every shattered family they leave behind—they own that.

And now, some of them are unraveling. Good. Let them. Let the shame chew through their sleep. Let their neighbors stare just a little too long. Let their families ask, "Why? Why that job?"

Because here’s the truth: many of these agents are already miserable. The quotas are impossible, the morale is in freefall, and the public does not—will not—like them. They're not heroes. They're not even protectors. They are functionaries of oppression, and the rot of their own conscience is finally surfacing. It’s not suffering. It’s reckoning.

We’ve heard all the excuses. “They’re just following orders.” So were the worst actors in history. “It’s a job.” So is teaching. So is nursing. So is stocking shelves. But none of those involve stalking people through neighborhoods and wrenching them from their families because of paperwork. The ICE job is different. It's soaked in malice. It's built on the idea that some people simply don’t belong—and that violence is a valid means of making them disappear.

And here’s where the strategy becomes brilliant: if we make it socially toxic to work for ICE, we undercut the very foundation it stands on. Recruitment dries up. Retention fails. The machine begins to grind against itself. Without willing foot soldiers, the system starves.

That’s why the idea of confronting ICE workers isn’t just cathartic—it’s pragmatic. It’s activism at the dinner table, on the sidewalk, in the HOA meeting. It’s refusing to let them compartmentalize their lives. No more anonymity. No more pretending it’s “just a job.” If you choose to become the boot, don’t be surprised when people stop inviting you to the barbecue.

And when the administration loses the bodies needed to carry out its sadistic immigration agenda, the gears seize. You can’t deport thousands without someone to load the buses. You can’t terrorize communities without the agents to enforce terror.

It all begins with a whisper. A conversation. A side-eye. “Have you heard about these raids? Sounds like the Gestapo is back in fashion.”

It escalates from there. A cold shoulder. A canceled invite. A protest sign in the front yard. A cousin who refuses to visit until you quit that damn job. Shame spreads like wildfire when the oxygen of silence is removed.

And make no mistake, shame matters. Because shame is the soul’s alarm bell. It’s the flicker of decency still struggling to survive under layers of rationalization and state-sanctioned cruelty. If some ICE agents are unraveling under that weight, let them. Let their unraveling be the thread that pulls the whole system apart.

Because they don’t get to be both the oppressor and the victim. They don’t get our sympathy and our silence. Pick a lane. Either you’re complicit, or you’re walking away. There is no middle ground left.

We’ve spent too long pretending this agency can be “reformed.” It can’t. It was born out of fear, baptized in racism, and raised on cruelty. Reforming ICE is like repainting the guillotine. What we need is abolition.

Dismantle it. From budget to badge.

And to those still inside—get out. Before the shame swallows you whole.

Abolish ICE. Not someday. Now.
