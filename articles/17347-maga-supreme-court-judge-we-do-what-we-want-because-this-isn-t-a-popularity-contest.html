MAGA Supreme Court judge: We do what we want because this 'isn't a popularity contest'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 6, 20263 min read

The Untenable Position of Justice Gorsuch: Unmasking Supreme Court Partisanship

The Untenable Position of Justice Gorsuch: Unmasking Supreme Court Partisanship

Molding Reality with Words

Justice Neil Gorsuch’s recent comments on a podcast, amidst widespread criticism of the Supreme Court’s partisanship, stand as a stark illustration of how those in power attempt to reshape public perception. Gorsuch’s assertion that the Court’s role isn’t meant to be popular dismisses valid public concerns over its recent rulings as mere popularity contests. This rhetoric serves to deflect from the substantive issues at hand: the reversal of critical precedents and the erosion of judicial impartiality.

The Irony of Historical Comparison

Gorsuch’s reference to the grievances of the 1776 colonists about politicized judges is an ironic twist. He uses historical context to legitimize the current Court’s actions, suggesting a parallel with the colonists’ fight for independent judiciary. However, the crux of the matter he conveniently overlooks is that today’s criticisms are precisely about the Court’s perceived lack of independence and its sway towards conservative causes, underscored by significant decisions like overturning Roe v. Wade.

Civil Disguise Over Deep Divisions

The claim of civility between justices, such as between Gorsuch and Justice Sonia Sotomayor, is used as a smokescreen to obscure deep ideological divides that have tangible impacts on American lives. Gorsuch’s anecdote about their friendship, juxtaposed with their stark disagreements, minimizes the consequences of these divides. It is one thing to maintain personal cordiality; it’s another to suggest that civility neutralizes the impact of fundamentally conflicting legal philosophies on crucial issues.

A Question of Safety and Respect

The reported rift between Gorsuch and Sotomayor over COVID-19 mask-wearing further illustrates the complexities of these “civil” relationships. Despite public statements downplaying the issue, the incident speaks volumes about respect and responsibility, particularly during a health crisis. Gorsuch’s refusal to wear a mask, despite sitting next to a colleague at high risk, not only strains the narrative of friendly disagreement but also highlights a disregard for the welfare of his peers.

The Larger Pattern: Accountability Avoidance

This narrative from Gorsuch and the broader behavior of the Supreme Court reflect a significant and troubling pattern: the avoidance of accountability under the guise of judicial independence and legal objectivity. By claiming that the Court is above public opinion and political influence, justices like Gorsuch sidestep the critical scrutiny necessary for a truly independent judiciary. This stance not only misdirects but undermines the foundational democratic principle that no one, not even Supreme Court justices, is above reproach.

Conclusion: Civility Cannot Mask Injustice

The Supreme Court’s current trajectory, marked by significant reversals of precedent and a stark ideological tilt, is a far cry from the non-partisan ideal that Gorsuch tries to project. While civility among justices is undoubtedly important, it must not be confused with the effective functioning of a judicial body in a democracy. Civility cannot, and should not, be used as a veil to mask the deep and consequential rifts that these judicial philosophies create in American society. Rather than redefining the narrative to fit a more palatable story, the Court must confront the realities of its profound impact on the political and social fabric of the nation.
