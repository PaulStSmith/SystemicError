'Real War on Christmas': Trump threatens toy industry ahead of holiday shopping season

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 28, 20254 min read

The “War on Christmas” — once described by Politico as “a never-ending cultural conflict during which traditional, explicitly Christian celebrations of the holiday season are under assault from the sinister forces of secular pluralism” — is a staple of right-wing talking points against Democratic leaders.

But Dean Baker, senior economist at the Center for Economic and Policy Research, says the “real War on Christmas” is coming to President Donald Trump’s America — thanks to the president’s tariffs.

Baker referenced an NBC News article detailing the impact of Trump’s trade war on “as much as 75 percent of the toy products [the United States] sells from China.” And industry leaders are sounding the alarm.

“Christmas is in danger,” Toy Association president and CEO Greg Ahearn told NBC News. Trump’s tariffs, Ahearn explained, slow toy production every time they’re “[racheted] up” on China.

In a statement, White House spokesman Kush Desai told concerned business owners: “If you’re worried about tariffs, the solution is simple. Make your product in America.” But industry officials told NBC News that Chinese manufacturers have a unique combination of expertise, “molds, dyes, labor and safety techniques” that are severely lacking in the U.S. supply chain.

“It would be virtually impossible to replicate the industry’s efficiencies in the U.S. in a reasonable time frame” to service the upcoming Christmas holiday, NBC News reports.

As a Germany-based toy manufacturer, Bruder Toys America is also facing a 10 percent tariff. Company head Beate Caso warned “prices will go up tremendously” and supply will be limited this holiday season.

Our Analysis:

The Real "War on Christmas": Tariffs and Their Toll

Ah, the annual melodrama of the "War on Christmas"—a saga spun by right-wing pundits who claim the holiday is under siege by the forces of secular pluralism. But let's cut through the hyperbole and focus on a genuine threat to the holiday season, not conjured from cultural conflict but born from economic policy. Enter, stage right, President Donald Trump's tariffs.

Dean Baker, a senior economist at the Center for Economic and Policy Research, has pointed out the irony in the situation. While some obsess over keeping the "Christ" in Christmas, a more tangible war on the holiday looms over us, thanks to the Trump administration's trade policies. Here's the rub: as per an NBC News article, Trump’s trade war could affect "as much as 75 percent of the toy products [the United States] sells from China." Yes, the toys—the very lifeblood of Christmas cheer for millions of children and, let's be honest, quite a few adults.

Industry leaders are not just concerned; they're sounding the alarm. Greg Ahearn, the Toy Association president and CEO, ominously declared, "Christmas is in danger," attributing the peril to the tariffs that slow toy production each time they’re increased on China. The response from the White House? A quintessentially simplistic solution from spokesman Kush Desai: "If you’re worried about tariffs, the solution is simple. Make your product in America." Ah, if only economic restructuring were as easy as flipping a switch.

This brings us to a critical point: the unique ecosystem of manufacturing in China, with its "molds, dyes, labor, and safety techniques" that the U.S. supply chain severely lacks. NBC News reports that replicating this system stateside in time for Christmas is a pipe dream. And it's not just about the toys; as Beate Caso of Bruder Toys America highlighted, the tariffs mean "prices will go up tremendously," ensuring a less merry holiday season for consumers.

Moreover, the industry faces the compounded issue of worldwide shipping container shortages, slowing production for all products from China, not just toys. The result? Limited supply and higher costs, all thanks to tariffs. So, everything becomes more expensive, and the consumer bears the brunt of these policies.

In essence, the "War on Christmas" narrative peddled by some factions is a smokescreen obscuring the real issues at hand. The true battle is not against secular pluralism but against economic policies that directly impact the affordability and availability of holiday cheer. This year, if Christmas feels a bit scant, thank the tariffs. It's a stark reminder that economic policy has real-world consequences, and in this case, it's stealing Christmas, not Grinches or secularists.

The bottom line? The tariffs presented as a strategy to bolster American manufacturing and economy may, in fact, be the biggest Scrooge of all, threatening to turn a season of joy into one of economic strain for millions. This is the real "War on Christmas," waged not with words but with policy, and it's time to recognize the frontlines are not in cultural debates but in the economic arena.
