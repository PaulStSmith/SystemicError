The Myth of the Gun That Ends the Argument

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 27, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

For decades, American gun culture has leaned on a reassuring story: most armed encounters end peacefully once a gun is shown. No shots fired. No blood. Just deterrence. The story is comforting, intuitive, and disastrously oversold.

It’s also one of the most misunderstood claims in the entire gun debate.

This article isn’t about denying that deterrence sometimes happens. It’s about interrogating what we actually know, what we don’t, and why a lack of data has been quietly repackaged as proof.

Where the Claim Comes From

The idea that “most defensive gun uses end without shots fired” traces back largely to self‑reported survey research from the 1990s, most famously associated with criminologist Gary Kleck. These surveys asked respondents whether they had ever used a gun defensively, broadly defined, and whether a shot was fired.

Many respondents said no shots were fired. From this emerged a powerful inference: the display of a gun alone often ends the threat.

But here’s the problem. That inference rests on data that is:

• Self‑reported
• Retrospective (sometimes years later)
• Unverified
• Dependent on subjective threat perception
• Untethered from police, hospital, or court records

In other words, it measures what people felt happened, not what can be independently shown to have occurred.

That distinction matters more than advocates are willing to admit.

Defensive Gun Use Is Not a Clean Category

“Defensive gun use” sounds precise. It isn’t.

In surveys, DGUs can include:
• A homeowner hearing a noise and holding a gun
• A driver brandishing during a road‑rage argument
• A shopkeeper scaring off a loiterer
• A person who believed someone posed a threat

Many of these situations never involved a confirmed crime, an identified attacker, or imminent violence. Some may have de‑escalated naturally. Others may have been misunderstandings amplified by fear.

When all of these are collapsed into one bucket, “ended without shots fired” becomes almost meaningless as a safety metric.

If nothing happened, we cannot know whether the gun caused that outcome — or whether nothing was going to happen anyway.

The Missing Counterfactual

The most important question is also the one least asked:

What would have happened if no gun were present?

Most DGU claims have no counterfactual. We do not know whether:
• The attacker would have fled anyway
• A verbal confrontation would have sufficed
• A non‑lethal deterrent would have worked
• The situation would have fizzled out on its own

Without that comparison, attributing causality to the gun is speculation, not evidence.

This is where the trope quietly crosses from observation into ideology.

What Hard Data Actually Shows

When we move away from surveys and toward incident‑level data — police reports, hospital records, and FBI active‑shooter summaries — a different picture emerges.

In documented violent incidents:
• Most end without civilian gunfire
• Many end through flight, interruption, or unarmed resistance
• Armed civilian intervention is rare
• The introduction of additional guns increases uncertainty, not clarity

This does not mean deterrence never occurs. It means it is not reliably measurable at scale, and it is not demonstrably superior to other forms of de‑escalation.

Importantly, trained police officers under stress hit their intended targets roughly 20% of the time. Civilians do not outperform this. The idea that the presence of a gun is inherently stabilizing ignores both human physiology and chaos dynamics.

Display Is Not Neutral

Showing a gun is not a pause button. It is an escalation threshold.

Once a firearm is introduced:
• Misinterpretation becomes more likely
• Third parties may perceive a threat incorrectly
• Police arriving on scene face identification ambiguity
• A momentary standoff can turn lethal in seconds

The fact that many such situations don’t turn lethal is not proof of safety. It is proof of luck.

Policy cannot be built on luck.

Why the Myth Persists

The “gun ends the encounter” story survives because it satisfies multiple psychological and political needs:

• It frames guns as protective rather than risky
• It converts uncertainty into reassurance
• It avoids confronting systemic failure
• It offers individual control in a chaotic society

Most importantly, it is almost impossible to disprove decisively — because the absence of harm is treated as evidence of success.

That’s a classic logical trap.

What an Honest Conclusion Looks Like

The evidence supports a far narrower claim than gun‑culture rhetoric allows:

• Some defensive gun uses appear to end without shots fired
• We do not know how often this occurs
• We do not know whether the gun caused the outcome
• We do not know whether overall harm was reduced

Anything beyond that is storytelling.

A society serious about safety would invest in:
• Standardized national reporting
• Clear definitions of defensive use
• Comparative outcome analysis
• De‑escalation training and prevention upstream

Instead, we get slogans.

Final Thought

The most dangerous lie isn’t that guns never deter. It’s the implication that deterrence scales cleanly, predictably, and safely across millions of interactions.

It doesn’t.

What ends most confrontations isn’t firepower. It’s ambiguity, hesitation, retreat, and the simple fact that most people do not actually want to kill anyone.

The gun doesn’t create that restraint. It merely coexists with it — and sometimes shatters it.

That’s not anti‑gun. That’s reality, unvarnished.
