Trump relative exposes 'humiliating' mashed potato incident he never forgot

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosNovember 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A bully interruptedA family theory becomes a political diagnosisThe limits of the origin story

Mary Trump says the mashed-potato incident her uncle never forgot began with five-year-old Donald Trump tormenting his younger brother Robert and ended when their older brother, Fred Jr., dumped a bowl of potatoes over Donald’s head. The family laughed. Decades later, Mary Trump argues, the episode helps explain the president’s fixation on humiliating others—but her family story supports a narrower conclusion more securely than a sweeping psychological diagnosis: Trump was already bullying someone smaller, and ridicule stopped him when parental authority did not.

A bully interrupted

In a Thanksgiving video posted to YouTube, Mary Trump described a family dinner at which Donald repeatedly harassed Robert while their mother, Mary Anne Trump, struggled to serve the meal. According to her account, he took pleasure in destroying things Robert valued, including gluing together his building blocks and dismantling his Tonka trucks.

Mary Trump said her grandmother pleaded and shouted but could not make Donald stop. Fred Jr., nearly eight years older than Donald, then chose a remedy that was forceful without being violent: He picked up a large bowl of mashed potatoes and emptied it onto Donald’s head.

“And you know what? It worked,” Mary Trump said.

The room initially fell silent, according to her telling, before the family—including Donald’s mother—began laughing. Donald’s sister later revived the story at family gatherings, including Thanksgiving and Christmas, turning the episode into recurring family lore.

The headline-ready object is the mashed potatoes. The consequential detail is the hierarchy: Donald was tormenting a younger child who could not stop him, ignoring a mother whose authority he rejected, and was finally checked by an older, stronger brother. The humiliation did not create the bullying described in the story. It interrupted it.

A family theory becomes a political diagnosis

Mary Trump, an author and clinical psychologist who has publicly opposed her uncle and his political agenda, interprets the dinner as a formative lesson. She said Donald concluded both that humiliation was something he must avoid and that it could be wielded as a weapon.

That is her psychological reading, not an independently established causal account. One childhood memory—even one repeatedly retold—cannot by itself explain an adult political career. Mary Trump’s interpretation also risks assigning too much power to the family’s laughter and too little to the conduct that preceded it. In her own account, the future president was already using domination as what she called a form of “self-soothing.”

She places that behavior within a broader family culture established by Fred Trump Sr. Her grandfather, she said, enjoyed humiliating people who were weaker and dependent on him. She argues that Donald’s motive differs: Where Fred Sr. allegedly took pleasure in degradation, Donald uses it defensively.

That distinction may matter clinically, but it offers little comfort politically. Whether humiliation is deployed for enjoyment or self-protection, the burden still falls on the chosen target. A president has access to institutions, audiences and loyal political actors that a five-year-old at a dinner table did not. The weapon, if Mary Trump’s theory is right, acquired vastly greater reach.

The limits of the origin story

Family anecdotes are irresistible because they compress a public figure into a single revealing scene. They can also misdirect responsibility. Explaining Trump’s behavior through a loveless upbringing, a domineering father or one embarrassing dinner can make his later conduct sound like the automatic expression of childhood damage.

It is not. Mary Trump’s account may illuminate a pattern, but it does not transfer agency away from the adult who repeats it or from the people and institutions that reward it. Childhood can shape a person without absolving the choices that person later makes with power.

The mashed-potato story is therefore less useful as a tidy origin myth than as an early illustration of a familiar structure: a stronger person targets someone weaker, ordinary authority fails to restrain him, and only an intervention carrying immediate social consequences makes him stop. Mary Trump remembers the humiliation. Her own account makes the unchecked cruelty that came first harder to ignore.
