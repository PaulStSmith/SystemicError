Trump reboots his most brazen scandal —and nobody's stopping him this time

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 5, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump plans to host the G20 summit at Trump National Doral Miami on December 14–15, 2026, directing world leaders and their entourages to his privately owned resort. Trump abandoned a similar plan for the 2020 G7 summit after Republican opposition, but the new arrangement has reportedly produced little protest.

Citing New York Times reporting, AlterNet describes the Doral decision as one part of a much larger pattern of self-enrichment during Trump’s second term. The cited examples include an estimated $2.2 billion increase in his wealth, acceptance of a luxury jet from Qatar, favorable treatment over potential back taxes, a Trump TV trademark alongside taxpayer-backed promotion, and public money used for Republican campaign advertising. The article does not independently establish each claim.

Gallup polling cited in the story found that 89 percent of American adults considered government corruption widespread, up 10 percentage points from the previous year. That measures public perception, not proof that any particular allegation caused the increase.

The beneficiary holds the power

This is not a conflict between evenly matched interests. Trump is simultaneously president, the official presiding over the summit decision, and the owner of the business positioned to benefit from it. Foreign delegations do not choose the venue on equal terms with the White House. The administration controls the event; Trump’s property receives the prestige, traffic and commercial exposure.

That concentration of public authority and private benefit is the central political fact. “Conflict of interest” almost sounds too mild because it suggests two competing obligations. Here, the person empowered to protect the public interest reportedly selected his own asset as the destination for a major government function.

Normalization is an institutional decision

The contrast with 2019 matters. Then, Republican resistance helped force Trump to abandon the plan. In 2026, according to the reporting, essentially the same maneuver is proceeding with little protest.

That change did not happen because the ethical issue became more complicated. It happened because officials, party leaders and other institutional actors stopped imposing the political cost that once mattered. Silence is not an atmospheric condition. It is the cumulative result of people with authority deciding that confrontation is less useful to them than accommodation.

Paul Rosenzweig’s description of the Doral arrangement as relatively small beside Trump’s other reported conduct captures the mechanism: repeated escalation makes yesterday’s scandal look trivial. But scale does not excuse the clearer offense. It reveals how saturation protects it.

The corruption fog

The article’s strongest point is that abundance can become camouflage. When alleged abuses arrive faster than institutions investigate, explain or punish them, each new case competes with the last one for a shrinking supply of attention.

That dynamic can also misdirect responsibility. Saying that “nobody” is stopping Trump blurs the distinction between people who lack enforcement power and those who possess it but decline to use it. Ordinary Americans can disapprove; Congress, executive-branch officials and governing-party leaders can impose consequences. Treating collective exhaustion as the cause lets powerful enablers disappear into the crowd.

The golf-cheating material is a distraction from that structure. Anecdotes about Trump’s conduct on the course may reinforce a character portrait, but they do not explain how presidential authority can be used to steer an international summit toward presidential property. The scandal is governmental, not sporting.

Disapproval without restraint

The Gallup result shows extraordinary public distrust: 89 percent described government corruption as widespread. Yet perception alone does not restrain an administration. Anger becomes politically harmless when institutions absorb it without changing their conduct.

That is the deeper asymmetry. The public bears the cynicism produced by alleged self-dealing, while officials retain the power to normalize it. The resulting distrust can even benefit those who treat government as private spoils: if corruption is assumed to be universal and unstoppable, specific responsibility dissolves into generalized disgust.

Impunity resets the baseline

The larger pattern is not merely that standards have weakened. Standards are being rewritten through repetition and non-enforcement. A practice that once triggered retreat can become routine when the governing coalition decides there will be no penalty.

Doral is therefore useful precisely because it is easy to understand. Public power reportedly selected the president’s private property, and the resistance that stopped the same arrangement before has largely vanished. The system did not become confused about who benefits. The people positioned to intervene learned to tolerate the benefit—and tolerance, exercised by those with power, is how impunity becomes policy.
