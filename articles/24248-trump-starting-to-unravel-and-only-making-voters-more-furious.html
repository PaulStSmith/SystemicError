Trump 'starting to unravel' — and only making voters more furious

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosSeptember 30, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump’s insistence that the economy is strong has collided with intense dissatisfaction among voters in four states he won by double digits in 2024. Navigator Research managing director Melissa Toufanian said polling in Alaska, Ohio, Iowa, and Texas put Trump’s net approval at minus 14 across the four states and underwater in each one.

Toufanian said Trump was 17 points underwater in Iowa, where 60 percent disapproved of his handling of the economy and 50 percent strongly disapproved. She also cited pessimism about his presidency and the war with Iran. The headline’s claim that Trump is “starting to unravel” comes from New Republic podcast host Greg Sargent’s interpretation of those findings—not from a clinical assessment or an established fact.

AlterNet also cites conservative columnist Tim Carney, who argued that Trump is harming farmers’ ability to sell crops and then trying to compensate them with government checks. Meanwhile, Trump has continued promoting polling that depicts sustained support among Republican or MAGA voters.

Power made the damage

Trump holds the presidency. His administration possesses the institutional power to shape the economic and foreign policies provoking the dissatisfaction described in the polling. Farmers and other voters absorbing the consequences do not.

That distinction matters because political coverage often treats economic anger like weather: it arrives, intensifies, and mysteriously damages approval ratings. Carney’s criticism restores the missing chain of agency. The government interferes with people’s livelihoods, then presents compensation as generosity. The check may soften the loss, but it does not erase who imposed it.

Compensation is not control

Carney compares Trump’s approach with a familiar Republican caricature of Democratic governance: eliminate someone’s job, then offer benefits. His point is not simply that the policy is unpopular. It is that financial compensation cannot replace economic control, professional identity, or confidence that a market will remain available.

Trump can place supporters on a stage beside relief checks. He cannot make that ceremony equivalent to an undamaged livelihood. When state power creates the disruption and then purchases visible gratitude from those affected, assistance becomes political theater layered over dependence.

Polling cannot carry the whole argument

The reported numbers are striking, especially the intensity of disapproval in Iowa. But the article pushes beyond what the evidence establishes when it treats polling in four states as proof that groups in Trump’s coalition are broadly “abandoning him” in 2026.

Toufanian herself supplied an important complication: these voters remain distrustful of Democrats. Disapproval of Trump does not automatically become Democratic support, and unfavorable polling does not by itself mean Republican Senate seats will change hands. Sargent called that possibility “really crazy,” which is enthusiasm about an electoral opening, not evidence that the opening will produce a victory.

The “unraveling” frame is also weaker than the underlying story. It personalizes a conflict produced by presidential decisions, reducing policy consequences to speculation about Trump’s condition. The relevant fact is not whether he appears emotionally composed. It is that voters in heavily Republican states reportedly disapprove of how he is using power.

The counter-polling defense

Trump’s promotion of friendlier polling is an attempt to control which measurement counts. AlterNet describes him highlighting figures showing continued backing among Republicans or the MAGA base while the Navigator results show deep dissatisfaction in states central to his return to office.

Those claims are not necessarily contradictory: a president can retain substantial support among committed partisans while losing approval across a broader electorate. Treating favorable subgroup numbers as a complete answer simply narrows the public until only loyalists remain visible.

The governing bargain breaks

The larger pattern is a government creating material disruption, offering compensation, and expecting partisan identity to close the gap between injury and gratitude. That bargain works only while supporters accept political loyalty as a substitute for results.

The reported anger does not prove a realignment, much less a Democratic revival. It shows something more immediate: concentrated presidential power produces identifiable consequences, and even loyal constituencies can recognize when government aid is being used to manage damage that government policy helped create.
