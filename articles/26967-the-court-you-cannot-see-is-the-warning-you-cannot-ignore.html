The Court You Cannot See Is the Warning You Cannot Ignore

Today it is an immigration case. The real danger is what happens when secret justice becomes acceptable.

There are moments when the most important part of a news story is not what the government has done, but what the government has discovered that it can do.

The Trump administration has just had one of those moments.

On September 11, 2026, the Justice Department announced the first completed removal in the history of the Alien Terrorist Removal Court, or ATRC—a specialized federal court created by Congress in 1996 and left unused by every administration for the next thirty years.

Nazira Haji Zada, a 47-year-old Afghan national and lawful permanent resident, was deported after the government accused her of supporting an ISIS-inspired Election Day attack plot involving her son and son-in-law. She was never criminally charged. Her relatives were. She ultimately conceded the government’s designation of her as an “alien terrorist,” waived appeal, and agreed to removal.

That is the immediate story.

It is not the important one.

The important story is that the United States government has just successfully activated a judicial mechanism specifically designed to allow the government to present evidence against someone without necessarily allowing that person—or even their ordinary attorneys—to see it.

And the administration doing the activating is one that has repeatedly demonstrated an appetite for stretching executive authority, punishing perceived enemies, politicizing government institutions, and treating legal restraints less as boundaries than as obstacles awaiting a sufficiently creative lawyer.

That should scare the hell out of anyone paying attention.

The Defendant Is Almost Irrelevant

We need to get something out of the way immediately.

This is not a defense of Nazira Haji Zada.

The allegations against her are serious. According to the Justice Department, she supported members of her family who were preparing an ISIS-inspired mass shooting on Election Day 2024. Her son and son-in-law were arrested after purchasing firearms and ammunition from an undercover FBI employee; both were subsequently convicted.

If the government can prove that Zada knowingly assisted a terrorist plot, there are perfectly legitimate reasons why the United States would seek to remove a noncitizen from the country.

But that is precisely why she makes such a useful first defendant.

Governments rarely introduce extraordinary powers by using them against sympathetic people.

They begin with someone frightening.

A terrorist.

A spy.

A saboteur.

Someone whose alleged conduct is so repulsive that questioning the government’s methods can easily be twisted into defending the defendant.

“You don’t support terrorism, do you?”

No.

We support due process.

And due process matters most when the defendant is someone we despise.

If procedural rights exist only for people whose innocence we already assume, then they aren’t rights at all. They’re courtesies.

Welcome to the Court Where the Government Knows What You Did—but You May Not

The Alien Terrorist Removal Court sounds like something invented for a dystopian political thriller.

Unfortunately, Congress invented it instead.

Under federal law, the Attorney General can initiate proceedings using classified information alleging that a noncitizen is an “alien terrorist.” Some proceedings are public, and the defendant has a right to counsel. Those safeguards matter and should not be erased from the description.

Then we reach the machinery underneath them.

The statute explicitly permits classified evidence to be examined by the judge ex parte and in camera—outside the presence of the defendant and ordinary defense counsel. Neither the defendant nor the public necessarily learns what that evidence is or where it came from. Instead, the government ordinarily provides an unclassified summary approved by the judge. Under extraordinary circumstances, even that summary can be withheld.

For lawful permanent residents, a special attorney can be appointed to inspect and challenge classified evidence—but that attorney is forbidden from revealing the classified information to the defendant or the defendant’s ordinary attorney. Violating that prohibition can carry an enormous criminal penalty.

The statute goes further.

Evidence obtained through certain FISA surveillance can be used while normal discovery can be restricted on national-security grounds. The defendant cannot suppress evidence merely by alleging that it was unlawfully obtained. The Federal Rules of Evidence do not apply. Portions of the final judicial order itself may remain hidden if they would reveal classified information.

This is not a completely lawless tribunal.

In some ways that makes the problem more disturbing.

It is lawfully constructed opacity.

Congress built a judicial process in which the government can effectively say:

“We have evidence against you. The judge can see it. You may not be able to.”

And for thirty years, administrations somehow managed not to use this remarkable instrument.

Then Donald Trump came back to Washington.

The Gun Was Already in the Drawer

It would be intellectually lazy to blame Trump for creating the ATRC.

He didn’t.

Congress created it in 1996 as part of the Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act.

That matters.

American democracy has an unfortunate habit of constructing extraordinary powers during periods of fear and then reassuring itself that responsible people will use those powers responsibly.

That is not institutional design.

That is leaving a loaded gun in a drawer with a Post-it note saying:

“For emergencies only.”

The Trump administration opened the drawer.

The Justice Department itself proudly emphasized that no previous administration had ever used the court.

Think about that.

The ATRC survived Bill Clinton.

It survived George W. Bush and September 11.

It survived the War on Terror.

It survived Barack Obama.

It survived Trump’s first administration.

It survived Joe Biden.

For three decades, including the most terrorism-obsessed period in modern American history, the federal government managed to deal with terrorism without completing a single removal through this court.

Then the current administration decided that this dormant mechanism needed to breathe.

That decision deserves considerably more scrutiny than the government’s triumphant press release is likely to encourage.

“Every Tool at Its Disposal”

Attorney General Todd Blanche described the result as a “landmark case” and declared that the Justice Department would use “every tool at its disposal” to protect the country.

That phrase should not be dismissed as boilerplate.

Because the question is not merely what tools exist.

The question is which tools a government chooses to reach for.

A chainsaw and a scalpel are both tools. Choosing one tells us something about what you intend to do.

This administration deliberately reached for a judicial mechanism unused for thirty years, built around classified evidence and reduced public scrutiny.

And it worked.

There was no major constitutional showdown.

There was no appellate ruling establishing the outer boundaries of the government’s authority.

Zada conceded the designation and waived appeal. Reuters reported that her attorneys explicitly argued that the process denied constitutional due process and warned that her agreement to removal should not be interpreted as endorsing the legitimacy of the court.

So the administration received something enormously valuable:

a successful first use without an appellate court seriously testing the constitutional architecture.

The machine has now been switched on.

Today, an Immigrant

Here we need to be precise.

The Alien Terrorist Removal Court cannot simply haul an American citizen before it tomorrow.

Its jurisdiction is statutory and limited to noncitizens accused of falling within the relevant terrorism provisions.

That distinction matters.

But anyone who thinks that distinction eliminates the broader danger has learned absolutely nothing from the history of emergency powers.

The danger is not that Donald Trump can wake up tomorrow morning and order the ATRC to imprison an American journalist.

He cannot.

The danger is that the underlying principle becomes normalized.

Secret evidence becomes acceptable.

Ex parte accusations become acceptable.

Reduced discovery becomes acceptable.

National security becomes sufficient justification for preventing the accused from knowing the complete case against them.

Special tribunals become acceptable.

And perhaps most dangerously:

the public becomes accustomed to justice it cannot inspect.

Once society accepts those premises for the frightening foreigner accused of terrorism, the political fight changes.

We are no longer debating whether secret justice is compatible with a free society.

We have accepted that it is.

Now we are merely negotiating who qualifies for it.

That is a much more dangerous argument.

Exceptional Powers Have a Peculiar Habit of Becoming Less Exceptional

History is littered with powers introduced under assurances that they would be used only against extraordinary threats.

The surveillance state created to hunt foreign terrorists did not remain neatly confined to cinematic villains hiding in caves.

The infrastructure of the War on Terror expanded.

Definitions expanded.

Databases expanded.

Surveillance expanded.

Executive interpretations expanded.

The category of people supposedly requiring extraordinary treatment has an irritating tendency to expand right alongside them.

That does not mean the ATRC itself inevitably will be expanded to citizens.

It means something more fundamental:

governments learn from successful exercises of power.

Lawyers learn which arguments courts tolerate.

Agencies learn which procedures survive challenge.

Politicians learn which abuses provoke outrage and which disappear into the news cycle.

The public learns what to regard as normal.

And future legislation can be written.

New tribunals can be created.

Existing national-security statutes can be stretched.

Old statutes can be excavated from whatever forgotten drawer they have occupied for decades.

The lesson the Trump administration has just received is not merely that it can deport Nazira Haji Zada.

It is that opaque judicial machinery can be activated, defended as national security, and successfully used.

That lesson travels.

And This Administration Has Already Told Us How It Thinks About Dissent

If this were an administration obsessively respectful of institutional boundaries, perhaps one could regard the ATRC’s activation as an unusual response to an unusual terrorism case.

It isn’t.

The administration has already tried using immigration authority against noncitizens over political expression. In August, a federal judge ruled unconstitutional its use of immigration law against pro-Palestinian activists based on their political views.

Just this week, another federal judge blocked the administration’s “loyalty question” for federal job applicants—an essay asking candidates how they would advance the president’s policies—finding that the practice likely violated federal law and First Amendment protections.

These are not hypotheticals about what some future authoritarian government might attempt.

They are evidence about the government currently holding the keys.

When someone repeatedly tries the doors, you don’t call it paranoia when people become concerned after he discovers another key.

The Most Dangerous Words in Government: “Trust Us”

There are legitimate reasons for governments to possess secrets.

Intelligence sources cannot always be exposed publicly. Surveillance capabilities sometimes must remain classified. Informants can be killed. Operations can be compromised.

Nobody serious disputes that.

But there is an enormous difference between:

“Some government information must remain secret”

and

“The government may take action against you using evidence you cannot meaningfully inspect.”

The first is an unavoidable feature of national security.

The second should make every civil libertarian’s hair stand on end.

Because the fundamental safeguard against government abuse is adversarial scrutiny.

The government says X.

The defense examines X.

The defense challenges X.

The court decides whether X survives scrutiny.

Take away meaningful access to X and something essential has changed.

Perhaps the government’s intelligence is impeccable.

Perhaps its source is trustworthy.

Perhaps the evidence has been interpreted correctly.

Perhaps the government has identified the right person.

Perhaps officials have accurately characterized what the evidence says.

Perhaps nobody involved has a political motive.

Perhaps nobody lied.

Perhaps nobody exaggerated.

Perhaps nobody made a mistake.

Perhaps nobody concealed exculpatory context.

Perhaps.

Trust us.

Democratic systems were not supposed to operate on “perhaps.”

The First Case Is the Easy Case

That is why focusing entirely on whether Zada deserved deportation misses the institutional danger.

Maybe she did.

The government certainly alleges serious conduct, and her family connections to an actual terrorist plot are documented.

But the first case was always going to be easy to sell.

The important case will be the tenth.

Or the hundredth.

The important case will arrive after the machinery is familiar.

After journalists stop explaining what the ATRC is every time its name appears.

After “alien terrorist removal proceeding” becomes another bureaucratic phrase.

After people stop being shocked that evidence can be hidden.

After the government discovers exactly how far judges will allow it to go.

Normalization does not require tanks in the streets.

Sometimes normalization looks like paperwork.

The Architecture Is the Threat

This is where Americans repeatedly make the same catastrophic analytical mistake.

They evaluate government powers according to the person currently targeted.

Wrong question.

The correct question is:

Would you willingly give this power to the worst president you can imagine?

Would you give that president the ability to accuse people using evidence they cannot see?

Would you allow that president’s Justice Department to decide what must remain secret?

Would you allow that administration to invoke “national security” to shield portions of the government’s case from public examination?

Would you build special judicial machinery around those assumptions?

If the answer changes depending upon who occupies the White House, then you have identified the problem.

Rights exist specifically because governments cannot be trusted to decide, case by case, who deserves them.

Transparency exists because power behaves differently when someone is watching.

Adversarial proceedings exist because government assertions are not self-validating.

Due process exists because the state can be wrong.

And constitutional government exists because sometimes the state is not merely wrong.

Sometimes it is malicious.

Congress Built It. Trump Used It. Congress Can Kill It.

There is plenty of blame to distribute here.

The Trump administration deserves scrutiny for deliberately resurrecting this tribunal.

But Congress built the damned thing.

It created a court capable of considering evidence hidden from the person whose life is being transformed by its judgment. It placed extraordinary authority behind the phrase “national security” and apparently assumed future administrations would exercise restraint.

That assumption has now met Donald Trump.

Congress should repeal the Alien Terrorist Removal Court provisions and return terrorism-related immigration proceedings to judicial processes containing robust adversarial protections. Classified information can be protected through existing procedures without maintaining a specialized removal tribunal designed around secrecy.

And Congress should go further.

It should review the sprawling collection of emergency, national-security and immigration authorities accumulated over decades and ask a question lawmakers should have asked before granting them:

What happens when these powers are controlled by someone acting in bad faith?

Because that person eventually arrives.

The American political system has spent decades building extraordinary powers under the assumption that extraordinary people would exercise them responsibly.

That assumption is dead.

Policy must catch up with reality.

And while we are discussing an immigration-enforcement system increasingly used as a vehicle for executive coercion, the answer to ICE is not another round of cosmetic guardrails or promises of better management.

ICE should be abolished.

An institution repeatedly used as the operational muscle for a system of mass detention, aggressive removal and political intimidation does not need better branding. Its functions need to be dismantled and whatever legitimate immigration responsibilities remain reassigned to institutions constructed around due process rather than raids and coercion.

Watch the Door, Not Just the Person Being Dragged Through It

Nazira Haji Zada is gone.

The court remains.

That is the story.

Tomorrow’s target cannot be an American citizen before this particular court unless the law radically changes. We should say that plainly, because exaggeration only gives defenders of authoritarianism something irrelevant to argue about.

But tomorrow’s principle absolutely can reach further.

Once a government establishes that secrecy is an acceptable substitute for scrutiny, the architecture is available for imitation.

Once courts tolerate extraordinary procedures in the name of national security, lawyers know where the boundaries currently stand.

Once citizens become comfortable with people losing rights through proceedings they cannot fully examine, expanding the category of acceptable targets becomes easier.

And once an authoritarian-minded administration learns that one supposedly extraordinary mechanism works, there is every reason to expect it to go looking for another.

The terrifying thing about authoritarianism is rarely that every institution disappears overnight.

Courts remain.

Statutes remain.

Judges remain.

Forms are filed.

Orders are signed.

Officials insist everything is legal.

The machinery continues operating.

It simply begins operating according to different assumptions about who deserves protection from whom.

For thirty years, the Alien Terrorist Removal Court existed as dormant machinery.

The Trump administration has turned it on.

The question Americans should be asking is not simply what happened to the first person who went through it.

They should be looking at the machine.

And wondering what this administration intends to switch on next.
