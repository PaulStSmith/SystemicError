When Facts Trigger the Block Button: A Case Study in America’s Prison Mythology

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 10, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

It started with a simple post about eight incarcerated men who saved the life of a guard suffering a heart attack. A rare moment where humanity slipped through the cracks of a system designed to crush it. A commenter chimed in with the usual well-meaning platitude about rehabilitation, insisting that corrections was built around helping people rebuild their lives.

That’s where I stepped in.

Because anyone who has even cursorily studied the structure of American incarceration — and especially anyone who has survived its machinery from the outside or the inside — knows the comforting narrative of “rehabilitation” has almost no overlap with reality.

What followed was a textbook example of how defenders of the system deflect, distort, and, eventually, flee when forced to confront the architecture of the beast they’re defending.

Act I: The Storybook Version of Corrections

The thread began with a commenter claiming that the purpose of corrections is rehabilitation. Not punishment. Not containment. Not feeding the maw of bureaucratic inertia or corporate profit. No, rehabilitation — the soft-focus mission statement etched into every DoC website.

If you’ve lived in America long enough to watch a politician tour a prison, you’ve heard the line: We’re here to help them become productive members of society.

The numbers tell a different story.

Recidivism in the United States doesn’t drift or wobble. It remains a near-flat line decade after decade. Roughly two-thirds of people released from prison are rearrested within three years. Over half return to incarceration. These are not the outcomes of a “Rehabilitation System”. These are the outcomes of a machine built for churn.

But the commenter didn’t want to hear that.

Act II: “I Worked There” as a Shield Against Reality

Their next move was predictable: the credential flex.

I worked with Probation and Parole! I typed the court orders! I saw rehabilitation firsthand!
How dare you challenge my experience?

This is the institutional version of a child covering their ears and shouting “LA LA LA” to drown out information that threatens their worldview. Working inside a system doesn’t mean you understand its outcomes. A bank teller is not an economist. A hospital receptionist is not a surgeon. And someone typing probation paperwork is not an authority on the long-term structural incentives of mass incarceration.

Experience matters. But outcomes adjudicate truth.

And the outcomes of the American carceral system are a historic, quantifiable failure.

Still, the commenter dug in, insisting the only thing necessary for rehabilitation is that the inmate “wants it.” If only it were that simple.

Act III: The American Schizophrenia Around Redemption

The next reply dismantled that fantasy.

Because even the most driven, self-motivated, determined individual is crushed beneath the weight of a system and society engineered for them to fail.

The post-conviction pipeline in America is not a ladder. It’s a treadmill coated in oil.

And while Americans love the idea of redemption — the underdog restoring their life, the gritty comeback story, the dramatic turnaround worthy of a Netflix special — we simultaneously erect more barriers to that redemption than any other industrialized nation.

We refuse to hire people with records.

We block ex-offenders from entire professions.

We make housing functionally impossible to obtain.

We redraw city maps with absurd “restriction zones” for registrants, defining a “park” as anything from a playground to a patch of grass beside a parking lot. All while decades of research show that the danger to children overwhelmingly comes from people they know, not strangers on registries.

We adore the story of rehabilitation. But we fund and legislate its failure.

Act IV: The Profit Motive — The Final Nail in the Coffin

Then there’s the deepest rot: for-profit prisons.

When incarceration becomes a revenue model, rehabilitation becomes a threat to profitability. Empty beds are bad for business. Stable lives don’t drive shareholder value. The logic of the market infects the logic of justice, and suddenly the state has subcontracted its cruelty to corporations whose success depends on perpetual human misery.

And yet, the defender of the system dug in further, increasingly agitated that their insider status wasn’t enough to override reality.

Act V: The Great Flounce

Finally, when the evidence became too loud and the illusions too thin to maintain, the commenter deployed the last tool in the reactionary arsenal:

Enough already. I worked for the system — but you have to be right because you know more than anyone!!!

Moments later, they blocked me.

The digital version of slamming a door and shouting through it: I’m not listening anymore! It’s the same reflex we see in systems of power everywhere: when confronted with uncomfortable truths, they isolate instead of reform.

The irony was surgical. In a conversation about institutions that avoid correction, a defender of the institution chose avoidance over correction.

Act VI: Blocking as Self-Own

Being blocked isn’t a loss. It’s confirmation.

When someone’s last argument is to cut the line and pretend they’ve won, they’re signaling one thing: they’ve run out of arguments. Facts cornered the storybook narrative, and, as always, myth retreated.

The story didn’t end with a mic drop. It ended with a soft click — the sound of someone fleeing their own collapsing position.

What This Episode Reveals About the American Carceral Mindset

This whole exchange is a microcosm of a national pathology.

People who work within the system are often steeped in its narratives. They’re trained to believe in the stated mission rather than the measurable outcomes. They’re not malicious; they’re institutionalized.

But incarceration is not measured by intentions. It’s measured by results.

And the results are devastating.

As long as rehabilitation is treated as a slogan instead of an infrastructure, as long as profit is tied to incarceration rates, as long as society treats returning citizens as radioactive waste, the cycle will grind on.

The human beings trapped inside will continue to be ground up by a system that pretends to save them while ensuring they never rise.

Moments like those eight inmates saving a guard aren’t exceptions. They’re reminders that the people caught in the system retain a humanity the system does not.

And maybe that’s why the system’s defenders get so angry when the truth surfaces.

Because deep down, they know the inmates aren’t the ones who need rehabilitation.

The system is.
