Republicans’ Supreme Court power grab could come back to haunt them

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Washington Post columnist Jason Willick believes Republicans’ 2016 blockade of Merrick Garland could now work against them. After Democrats reportedly secured House and Senate majorities, he argues that a Democratic Senate would be unlikely to confirm a future Republican president’s Supreme Court nominee, leaving the court’s 6–3 conservative majority vulnerable as several justices age.

Willick points specifically to Clarence Thomas, 78, and Samuel Alito, both among the conservative justices who could reach their 80s before senators elected this week next face voters in 2032. Alaska’s Senate result remained undecided at the time of the report, but Willick’s argument is that Democrats would still control the chamber even if Republicans won the presidency in 2028.

AlterNet also summarizes Willick’s warnings about Democratic subpoenas, possible fights over preemptive pardons, and Democratic state trifectas. His claim that those trifectas could eliminate the Electoral College is not explained in the supplied reporting, which provides no proposed mechanism or decision establishing that outcome.

Republicans wrote the rule

The decisive act was not partisan “hardball” floating mysteriously above Washington. Senate Republicans refused to consider Barack Obama’s Garland nomination in 2016, preserving the vacancy for Donald Trump. Four years later, they confirmed Amy Coney Barrett by 52–48 just eight days before the presidential election.

Those choices established the operative principle: Senate power, not a consistent standard, determines whether a president receives a Supreme Court confirmation. A future Democratic refusal would not erase responsibility for that precedent. It would apply the rule Republicans demonstrated when they had the votes.

Power sits in the Senate

The justices control cases before the Court, but they do not control succession. That authority belongs to presidents who nominate and senators who confirm—or refuse to act.

Willick therefore identifies a real institutional vulnerability. A 6–3 majority is not permanent property. It depends on elections, vacancies, timing, and the willingness of Senate leaders to use their constitutional leverage. Republicans maximized that leverage to build the majority; Democrats now reportedly possess the same choke point.

Thomas and Alito also retain agency over retirement. Willick says their reasons for not leaving while Trump could replace them are unknown. Anything beyond that—pride, miscalculation, or confidence that Republican control would continue—would be speculation.

Consequence becomes “constitutional crisis”

The framing grows slippery when Republican tactics are treated as past hardball but Democratic retaliation becomes a looming “constitutional test.” Garland’s nomination was not defeated through ordinary consideration. Republicans denied it consideration and later abandoned their election-year rationale when Barrett’s seat became available.

That history does not make every possible Democratic response wise. It does make selective alarm dishonest. If blocking nominees is legitimate whenever the Senate can manage it, Republicans cannot plausibly claim institutional innocence when another majority reaches the same conclusion.

The same distortion appears in the pileup of subpoenas, pardons, court vacancies, and the Electoral College. These are different powers with different legal limits. Bundling them into an “avalanche” creates menace without doing the harder work of establishing what Democrats proposed, what authority they possessed, and what consequences would actually follow.

Statesmanship after capture

Willick hopes “statesmanship” will produce durable settlements. But calls for restraint become convenient after one faction has already secured a durable advantage. Republicans captured three Supreme Court seats under rules they altered according to circumstance; asking everyone to transcend temporary advantage only after that victory risks laundering asymmetry into civic virtue.

A durable settlement would require explicit, consistently applied rules governing nominations and Senate action. Personal restraint is not a substitute. It lasts only until powerful actors decide that violating it is profitable.

The system rewards escalation

This story exposes a political system that converts institutional control into permission and then disguises the resulting conflict as mutual disorder. The Senate’s gatekeeping power enabled Republicans to shape the Court. Aging justices and a changed Senate majority may now expose the fragility of that achievement.

The haunting, if it comes, will not be supernatural or accidental. It will be the foreseeable consequence of treating constitutional procedure as a weapon while expecting the opposition to keep observing norms that power has already emptied of force.
