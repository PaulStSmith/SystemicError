'Go home!' Ivanka Trump inundated with backlash over dubious private island scheme

A Private Island, a Public Betrayal

The Power Map Is Obvious

This is not a story about “controversy” around a resort. It is a story about state power being used to clear the way for a $4 billion project tied to Ivanka Trump and Jared Kushner, and about Albanians recognizing exactly what that means. Protesters are not guessing at the stakes: they are objecting to a luxury development on Sazan Island and the protected Vjosa-Narta wetlands, with police already answering them with water cannons while prosecutors opened a formal investigation.

Who Can Make This Happen

The decisive actor here is Prime Minister Edi Rama. The source makes that plain. He called the project an “extraordinary investment,” which is the kind of phrase governments use when they want to dress political choice up as inevitability. Investors do not get this kind of access without state permission, and protected land does not get recast as a luxury frontier without officials doing the work of translation, approval, and enforcement.

That is the actual chain of power: not romance, not entrepreneurship, not “vision,” but government authority applied to public territory for private gain.

The Branding Is the Cover Story

Ivanka Trump’s podcast language is the tell. The project is presented as a dreamy personal revelation: barefoot hikes, captivated visitors, a “beautiful” island, a lifestyle fantasy. That is how elite real estate laundering works. It strips away politics and replaces it with taste, leisure, and aspiration.

But the Albanians protesting in Tirana are not reacting to a vacation brochure. They are reacting to a transfer of public value into private hands, backed by a prime minister and wrapped in the prestige of the Trump name. The glossy story is not neutral description. It is an attempt to make dispossession sound chic.

The State Protects the Project, Not the Public

When police fire water cannons at demonstrators, the hierarchy becomes visible. The state does not respond to a threatened wetland with the same urgency it shows a luxury development. It responds to citizens with force and to investors with flattering language.

That is the pattern: public order is invoked when ordinary people resist elite arrangements, while “development” is invoked when power wants to strip-mine land, law, and restraint. The protesters chanting “Albania is not for sale” are naming the real issue more honestly than the officials do.

Blame Lands Where It Belongs

The source points directly at Rama because he is the elected official with the authority to enable this project. It also points to the broader machinery around him: state institutions that treat a protected landscape as negotiable, and anti-corruption prosecutors who are now forced to examine the deal because public anger made the arrangement too visible to ignore.

The people in the streets are not the problem. The problem is the familiar combination of political patronage, elite branding, and institutional cowardice that turns national assets into bargaining chips for the well-connected.

The Larger Pattern

This is how oligarchic politics functions in plain sight. A government advertises “investment,” a famous family lends glamour, protected land becomes a project site, and the public is told to admire the opportunity. When people object, they are met with policing, not explanation.

The deeper lesson is simple: corruption does not always look like a hidden transaction. Sometimes it looks like a prime minister praising a private resort on public land while citizens are pushed back with water cannons.
