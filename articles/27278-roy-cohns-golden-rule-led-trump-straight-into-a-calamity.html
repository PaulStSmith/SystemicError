Roy Cohn’s Golden Rule led Trump straight into a calamity

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 15, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Roy Cohn’s rule was simple: never concede error, never retreat, and never let contrary evidence alter the performance of certainty. New Republic Editor-in-Chief Win McCormack argues that Donald Trump carried that doctrine into his second presidency—and into a series of self-inflicted failures, from tariffs to war with Iran. The calamity, in McCormack’s account, is not merely that Trump made grave mistakes. It is that he built an administration less capable of stopping him from repeating them.

That is a consequential argument, but AlterNet’s framing outruns the evidence it presents. The article declares a collapse of Trump’s power while offering McCormack’s interpretation, references to unspecified midterm results, and a psychological account of Trump’s upbringing. What it establishes more clearly is a governing structure organized around presidential impulse and the suppression of internal dissent.

Cohn’s doctrine becomes a staffing policy

McCormack traces Trump’s refusal to yield partly to his childhood. He cites Trump’s niece, Mary L. Trump, who has described her father’s brother as the product of a “cold, ruthless, mercenary father” and a frequently absent, ill mother. McCormack then places Roy Cohn—the ruthless lawyer who served as chief counsel to Sen. Joseph McCarthy—in the role of political tutor.

The lesson McCormack attributes to Cohn was not persistence in pursuit of a sound objective. It was allegiance to a position after reality had discredited it: never bend, regardless of how wrong the claim becomes.

Biographical interpretation cannot substitute for reporting on presidential decisions. The politically relevant part is not whether childhood deprivation explains Trump’s conduct. It is that, according to McCormack, Trump translated an aversion to contradiction into an institutional design. After reportedly bristling at advisers such as retired Marine Gen. John Kelly, Trump filled his second-term Cabinet with people McCormack characterizes as compliant and mediocre—officials unlikely to challenge even his most erratic inclinations.

That choice belongs to Trump. Advisers can fail to resist, but the president selects them, assigns their authority and decides whether unwelcome facts reach the room. A Cabinet constructed for obedience turns personal error into government policy.

Tariffs reward the refusal to learn

McCormack uses tariffs as an example of the cost. Trump, he says, remains committed to the false idea that foreign exporters pay them, despite the burden falling on American importers and ultimately reaching US businesses and consumers through higher costs.

The distinction matters because Trump’s account misdirects responsibility. A tariff imposed by Washington is not a bill that the exporting country simply pays. Presenting it that way lets the president market a domestic tax burden as punishment inflicted abroad.

Under Cohn’s rule, correction itself becomes evidence of disloyalty. Expertise is treated not as a constraint on presidential error but as an obstacle to the story the president wants to tell. Officials chosen for pliancy have every incentive to preserve the fiction and little power—or appetite—to force a reckoning with its consequences.

Military escalation without a corrective mechanism

McCormack extends the same diagnosis to Trump’s attack on Iran. In his account, Trump drew confidence from the successful seizure of Venezuela’s president and imagined that Iran—a country McCormack describes as having an estimated 93.4 million people, roughly 610,000 military personnel and the Revolutionary Guard—could also be subdued.

The reported result was not submission. McCormack says the US attack killed Supreme Leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei along with more moderate Iranian leaders whom US analysts believed might have negotiated over Iran’s nuclear program.

Those claims carry enormous weight and, in the supplied account, come through McCormack. They should be treated accordingly. But the political mechanism he identifies is clear: success in one coercive operation became a rationale for attempting a far more dangerous one, while the administration’s internal structure reduced the likelihood of effective opposition.

This is where the Cohn method stops being a personality trait and becomes a national hazard. A president who cannot admit error can impose costs far beyond his own reputation. When advisers are selected for submission, there is no dependable institutional brake between a leader’s analogy and military force.

“Collapse” does not mean the machinery stops

McCormack points to midterm election results as evidence that Trump’s approach ended in political calamity. Yet the AlterNet account supplies no details about those results, and McCormack’s forecast that Trump may spend much of the remainder of his term attending to construction projects is speculation, not a demonstrated transfer of power.

More importantly, political damage does not neutralize presidential authority. McCormack himself warns that Trump could still pursue what he describes as a central project: reversing the country’s demographic transformation through attacks on affirmative-action programs and accelerated deportations of noncitizen residents of color. He suggests those efforts could intensify if Stephen Miller becomes chief of staff.

McCormack interprets that agenda as an attempt to restore an overwhelmingly white United States. The policies he identifies are concrete even where the judgment about Trump’s ultimate objective remains McCormack’s: dismantling affirmative-action programs and expanding rapid deportation. Those actions would be carried out not by Trump’s will alone, but by executive agencies, political appointees and officials willing to convert that will into administrative force.

McCormack closes by predicting that Trump will fail in a broader effort to drag American excellence down to his own level. That flourish is less useful than the institutional warning underneath it. Failure at elections, economics or foreign policy does not necessarily make a president harmless. It can leave an aggrieved officeholder with substantial legal power, fewer internal restraints and an administration trained to treat contradiction as betrayal.
