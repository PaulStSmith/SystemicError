Trump Shrinks U.S. Power, Then Pretends It Is Strategy

The move, in plain terms

Donald Trump ordered the Pentagon to “substantially reduce” joint military exercises with South Korea, just days before the drills were set to begin. He dressed it up as toughness toward North Korea, even as he described the regime as “unthreatening and respectful” and implied South Korea had somehow earned punishment for not joining a war with Iran. That is the basic record. The rest is damage control.

Who actually has the power

The institutional power here is obvious: the president can direct the Pentagon, and Pete Hegseth is there to execute, not to think independently. But the deeper point is that Trump is using formal command authority as personal impulse. Alliance policy is being shaped like a private grievance, not a state decision. That matters because U.S. military exercises in the Asia-Pacific are not decorative. They are how Washington signals commitment, deters adversaries, and reassures allies.

When the White House treats those drills as disposable, it is not “flexibility.” It is the United States announcing that its security architecture can be reversed on a whim.

The blame game is the point

Trump’s story is not coherent. South Korea declining to join a war with Iran has nothing to do with the value of joint exercises on the Korean Peninsula. The connection is political theater: punish an ally, flatter a dictator, and call it leverage. That is not strategic clarity. It is misdirection meant to make coercion sound like policy.

The article’s framing is too polite about this. It treats the move as potentially backfiring, which is true, but understated. The real issue is that Trump is not merely making a risky choice. He is normalizing the idea that alliances are conditional on obedience to his personal agenda. That is how you turn diplomacy into extortion.

China is not confused about any of this

The source notes, correctly, that China stands to benefit. Jane Hardy’s point is blunt: China benefits from “almost everything” Trump does, and the country is increasingly able to rival the United States across industry and AI. That is the strategic context Trump keeps shrinking.

Every time he weakens the U.S. presence in the Asia-Pacific, he creates more room for Beijing to pressure neighbors, test limits, and present itself as the more reliable power. The contradiction is brutal and simple: Trump claims to project strength while handing one of Washington’s chief rivals an easier regional field.

North Korea as props, allies as targets

Trump has no diplomatic relationship with North Korea, despite years of theatrical outreach to Kim Jong-un. Yet he keeps returning to the same pattern: praise the dictator, undercut the ally, call it dealmaking. That is not peace strategy. It is vanity politics wearing foreign policy’s clothes.

South Korea is being treated as a subordinate audience, not a sovereign partner. Meanwhile North Korea gets the rhetorical courtesy usually reserved for legitimate interlocutors. This is what happens when authoritarian admiration and executive ego replace alliance discipline: the adversary is normalized, and the ally is made to absorb the pressure.

The larger pattern

This is not an isolated blunder. It is part of a wider pattern in which Trump diminishes democratic alliances, rewards strongmen, and treats U.S. institutions as instruments of personal mood. The consequence is not just diplomatic embarrassment. It is strategic drift in a world where China is already expanding its leverage.

The democratic answer is not to pretend this is a harmless lapse or a personality quirk. It is to name the structure: a president using state power to vandalize trust, weaken deterrence, and confuse submission for strength. That is not how a serious republic behaves.
