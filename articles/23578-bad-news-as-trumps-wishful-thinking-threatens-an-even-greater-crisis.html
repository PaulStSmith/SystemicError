'Bad news' as Trump’s 'wishful thinking' threatens an even greater crisis

Based on an article by Alex Henderson

Paulo SantosSeptember 20, 20263 min read

Trump Lit the Fuse. Now the Blowback Is Reaching the Pumps.

The Decision Came From Washington

Donald Trump chose to join Israel’s attack on Iran. That decision—not some spontaneous outbreak of “Middle East instability”—is the political starting point of the resulting crisis. The war has driven Brent crude to $109.80 a barrel, while attacks and threatened blockades now endanger shipping lanes and Saudi energy infrastructure.

The institutional power here belongs overwhelmingly to states: the United States, Israel, Iran, and Saudi Arabia. Armed groups can exploit the chaos, but they did not place the United States at war with Iran. Trump did that while claiming military action was necessary to prevent Iran from developing nuclear weapons.

“Instability” Is a Euphemism for Consequences

Coverage that describes the region as becoming “unstable” risks turning a chain of decisions into geopolitical weather. Nothing in this account is weather.

The Houthis have declared the Bab al-Mandab Strait closed to Saudi shipping. A Saudi oil pipeline has reportedly been struck by drones. Insurance costs alone could push commercial vessels away from a passage carrying nearly 9 percent of global seaborne trade, including 13 percent of crude oil. These are foreseeable pressure points in a regional war, not mysterious complications arriving from nowhere.

Trump’s responsibility does not depend on whether he intended every consequence. Presidents who widen wars assume responsibility for the escalation pathways they activate.

The Houthis Are the Lever, Not the Power Behind the War

The Houthis possess real coercive capacity. Their weapons can threaten tankers, pipelines, and refineries. Their relationship with Iran gives Tehran a means to impose costs without issuing a publicly traceable command for every attack.

But focusing exclusively on a militiaman with a rocket-propelled grenade would misdirect attention from the governments that constructed the conflict around him. The United States can launch a war, protect shipping, strike Yemen, and shape global energy markets. Iran can arm regional partners and coordinate pressure against its adversaries. Saudi Arabia’s infrastructure sits exposed between those powers.

The weakest visible combatant may pull a trigger. That does not make him the principal architect of the battlefield.

Wishful Thinking Is Not Strategy

Trump says the Houthis contacted the United States and do not want a fight. The material facts described in the reporting are less reassuring: Saudi shipping faces a declared closure, a major pipeline has been attacked, and one of the world’s most consequential maritime chokepoints is vulnerable.

Presenting an adversary’s supposed reluctance as protection against escalation is not evidence of control. It is an attempt to substitute personal assurance for strategic containment. The same administration that expanded the war now appears to be hoping every armed actor will observe boundaries that Washington itself helped erase.

If further attacks cripple Saudi oil facilities, the United States will face pressure to intervene in Yemen. That would not be a separate crisis. It would be another front generated by the original decision to attack Iran.

The Political Cost Is Being Disguised as an Energy Problem

For Republicans approaching the 2026 midterms, higher fuel prices are being treated as the war’s dangerous domestic side effect. That framing is politically convenient because it reduces state violence and regional escalation to an unfavorable number at the gas station.

But the price spike is not incidental. It is one of the mechanisms through which war redistributes pain. Political leaders choose escalation; civilians, workers, importers, and consumers absorb the costs. The administration exercises agency, while the public is told that “the Middle East” has produced another crisis.

That language launders responsibility. Trump’s vulnerability is not merely that gasoline became expensive. It is that his government made a consequential military choice and cannot contain the consequences.

An Imperial Habit of Denying the Second Move

The larger pattern is familiar: American power treats the opening strike as a discrete decision and every countermove as an irrational escalation by somebody else. Washington claims agency when displaying force, then pleads complexity when force produces retaliation, economic disruption, and demands for further intervention.

That is how a war becomes a shipping crisis, then an oil crisis, then a campaign liability, and perhaps another war in Yemen. Each stage is presented as a new emergency. In reality, they are connected outcomes of political choices made by actors powerful enough to know that fire spreads—and arrogant enough to behave as though only other people will burn.
