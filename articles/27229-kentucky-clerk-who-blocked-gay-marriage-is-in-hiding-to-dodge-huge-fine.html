Kentucky clerk who blocked gay marriage is in hiding to dodge huge fine

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 13, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that former Rowan County, Kentucky clerk Kim Davis still had not paid David Ermold and David Moore after refusing them a marriage license following the Supreme Court’s recognition of same-sex marriage. A federal judge ruled against Davis in 2022, and a jury awarded the couple $50,000 each in 2023. With legal fees and expenses accumulated through unsuccessful appeals, the reported obligation reached $565,285.91.

The Supreme Court declined to hear Davis’s appeal. In July, the couple alleged that she had transferred inherited property worth more than $400,000 to her husband without payment, shielding it from collection while claiming she was broke. Their attorney, Michael Gartland, said deeds contradicted Davis’s sworn denial that she had transferred property worth more than $2,000 during the previous five years.

Liberty Counsel disputed that Davis lied, arguing that the question concerned what she “owns,” not what she previously owned. The organization later stopped representing her. AlterNet reports that a judge on Monday demanded an explanation for why Davis had not disclosed the inheritance; the property-transfer allegations remained subject to litigation.

The power she used

Davis was not a private citizen declining personal participation. She was a county clerk wielding state authority. Her office controlled access to a civil license, and she used that institutional gatekeeping power to obstruct a couple’s legal right because she objected to their marriage.

Calling this merely a clash between religious belief and gay rights obscures the hierarchy. Ermold and Moore needed the government to perform a lawful duty. Davis controlled the relevant government office. Her beliefs did not acquire constitutional force simply because she imposed them through a public desk.

Defiance became a strategy

The initial violation produced a judgment. Continued litigation then increased the amount Davis reportedly owed by hundreds of thousands of dollars. That escalation was not an inexplicable administrative mishap; it was the foreseeable cost of repeatedly contesting liability after courts rejected her defenses.

Liberty Counsel supplied the legal machinery for that resistance, framing immunity from consequences as “religious freedom.” The practical claim was more expansive: an official should be permitted to deny lawful government service and then escape personal liability for doing so. Courts rejected that argument, but delay itself had value. It postponed payment and prolonged Davis’s political role as a symbol of defiance.

The new dispute is about assets, not faith

The inheritance litigation strips away the ideological pageantry. The question is now whether Davis transferred valuable property to prevent creditors from reaching it and whether her sworn answer concealed that transfer. Those are factual and legal disputes about deeds, disclosure, and debt collection.

Liberty Counsel’s distinction between “owns” and “owned” is revealing as advocacy, even if a court must determine its legal significance. It substitutes grammatical maneuvering for the underlying issue: Davis was asked about recent transfers, and Gartland says recorded deeds show transfers she denied making. Once accountability arrives, the grand language of conscience contracts into an argument over verb tense.

Who pays for obstruction

Davis’s conduct imposed costs far beyond the original denial. Ermold and Moore had to litigate for recognition of a right the Supreme Court had already established. Courts had to enforce duties that a public official had sworn to perform. Additional appeals generated additional fees, while the plaintiffs still reportedly awaited payment.

That is a recurring feature of reactionary government: the official exercises power immediately, while the injured party receives relief only after years of procedure. The obstruction is swift. Accountability is slow, expensive, and vulnerable to further obstruction.

Impunity is the political project

This case is not fundamentally about one clerk’s stubbornness. It shows how public officials can convert personal ideology into state action, then recast enforcement as persecution. Allied organizations transform resistance to law into a fundraising and movement-building cause; the people denied service are reduced to obstacles in someone else’s culture-war narrative.

The larger pattern is organized impunity. First, an official uses government power against a weaker party. Then defenders misdescribe the abuse as a dispute among equal private beliefs. Finally, every attempt to impose consequences becomes another alleged injury to the official. The reported property transfers, if voided as the plaintiffs seek, would not represent oppression. They would represent the legal system finally reaching the assets of someone who has spent years resisting a judgment arising from her own use of public power.
