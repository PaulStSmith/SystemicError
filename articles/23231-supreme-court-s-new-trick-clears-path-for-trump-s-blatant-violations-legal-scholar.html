Supreme Court's new trick clears path for Trump's 'blatant violations': legal scholar

Based on an article by Bennito L. Kelty

Paulo SantosSeptember 8, 20263 min read

The Court’s Favorite Trick: Let the Law Break Quietly

A narrow summary

Erwin Chemerinsky is warning about a pattern, not a single ruling: the Supreme Court’s conservative majority has been disposing of challenges to Donald Trump on procedural grounds instead of deciding whether his actions are lawful. The latest examples cited in the source involve the White House East Wing demolition and Trump’s roughly $600 million ballroom project, along with an earlier case over his effort to reshape federal election procedures. In both, the Court reached for standing, ripeness, and “no injury” arguments rather than confronting the underlying conduct.

Power, Not Procedure

The institutional power here sits with the Court’s conservative majority. That is the real decision-maker, not the plaintiffs they are brushing aside and not the abstract doctrine they are hiding behind. Procedural dismissals are not neutral housekeeping when they function as a shield for executive conduct the Court will not squarely evaluate.

This is the point Chemerinsky is making, and it matters: if no one can get into court, then illegality does not become legality. It becomes permanence.

How Impunity Is Built

The Court does not need to bless Trump’s conduct to help him. It only needs to make sure the challenge dies before the merits arrive. That is the quiet machinery of impunity: not a ringing defense of what Trump is doing, but a refusal to decide whether it is allowed.

The source makes the mechanism plain. In the East Wing case, the majority said the plaintiff suffered no real injury. In the election-procedures case, it used standing and ripeness. In neither case, Chemerinsky notes, did the majority say the underlying action was lawful. That omission is not restraint. It is avoidance with consequences.

The Misdirection

This is where the public gets cheated. Procedural rulings sound technical, even boring, so they attract less backlash. That is exactly why they are useful to a Court that wants the political effect of a Trump victory without the embarrassment of saying so outright.

The framing shifts attention away from who is doing what to whom. It turns a fight over power into a seminar on justiciability. That is not neutrality. It is misdirection that launders institutional choice through legal jargon.

What This Pattern Reveals

The larger pattern is simple: constitutional limits mean little if the Court refuses to enforce them. Courts exist to say no when elected power crosses a line. If they decline that role whenever the violation is politically convenient, then the executive does not need permission to overreach. It only needs judges willing to avert their eyes.

That is the deeper scandal in the source: not that Trump is testing the system, but that the system’s conservative guardians are helping him by making the challenge disappear before the law can speak. Proceduralism becomes the respectable language of surrender.

The Real Record

The country is not watching a principled Court preserve order. It is watching a majority narrow the path to accountability while leaving the underlying conduct untouched. That is how institutional cowardice works at scale: no dramatic endorsement, no explicit constitutional defense, just a steady refusal to enforce limits when enforcement would be politically costly.

The result is a legal order that still performs its rituals while excusing the substantive damage. When courts refuse to reach the merits, they are not staying above politics. They are choosing which abuses get to proceed unchallenged.
