Secret donors paid millions to Clarence Thomas-linked law firm with Supreme Court cases

The Courtroom Is the Product

A Litigation Machine, Not a Mystery

Raw Story reports that Consovoy McCarthy received more than $33.5 million from nonprofit networks associated with conservative activists, according to tax filings analyzed by the progressive research group True North Research. More than $29 million reportedly came from organizations linked to Leonard Leo; nearly $8.8 million came from Students for Fair Admissions, the Edward Blum-founded group behind the Supreme Court case that effectively ended affirmative action in college admissions in 2023.

Follow the Institutional Power

The decisive actors are not the unnamed donors alone. Nonprofits select and finance lawsuits. Consovoy McCarthy supplies specialized legal advocacy. The Supreme Court chooses which cases to hear and issues rulings with nationwide force.

That division of labor matters. Donors may remain concealed, but the organizations and lawyers converting money into litigation are identifiable. Students for Fair Admissions paid the firm more than $1.8 million in 2018. Speech First reportedly paid it more than $7.3 million from 2018 through 2024. Defending Education paid $4 million beginning in 2021. Leo’s 85 Fund paid $5.65 million between 2016 and 2023.

These are not scattered contributions to a general ideological conversation. They are payments to legal institutions pursuing concrete disputes over affirmative action, voting rights, education and other contested policies.

Secrecy Is the Enabling Structure

The political advantage of nonprofit financing is not merely that it supplies money. It separates the people funding legal campaigns from the cases carrying their interests into court.

Because the nonprofits described in the reporting do not have to disclose their donors, the public cannot determine from these filings whether particular wealthy individuals or special interests underwrote particular litigation. That uncertainty is not evidence of a secret quid pro quo. It is evidence of an accountability system designed to leave a central question unanswered: whose resources are financing attempts to rewrite public policy through judicial decisions?

Calling this “mystery money” risks making the arrangement sound accidental. The opacity is a legal feature available to the organizations using it.

Personnel Are Access, Not Proof of Capture

Six of Consovoy McCarthy’s 11 partners clerked for Justice Clarence Thomas, while another clerked for Chief Justice John Roberts. Those relationships can confer expertise, prestige and commercial value. As Issue One’s Michael Beckel told Raw Story, former clerks may possess unusual insight into how a justice approaches cases.

But the reporting does not establish that Thomas directed the firm, favored its lawyers or coordinated with its clients. Clerkships demonstrate proximity to judicial power, not corruption. The stronger and supportable point is structural: a well-financed political network can hire lawyers whose careers were shaped inside the institution the network seeks to influence through litigation.

That is how elite access reproduces itself without requiring an explicit conspiracy.

The Weak Framing Is “Self-Enrichment”

True North’s Alyssa Bowen described the arrangement as permitting “self enrichment” and, “if not corruption, perceived corruption.” The evidence presented supports substantial payments and ideological alignment. It does not establish illegal corruption or an improper exchange, and collapsing those categories weakens the case.

The more consequential fact is also the less sensational one: the system can produce sweeping political outcomes through lawful, coordinated investment. Consovoy McCarthy represented Students for Fair Admissions in litigation that ended affirmative action in college admissions. It also represented Donald Trump in his effort to conceal his tax returns. The firm’s clients chose the objectives, its lawyers advanced them, and courts exercised the state power that made the resulting judgments binding.

No bribery allegation is necessary to recognize the political machinery.

Policy by Sponsor, Power Without Disclosure

This story reveals a durable model of governance: privately financed organizations recruit plaintiffs, fund expert litigators and pursue rulings capable of overriding policies far beyond any single courtroom. The donors may be invisible, but the consequences are public.

The misdirection would be to treat the nonprofits as mere advocacy groups, the firm as a passive vendor or the resulting cases as spontaneous eruptions of cultural conflict. The available evidence instead describes an organized legal infrastructure sustained over years by tens of millions of dollars.

Courts remain responsible for their rulings. Lawyers remain responsible for the arguments they make. Nonprofits remain responsible for the cases they finance. The systemic error is an accountability regime that exposes the judgment while concealing much of the capital assembled to produce it.
