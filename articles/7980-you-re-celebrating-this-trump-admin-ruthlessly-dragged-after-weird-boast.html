The Trump administration’s odd social media boast lit up the internet on Friday, as bewildered critics wondered whether the economic brag was meant as satire.

The Treasury Department’s official account claimed that U.S. Treasuries are having their best year since 2020 and that investors who had “confidence and faith in President Trump’s economic policies have been richly rewarded.” a

“Never bet against [the president] or America,” the post said.

The post attached a graph showing bond market returns at about 12.5% in 2020, the last year Trump was in office, before falling to -4% in 2021, -16% in 2022, -1% in 2023, 2% in 2024, and 9% in 2025.

Scott Bessent, the Treasury secretary, touted the results using a meme of children’s book hero Franklin the Turtle fanning out $100 bills and wearing a MAGA hat in front of the Capitol.

But savvy social media users ripped the boast to shreds.

Amateur researcher Garrett Neary chided on X, “This chart goes insanely hard if your brain is smooth.”

Charles Horn, who does communications for The Center for a New American Security, wrote on X, “Extremely weird flex.”

Nishan Seal, who describes himself as a fractional CFO for small businesses, fact-checked the brag on X, writing, “The larger bars mean investors demand a higher interest rate for US debt because of the additional risk from the massive debt load caused by the Big Beautiful Bill and partisan turmoil, both of which were caused by Trump.”

Professor and historian Aaron Astor chimed in, “Higher long-term interest rates are a big reason housing is unaffordable. You’re celebrating this? If you were trying to pull a Paul Volcker and jack up interest rates to beat inflation, that’d be one thing. But...that’s not what you’re doing here at all. Oy.”

Mike Bird, Wall Street editor at The Economist, shared a photo of Franklin the Turtle frowning and holding a chart showing growth expectations plummeting.

X user Ken Goodrich added, “Seriously thought this was a parody account. Did anyone currently employed at the Treasury ever take an econ class?”

MSNBC anchor Chris Hayes echoed the sentiment, writing, “this chart is so funny I thought it was a parody account.

Our Analysis:

Unpacking the Treasury Department’s Misguided Brag

In a move that blurs the line between genuine economic analysis and outright satire, the Trump administration’s recent social media boast about U.S. Treasury bonds has lit the internet ablaze with confusion and criticism. Let’s dissect this bewildering claim and the subsequent fallout with the precision of a scalpel cutting through propaganda.

The Claim: A Year to Remember

The Treasury Department’s official account boldly proclaimed that U.S. Treasuries are having their best year since 2020, attributing this success to the “confidence and faith in President Trump’s economic policies.” Such a statement is laughably simplistic, considering the complex, multifaceted nature of the global economy and the myriad factors influencing bond market performance. The accompanying graph does more to undermine their point than to bolster it, showing a precipitous decline in bond market returns immediately following 2020.

The Meme: Franklin the MAGA Turtle

Scott Bessent, the Treasury Secretary, decided that a children’s book character, Franklin the Turtle, decked out in a MAGA hat and brandishing $100 bills, was the appropriate mascot for this economic victory lap. This choice of imagery is not only juvenile but strikingly tone-deaf, reducing serious economic discourse to the level of a meme. It’s a visual metaphor for the administration’s approach to economic policy: simplistic, disconnected, and embarrassingly unserious.

The Backlash: A Chorus of Critique

The internet’s response was swift and merciless, with commentators from all walks of life pointing out the absurdity of the Treasury’s boast. Garrett Neary’s quip about the chart appealing to those with “smooth brains” is a scathing indictment of the administration’s failure to anticipate the obvious criticisms and implications of their data presentation.

Nishan Seal’s point about the larger bars indicating a higher demand for interest rates due to increased risk — a direct consequence of Trump’s policies and the resultant partisan turmoil — is particularly damning. This analysis cuts through the administration’s self-congratulation to reveal the uncomfortable truth: Trump’s economic legacy is one of increased risk and instability.

Aaron Astor’s commentary brings the conversation back to the tangible effects of these policies on everyday Americans, noting the role of higher long-term interest rates in exacerbating the housing affordability crisis. This highlights a critical oversight in the administration’s narrative — the real-world consequences of economic policy are far from celebratory for many Americans.

The Verdict: A Parody of Itself

The widespread disbelief and mockery, encapsulated by Chris Hayes’ assumption that the chart was from a parody account, speaks volumes. The Trump administration’s attempt to tout its economic achievements has backfired spectacularly, revealing a profound disconnect between its perception of success and the realities of its policy impacts.

In an era where the truth is often stranger than fiction, the Treasury Department’s post stands out as a particularly egregious example of self-satire. It’s a stark reminder that in the realm of economic policy, seriousness, depth of understanding, and a connection to the lived experiences of citizens should be non-negotiable. Anything less is not just laughable; it’s deeply concerning.
