'It was dark': Judge's 'chilling' response to Trump's lawyers stuns experts

By Paulo Santos

U.S. President Donald Trump gestures as he arrives on the South Lawn of the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., September 30, 2025. REUTERS/Ken Cedeno

A federal judge ruled that President Donald Trump’s administration violated the First Amendment rights of pro-Palestinian students who are in the country on student visas by attempting to deport them. But, analysts on Wednesday were more stunned by the language the judge used in his opinion than by the crimes the administration purportedly committed.

On Tuesday, Judge William G. Young of the U.S. District Court for the District of Massachusetts ruled that Secretary of State Marco Rubio’s efforts to deport student visa holders because of their political views violated the students’ free speech rights.

“This case—perhaps the most important ever to fall within the jurisdiction of this district court—squarely presents the issue whether non-citizens lawfully present here in [the] United States actually have the same free speech rights as the rest of us,” Young wrote in his opinion. “The Court answers this Constitutional question unequivocally ‘yes, they do.’ ‘No law’ means ‘no law.’ The First Amendment does not draw President Trump’s invidious distinction, and it is not to be found in our history or jurisprudence.”

Sam Stein, managing editor of The Bulwark, and editor Jonathan V. Last discussed the ruling on a new episode of “Bulwark Takes.”

“When I read it, the first 135 pages are dismantling the case, but then the last 12 pages or so are just him sort of undressing Donald Trump, and like in a way that I think probably was gratifying for him,” Stein said. “It’s become sort of this new fashion for judges to try to one-up each other about how badly they can rebuke Trump.

“But in this case, it was kind of dark underneath it,” he added. “It’s like we’ve really entered it.”

Last added that Young’s decision echoed one of the Republicans’ favorite jurists, Antonin Scalia.

“What Judge Young is saying with this is that the real test on this case is do First Amendment rights apply to people who are lawful residents of the United States?” Last said. “So non-citizen lawful residents, and his contention is, by any reasonable reading of the Constitution, the answer is yes.”

Our Analysis:

The Stark Reality of Free Speech and Judicial Rebukes in Trump’s America

In a ruling that resonates like a clarion call for the First Amendment, Judge William G. Young of the U.S. District Court for the District of Massachusetts delivered a searing indictment against the Trump administration’s attempt to deport pro-Palestinian students based solely on their political views. The case, brought against the backdrop of an administration notorious for its contentious relationship with immigration and free speech, echoes a fundamental question: Do non-citizens lawfully present in the United States enjoy the same free speech rights as citizens? Young’s response was not just affirmative but resolute, marking a significant moment in the ongoing battle for civil liberties.

The First Amendment: No Exceptions for Invidious Distinctions

The administration, under the guise of Secretary of State Marco Rubio’s efforts, sought to draw a line that the First Amendment refuses to acknowledge. The attempt to deport student visa holders for their political beliefs is not just a violation of free speech rights; it’s a glaring testament to the administration’s disregard for the constitutional protections that stand at the very core of American democracy. “No law means no law.” This statement from Young’s ruling underlines a fundamental truth about the First Amendment: its protections do not wane at the convenience of political power.

The ruling’s significance is magnified by the judge’s explicit acknowledgment that non-citizens lawfully present in the U.S. do, in fact, share the same free speech rights as citizens. This is a stark reminder that the rights enshrined in the Constitution are not conditional, nor are they subject to the whims of those in power. The administration’s failure to recognize this basic principle is not just an oversight; it’s a willful ignorance that underpins much of its approach to governance.

The Judicial Rebuke: A Reflection of Deepening Divides

The commentary surrounding Judge Young’s decision, especially the pointed rebuke of Donald Trump, signals a broader discourse on the judiciary’s role in the current political climate. Analysts Sam Stein and Jonathan V. Last’s observations suggest a judiciary increasingly compelled to counter the administration’s more egregious acts. Stein’s remark about judges trying to “one-up each other about how badly they can rebuke Trump” reflects a judiciary that finds itself at the forefront of safeguarding constitutional rights against an administration often perceived as hostile to those very principles.

However, beneath this judicial pushback lies a “dark” reality, as Stein puts it. The necessity for such rebukes underscores a troubling era for American democracy, where foundational rights and norms are not just challenged but blatantly disregarded. That a federal judge’s defense of the First Amendment is seen as a remarkable act rather than a given is indicative of the systemic erosion of democratic safeguards under Trump’s watch.

Echoes of Scalia: The Universal Application of Constitutional Rights

The analysis by Last, drawing parallels between Young’s decision and the judicial philosophy of Antonin Scalia, further illuminates the non-partisan nature of constitutional rights. Scalia, a conservative icon, was known for his strict textualist interpretation of the Constitution. The comparison serves as a powerful reminder that the protection of civil liberties should transcend political ideologies. Judge Young’s ruling, grounded in a “reasonable reading of the Constitution,” reaffirms that the rights it protects are not exclusive to citizens but extend to all lawfully present individuals.

Conclusion

Judge William G. Young’s ruling against the Trump administration’s attempt to deport pro-Palestinian students is more than a legal rebuke; it’s a resounding affirmation of the First Amendment’s unassailable stance on free speech. This case, “perhaps the most important ever to fall within the jurisdiction of this district court,” as Young himself noted, is a watershed moment in the defense of civil liberties. It serves as a crucial reminder of the judiciary’s paramount role in upholding the principles that define the United States, even, and especially, when those in power seek to undermine them. The decision is not just a victory for the students involved; it’s a triumph for the Constitution and for all who reside under its protection.
