Trump draws scathing rebuke in paper owned by billionaire GOP megadonor

Trump’s Iran Deal and the Theater of Strongmen

Power Wears the Pen

The reporting here is simple: Donald Trump signed a ceasefire deal with Iran, and even a pro-Trump Israeli outlet tied to billionaire Republican donor Miriam Adelson is calling it a failure. Danny Zaken, writing in Israel Hayom, says the deal will damage Israel, the United States, and the region, and the paper goes further, framing it as a betrayal of Trump’s own promises.

Who Actually Decided

The only actor with real institutional power in this story is Trump. Not the vice president. Not the envoy. Not the pundits now performing outrage. He is the president, and the decision is his unless he chooses to pretend otherwise. The article itself quietly reveals the usual choreography of elite blame management: if the deal goes bad, advisers get mentioned, but the signature belongs to the man at the top.

The Convenient Myth of Betrayal

The strongest trick in this kind of coverage is to describe a political choice as a lapse, a disappointment, or a broken promise, as if the harm emerged from confusion rather than intent. Zaken’s language is melodramatic, but the underlying mechanism is familiar: a powerful leader makes a strategic bargain, then his allies sell the resulting damage as a tragic deviation from greatness. That framing protects the decision-maker by turning policy into personality. It suggests Trump “failed” to live up to a heroic self-image, rather than confronting the more basic point that he exercised power and produced consequences.

The Real Audience

This is not just about Iran. It is about the factional economy of the pro-Trump right and its satellite institutions. An outlet owned by a Republican megadonor can praise Trump one day and denounce him the next, but the structure remains the same: access politics first, principle second, accountability never. When a deal becomes embarrassing, the outrage is calibrated for insiders, not for the people who will live with the fallout.

Humiliation as Policy

The article’s most revealing line is not the praise Trump supposedly deserved, but the accusation that he has instead “humiliated” America and damaged its strategic power. That is the imperial vocabulary of American politics in its purest form: greatness measured by dominance, peace judged by whether it looks like surrender, and any agreement with a hostile regime treated as a stain unless it can be sold as victory. The result is a politics that cannot distinguish diplomacy from weakness, except when the strongman is the one collecting the credit.

The Pattern Beneath the Story

The larger pattern is institutional cowardice wrapped in authoritarian aesthetics. Trump is expected to act like a singular decisive ruler, but his allies still need room to disown him when the decision becomes politically costly. So the blame gets scattered downward, the promises are rewritten after the fact, and the damage is narrated as surprise. That is how elite systems preserve themselves: not by admitting bad choices, but by laundering them through disappointment, outrage, and retrospective confusion.
