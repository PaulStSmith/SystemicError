Kirk's killing unleashed 'dark forces' in MAGA movement as conservatives spiral: analysis

President Donald Trump‘s MAGA coalition has fractured in the months following the assassination of right-wing influencer Charlie Kirk and turned on itself over its internal battle over anti-Semitism.

Following Kirk’s death, his Turning Point USA organization had anticipated bringing together MAGA loyalists to continue Kirk’s attempt to unify the group during its AmericaFest in December — but that backfired when “one by one, MAGA’s leading lights took the stage and began shivving one another in public,” according to an analysis from Yair Rosenberg in The Atlantic published Wednesday.

“When Kirk was killed, conservatives believed that his death would galvanize his cause,” Rosenberg wrote. “’Millions of Charlie Kirks were created today,’ declared Representative Lauren Boebert of Colorado. But as it turned out, Kirk’s assassin didn’t kill just one man; he destabilized the entire Trump coalition by removing a pivotal person who had been holding it together. In doing so, the killer helped unshackle dark forces—chief among them anti-Semitism—that now threaten to overtake the conservative movement.”

Kirk had viewed the rise of anti-Semitism as a threat to the MAGA movement and was actively fighting against it both on college campuses and within his own coalition.

“Before his life was ended by an assassin’s bullet, Charlie Kirk was trying to save the conservative coalition from turning on itself. To liberals, the late activist was known for debating left-wing students on college campuses. But on the right, Kirk was waging another battle, against people on his own side,” Rosenberg wrote.

Despite Kirk’s attempts, the rise of hate speech hasn’t stopped.

“’In the last six months, I’ve seen more anti-Semitism on the right than I have at any time in my life,’ Senator Ted Cruz told the Federalist Society’s National Lawyers Convention in November,” Rosenberg wrote. “’It is growing. It is metastasizing. There are about a half-dozen vocal apostles, and it is in particular finding purchase with the young.’ Soon after, the Princeton professor Robert George, once dubbed ‘the reigning brain of the Christian right,’ resigned from the Heritage Foundation’s board. Dozens of staffers reportedly left the organization. One month later, Turning Point’s flagship conference descended into recriminations over the very controversies and conspiracies that its founder had endeavored so assiduously to suppress.”

And although Trump appears to have kept his hold on his MAGA base, what Kirk had hoped to avoid has appeared to have only grown.

“On one level, this conflict is about Jews and Israel. But on another, this debate is downstream from something much bigger: a power struggle over who will define and control the MAGA movement once Trump is gone. By painting rivals as tools of the Jews, hard-right influencers such as [Tucker] Carlson and [Steven] Bannon hope to delegitimize the competition not by besting their ideas, but by slurring their loyalties and identity.”

Our Analysis:

The Crumbling MAGA Coalition: A Post-Mortem of Unity and Anti-Semitism

In the wake of Charlie Kirk’s assassination, the MAGA coalition’s façade of unity has not just cracked; it has shattered, exposing the toxic underbelly of anti-Semitism that Kirk himself fought to contain within the ranks. This tragic unraveling, as detailed in Yair Rosenberg’s analysis for The Atlantic, serves as a grim reminder of the fragile alliances and dangerous ideologies that can thrive in the shadow of political movements. Let’s dissect the chaos that ensued following Kirk’s death and the implications it holds for the conservative movement and beyond.

The Assassination That Shook the MAGA World

Charlie Kirk’s assassination marked a pivotal moment for the MAGA coalition, a group that, until then, seemed somewhat coherent under the banner of Donald Trump’s Make America Great Again slogan. Kirk, as the founder of Turning Point USA, was not just another right-wing influencer; he was a unifying force attempting to steer the conservative movement away from the precipice of internal destruction, particularly the growing specter of anti-Semitism.

The notion, as Congressman Lauren Boebert put it, that Kirk’s death would create “millions of Charlie Kirks” was not just overly optimistic—it was fundamentally misguided. Instead of galvanizing the movement, Kirk’s death unveiled the extent to which he was the glue holding together a coalition fraught with division and hate.

Anti-Semitism: The Unchecked Cancer

Kirk’s battle against anti-Semitism within his own ranks was a testament to his recognition of the threat it posed not just to Jews but to the integrity and future of the MAGA movement itself. Yet, despite his efforts, figures like Senator Ted Cruz have observed a disturbing uptick in anti-Semitic rhetoric on the right, a trend that is both growing and finding a receptive audience among the youth.

The resignation of Robert George from the Heritage Foundation’s board, followed by a mass exodus of staffers, and the chaos at Turning Point’s flagship conference, are not isolated incidents. They are symptoms of a deeper malaise that Kirk’s assassination has brought to the fore.

The Power Struggle and the Jewish Question

At the heart of the MAGA coalition’s implosion is not just a debate about Jews and Israel. It’s about the power vacuum left in the wake of Trump’s presidency and the battle for the soul of the conservative movement. Figures like Tucker Carlson and Steven Bannon have weaponized anti-Semitism as a tool to discredit their rivals, not through the strength of their arguments, but by questioning their loyalties and identities.

This tactic is not new. History is replete with examples of political movements turning on the Jews as scapegoats for deeper ideological and power struggles. What is particularly insidious about the current situation is the willingness of so-called leaders to embrace and amplify these divisive and dangerous themes.

Conclusion: A Movement at a Crossroads

The MAGA coalition, in the aftermath of Charlie Kirk’s assassination, stands at a crossroads. Will it continue down the path of self-destruction, fueled by hate and conspiracy theories? Or will it heed the warning signs and reclaim a semblance of the unity and purpose that Kirk sought to foster?

The answers to these questions are not just critical for the future of the conservative movement in America. They are indicative of the broader societal and political challenges facing a nation increasingly divided by ideology, identity, and a refusal to confront its darkest impulses.

In the end, Charlie Kirk’s death may have done more than destabilize a political coalition. It may have exposed the systemic errors at the heart of a movement struggling to define itself in the shadow of its own worst tendencies.
