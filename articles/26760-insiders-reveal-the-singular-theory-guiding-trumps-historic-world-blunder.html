Insiders reveal the singular theory guiding Trump’s historic world blunder

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 22, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that three people close to President Donald Trump told The Atlantic he believes Ukraine is attacking Russian oil refineries partly to raise energy prices and damage Republicans in the 2026 midterms. That is the “singular theory”: Ukraine, in Trump’s view, is not merely pursuing its war against Russia but meddling in an American election to weaken him.

The reporting says Trump announced a ceasefire that neither Ukraine nor Russia had approved; Ukraine paused while Russia continued attacking, prompting President Volodymyr Zelenskyy to declare, “We’re done,” according to NBC News. As Ukraine concluded that striking Russian oil infrastructure remained militarily useful, some people around Trump reportedly proposed ending US intelligence sharing with Kyiv.

AlterNet also reports that Trump was open to meeting Vladimir Putin and Xi Jinping in China to discuss Ukraine, while a Ukrainian official complained that decisions appeared to be unfolding without Ukraine. The article does not establish that Kyiv is conducting an electoral interference campaign. It establishes only that Trump reportedly believes it is.

The power is in Washington

Ukraine can choose its military targets, but Trump controls the institution capable of rewarding or punishing that choice. The United States can provide intelligence, withdraw it, shape negotiations and use its leverage to decide whether Ukraine enters talks as a participant or an object.

That distinction matters. Trump’s theory does not need to be proved before it produces consequences. If he acts on it, an allegation about Ukrainian motives becomes grounds for withholding assistance from a country under Russian attack. Presidential suspicion acquires the force of state policy.

The officials reportedly suggesting an intelligence cutoff would be enabling that transformation. They are not spectators trapped inside Trump’s worldview. They are offering a mechanism for imposing it.

A battlefield decision becomes an election conspiracy

Russia’s refineries support Russia’s war-making capacity. According to the reporting, Ukraine considers continued attacks on them to be in its military interest. Trump reportedly interprets those operations through a narrower concern: fuel prices before the midterms.

That reframing shifts attention away from the attacker bombing Ukrainian civilians and infrastructure and toward the country trying to impair the attacker’s resources. AlterNet says Trump minimized Russia’s conduct by remarking that it had not “been doing much,” after which Zelenskyy responded with photographs of children killed in a park.

This is not demonstrated Ukrainian election meddling. It is a claim about intent attributed to Trump’s private conviction. The article’s evidence does not bridge the gap between Ukrainian military strategy and a coordinated effort to defeat Republicans.

The stronger actor disappears from the indictment

The reporting describes Russia continuing its attacks after Ukraine paused, yet the political penalty under discussion would fall on Ukraine. Putin retains the capacity to bomb, negotiate and wait for Washington to pressure his opponent. Kyiv faces the possible loss of intelligence because its conduct allegedly complicates Trump’s domestic politics.

That is the central misdirection. Russia’s agency recedes while Ukraine is assigned responsibility not only for fighting the war but for protecting the electoral interests of the American president managing its support.

AlterNet further argues that broader disruption tied to the Iran war and the blockade of the Strait of Hormuz is driving energy pressure. If that account is correct, blaming Ukrainian refinery strikes substitutes a politically convenient target for the larger forces affecting oil prices. The story, however, does not quantify the contribution of each factor, so it cannot establish Ukraine’s precise effect on US fuel costs.

Peace without the invaded country

Trump’s promised settlement rests, in this account, on personal diplomacy among powerful leaders. The contemplated meeting with Putin and Xi fits a model in which major states discuss Ukraine’s future while Ukraine waits outside the room.

Calling that arrangement a peace process does not change its structure. A ceasefire announced without the combatants’ agreement is not a ceasefire. A negotiation about a country conducted without that country’s meaningful participation is a division of influence, not consent.

The reported fascination with American, Russian and Chinese spheres of influence makes the hierarchy unusually plain: powerful governments claim authority to arrange the security of weaker ones. Ukraine’s refusal to accommodate that arrangement can then be recast as obstruction—or, now, electoral sabotage.

Suspicion as foreign policy

The larger pattern is government by personalized grievance. A president reportedly interprets another country’s wartime strategy as an attack on his party, advisers propose using intelligence access as punishment, and an adversary gains room to operate while its victim is pressed to serve Washington’s electoral calculations.

The danger in this structure does not depend on proving Trump’s motive or accepting every inference in AlterNet’s framing. It lies in the documented asymmetry of power: an unsupported theory at the top can redirect US policy, impose costs on Ukraine and spare Russia pressure.

When state power is organized around a leader’s belief that foreign events are secretly about him, evidence becomes secondary. Policy turns into retaliation, allies become suspects, and the government’s immense institutional machinery is made to enforce one man’s political narrative.
