Every Generation Rediscovers Brazil

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 17, 20267 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Brazil is “in fashion” again. That says less about Brazil than it does about the remarkably short cultural memory of the rest of the world.

Apparently, Brazil is fashionable again.

The Associated Press recently reported on the international rise of “Brazilcore”: Brazilian colors on runways, football shirts worn as fashion, Havaianas, Brazilian music spreading through social media, international celebrities descending on Rio, growing tourism, and an increasingly visible fascination with Brazilian culture.

There is even, wonderfully, “caramelo pride”: the humble vira-lata caramelo, that mysterious standardized Brazilian mutt apparently issued to every neighborhood by the federal government, has become a cultural symbol.

It is tempting to look at all this and conclude that something new is happening.

But perhaps we have seen this movie before.

Several times.

Because Brazil has an odd relationship with the rest of the world’s cultural attention.

Every generation seems to have to discover Brazil again.

Before Brazilcore, there was samba

Long before TikTok algorithms started throwing Brazilian funk into the headphones of teenagers in Europe, Brazil had already developed one of the richest popular musical cultures on the planet.

Noel Rosa died in 1937, only 26 years old, but by then he had helped transform samba into something capable of describing urban Brazilian life with extraordinary wit and precision.

Brazil knew what it had.

The rest of the world mostly didn’t.

Then came Carmen Miranda.

And suddenly the world discovered Brazil.

Sort of.

Hollywood turned Carmen into an international phenomenon, although what it sold was less “Brazil” than a Technicolor explosion of Latin American stereotypes assembled for American consumption. The fruit-covered hats became more recognizable internationally than much of the culture they supposedly represented.

But for millions of people, Carmen Miranda was Brazil.

Brazil had been discovered.

Again.

Then somebody heard a strange guitar

A couple of decades later, another Brazil appeared.

This one whispered.

João Gilberto played guitar differently. Antônio Carlos Jobim wrote harmonies that sounded simultaneously effortless and impossibly sophisticated. Vinicius de Moraes supplied poetry.

Bossa nova escaped Brazil.

Then The Girl from Ipanema happened.

Suddenly Brazil wasn’t Carmen Miranda’s tropical spectacle anymore. Brazil was cool. Intimate. Sophisticated. Modern.

Stan Getz recorded with João Gilberto. Astrud Gilberto became internationally famous. Jobim became one of the most recognizable Brazilian composers outside Brazil.

Jazz musicians adopted Brazilian compositions. American singers recorded them. European audiences embraced them.

The world had discovered Brazil.

Again.

Then Pelé kicked down the door

Music wasn’t the only route.

In 1958, a 17-year-old named Pelé helped Brazil win its first World Cup.

By 1970, television was carrying the Brazilian national team around the planet, and Brazil’s third World Cup victory produced one of the enduring images of the sport.

For millions of people who couldn’t identify Jobim and had never heard Noel Rosa, Pelé was Brazil.

Not merely a Brazilian athlete.

Brazil.

The yellow shirt became international shorthand. The idea of futebol-arte became attached to the country. Brazil acquired an almost mythological reputation as the place where football players did things with a ball that seemed to violate reasonable expectations of human coordination.

Once again, the world discovered Brazil.

And then it kept happening

Ayrton Senna became another international incarnation of Brazil.

For a generation of Formula One fans, particularly in Europe, Senna wasn’t merely a racing driver. His intensity, talent, rivalry with Alain Prost and extraordinary performances in the rain turned him into one of motorsport’s defining figures.

Then came another football generation.

Ronaldo.

Ronaldinho.

Later, Neymar.

Every time, a new audience encountered some manifestation of Brazilian culture or talent and behaved as though somebody had just pulled a curtain away from a previously unexplored continent.

And Brazil would once again become fashionable.

For a while.

The difference this time is the gatekeeper

There is, however, something genuinely different about the current Brazilian moment.

Previous waves usually required an ambassador.

Carmen Miranda needed Hollywood.

Bossa nova needed international record companies, jazz musicians and American radio.

Pelé needed the World Cup.

Senna needed Formula One.

Ronaldo and Ronaldinho benefited from the enormous international machinery surrounding European football.

In other words, Brazilian culture usually had to pass through somebody else’s distribution system before the rest of the world noticed it.

That gatekeeper is disappearing.

The Internet doesn’t particularly care where something comes from.

Neither does TikTok.

Neither does Spotify.

Neither does YouTube.

A Brazilian teenager can upload something in Belo Horizonte tonight and somebody in Berlin can be dancing to it tomorrow without either of them ever having spoken to a record executive.

And Brazilians are extraordinarily good at this environment.

We meme things.

We remix things.

We swarm comment sections.

We turn random events into national jokes within hours.

We adopt unsuspecting foreigners who say something complimentary about Brazil with an enthusiasm that probably causes mild concern among their relatives.

American writer Courtney Henning Novak discovered this when she began reading Machado de Assis and talking about it online.

Her reaction was essentially:

“Hey, this Brazilian writer is incredible.”

The Brazilian Internet’s reaction was essentially:

ONE OF US.

Machado de Assis died in 1908.

Brazilian social media apparently decided that his international marketing campaign was merely running a little behind schedule.

This time, Brazil isn’t one person

And that may be the most important difference.

Carmen Miranda could become a caricature because one person was being asked to represent an entire country.

Pelé could become synonymous with Brazil because international audiences saw comparatively little else.

But what exactly represents Brazil in 2026?

Anitta? Funk? Football? Havaianas? Machado de Assis? Ainda Estou Aqui? Carnival? Brazilian memes? A caramel-colored dog sleeping underneath a plastic table outside a padaria?

Yes.

That’s the point.

There is no longer a single cultural checkpoint through which Brazil must pass.

Someone can discover Brazilian literature without caring about football.

Someone can discover funk without knowing bossa nova.

Someone can encounter Brazilian cinema without ever setting foot in Rio.

Someone can watch a Brazilian meme, become curious about the language, stumble onto Brazilian music, discover Brazilian food and eventually find themselves trying to explain to another foreigner why Brazilians are arguing about whether biscoito or bolacha is the correct word.

At that point, I’m afraid the infection has become systemic.

The world discovers what was already here

There is an amusing arrogance buried inside declarations that some country has suddenly become “fashionable.”

Fashionable to whom?

Brazil has more than 200 million people.

It has spent centuries producing music, literature, food, art, slang, comedy, architecture, cinema and approximately fourteen billion regional arguments about how food should properly be prepared.

None of that sprang into existence because somebody in Paris wore a Brazilian football shirt.

The thing that changed was attention.

That distinction matters.

Brazil didn’t become interesting.

People became interested in Brazil.

Those are very different statements.

And we’ve seen the latter happen repeatedly.

Noel Rosa.

Carmen Miranda.

Jobim and João Gilberto.

Pelé.

Senna.

Ronaldo.

Ronaldinho.

And now Brazilcore, funk, cinema, memes and the mighty vira-lata caramelo.

Each generation receives its own invitation.

Maybe this rediscovery will last longer

Fashion won’t.

Fashion never does.

At some point yellow-and-green clothing will disappear from international runways. Some other country will become the fashionable cultural reference. Algorithms will move on. Influencers will discover something else.

That’s fine.

Brazil survived the decline of Carmen Miranda’s Hollywood moment.

It survived bossa nova ceasing to be the international sound of sophisticated cool.

It survived Pelé retiring.

It survived Senna’s death.

It survived Ronaldo and Ronaldinho leaving the pitch.

It will survive Brazilcore becoming unfashionable.

But something has changed underneath the fashion.

The distribution network is no longer entirely controlled somewhere else.

Brazil no longer needs Hollywood to manufacture a Carmen Miranda before Americans notice Brazilian culture. It doesn’t need an American jazz musician to validate Brazilian music. It doesn’t need the World Cup to introduce a Brazilian personality to hundreds of millions of people.

Brazil can increasingly export Brazil directly.

Messily.

Chaotically.

Contradictorily.

Sometimes brilliantly.

Sometimes ridiculously.

Which, come to think of it, may be a considerably more accurate representation of the country than any carefully manufactured cultural ambassador ever was.

So enjoy Brazilcore while it lasts.

Enjoy the football shirts, the Havaianas, the music, the memes and our newly appointed four-legged ambassador of soft power.

Sooner or later, international fashion will move somewhere else.

Then, perhaps around 2045, another generation will encounter some Brazilian musician, athlete, filmmaker, writer or cultural phenomenon that none of us can possibly predict today.

They’ll stare at it in amazement.

They’ll tell their friends.

Articles will be written.

Trend analysts will explain the exciting new cultural force emerging from South America.

And somebody will solemnly announce:

“Brazil is having a moment.”

At which point 200 million Brazilians will once again have the opportunity to reply:

“Sim, caramba. A gente mora aqui.”

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
