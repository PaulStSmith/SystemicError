Proud Trump shows off new cartoon of himself — apparently blind to its true meaning

Imperial Fantasy, State Messaging

A cover that says the quiet part loudly

Donald Trump amplified a Newsweek International cover on Monday that cast him as a giant figure over North and South America, with the White House joining in to post “TRUST IN TRUMP.” The image was paired with the claim “TRUMP WAS RIGHT (ABOUT VENEZUELA),” while the visual language closely echoed a notorious 1892 Cecil Rhodes caricature. That is the basic fact pattern. The political meaning is uglier: the presidency is not merely consuming propaganda, it is helping manufacture it.

Power is the point

The relevant institutional power here is not a pundit, a magazine, or an online reaction cycle. It is the White House. When the president reposts an imperial tableau and the White House reinforces it, the state is not observing symbolism from a distance. It is endorsing a governing aesthetic of dominance, entitlement, and hemispheric hierarchy. The message is not subtle: Trump stands above the continent; everyone else is scenery.

Rhodes was not an accident

The comparison to Cecil Rhodes matters because Rhodes was not just a rich man with bad opinions. He was a diamond magnate who used power to seize territory, erase land rights, and build structures of racial domination. The source reminds us that his conquest in what is now Zimbabwe left more than 20,000 dead and helped lay groundwork for apartheid-era discrimination. The point of invoking that history is not decorative outrage. It is to identify the template: wealth plus state power plus racialized fantasy, then packaged as destiny.

Propaganda with a fuel pump

The cover does not present policy as policy. It presents it as providence. Trump is shown as a giant in safari gear holding a fuel pump, a cartoon of extraction and command. That is not a neutral illustration of geopolitical competence. It is imperial theater, built to make coercion look like expertise and domination look like common sense. The framing launders aggression into confidence. It asks readers to admire the hand that is already reaching for the map.

What the misdirection hides

The weaker actors in this story are not the targets of the spectacle. The weaker actors are the public being told to read this as clever branding, and the institutions that normalize it by treating it as just another political image. The real decision-maker is the president, and the real enabling structure is a White House that sees no reason to distance itself from a visual vocabulary of conquest. Calling that confusion would be dishonest. It is deliberate. The image is built to signal who gets to rule and who is expected to accept the terms.

The pattern underneath

This is what authoritarian politics does when it wants to look stylish: it borrows the symbols of empire, wraps them in media polish, and asks followers to mistake intimidation for vision. Trump’s post is not an isolated stunt. It is part of a broader pattern in which power performs itself as natural, racial hierarchy is aestheticized, and the state helps circulate the performance. That is the danger here. Not just the lie, but the confidence with which institutional power teaches people to admire it.
