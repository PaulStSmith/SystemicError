Six-figure foreign secret ties an Idaho campaign to Russia

Based on an article by Clark Corbin, Idaho Capital Sun

Paulo SantosOctober 17, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe campaign holds the answersInfluence without accountabilityThe disclosure gap is the storyA political system built for deniability

What happened

AlterNet reports that political consultant Chris LaCivita, whom Idaho Gov. Brad Little’s reelection campaign named a senior adviser in February, was also paid hundreds of thousands of dollars to advise the Russia-aligned government of Republika Srpska, an entity within Bosnia and Herzegovina. The New York Times reported on Oct. 6 that LaCivita and another adviser traveled there ahead of a recent election.

LaCivita was also identified as Donald Trump’s 2024 campaign co-manager and an adviser to the MAGA Inc. super PAC. Little’s campaign had celebrated his experience and ability to craft “winning messages and tactics,” but the reporting does not establish what work he performed for Little or whether he remained on the campaign.

LaCivita could not be reached. Little campaign manager Joan Vargas did not answer repeated questions from the Idaho Capital Sun about LaCivita’s current role or whether Little knew of the trip.

The campaign holds the answers

The institutional power in this story rests with Little and the campaign officials who hired LaCivita, defined his access and can disclose whether that relationship continues. LaCivita chose to undertake the reported foreign work. Little’s campaign chose to bring him inside its operation and publicly endorse his political judgment.

The available reporting does not establish that Little knew about the Republika Srpska engagement or that LaCivita performed foreign-related work for the Idaho campaign. It does establish that the campaign possessed the information needed to clarify its relationship and declined to provide it.

That silence is not proof of misconduct. It is an exercise of power: the campaign can withhold basic facts while reporters and voters are left to reconstruct the arrangement from announcements and unanswered messages.

Influence without accountability

Political consultants can move among domestic campaigns, super PACs and foreign clients while carrying techniques, contacts and reputational authority across those roles. The Times noted that foreign political work is common in the industry. Common, however, is not the same as politically trivial.

LaCivita was valuable to Little precisely because of the experience and strategic influence the governor praised. When someone with that standing advises a Russia-aligned foreign government while assisting Trump-aligned candidates before the Nov. 3 midterms, the relevant issue is not mere proximity. It is whether campaigns impose any disclosure or conflict standards on the operatives they place near political decision-making.

The reporting cannot answer that question because Little’s campaign would not explain its own arrangement.

The disclosure gap is the story

The weakest framing would treat this as an exotic tale about a consultant traveling abroad. That shifts attention away from the American institutions that hire such consultants and decide how little the public will be told.

No evidence presented here shows that Idaho policy was affected, that campaign information crossed borders or that Little approved LaCivita’s foreign engagement. Those gaps limit what can responsibly be alleged. They also expose the accountability failure: voters cannot evaluate possible conflicts when campaigns refuse to disclose the consultant’s duties, tenure or rules.

The unanswered questions are therefore not atmospheric suspicion. They are specific omissions controlled by named actors.

A political system built for deniability

Modern campaign power increasingly runs through private consultants and outside spending groups that wield influence without accepting the disclosure obligations associated with public office. Elected officials receive their expertise and networks, then retreat behind campaign staff when those relationships attract scrutiny.

This arrangement distributes influence while concentrating deniability. The consultant can remain unreachable. The campaign manager can remain silent. The governor can benefit from the adviser’s prestige without publicly accounting for the boundaries of his role.

The larger pattern is institutional cowardice disguised as message discipline. When political organizations will not state who advises them, what those advisers do or whether outside clients create conflicts, opacity is not an accidental gap in the story. It is part of how power protects itself.
