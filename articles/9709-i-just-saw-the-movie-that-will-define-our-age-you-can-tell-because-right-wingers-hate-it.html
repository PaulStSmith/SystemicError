I just saw the movie that will define our age — you can tell because right-wingers hate it

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 10, 20257 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:A Critical Dissection of an Unsettling RealityThe Power of Cinema in Political DiscourseThe Historical Echoes in Contemporary StrugglesThe Enduring Battle Between Art and PowerIn Conclusion: A Call to Conscience

Leonardo DiCaprio attends the London premiere for the movie One Battle After Another. REUTERS/Katie Collins

This week has felt like one battle after another. We all watched video after video of ICE agents dropping from helicopters onto a Chicago apartment block, kicking in doors and terrorizing Black and Hispanic American and immigrant families, then trashing and stealing their possessions without ever presenting a warrant signed by a judge.

The GOP pushed the country to the brink again with another government shutdown threat while right-wing legislatures redrew districts to erase the votes of millions. We heard more talk of arresting journalists for doing their jobs, and watched as the military rolled through American cities as if people here are the enemy.

Each day has felt like a slow-motion assault on democracy itself.

Louise and I went to see Leonardo DiCaprio’s new movie, One Battle After Another, last weekend, and I was stunned. It’s a film of rare courage and artistry. From the first scene to the last, Paul Thomas Anderson reminds us that cinema can still tell the truth about power and conscience. It’s a film that demands attention, not permission.

The movie runs about two-and-a-half hours, but it’s so action- and drama-packed that it felt like it flew by in less than an hour. I knew people similar to those characterized in this movie when I was in East Lansing SDS back in 1968-69: seeing them portrayed like this was a hoot! This is truly brilliant film-making.

Predictably, conservatives rushed to condemn it. Some labeled it “irresponsible” or claimed it “glorifies violence.” What they really mean is that it unsettles them. They prefer art that flatters authority and soothes the comfortable. This film refuses to do either.

The world Anderson portrays is not a fantasy. When federal agents execute suspects, when protests are manipulated to justify repression, when truth is distorted by propaganda, that is not simply fiction. It reflects the deep anxiety of a society that’s watched Trump’s executive power become far too concentrated and way too cruel. Anyone paying attention to the news knows how real that danger feels.

The rightwing National Review published a piece titled “There Will Be Bloodlust in ‘One Battle After Another’” that accused Anderson of romanticizing 1960s radicalism. Yet DiCaprio, who stars in the film, called it a “timely satire.” Speaking to Reuters, he said, “It’s not a film where people are imposing any political beliefs on anyone else. It’s satire on both ends.”

That contrast says everything. Conservatives want to see chaos; Anderson and his cast are inviting reflection. The violence in the film is not triumphant; it’s painful, personal, and tragic. It shows what happens when injustice festers until ordinary people begin to break, as I saw in the people I knew in the Weather Underground back in the day.

History reminds us that art has always frightened the powerful. Uncle Tom’s Cabin was banned in the South because it forced them to confront slavery. Upton Sinclair’s The Jungle was smeared by industrialists for revealing the cruelty of unregulated capitalism. Protest music of the 1960s and artists like Bob Dylan and Pete Seeger were condemned as “unpatriotic” by the same crowd that called Dr. King a radical.

When art tells the truth, power always howls.

Today the same pattern repeats. The same rightwing billionaires funding “outrage” over this film are working to silence teachers, censor libraries, and rewrite history to protect privilege. They fear a nation that can still feel empathy; they fear what happens when people start asking why power serves so few.

One Battle After Another is not a call to arms. It is, instead, a warning about what happens when corruption becomes normal and compassion becomes rare. It asks us to look at the machinery of cruelty and decide whether we’ll stand by or resist. That choice is the same one that generations before us have faced.

If this film makes people uncomfortable, that’s its purpose. Democracy doesn’t survive by comforting the powerful. It survives when ordinary people demand justice and truth, even when it stings.

One Battle After Another will be called divisive by those who profit from division. They’re wrong. The real division in this country is between those who believe art should serve power and those who believe art should challenge it.

I stand with the challengers, because when we fall silent, we serve power; when we speak, we hold it to account.

Our Analysis:

A Critical Dissection of an Unsettling Reality

This week’s events, as detailed in the article, paint a grim portrait of a democracy under siege. It’s a narrative that feels ripped straight from the pages of a dystopian novel, yet here we are, witnessing it unfold in real-time. The description of ICE agents descending like an occupying force onto Chicago neighborhoods, the GOP’s brinkmanship with government shutdowns, attempts to muzzle the press, and the military’s unnerving presence in American cities are not just troubling developments but a slow-motion assault on democracy itself.

The Power of Cinema in Political Discourse

Enter One Battle After Another, a film that dares to hold a mirror up to the power and its conscience, or the evident lack thereof. Paul Thomas Anderson’s creation, as vividly described, is not just a piece of entertainment but a bold statement about the state of our society. Its reception, predictably polarized, highlights the chasm in contemporary political and cultural discourse. While conservatives decry its perceived glorification of violence and radicalism, it’s essential to pierce through this superficial critique to understand the film’s core message.

The criticism from the right, as noted, is hardly surprising. The film’s unsettling portrayal of state violence and systemic oppression is a direct challenge to the narratives often peddled by those in power. The discomfort it causes among conservatives is telling; it disrupts the sanitized version of reality they prefer — one where authority is always just and dissent is inherently dangerous. This discomfort isn’t a mark against the film but a testament to its effectiveness. Art that does not challenge or provoke is nothing more than propaganda in disguise.

The Historical Echoes in Contemporary Struggles

The backlash against One Battle After Another mirrors the historical resistance faced by works of art and literature that dared to confront societal ills head-on. From Uncle Tom’s Cabin to The Jungle, art has long been a vehicle for exposing the uncomfortable truths about our society. The right’s reaction to this film is part of a long tradition of attempting to silence narratives that question the status quo.

This film, through its depiction of violence and dissent, not as tools of glorification but as expressions of deep-seated anguish and desperation, forces us to confront the realities of injustice and the human cost of indifference. It’s a reminder that history is replete with moments where ordinary people reached their breaking point, leading to significant social and political upheavals.

The Enduring Battle Between Art and Power

The article eloquently positions One Battle After Another as more than just a film; it’s a microcosm of the ongoing struggle between art and power. The real division in America, as highlighted, is not between different political ideologies but between those who believe art should serve as a lapdog to power and those who see it as a catalyst for change.

In an era where truth is increasingly under attack, and empathy is seen as a weakness, art like this serves as a crucial counterbalance. It asks us to reflect on who we are, what we stand for, and how we respond to the injustices perpetrated in our names. This isn’t about sowing division but about demanding accountability and fostering a society that values truth over comfort.

In Conclusion: A Call to Conscience

One Battle After Another is not just a film; it’s a wake-up call. It challenges us to look beyond the surface, to question the narratives fed to us by those in power, and to confront the uncomfortable truths about ourselves and our society. If art can still provoke such intense reactions, it means it still has the power to inspire change.

The discomfort felt by some viewers is not a symptom of the film’s failure but a sign of its success. In a world that increasingly demands conformity and silence, art that challenges, provokes, and unsettles is more important than ever. Democracy survives on dissent, not compliance. In the end, we must decide whether to stand by in silence or to resist. The choice, as always, is ours.
