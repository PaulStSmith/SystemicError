MAGA influencer's 'shockingly pathetic' attack instantly backfires: 'You told on yourself'

When a Troll Mistakes Exposure for Power

The Stunt

Cam Higby tried to turn a congressional aide’s private life into a public shame ritual. Instead, Rep. Dan Goldman turned the camera back on the person holding it. Higby, a right-wing content creator with a Pentagon press credential and a taste for viral confrontation, posted personal photos and insinuations about Goldman’s deputy chief of staff, John Blasco. Goldman answered by making the real issue unmistakable: the attack was petty, opportunistic, and ugly.

Power Was Never With the Poster

The story begins with a basic imbalance the article almost has to drag into view. Blasco is a staffer. Higby is a content creator using spectacle as a business model. Goldman is an elected member of Congress with formal institutional power, a public platform, and the authority to protect his staff. Higby’s move depended on pretending that posting private images and sexualized insinuation was some kind of civic exposure. It was not. It was an attempted humiliation campaign.

Goldman understood the power map immediately. He did not defend himself by shrinking the attack into an etiquette dispute. He named it as what it was: a cheap, cowardly attempt to go after staff instead of the member. That matters because political attacks are often designed to blur hierarchy. Here the hierarchy is the point. Higby was not confronting power. He was trying to harass someone with less of it.

The Framing Was the Weak Spot

The article’s strongest fact is also its indictment of the frame Higby tried to impose. He wanted the audience to see Blasco’s private life, salary, and identity as the scandal. But the scandal was the decision to weaponize them in the first place. Once Goldman replied, the attack exposed the attacker’s motives more clearly than the target’s conduct.

That is the familiar trick of modern online political cruelty: dress up intrusion as accountability and prejudice as transparency. If the target is gay, make that the headline without admitting that the real operation is to shame queerness itself. If the target works in government, pretend the salary alone makes private life fair game. If the target is staff, suggest that proximity to power makes them disposable. Higby’s post relied on all of that at once.

Goldman’s “you told on yourself” line landed because it identified the mechanism, not just the insult. The post did not reveal Blasco’s character. It revealed Higby’s need to convert another man’s life into a right-wing performance.

Deliberate Cruelty, Not Confusion

The article gives Higby no plausible innocence, and it should not. He was not mistaken. He was not confused. He was not doing journalism in any serious sense. He was staging a spectacle that depended on personal exposure, innuendo, and humiliation. That he got the opposite result is politically interesting, but it does not soften the intent.

This is the part too many political stories sand down into “backlash” or “controversy.” The right language is simpler: targeted cruelty. The point was to make private life feel punishable, especially for a gay staffer visible in public-facing work. The fact that Blasco has been involved in immigration oversight and assistance for detained families only sharpens the contrast. One person was doing the unglamorous work of government. Another was trying to turn a man’s identity into engagement bait.

Adam Kinzinger’s brief response, “But you support an Epstein dude,” is a reminder that these performance attacks travel in a media ecosystem where outrage is selective and self-exculpating. But the deeper problem is not hypocrisy alone. It is the normalization of harassment as political method.

The Media Incentive Structure

Higby’s credentialing into the Pentagon press corps after traditional outlets gave up their passes is not a minor side note. It is part of the same ecosystem. Institutions that once filtered access now hand it to people who monetize confrontation. That does not produce scrutiny. It produces incentives for provocation, selective leaks, and cheap theater dressed as adversarial reporting.

The article notes that Higby built his profile on viral conflict and dubious spectacles, including a Signal-chat stunt from Minneapolis that legal experts found showed no illegal activity. That pattern matters more than any single post. It shows a class of operator who treats attention as proof, outrage as evidence, and private people as props. Once that model gets proximity to official institutions, the damage is not just reputational. It distorts what counts as political information.

Goldman’s response cut through that distortion because it rejected the frame on its own terms. He did not accept the premise that staff are fair targets for ideological sadism. He identified the institution, the officeholder, and the boundary.

The Larger Pattern

This story is not about one embarrassing post. It is about a political culture that increasingly rewards men who confuse access with authority and cruelty with courage. The weak point in that system is not that it can’t produce outrage. It’s that it depends on a public willing to mistake harassment for revelation.

Higby’s stunt failed because Goldman forced the question the spectacle was designed to hide: who has power, who is abusing it, and who is being used as a target. That question should not be exceptional. It should be the first thing people ask whenever a self-appointed watchdog tries to turn someone’s private life into a public punishment.
