'How stupid do you think we are?' Morning Joe rips billionaire's censorship

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 20, 20263 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that Meta temporarily disabled the main “No Kings” account during nationwide protests on Saturday after its systems flagged unusual activity and suspected bot involvement. Meta later said engineers determined the account should not have been removed, restored access after an additional delay, and issued what it described as a sincere apology.

On MSNBC’s “Morning Joe,” Joe Scarborough and Mika Brzezinski rejected Meta’s explanation as implausible given the large demonstrations driving traffic to the account. Jonathan Lemire argued that skepticism was warranted in light of Meta founder Mark Zuckerberg’s overtures to President Donald Trump, while Eugene Robinson and Scarborough widened the criticism to the concentrated power of Zuckerberg, Elon Musk, and other technology billionaires.

The power was Meta’s

The protesters generated attention. Meta decided whether their organizing account remained visible. That distinction is the story’s political core.

A private corporation controlled access to a communications system used by a national movement, acted without public process, and supplied its explanation after the disruption. Meta acknowledged error, but an apology does not restore the lost organizing time or attention. Platforms exercise power in the interval between disabling an account and admitting they should not have done it.

An algorithm did not absolve anyone

Meta attributed the removal to systems detecting suspicious activity. That describes a mechanism, not an absence of agency. Executives establish enforcement rules, determine acceptable error rates, fund review systems, and decide how quickly politically consequential mistakes receive human scrutiny.

The reporting does not establish that Zuckerberg ordered the account removed or that Meta deliberately censored the protests. It does establish that Meta’s machinery suppressed the account during the event and that the company later conceded the action was wrong. Treating that sequence as mere technical confusion would conceal the institutional choices behind the technology.

Skepticism requires discipline

Scarborough and his colleagues pointed to Zuckerberg’s closeness to Trump as grounds for distrust. That relationship creates a legitimate conflict-of-interest concern when Meta restricts a movement organized around opposition to presidential power. But suspicion is not proof of political direction, and AlterNet supplies no evidence connecting Zuckerberg or the White House to this specific decision.

The strongest indictment needs no invented conspiracy: one billionaire-controlled company possessed the authority to obstruct political communication at a critical moment, while those affected had to wait for that same company to investigate itself. The structural danger exists even if the immediate cause was exactly the automated mistake Meta claims.

The Constitution is not the moderator

Robinson’s claim that Americans must go to Zuckerberg for First Amendment rights blurs an important legal boundary. The First Amendment constrains government; it does not ordinarily require Meta to distribute anyone’s speech.

The sharper point is political rather than constitutional. Public debate now depends heavily on privately governed infrastructure whose owners face weak democratic accountability. Users may have formal freedom from government censorship while still confronting a corporation capable of making their speech functionally disappear when timing matters most.

Concentration makes errors political

The segment connected Meta’s control of political communication with Musk’s influence over communications infrastructure and billionaire ownership across major media companies. Some of its rhetoric outran the evidence presented about this incident, but the concentration it identified is real within the story’s account: a small group of owners can shape access to audiences, information, and essential technology.

That arrangement turns even a genuine mistake into an exercise of unaccountable power. The weak actor is the organizer seeking restoration; the strong actor owns the gate, writes the rules, reviews the appeal, and announces whether its own conduct was acceptable.

The system asks for trust without surrendering control

The central problem is not whether Meta’s apology sounded sincere. It is that political movements must trust institutions that can interrupt them, disclose little about the decision, and face no equivalent interruption of their own authority.

This is how concentrated power protects itself without proving a plot: consequential decisions are presented as automated anomalies, responsibility dissolves into technical language, and restitution arrives after the decisive moment. Meta may have corrected an error. It did not correct the system that made its error politically decisive.
