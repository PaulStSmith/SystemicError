'Blindsided' federal agency staffers 'revolt' and threaten to quit over 'the new Noem'

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosJuly 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

ICE Is Not Being Managed. It Is Being Distributed.

The Appointment

Donald Trump has installed Richard “Lance” Schroyer, an Oklahoma state trooper and former Marine, to run ICE. The source material makes the basic facts plain: he has no experience leading a major agency, yet he is being handed a $78 billion bureaucracy with 32,000 employees. DHS insiders are alarmed, some are already talking about leaving, and the appointment landed as a shock inside the department.

The Real Power Center

The important question is not whether Schroyer is qualified. The question is who is actually steering immigration power. The reporting says insiders see Stephen Miller as the real puppeteer over ICE, with Tom Homan also in the mix, and that many expected one of them to get the job. That expectation matters. It shows the agency is understood internally as a prize in an ongoing factional struggle, not as an institution governed by stable standards.

Trump made the appointment. Markwayne Mullin, who already had Schroyer as a senior adviser and previously had him on his residential security detail, is the political patron attached to this pick. That is the operating model here: not administration, but placement.

Patronage Over Competence

Schroyer’s biography is not the issue; his lack of any meaningful leadership record is. The source says he has never run an office, never managed a budget, and has little experience with 287(g) operations. That should disqualify him on its face. Instead, it appears to have qualified him.

This is the old patronage machine with modern branding. Install a loyal friend, keep the chain of command personal, and call it staffing. The point is not efficiency. The point is control.

The Framing Does Some Work For Them

The reporting emphasizes staff shock, possible resignations, and fears that Miller or Homan are being “boxed out.” That is real enough as intra-regime drama, but it also risks mistaking a palace dispute for the underlying crime. The deeper problem is not that one hardliner may be losing leverage. It is that the agency is being treated as a movable piece in a loyalty contest among Trump-aligned operatives.

Even the talk of rebranding ICE as “NICE” fits the pattern. Cosmetic softening on top, coercive machinery underneath. A slogan does not change the function of the institution. It only gives it a cheaper mask.

The Revolt Is the Symptom

The threatened departures are not evidence of principled institutional resistance so much as evidence that the place is being run into humiliation. Senior officials fear looking “like idiots,” and that is telling. When leadership is chosen for proximity instead of competence, bureaucracies don’t become noble. They become brittle, petty, and self-protective.

That is the consequence of turning a police agency into a loyalty club. People do not stay to serve the mission. They stay to survive the next reshuffle, or they leave before the rot reaches them.

The Pattern

This story is not really about one obscure appointee from Oklahoma. It is about the conversion of public power into personal property. Trump picks the loyalist, Mullin supplies the conduit, and DHS absorbs the insult. The agency’s purpose matters less than who can plant “his guy” inside it.

That is the larger political pattern: institutions hollowed out by favoritism, then managed through intimidation, branding, and factional rivalry. The result is not a strong state. It is a captured one, where competence is treated as an inconvenience and power is awarded like a favor.
