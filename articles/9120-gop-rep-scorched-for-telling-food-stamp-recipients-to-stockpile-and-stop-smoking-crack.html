GOP rep scorched for telling food stamp recipients to stockpile and 'stop smoking crack'

Rep. Clay Higgins (R-La.) ignited a firestorm of outrage after a recent tweet in which he blamed the 42 million Americans set to lose their Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) benefits this weekend for their own plight.

On Thursday, Higgins posted to his official X account: “There are 22 million American households receiving SNAP benefits for groceries, at $4200 per year on average. Try to get your head wrapped around how many pantries you can stock with $4200 dollars in properly shopped groceries. Any American who has been receiving $4200 dollars per year of free groceries and does NOT have at least 1 month of groceries stocked should never again receive SNAP, because wow, stop smoking crack.”

Higgins’ post was met almost immediately with anger and ridicule. Children’s author Kristine Rudolph wrote on Bluesky: “Tell us you don’t do the grocery shopping in your house without telling us you don’t do the grocery shopping in your house.”

Pennsylvania Capital-Star editor-in-chief Tam Lambert posted that $4,200 per year in SNAP benefits amounts to “about $80 a week.”

Retired air traffic controller Vivian M. Lumbard argued that Higgins’ post reveals how “none of these Republicans seem to understand how much groceries actually cost, especially if you have kids.”

“$4200 equates to $350/month,” she wrote. “I doubt I could cover all my groceries just for myself for that amount of money, even if I gave up meat.”

Political consultant Jamison Foster quoted Lucille Bluth from the sitcom “Arrested Development” (who famously said: “It’s one banana Michael. What could it cost? $10?) by writing: “It’s one month of groceries, Michael. How much room can it take up? Ten closets?”

Political scientist Miranda Yaver broke down Higgins’ post by pointing out that Republicans simultaneously expect Americans to “Stop eating processed foods. Make healthy choices: eat more fresh food” while claiming SNAP recipients are “irresponsible” if they “don’t have a month’s food supply on hand to live on when we can’t keep the government open.”

Our Analysis:

The SNAP Controversy: A Deep Dive into Rep. Clay Higgins’ Misguided Commentary

In a recent display of remarkable disconnect from the realities of low-income Americans, Rep. Clay Higgins (R-La.) has managed to ignite a storm of backlash with a tweet that can only be described as both insensitive and out of touch. Higgins suggested that the 42 million Americans set to lose their Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) benefits this weekend are at fault for not having at least a month’s worth of groceries stockpiled. His suggestion that $4,200 per year (or roughly $350 per month) in SNAP benefits is more than enough to achieve this is not just misguided; it’s a stark illustration of the disconnect between some policymakers and the constituents they claim to represent.

The Math Doesn’t Add Up

First, let’s tackle the math. Higgins’ figure of $4,200 per year translates to about $80 per week. For anyone who has stepped foot in a grocery store recently, the idea that $80 a week can stock a pantry in a way that allows for a surplus is laughable. This amount is barely sufficient for a modest weekly shop, especially in families with children or special dietary needs.

The Reality of Grocery Prices

The ridicule Higgins faced from various quarters is well-deserved. Critics, ranging from children’s authors to political scientists, have pointed out a glaringly obvious fact: groceries are expensive. The cost of living has risen dramatically, and with it, the price of healthy, fresh food. The notion that individuals on SNAP benefits are squandering their assistance on frivolous purchases rather than stocking up on groceries is not just wrong; it’s insulting.

The Fresh Food Paradox

The commentary from political scientist Miranda Yaver highlights a particularly irksome paradox in conservative rhetoric. On one hand, there’s a push for Americans to eschew processed foods in favor of fresh, healthy options. On the other, there’s criticism when those receiving SNAP benefits fail to maintain a stockpile of food. Fresh produce, by its nature, does not lend itself to long-term storage. This expectation is not just unrealistic; it’s fundamentally flawed.

A Lack of Empathy

Higgins’ post is emblematic of a broader issue: a lack of empathy and understanding. The assumption that individuals on SNAP can simply “shop smarter” to overcome systemic economic challenges is a gross oversimplification of complex issues. It fails to account for the myriad factors that contribute to food insecurity, including but not limited to employment instability, health crises, and the rising cost of living.

Conclusion: More Than Just Out of Touch

Rep. Higgins’ comments are more than just out of touch; they are a dangerous misrepresentation of the realities facing millions of Americans. SNAP benefits are a lifeline, not a luxury. The suggestion that those in need are simply not managing their assistance properly is not only misguided, it’s harmful. It shifts the blame onto the individuals least able to bear it, diverting attention from the systemic reforms needed to address food insecurity.

As we navigate these discussions, it’s crucial that we ground our conversations in reality and empathy. Policy makers like Higgins must be held accountable for their words, as they shape the narratives around poverty and assistance in America. Let’s not allow such baseless rhetoric to go unchallenged.
