DC insider lays out GOP nightmare  —and 'final repudiation of MAGA'

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 26, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Steve Schmidt’s promised “final repudiation of MAGA” is not an election result. It is a sweeping forecast: the former Republican strategist told journalist Tara Palmeri on Tuesday that Donald Trump has damaged the Republican Party so deeply that Democrats could dominate Washington after 2028 and Republicans might not regain the presidency before 2040. The prediction is extraordinary, but the institutional failure behind it is already plain. Republican leaders repeatedly chose Trump, empowered him and made his conduct the party’s inheritance.

A forecast, not a settled realignment

Schmidt predicted that the next electoral backlash against Republicans would exceed the one he described as having hit the party in the midterms. He envisioned a Democratic president in 2028, a Senate majority exceeding 60 seats and a House advantage of 100 seats. From there, he said, the United States could function as a predominantly one-party country for longer than eight years, with political competition occurring mainly among Democratic factions.

Those numbers are Schmidt’s projections, not reported outcomes. His argument is that Trump’s political residue will outlast Trump himself: even after the present order has been dismantled, he said, future Republican candidates will still face voters asking, “but what about Trump?”

That is the “GOP nightmare” in concrete terms. Schmidt believes Republicans have attached their identity so completely to one president that changing candidates will not erase the association. A party cannot spend years making Trump its leader, message and test of loyalty, then expect voters to regard the next nominee as an unrelated product.

Schmidt illustrated the complacency he hears among Republican associates with a scene from The World According to Garp. After an airplane crashes into a house under consideration, Robin Williams’ character concludes that the property is now safe because another crash is astronomically unlikely. The analogy is pointed: Republicans are treating one electoral wreck as protection against another when it may instead be evidence of the same unresolved danger.

Democratic weakness is not Republican absolution

Schmidt did not describe Democrats as popular. He said the party’s congressional brand remains “as unpopular as MAGA” and predicted that prospective presidential candidates would try to separate themselves from leaders such as Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer and House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries.

That distinction matters because AlterNet’s account is not evidence of a Democratic revival driven by public confidence in Democratic leadership. Schmidt’s case is that Republicans have made their own liability so large that Democratic unpopularity may not save them.

This is less a compliment to Democrats than an indictment of the political system’s narrow choices. Voters can dislike the Democratic establishment and still reject a Republican Party bound to Trump. Treating every Democratic gain as an endorsement of Schumer, Jeffries or the party’s congressional operation would conceal the mechanism Schmidt is describing: a governing party can manufacture victories for an unpopular opposition by making itself even less acceptable.

It would also misdirect responsibility. Democratic leaders may fail to inspire voters, but they did not force Republican officials to subordinate their party to Trump. Republicans held the agency to nominate him, defend him and make loyalty to him a prerequisite for influence. Schmidt’s forecast assigns the resulting exposure to the institution that created it.

The bill belongs to the enablers

“Everything must be knocked down, and the rule of law restored,” Schmidt said. He argued that Republicans will spend years answering for enabling what he called Trump’s corruption and that rebuilding the party afterward will be slow.

That claim identifies the real power structure more accurately than narratives centered on Trump’s personality alone. A president does not transform a major party without lawmakers, operatives and candidates deciding that compliance is safer or more profitable than resistance. Schmidt’s prediction may prove wrong in its timing, scale or certainty, but the accountability he describes cannot honestly stop with Trump.

Nor does Schmidt see an obvious Republican successor capable of escaping the trap. He said a viable candidate would need to think quickly and articulate a coherent vision, purpose and message—and added that several names currently discussed near the top of the field do not meet that standard.

The Republican problem, then, is not simply finding a more polished nominee. It is asking voters to detach the party from conduct its own leaders enabled while offering candidates produced by the same political machinery. Schmidt has supplied a dramatic timetable for collapse. The more immediate fact is harsher: Republicans made Trump’s record their collective record, and no rebranding exercise can decide when voters will stop remembering it.
