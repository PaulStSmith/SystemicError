Trump’s new attempt to overthrow an election was derailed from the inside

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 7, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that former Assistant U.S. Attorney Andrew Weissmann offered three reasons President Donald Trump did not succeed in challenging the 2026 midterm election after Republicans lost power. The article identifies only one in detail: some administration lawyers allegedly signaled through heavily qualified court filings that claims about 20,000 California ballots lacked a firm evidentiary basis.

Weissmann pointed specifically to a Supreme Court brief filed by Solicitor General John Sauer. In Weissmann’s reading, its repeated caveats amounted to an indirect warning that the application was unsupported. He contrasted that posture with Attorney General Todd Blanche, whom he accused of fully serving Trump’s effort.

AlterNet does not provide the brief, the court’s ruling, the underlying ballot evidence, or a complete account of the other two reasons Weissmann mentioned. It therefore supports a narrower conclusion than its headline: Weissmann believes internal resistance helped derail an election challenge, but the reporting does not independently establish the scope of the alleged attempt or prove what ultimately stopped it.

Power was concentrated at the top

The institutional power in this account belonged to Trump and the senior officials directing the federal government’s legal machinery. Rank-and-file voters did not order the solicitor general to approach the Supreme Court. California election workers did not choose to place a disputed list of ballots before the justices. Those decisions came from officials with access to the Justice Department and the authority to turn a political demand into federal litigation.

That distinction matters because political coverage routinely shifts attention from the people wielding state power to the people forced to respond to it. The central issue was not whether unnamed insiders felt uneasy. It was that the presidency could push an inadequately supported election claim far enough to require a Supreme Court filing.

Caveats are not resistance

If Weissmann’s description is accurate, Sauer’s brief was written to advertise its own weakness. That may have helped judges recognize the absence of solid support. It was still filed.

A lawyer who buries doubt inside an official submission is not exercising the same agency as one who refuses to advance an unfounded claim, resigns, or publicly identifies the evidentiary failure. The caveats may show professional discomfort. They do not erase institutional participation.

This is the cramped morality of insider government: officials are praised for leaving clues that they knew better while continuing to lend their offices, names, and public authority to the project. The system then treats hesitation as heroism because outright refusal has become almost unimaginable.

The article outruns its evidence

AlterNet frames the episode as a new attempt to “overthrow” an election. That is an extraordinarily serious characterization, but the article supplies little beyond Weissmann’s podcast analysis and his interpretation of one filing. It does not show what Trump personally ordered, what relief the application requested, how the ballot list was produced, or why the litigation failed.

That weak framing creates its own misdirection. It asks readers to accept the largest conclusion while withholding the factual chain necessary to evaluate it. Weissmann may be correct about the brief’s subtext, but sarcasm about its caveats is not a substitute for presenting the document and explaining the proceeding.

The piece also quotes Weissmann’s condemnation of Blanche without documenting Blanche’s specific conduct in this election dispute. His accusation should remain what the reporting establishes it as: the judgment of a former federal prosecutor, not an independently demonstrated finding.

Loyalty changes the guardrails

The larger pattern Weissmann identifies is still politically important. Institutions do not restrain presidents by magic. They restrain them only when officials use their authority to refuse improper demands. A government staffed for personal loyalty weakens that restraint because legal judgment becomes subordinate to the leader’s immediate interest.

But covert signaling is a brittle safeguard. It depends on judges noticing the caveats, bureaucrats retaining enough autonomy to write them, and political superiors tolerating even disguised dissent. None of those conditions is guaranteed. When the defense against an unsupported use of federal power is that a subordinate quietly writes an embarrassed brief, the institution has already surrendered most of its independence.

A system surviving by insinuation

This episode, as AlterNet presents it, is less a story of robust checks than of institutional decay stopped somewhere short of completion. Officials with actual power allegedly pushed an election claim into court; officials with less power may have weakened it through careful wording; and the public is left reconstructing responsibility from hints.

That is not accountability. It is government by plausible deniability, with senior actors issuing demands and subordinates encoding their objections in footnotes and qualifiers. A political system that relies on insiders whispering “this is baseless” while formally carrying out the order has not solved the abuse of power. It has merely made the abuse easier to obscure.
