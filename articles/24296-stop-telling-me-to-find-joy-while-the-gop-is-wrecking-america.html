Stop telling me to 'find joy' while the GOP is wrecking America

Based on an article by D. Earl Stephens

Paulo SantosOctober 1, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedJoy is not the disputed policyPower belongs to institutionsThe burden gets pushed downwardAesthetic politics protects failure

What happened

AlterNet columnist D. Earl Stephens rejects the claim that Democrats need to project more “joy” to reconnect with voters. His response was prompted by PBS NewsHour co-host Geoff Bennett’s interview with Fox News commentator Jessica Tarlov, who argued that Democrats need sustained engagement with conservative audiences, more cultural connection, and leadership that includes joy.

Tarlov did not tell frightened voters simply to cheer up. She made an electoral argument: appearing on Fox News, attending events such as “College GameDay,” and entering voters’ cultural worlds can strengthen political persuasion. She also warned that favorable midterm polling might reflect rejection of Republicans rather than genuine confidence in Democrats.

Stephens answers that demand with a catalogue of Republican actions and alleged harms, from attempts to overturn the 2020 election to attacks on rights, public programs, environmental protections, and free speech. Some accusations in the column are stated without supporting evidence, but its central objection is clear: requiring an upbeat performance from people confronting material losses mistakes political presentation for political power.

Joy is not the disputed policy

Tarlov’s diagnosis is narrower and more serious than Stephens sometimes allows. She is talking about persuasion: relationships, cultural fluency, and the failure of Democratic politicians to inhabit spaces where persuadable voters actually live. Those are legitimate concerns. Political communication cannot consist solely of reciting dangers to people who have stopped listening.

But “joy” is the weakest part of that prescription because it converts a strategic failure by party institutions into an emotional deficiency among their voters. Democratic leaders control campaign budgets, candidate recruitment, messaging, media appearances, and legislative priorities. Ordinary liberals do not. If the party lacks cultural reach, the responsibility belongs first to the professionals paid to build it—not to citizens insufficiently delighted by the conditions those professionals failed to prevent.

Power belongs to institutions

The people with actual power in this account are officeholders, party leaders, media executives, legislators, and wealthy interests capable of shaping policy and public attention. Fox News commands an enormous platform. Republican officials exercise governmental authority. Democratic officials decide how to use the offices and resources they possess.

Stephens is strongest when he refuses to let mood obscure that hierarchy. The expiration of the expanded Child Tax Credit, for example, was not caused by liberal dourness. The events after the 2020 election were not a failure of cultural connection. According to the column, Trump resisted the result and sought to alter vote totals; his supporters then attacked the Capitol on January 6, 2021. Whatever value optimism has as campaign branding, it does not explain institutional decisions or erase their consequences.

The burden gets pushed downward

The familiar maneuver is to discuss politics as a problem of voter temperament. Democrats scold. Liberals lack joy. Activists sound alarmed. The people reacting to policy are placed under examination, while the people making policy recede into the background.

That is misdirection. It is easier to coach anxious citizens on tone than to confront why Democratic victories have not secured the rights and protections those citizens expected. It is easier to prescribe football appearances than to explain how a party will wield power. Cultural presence may open a conversation, but it cannot substitute for a credible account of who will materially benefit from governing decisions.

Stephens commits some misdirection of his own. His sweeping language—calling Republicans “terrorists,” describing environmental damage as intentional poisoning, and attributing policy to billionaire “owners”—often outruns the evidence supplied in the column. Fury does not relieve a writer of the duty to distinguish documented conduct from inference. Precision makes an indictment harder to evade; exaggeration gives its targets an escape hatch.

Aesthetic politics protects failure

The larger pattern is the conversion of governance into performance criticism. When policy produces fear or anger, commentators ask whether the opposition looks pleasant enough. When a party cannot retain trust, consultants diagnose insufficient relatability. Institutional failure is repackaged as a branding problem.

That framing is useful to nearly everyone already holding power. Republican officials face less scrutiny for the decisions Stephens condemns. Democratic leaders can blame voters’ attitudes instead of their own record and strategy. Media organizations receive another personality dispute to stage. The audience is left arguing over whether alarm should smile.

Joy can sustain political movements, and Tarlov is right that persuasion requires human connection. But joy is neither a governing program nor an alibi. The systemic error is asking citizens to improve the emotional packaging of politics while powerful institutions continue deciding whose rights, security, and material needs are negotiable.
