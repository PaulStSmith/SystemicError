Republican loser says even Jesus couldn’t have survived GOP wipeout

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Republican Rep. Mike Lawler lost New York’s 17th Congressional District to Democrat Cait Conley during a Democratic wave that shifted control of both houses of Congress. CNN’s Manu Raju said Lawler believed no campaign could have overcome the political environment: “Jesus Christ could have run and would have lost.”

Preliminary results cited by the New York Post showed Lawler dominating heavily Orthodox Ramapo and leading in Rockland and Republican-leaning Putnam County. Conley prevailed in key Rockland towns and benefited from Democratic and anti-Trump voting in Westchester, overcoming Lawler’s regional advantages.

Raju described a Republican “reckoning” after the losses. Some reelected Republicans blamed party leaders, White House personnel, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, or President Donald Trump’s handling of the economy. Outgoing Republican Rep. Don Bacon called the result a referendum on Trump.

Power did not reside in the candidate

Lawler’s remark is melodramatic, but its political meaning is straightforward: the election became larger than his individual campaign. He was one of only three Republicans running in districts Kamala Harris carried in 2024, and voters could use his race to judge the national government rather than merely the incumbent on the ballot.

That does not erase Lawler’s agency. He ran under the Republican label and depended on its coalition. But the people with the greatest institutional power were Trump, his White House, congressional Republican leaders and senior administration officials—not a single vulnerable House member trying to outperform the national brand.

The alibi is inevitability

Invoking Jesus converts a political defeat into an act of nature. “The bottom fell out” similarly describes the result while avoiding what removed the supports.

The reporting identifies a more concrete explanation: anti-Trump voting, economic dissatisfaction and anger directed at national Republican governance. Bacon’s referendum assessment is therefore more revealing than Lawler’s supernatural hypothetical. Voters were not grading an imaginary perfect candidate. They were judging an actual governing party.

Republicans are arguing over the instrument

The post-election blame campaign spreads responsibility across convenient targets. Rep. Greg Steube faulted leaders for accommodating moderates. Rep. Marlin Stutzman suggested firing someone at the White House and specifically named Stephen Miller. Others pointed to Hegseth, who, according to the report, faced a possible impeachment vote over his conduct in Trump’s war with Iran.

These accusations may reflect genuine disputes, but they also narrow the reckoning to personnel and tactics. The deeper fact reported here is that Republicans held national power and voters removed them from congressional power. Complaining about insufficient ideological purity, one deputy chief of staff or one Cabinet official does not answer that verdict. It rearranges responsibility beneath the president and the party that empowered the administration.

The geography defeats the scapegoat

Lawler’s strength in Ramapo did not save him. Nor did his leads in Rockland and Putnam. Conley assembled enough support elsewhere, including in Westchester and several Rockland towns, to overcome those margins.

That matters because losing parties often search for one defective constituency, one insufficient turnout operation or one disloyal faction. The preliminary district results instead describe a coalition problem: concentrated support could not compensate for broader rejection. Blaming weaker electoral blocs would misstate where agency lay. The governing party created the national conditions under which its local advantages became inadequate.

A governing party owns its consequences

The systemic pattern is familiar. Political parties celebrate centralized power when it delivers appointments, legislation and obedience, then decentralize responsibility when voters punish the results. Presidents become background weather. Leaders become hapless intermediaries. Defeated incumbents become victims of an unstoppable wave.

But electoral waves are made from accumulated judgments about people exercising power. AlterNet’s account does not establish which policy or official caused each vote, and preliminary returns cannot supply every motive. It does establish the direction of accountability: Republicans governed, voters rejected them, and Republican officials immediately began looking downward and sideways for someone else to blame.
