Psychologist exposes the grim mission driving Trump’s ultimate endgame

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 27, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that psychologist Alan D. Blotcky believes Donald Trump’s “ultimate” objective is to dominate a world destabilized around him—to become, in Blotcky’s words, “the last standing hero.” That is Blotcky’s interpretation of Trump’s public conduct, not a clinical diagnosis or an established fact.

Blotcky argues that Trump is neither psychotic nor unable to distinguish truth from falsehood. In his view, Trump knowingly repeats false claims because repetition, presidential authority and ideological alignment can make supporters accept them. He likens Trump’s public behavior to that of a con man who treats every unstopped abuse as permission for the next one.

Blotcky also acknowledges a decisive limitation: he has never interviewed or evaluated Trump. His assessment rests on public statements and recorded behavior. The interview therefore establishes what Blotcky thinks Trump is doing; it cannot establish Trump’s private mental state or prove the sweeping motive attributed to him.

Power is not a personality trait

The article’s psychological frame is vivid, but institutional power is the operative fact. Trump occupies the presidency. Congress controls legislation, spending and oversight. Media owners, university administrators, law-firm leaders and other executives control institutions capable of resisting government pressure. Their choices determine whether presidential demands remain provocations or become policy.

Blotcky identifies congressional Republicans as crucial enablers, arguing that their refusal to push back has expanded Trump’s freedom of action. He and interviewer Matthew Rozsa also describe powerful institutions capitulating because their leaders fear lawsuits, lost federal money or financial damage. Whether that account explains every cited institution is not demonstrated here, but it correctly directs attention toward people with actual authority.

Millions of followers may reward Trump’s conduct. They do not possess Congress’s votes, the executive branch’s machinery or corporate management’s control over major institutions. Treating mass devotion as the primary engine risks obscuring the officials and executives who can say no—and choose not to.

Diagnosis can become misdirection

Calling Trump a “malignant narcissist” offers a compact explanation for otherwise disparate conduct. It can also shrink a political system into one man’s pathology. Annexation rhetoric, institutional branding and attacks on marginalized groups matter because officeholders and organizations give them reach, not because a psychological label makes them legible.

Blotcky is unusually explicit about the boundary: he has not examined Trump, and Trump’s private self could differ from his public persona. The article nevertheless builds its central claim around a confident account of Trump’s inner mission. That exceeds what the interview can establish.

The firmer case is behavioral. According to the reporting, Trump repeatedly advances falsehoods, personalizes public institutions and uses presidential power in ways that produce compliance. Those actions can be assessed without converting political analysis into remote diagnosis.

The permission structure

The strongest insight in the interview is not about Trump’s mind. It is Blotcky’s description of a con man’s governing logic: if nobody stops the act, the absence of resistance becomes retroactive permission.

That logic thrives when gatekeepers calculate that surrender is cheaper than conflict. Congress declines confrontation. Institutional leaders protect revenue and access. News organizations soften coverage or fail to sustain attention. Each retreat teaches the executive that the next demand may also go unanswered.

This is agency, not confusion. The article sometimes presents capitulation as something that simply spreads through frightened institutions. But institutions do not feel fear; their leaders make decisions. They weigh public obligations against private exposure and choose which one to protect.

Followers are leverage, not the sovereign

Rozsa argues that Trump’s supporters accept claims that flatter existing racism, xenophobia or transphobia. Blotcky agrees, saying ideological agreement can outweigh concern about dishonesty or misconduct. That interpretation may explain part of Trump’s durable support, but the interview supplies no polling or other evidence establishing supporters’ motives as a whole.

What the account does show is how a committed constituency can become leverage. Its loyalty raises the cost of dissent for Republican lawmakers and gives institutional leaders a ready excuse for submission. The crowd matters, but chiefly because powerful actors treat it as a veto over their own responsibilities.

Blaming followers alone would be convenient for elites. It would turn calculated accommodation by lawmakers and executives into an unavoidable response to public passion. The institutional record described by Blotcky points in the opposite direction: elite surrender magnifies the faction’s power.

The system behind the spectacle

The larger pattern is personalization reinforced by institutional cowardice. A president makes public power serve personal image and grievance; supporters normalize the demand; officials and executives decide resistance is too costly; each concession enlarges the next one.

AlterNet packages that pattern as a revelation about one man’s grim endgame. The more consequential revelation is about everyone positioned between impulse and consequence. A leader’s appetite does not implement itself. It requires votes, administrators, money, publicity and repeated decisions by people who retain agency while pretending they have none.

The system fails most predictably when those with the power to impose limits describe their own surrender as inevitability. Trump’s psychology may remain disputed. The permission structure surrounding him is visible in the conduct the article describes.
