What happened after New York raised taxes on the rich

The Millionaire Exodus Is a Protection Racket

A Threat Disguised as a Forecast

Fox News, hedge fund manager Bill Ackman, and grocery executive John Catsimatidis warned that wealthy New Yorkers would flee if Zohran Mamdani raised their taxes. Yet IRS data from an earlier state tax increase shows the opposite: New York added roughly 2,000 millionaire filers in 2023, while the number earning between $500,000 and $1 million rose by more than 8,500.

Those warnings were not disinterested economic analysis. They were political pressure from people with money, platforms, and direct access to power. Their message was simple: tax us, and we will punish everyone else.

The Wealthy Are Not Hostages

The “tax flight” story portrays rich residents as unusually fragile creatures whose continued presence must be purchased through favorable policy. In reality, wealth is embedded in valuable networks: firms, clients, property, social circles, cultural institutions, and access to other powerful people. New York helps create and maintain those advantages.

Ackman’s claim that a “handful” of departures could decimate the tax base turns concentrated wealth into a veto. The more dependent government becomes on the rich, the argument goes, the less authority voters should have to tax them. Inequality is thus offered as the reason inequality cannot be challenged.

Follow the Decision-Makers

Mamdani’s policy places a 0.8% to 1.3% tax on second homes worth at least $5 million, with an estimated annual yield of $500 million. That is a decision made through municipal power: identify extraordinary property wealth and use a fraction of it to finance public services.

The countermove came from private power. Ackman supplied financial authority, Catsimatidis supplied the aggrieved-business-owner script, and Fox News supplied mass distribution. None needed to prove that an exodus was occurring. They only needed officials and voters to fear one.

The Data Breaks the Script

After New York raised its top rate on the highest earners in 2022, the number of filers reporting more than $1 million grew about 3%. This happened while the national millionaire count declined. Meanwhile, the average adjusted gross income of people leaving New York fell 15%, to $106,900.

That does not prove taxes never influence an individual move. It does expose the dishonesty of presenting mass millionaire flight as the automatic consequence of asking wealthy residents to contribute more. The predicted catastrophe was treated as common sense before the relevant evidence arrived—and will likely survive after the evidence contradicted it.

The People Actually Being Priced Out

The weakest part of the standard coverage is its fixation on whether millionaires feel sufficiently welcome. The people with the greatest capacity to relocate are treated as captives; workers with fewer resources are treated as scenery.

Groundwork Collaborative points toward the real vulnerability: lower-income workers who sustain the city may be forced out. They do not leave because a modest levy has bruised their portfolio. They leave when housing and daily life become unaffordable. Political attention nevertheless follows the complaints of those with the most leverage, not the displacement of those bearing the greatest cost.

Fear Is an Asset of Power

The millionaire-exodus myth persists because it is useful. It converts a demand for preferential treatment into a warning about collective survival. It lets wealthy interests describe their own tax avoidance as concern for municipal finances. And it pressures elected officials to govern around hypothetical elite retaliation rather than documented public need.

This is the larger pattern: concentrated wealth first makes institutions dependent on it, then cites that dependence as proof it must never be constrained. The squawking is not evidence of flight. It is evidence that the threat itself remains politically profitable.
