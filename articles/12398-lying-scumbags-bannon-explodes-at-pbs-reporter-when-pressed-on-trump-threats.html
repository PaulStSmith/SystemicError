'Lying scumbags!' Bannon explodes at PBS reporter when pressed on Trump threats

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 26, 20255 min read

Steve Bannon. (Gage Skidmore)

Longtime Donald Trump ally Steve Bannon exploded at PBS reporter Mike Wiser during a Frontline interview, first recorded in April and published this week, when confronted over Trump's constant threats and insults against federal judges and asked whether this was "the type of thing a president should say."

The interview was released just as Supreme Court Justice Elena Kagan spoke out on the issue during a gathering of judges in California.

In addition to criticizing her right-wing colleagues for a flood of unsigned, unexplained emergency orders canceling lower-court judges' blocks on Trump's effort to dismantle the administrative state, Kagan said of Trump's attacks, "Judges are fair game for all kinds of criticism, strong criticism, pointed criticism, but vilifying judges in that way is a step beyond and ought to be understood as such."

Bannon, enraged over criticism, lobbed an obscene tirade at Wiser.

“By the way, was there any complaint from PBS, or was there any complaint by the Democrats, on the rhetoric used against the judges when the Roe v. Wade decision came out, and they had to have police protection at their houses? I don’t remember anything about the rhetoric being too high," said Bannon. “You guys are so f------ phony on the face of it. This is why we’re defeating the left. The American people, as they see this, they go, ‘These guys are a bunch of f------ lying scumbags,’ which you are a bunch of f------ lying scumbags. You terrorize — you tried to terrorize Supreme Court justices over a decision that didn’t go your way.”

"They’re all gutless cowards," Bannon continued. "They’ve all hid behind the system for decades and decades and decades. So the university administrations? They’re not that tough. The big law firms? They’re not that tough. The media? Look who’s cratered. How many times — look at the settlement with Trump. They’re not tough."

By contrast, he said, with Trump supporters, “You can put us in f------ prison, you can take Trump up there and put him on trial every day, the people around Trump are battle-hardened, okay? You’re not gonna scare us and we’re not gonna stop, and what we know is you guys are a bunch of p------. You will crater. PBS is gonna crater. You know why? You’re not tough.”

The GOP has waged a crusade against public media in recent months, passing a Trump-proposed bill to claw back billions of dollars from the Corporation for Public Broadcasting. NPR and PBS are primarily funded by viewer donations and sponsorships, but grants from the CPB are vital to maintaining the budgets of newsrooms in lower-income and more remote areas.

Our Analysis:

Bannon's Bombast: A Deep Dive into the Rhetoric of Rage

In a spectacle that could only emerge from the tumultuous waters of contemporary American politics, Steve Bannon, a figure synonymous with the mercurial rise of Donald Trump, has once again ignited controversy. This time, his ire was directed at PBS reporter Mike Wiser during a Frontline interview. The stage for Bannon's tirade was set against a backdrop of Supreme Court Justice Elena Kagan's recent remarks, which, while addressing a gathering of judges in California, lamented the unchecked vilification of judges—a trend alarmingly normalized in recent years.

The Heart of the Matter

At the core of Bannon's explosive response is a fundamental question: Should a president, or his allies, engage in relentless attacks against federal judges? This query becomes particularly poignant in light of Kagan's reflections on the "flood of unsigned, unexplained emergency orders" and the broader implications of such actions on the judiciary's independence and the administrative state's dismantling efforts.

Bannon's Retort: A Study in Deflection and Rage

Bannon's response, laden with expletives and accusations, serves not only as a vehement defense but as a mirror to the polarized and vitriolic nature of current political discourse. His tirade, accusing Wiser and, by extension, critics of the administration's approach to the judiciary of hypocrisy, attempts to redirect the conversation from the criticism at hand to perceived injustices against conservative figures and decisions, such as the backlash following the controversial Roe v. Wade decision.

Key Points from Bannon's Outburst:

  • Hypocrisy Allegations: Bannon's assertion hinges on the idea that criticism of Trump's rhetoric towards judges is selective and overlooks similar rhetoric from the left, particularly in the wake of significant judicial decisions.

  • An Indictment of the Media and Institutions: Bannon extends his critique to encompass a broader distrust and disdain for major institutions, including universities, law firms, and the media, painting them as "gutless cowards."

  • A Declaration of Resilience: Perhaps most telling is Bannon's portrayal of Trump supporters as "battle-hardened" and impervious to intimidation or legal challenges—a sentiment that underscores the deep divide and the combative stance of this faction.

The GOP's Crusade Against Public Media

Bannon's attack dovetails with a broader GOP initiative to undermine public media, underscored by a proposed bill to retract significant funding from the Corporation for Public Broadcasting. This move, Bannon suggests, is a testament to the perceived bias and frailty of institutions like PBS and NPR, which he predicts will ultimately "crater" under pressure.

Analysis and Perspective

Bannon's diatribe, while shocking, is not without precedent in the current political climate. It exemplifies a strategy of aggressive deflection and counter-attack that has become a hallmark of certain political figures when faced with criticism. However, this approach raises critical questions about the state of political discourse, the role of the media, and the long-term implications for democratic institutions and norms.

The broader context— the attack on public media funding, the vilification of the judiciary, and the polarized reactions to such tactics—paints a troubling picture of a democracy at a crossroads. The rhetoric of rage, as demonstrated by Bannon, while galvanizing to some, serves as a clarion call to others about the risks of further division and the erosion of civil discourse.

In essence, Bannon's outburst is not merely an isolated incident but a symptom of a larger systemic malaise—a malaise characterized by deepening divides, distrust in institutions, and a shift towards a more confrontational and polarized political landscape.
