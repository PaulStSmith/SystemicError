Potential Trump Achilles heel laid bare in new memo

Based on an article by Erik De La Garza

Paulo SantosAugust 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Immigration Messaging Is Not the Problem. ICE Is.

The Useful Opening

A Democratic-aligned memo, reported by The Bulwark, says two immigration messages tested with voters each lifted support for Democrats by about 2 points. The pitch is straightforward: emphasize immigrants’ economic role, and contrast that with Trump-era cruelty, including masked police, cuts to basic services, and more money flowing to ICE.

That is not a breakthrough so much as a reminder of where the real conflict sits. The issue is not whether Democrats can find cleaner language. It is whether they are willing to name the machinery doing the damage.

Who Holds Power

Trump is the figure in the headline, but the institutional power in this story is bigger than Trump’s personal style. The relevant actors are the federal enforcement apparatus, the political coalition funding it, and the party leadership deciding whether to fight it or merely message around it.

The memo itself admits the terrain: Republicans have “established bona fides” on immigration because they own the punitive frame. That is what power looks like here. They do not merely debate immigration. They define it as policing, fear, and exclusion, then demand everyone else argue on their terms.

The Real Decision Makers

The article tries to present this as a communications puzzle. It is not. It is a governance choice.

Someone decided to put more money into ICE while cutting food and health care assistance. Someone decided that masked agents in the streets would be normalized enough to test as a political message. Someone decided immigrant labor should be treated as useful only when it can be harvested and invisible, then denounced when it becomes electorally convenient.

That chain of responsibility is the point. The cruelty is not accidental confusion. It is policy, funded and enforced.

What the Framing Tries to Hide

The memo’s economic argument is tactically useful, but it also reveals the trap Democrats keep walking into. The language of “immigrants’ contributions” is safer than confronting the state violence that makes the issue politically combustible in the first place.

That is the misdirection. Voters are being asked to process immigration as a labor-market story when the state is using police power to terrorize people in public. The article flirts with the obvious truth when it mentions masked police “taking people in the streets,” then retreats into messaging advice as if the violence were a branding problem.

It is not a branding problem. It is an enforcement regime.

Abolish ICE

If the story is about ICE, the answer is not reform theater, better training, or a few bad actors. ICE is the problem. It is the institutional expression of anti-immigrant punishment dressed up as administration.

Democrats do not need to sound tougher. They need to stop accepting the premise that a secretive deportation machine belongs in a democratic society. Any politics that treats ICE as a normal agency rather than a structural outrage has already conceded too much.

The Pattern Beneath It

This is the broader Democratic problem in miniature: the party often recognizes public disgust with authoritarian cruelty only after the cruelty has become visible enough to poll badly. Then it reaches for language, not power. It talks about immigrants as workers, communities, and taxpayers because that is electorally legible, while leaving the enforcement state itself largely intact.

That leaves Democrats always one step behind the moral reality they claim to oppose. The country does not need a more polished explanation for why immigrant labor matters. It needs a party willing to say that masked police, racialized raids, and ICE as an institution are the symptoms of a state organized to punish the vulnerable and protect the powerful.
