Federal judge mocks ‘squirming’ Trump lawyer’s ‘cringe’ golf course argument

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 14, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A federal judge confronted the Trump administration on Friday over shifting explanations for closing a public golf course and allegations that officials destroyed related records despite a court order. When a Justice Department lawyer tried to justify Donald Trump’s plans for the East Potomac Golf Links by invoking his electoral mandate and real-estate résumé, U.S. District Judge Ana Reyes exposed the argument’s basic defect: voters did not elect Trump to redesign a District of Columbia park.

The exchange mattered beyond the embarrassment of a government attorney. The administration was asking a court to treat presidential preference as public authorization while questions remained about compliance with an injunction and the preservation of records sought for future congressional scrutiny.

An electoral mandate does not convey a city park

Principal Deputy Assistant Attorney General Adam R.F. Gustafson argued that voters chose Trump because “he’s a builder,” according to Lawfare senior editor Molly Roberts. Reyes responded: “People elected him so he could do golf courses in the District?”

Gustafson repeated that voters knew Trump’s experience as a builder and his success in real estate. Appellate attorney Gabriel Malor, observing the hearing, called the argument “very cringe.”

The problem was not merely clumsy advocacy. Gustafson converted a national election into purported consent for one specific development project—a plan to transform East Potomac Golf Links and the surrounding park into what Trump has described as a “World Class, championship-level golf course.” Local groups have sued to stop it.

That theory erases every institution between a president and a public asset: courts, local residents, preservation requirements and the limits of executive authority. Winning an election grants defined governmental powers. It does not make the president’s personal development instincts legally controlling.

The rationale changed after the court intervened

Reyes also pressed the administration on its evolving account of why the park needed to close. Trump initially presented the closure as necessary for renovations, according to the courtroom reporting. After Reyes blocked that plan, the administration invoked safety concerns.

Under questioning, Gustafson conceded that renovation remained one rationale. Reyes then pointed to Trump’s own conduct: he had visited the course during the summer, yet apparently had not demanded immediate action to address any danger to people.

“If he had seen safety concerns to human individuals, wouldn’t he have insisted that something get done immediately?” she asked.

Court reporter Chris Geidner described Reyes’s questioning as a series with “only one answer” unfavorable to Gustafson, who was “squirming.” The substantive issue beneath that discomfort was whether the administration had repackaged a blocked renovation project as an urgent safety measure. The hearing did not resolve that question, but the government’s admission prevented it from cleanly separating the asserted hazard from Trump’s development ambitions.

Records are now part of the fight

The hearing also arose amid whistleblower allegations that administration officials were destroying documents connected to Trump’s D.C. projects in anticipation of investigations by an incoming Democratic congressional majority. Those remain allegations, but Reyes made clear that substantiated destruction would bring immediate consequences.

“If I find credible evidence of any document destruction, I will start proceedings on sanctions or contempt within a zeptosecond,” she said.

The Independent reported that Democrats had demanded that officials preserve existing record collections and productions, recover deleted material and suspend destruction practices. That demand identifies where the institutional stakes now sit: an administration facing prospective oversight controls the records that investigators would need to reconstruct its decisions.

Document preservation is not clerical housekeeping in that setting. It determines whether Congress and the courts can test official explanations against the government’s own internal record. If relevant material was destroyed, the damage would extend beyond this golf-course dispute to the ability of other branches to impose accountability at all.

The public was present, even if the argument excluded it

Gustafson also resisted acknowledging opposition to the design after Reyes told him that people in Washington were “very upset” about it. The judge then asked those in the courtroom who opposed the project to raise their hands.

More than 100 people were present, according to the account, and Reyes said the “vast majority” raised their hands. She instructed Gustafson to turn around and look.

A courtroom show of hands is not a legal finding or a scientific measure of citywide opinion. It did, however, puncture the government’s rhetorical maneuver. The administration’s lawyer had invoked an abstract national electorate to justify Trump’s preferred use of a local public space while declining to confront the residents physically assembled behind him.

That is the misdirection at the center of the case: presidential power presented as popular will, a renovation repackaged as safety, and a contested vanity project treated as though election victory settled every subsequent question. Reyes’s warning placed the actual unresolved issue back where it belongs—not on Trump’s brand as a builder, but on whether his administration obeyed the court and preserved the evidence needed to find out.
