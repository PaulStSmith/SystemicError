A twisted new legal ruling means the White House could soon disappear

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosSeptember 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Court’s Favorite Word Is Standing. Its Favorite Reality Is Power.

A demolition disguised as a procedural question

The source story is straightforward on its facts: Trump tore down the White House’s East Wing last fall, replaced it with the beginning of a 90,000-square-foot luxury ballroom, and the Supreme Court’s five Trump-sympathizers let construction continue by saying the challengers lacked “standing.” The National Trust for Historic Preservation, a congressionally chartered nonprofit meant to guard public buildings, was told it was not directly harmed enough to question the destruction of the People’s House.

That is not a neutral legal posture. It is a political decision wrapped in a doctrinal excuse.

Power belonged to the people who could stop it

Actual institutional power sat in three places: Trump, who ordered the demolition; the Court’s majority, which declined to halt it; and congressional Republicans, who have the ability to intervene and won’t, because they would rather be invited than obstructive. The source says that plainly, and it is the crucial fact. No one is mistaking who has leverage here.

The National Trust did not have the power to destroy or preserve the White House. It had the burden of objecting after the damage was already underway. The Court then treated that asymmetry as a reason to deny relief. That is not law standing above politics. That is power protecting power by demanding the powerless prove injury in the exact form the powerful prefer.

Standing is the mask, not the argument

“Standing” is doing ugly work here. It converts an obvious public injury into an abstract eligibility dispute. The White House is not private property. It is an iconic public building with constitutional and symbolic weight. Yet the majority’s logic, as the source notes, would allow a president to demolish the whole place and dare anyone to prove the right kind of harm before the wrecking is complete.

That is misdirection in judicial form. The court is not saying the demolition is fine. It is saying the people trying to stop it are procedurally unworthy. The distinction is convenient for the justices and catastrophic for anyone who still thinks law should constrain official vandalism.

Roberts drew the line the majority would not

Chief Justice John G. Roberts Jr. joined the three Democratic-appointed justices in dissent and called the construction “likely unlawful.” He also made the key point the majority tried to evade: those responsible should follow the rules when deciding what to tear down and what to build up at the People’s House.

That matters, but only up to a point. Roberts is not suddenly a champion of democratic accountability. He is a judge preserving the appearance of institutional order after his court’s majority treated a presidential vanity project as a legal non-event. His dissent acknowledges the breach; it does not erase the court’s complicity in permitting it.

This is billionaire politics made physical

The ballroom is not just a building. It is a monument to class power. The source calls it the symbol of the billionaire class that briefly ran Washington, and that is the right frame. Trump did not merely alter architecture. He converted the White House into a stage for elite self-display, and the Court’s majority helped launder that conversion through procedural jargon.

Congressional Republicans are part of the same arrangement. They are not neutral bystanders. They are enabled beneficiaries who would never challenge Trump because they want access to the spoils. Their cowardice is not accidental. It is the operating system of modern Republican governance: protect the strong, indulge the rich, and treat public institutions as instruments to be captured.

The democratic answer is restoration, not reverence

The source ends with a provocative idea: a future Democratic president should demolish Trump’s ballroom and restore the People’s House. The symbolism is obvious, and so is the deeper point. Democratic politics cannot keep responding to authoritarian damage as if the damage were a policy disagreement. Some acts are not initiatives to be managed. They are seizures of public space.

The larger pattern is clear. The right uses institutional forms to normalize personal rule, oligarchic taste, and public theft. The courts often translate that theft into technicalities. And the opposition is told to respect the process while the building burns. That is how democratic erosion happens in plain view: not only through force, but through officials who insist the rules matter most when they are invoked on behalf of the people least able to enforce them.
