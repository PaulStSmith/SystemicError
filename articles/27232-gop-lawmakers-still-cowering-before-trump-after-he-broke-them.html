GOP lawmakers still cowering before Trump after he broke them

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 13, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power is on Capitol Hill, tooCowardice disguised as uncertaintyLoyalty has material consequencesThe framing understates the choiceA party trained against accountability

What happened

AlterNet reports that congressional Republicans refused to hold President Donald Trump responsible after the party lost more than 40 House seats and at least eight Senate seats in the 2026 midterm elections. Exit polling cited by NBC News attributed the defeat to voters’ anger over living costs and their belief that Trump’s policies had made those costs worse.

Representative Gabe Evans, who reportedly lost his Colorado district by 15 points, said Republicans were still examining the data when asked about Trump’s tariffs and the Iran war. Senators Jim Justice and John Kennedy acknowledged the economic backlash but avoided criticizing Trump or proposing that he reconsider his policies. NBC News reported that surviving Republican lawmakers remained reluctant to use Congress’s power to change the policies they said required a “course correction.”

The power is on Capitol Hill, too

Trump holds presidential power, but congressional Republicans are not spectators trapped beneath it. They can legislate, investigate, control appropriations, organize opposition and force politically costly votes. Their refusal to exercise those powers is itself a decision.

Justice’s claim that Trump processed advice on Iran “as best he possibly can” shifts attention from policy consequences to the president’s supposed effort. That is not oversight. It is an excuse offered by a senator whose job includes judging executive decisions, not grading presidential intentions.

Cowardice disguised as uncertainty

Evans’s appeal to unspecified data is especially hollow because the reporting already describes a sweeping electoral reversal and exit polls identifying the cost of living as the central grievance. More analysis may refine the diagnosis; it does not prevent a lawmaker from stating who enacted the policies under scrutiny.

Kennedy performed the same evasion more artfully. He called the result “an economic election,” then declined to advise the president whose agenda Republicans had implemented and defended. “Self-examination” becomes a foggy collective ritual in which everyone reflects and nobody bears responsibility.

Loyalty has material consequences

This deference is not merely embarrassing behavior after a defeat. NBC News reports that Republicans were unwilling to use their congressional authority against policies associated with higher gas, diesel and food costs. If lawmakers recognize the damage but preserve the policy because confronting Trump is dangerous to their careers, households absorb the price of that calculation.

The agency belongs to the officials still holding office. Trump chose and pursued his agenda. Republican lawmakers enabled it, defended it and, according to the reporting, remained unwilling to restrain it even after voters punished their party.

The framing understates the choice

Calling Republicans “shell-shocked” risks turning calculated submission into temporary confusion. Their answers show that they understand the political problem: voters are hurting, the election was economic and the party suffered a historic defeat. What they refuse to do is connect those facts publicly to the leader of their party.

That is misdirection toward abstraction—“the economy,” “everything,” “advice,” “data”—when identifiable officials made identifiable decisions. The weaker actors in this account are voters facing higher costs. Yet responsibility is blurred until their hardship appears to be an atmospheric condition rather than a consequence of policy.

A party trained against accountability

The larger pattern is institutional cowardice hardened into party governance. Personal loyalty to the president displaces Congress’s independent obligations, while electoral defeat produces rhetorical adjustment instead of a change in conduct.

A political system cannot correct policy failure when the officials empowered to intervene treat naming its author as forbidden. Republicans were not simply broken by Trump. By refusing to use their own authority after voters delivered an unmistakable rebuke, they continued breaking the institution themselves.
