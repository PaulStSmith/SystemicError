'They're going wobbly': MSNBC host reams Republicans for drastic U-turn

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 29, 20255 min read

Donald Trump speaking with supporters at a campaign rally at Veterans Memorial Coliseum at the Arizona State Fairgrounds in Phoenix, Arizona. (Gage Skidmore/Flickr)

Republicans and their punditry have turned on the concept of due process as courts rule on those grounds against President Donald Trump's mass deportations of migrants — but just a few years ago, Republicans were screaming for it for themselves, MSNBC's Ari Melber said on "The Beat" Tuesday evening.

He listed a number of examples, from Republican governors to Supreme Court Justice Samuel Alito complaining about lack of "due process" for people who were penalized for refusing COVID vaccinations — but one of the starkest examples in his supercut was former Fox News primetime host Tucker Carlson.

"How do you have the authority to order something that so clearly contravenes the Bill of Rights of the United States, the U.S. Constitution?" said Carlson in a clip from 2020. Then this year, he said in a separate clip, "I am for due process specifically for me. I'm not that concerned whether someone who broke a law, my law, to get into my country and is taking welfare from my pocket, gets due process."

The due process debate has exploded in recent weeks as immigrants have been deported or detained, with many being denied their Constitutional right to argue their case in court.

"Due process for me, not for them or you," said Melber, himself an attorney by training. "But that's the whole point."

"Remember, due process attaches regardless of what someone's accused of, because that's the whole point," he said. "And there are people who've been convicted of terrible things all the way up, including murder, who under our system are entitled to due process. And murderers, whatever their nationality, obviously, by the end of the process, you feel a lot of judgment of them, but they get the due process along the way, and a lot of reasonable people would say a murderer is worse than an unlawful migrant, even if both broke different rules."

"So there you have the conservatives, who are on record quite recently in a real-world example, saying Bill of Rights, due process, rules COVID objections, and now they're going soft, they're going wobbly, some of them sleeping," said Melber. "Other people's rights to the side, sometimes admitting as a matter of rhetoric, or perhaps confession that they just want to do it selectively. And if you want to do it selectively, then you don't support human rights because they are for all humans."

"That's why what we're hearing ... about deportation, and this will only apply to them, or maybe a few more of them, or even more, but maybe not you, is getting the whole thing backwards," Melber continued. "There's either baseline protections and a Bill of Rights that protects people against government overreach, including the abuse of detention powers that the founders were concerned about from the beginning. Either we have that or we don't."

Our Analysis:

The Irony of Selective Due Process: A GOP Paradox

In a recent segment on MSNBC's "The Beat", Ari Melber, attorney-turned-journalist, tore into the glaring hypocrisy bedeviling the Republican stance on due process. It's a rich tapestry of contradiction, where due process is a heralded pillar of democracy—until it inconveniences the political narrative, particularly around immigration and Trump's hardline policies. Let's dissect this with the razor-sharp clarity it deserves.

Due Process: A Convenient Shield or Disposable Tool?

The GOP's sudden amnesia on due process, as Melber skillfully highlights, is nothing short of astounding. We're talking about a party that has, in the past, cloaked itself in the Constitution like it's a divine mantle when it suited their needs. Governors and justices alike have bemoaned the ghastly specter of due process violations when it came to COVID-19 measures. Yet, now, as Trump's draconian mass deportations face legal hurdles on due process grounds, the narrative flips with a gymnast's grace.

Tucker Carlson, the former Fox News primetime host, embodies this hypocrisy so perfectly, it's almost poetic. His transformation from a staunch defender of the Bill of Rights—when it served his narrative—to dismissing due process for immigrants because they don't fit into his myopic view of who deserves rights, is a spectacle of political opportunism.

The Crux of the Matter

Melber hits the nail on the head: "Due process for me, not for them or you." This isn't just about political flip-flopping; it's a dangerous game with the foundational principles of justice. Due process isn't à la carte; it's an all-or-nothing deal. It's the bedrock of the legal system, ensuring fairness and justice for all, regardless of the crime or the accused's nationality.

The argument Melber presents is irrefutable. Even those convicted of heinous crimes, up to and including murder, are entitled to due process. This isn't because the system sympathizes with criminals but because it recognizes the inherent rights of all humans. To argue otherwise, to suggest that some are more deserving of these rights than others based on their immigration status or any other criteria, is to fundamentally misunderstand the concept of human rights.

GOP's Wobbly Stance on Human Rights

What Melber uncovers is a disturbing trend towards selective human rights—a stance that is not only fundamentally un-American but starkly inhumane. The readiness with which some Republicans are willing to abandon these principles when it's politically expedient is a glaring testament to their opportunistic approach to governance.

The cornerstone argument that due process and the Bill of Rights serve as a bulwark against government overreach seems to crumble when the government's actions align with their own biases and agendas. This selective amnesia about the foundational principles of American democracy doesn't just undermine their own credibility; it erodes the very fabric of justice and equality the country purports to stand for.

In Conclusion

Ari Melber's segment isn't just a critique; it's a clarion call for consistency, integrity, and the unwavering support of due process for all. The GOP's current trajectory, as outlined by Melber, is a dangerous one, fraught with hypocrisy and a blatant disregard for the principles they claim to hold dear. It's a stark reminder that in the fight for justice and human rights, selective memory serves no one—and selective due process serves even less.

In the grand scheme, this isn't just about immigration policies or partisan politics; it's about the very soul of American democracy. Either we stand for due process and the rights enshrined in the Bill of Rights for everyone, or we stand for nothing at all.
