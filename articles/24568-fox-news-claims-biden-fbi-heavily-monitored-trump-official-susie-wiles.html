Fox News claims Biden FBI heavily monitored Trump official Susie Wiles

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 9, 20263 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that Fox News obtained White House-released documents showing the FBI cataloged Susie Wiles’s phone activity from at least February through May 2023 during the federal investigation into Donald Trump’s handling of classified documents and presidential records. The records reportedly identify contacts with at least 21 attorneys, as well as journalists, campaign staff, Trump Organization personnel and Secret Service contacts.

The documents show who contacted whom, when and for how long, but not what was discussed or what investigators intended to prove. The FBI and Justice Department opened the investigation in March 2022 after authorization from FBI Director Christopher Wray and approval from Attorney General Merrick Garland; special counsel Jack Smith assumed oversight after his November 2022 appointment. Wiles, then running Trump’s political operation, was not yet White House chief of staff.

White House spokesperson Lauren Bis called the monitoring an abuse of government power and accused Smith of spying on political opponents. The reporting presented here does not establish that President Joe Biden ordered, directed or knew about the collection.

Metadata is still state power

A catalog of calls is not a recording of conversations, but it is not trivial. Mapping a political adviser’s contacts can expose the structure of a campaign, its legal coordination, its media relationships and its connections to security personnel. Federal investigators wielded that institutional power through an inquiry approved at the highest named levels of the FBI and Justice Department.

That deserves scrutiny. The relevant questions are whether investigators had lawful authority, what evidence justified collecting the records, how broadly the subpoenas were drawn and what safeguards governed sensitive contacts with lawyers and journalists. The documents, as described, do not answer those questions. Their silence supports investigation, not a predetermined verdict.

“Biden FBI” is a political substitution

The headline framing replaces the officials identified in the reporting with Biden. That is misdirection unless evidence connects him personally to the decision. Wray sought authorization, Garland approved the investigation and Smith later supervised it. Those are the actors the available account names.

Collapsing an entire department into the president’s partisan possession also serves Trump’s preferred theory of government: law enforcement is legitimate when it protects him and “weaponized” when it investigates him. Fox’s framing does not merely describe institutional power. It personalizes that power selectively, turning an evidentiary dispute into an accusation against a political enemy without supplying the missing link.

The White House controls the counternarrative

The current White House released the documents and supplied the language through which allies could present them. Wiles is no longer merely a private citizen whose records were subpoenaed; she is chief of staff to the president whose conduct prompted the underlying investigation. The administration therefore holds enormous power to publicize selected records, define villains and convert investigative procedure into political ammunition.

Bis’s claim that this was “spying on political opponents” goes beyond what the reported documents establish. They show contact mapping, according to Fox, but do not reveal investigators’ purpose or the substance of any calls. Calling the operation disgraceful before those facts are established is advocacy dressed as adjudication.

Accountability requires the missing facts

The strongest criticism of the collection is not that Wiles was politically important. Political status cannot create immunity for people operating near a subject of a federal investigation. Nor can an investigation’s existence prove every surveillance method was proportionate.

The central issue is legal and institutional: what process authorized access, what investigative need justified its breadth, and how protected relationships were handled. Fox’s “sprawling” label describes scale, not legality. The White House’s accusation supplies motive without evidence. AlterNet is right to identify the partisan relitigation, but mockery of Trump allies does not resolve the underlying civil-liberties questions either.

Power rewrites the investigation

This episode shows how control of the executive branch can be used to retry a previous administration’s investigation in public. Records collected under one government become narrative weapons under the next. Officials with present power portray themselves as victims of past power while withholding, at least in the account provided, the facts needed to judge whether that power was abused.

The systemic failure is not simply aggressive federal investigation or partisan media distortion. It is the replacement of standards with factional ownership: subpoenas are tyranny when aimed inward and justice when aimed outward. That arrangement protects neither civil liberties nor impartial enforcement. It protects whichever political machine controls the documents, the podium and the labels.
