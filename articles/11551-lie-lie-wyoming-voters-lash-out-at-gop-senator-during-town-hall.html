Comments from climate change denier Rep. Harriet Hageman (R-Wyo.) went badly at a recent town hall in rural Pinedale.

WyoFile reports the community of Pinedale suffered alarming ozone spikes in the late 2000s, with one measurement surpassing that of Los Angeles. Residents — particularly those with preexisting respiratory conditions and asthma — had to avoid outdoor exercise on high-ozone days. WyoFile reports a doctor even advised one mother to drive her hypoxic newborn to a safer place 78 miles away from home.

So when Hageman appeared at a town hall and announced her support for “repealing” a landmark 2009 doctrine classifying greenhouse gases as pollutants and legally binding federal agencies to regulate them, attendees shouted her down.

“The endangerment finding is absolutely based upon false science,” Hageman said, prompting a chorus of boos from the July 29 crowd along with guffaws and several loud shouts of “No!”

WyoFile reports Hageman tried to continue over the outburst: “CO2 [carbon dioxide] is not a pollutant. As far as the validity and the science that was the foundation for that, they cooked the books.”

But Sublette County constituents weren’t having it, reports WyoFile: “The booing continued, along with one person shouting, ‘Lie! Lie!’ as Hageman pressed on.”

Environmental Defense Fund Legislative Manager John Rutecki told WyoFile that “Wyoming didn’t get cleaner by accident,” referring to efforts to reduce ozone-inducing pollutants in Pinedale’s Upper Green River Basin. “It took years of effort by the Pinedale community and so many others.”

Our Analysis:

The Ozone Outrage: Rep. Hageman's Climate Change Denial Meets Vocal Resistance

In what could only be described as a stark display of the chasm between political rhetoric and the harsh realities of climate change, Rep. Harriet Hageman's recent town hall in Pinedale, Wyoming, served as a glaring testament to the disconnect some politicians have with the environmental crises at their doorstep. Hageman's comments, aimed at discrediting the scientific consensus on climate change and undermining efforts to regulate greenhouse gases, were met with the kind of vocal resistance that underscores a community pushed to its limits.

The Context: Pinedale's Ozone Crisis

Let's set the stage: Pinedale, a small community in Wyoming, has been grappling with alarming ozone spikes since the late 2000s, a scenario more befitting a bustling metropolis with heavy traffic than a rural town. These aren't mere statistics; they represent days where the very air residents breathe poses a threat to their health. Imagine being advised to drive your hypoxic newborn 78 miles away because the air in your town is too polluted for their fragile lungs. This is the reality Pinedale faces, a reality Hageman seems blithely unaware of or chooses to ignore.

Hageman's Contentious Stance

During the town hall, Hageman boldly proclaimed her support for "repealing" a 2009 doctrine that classifies greenhouse gases as pollutants, a legal framework that compels federal agencies to regulate them. Her assertion that the "endangerment finding is absolutely based upon false science" and that "CO2 is not a pollutant," is not just factually erroneous; it's dangerously misleading. The reaction from the crowd—boos, guffaws, and cries of "Lie! Lie!"—was not just a rejection of her statements but a visceral response to the threat her stance poses to their community and, by extension, to communities worldwide facing similar environmental hazards.

The Science Speaks for Itself

Hageman's claim that the science behind greenhouse gas regulation is "cooked" flies in the face of the overwhelming consensus within the scientific community about the role of CO2 and other greenhouse gases in climate change. To argue otherwise is to engage in a form of denialism that can have dire consequences for public policy and, more importantly, public health.

A Community's Fight for Clean Air

What Hageman's rhetoric fails to acknowledge is the resilience and dedication of communities like Pinedale, which have fought hard to improve their air quality. The "non-attainment" status and subsequent regulations weren't arbitrary punishments but necessary measures to protect public health. The progress made in reducing ozone levels in Pinedale's Upper Green River Basin didn't happen by chance; it was the result of concerted efforts by the community, regulators, and even the companies involved. As Pinedale resident Mary Lynn Worl aptly put it, the goal was never to halt drilling and development but to ensure it was done responsibly and safely.

Conclusion: The Path Forward

The resistance Hageman faced at the town hall is a powerful reminder that the fight against climate change and environmental degradation is not just about data and regulations; it's about people's lives and their right to a safe, healthy environment. When politicians like Hageman choose to deny the science of climate change or advocate for rolling back crucial environmental protections, they're not just engaging in a policy debate; they're directly challenging the well-being of the communities they serve.

In the face of such denialism, the voices of Pinedale's residents and others like them are essential. They remind us that progress is possible when communities come together to demand better, even in the face of political opposition. As we move forward, let's not forget the lessons from Pinedale: that science, not rhetoric, should guide our environmental policies, and that the health of our communities is non-negotiable.
