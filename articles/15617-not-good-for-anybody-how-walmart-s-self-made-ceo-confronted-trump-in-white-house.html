Doug McMillon, the CEO of Walmart, reportedly told President Donald Trump -- alongside other business leaders who met the president in the Oval Office on Monday -- that his tariff policy is "not good for anybody."

According to a report published in The Daily Beast on Friday, McMillon and the other CEOs reportedly warned Trump that "he was creating a situation that would not be good for anybody."

The report noted that he began working at Walmart at the age of 17 and accidentally rear-ended his boss's car as he arrived for his first day of stocking shelves. Now 58 years old, he has served as the CEO of the country's largest retailer for over a decade, achieving $648 billion in sales last year.

They reportedly told him about the adverse impacts of his tariffs. The CEOs particularly mentioned the 145 percent tariff imposed on China, warning that his policies might soon result in empty store shelves across the country.

On Tuesday, Trump said at a press conference in the Oval Office that he had reconsidered the tariffs on China.

But the president's version of his meeting with the CEOs was different from what has been reported.

“I had the head of Walmart. I had the head of Home Depot and the head of Target in my office. And I’ll tell you what they think, they think what I’m doing is exactly right," he said in a recent interview.

Trump said the United States has entered into 200 trade agreements, though he did not disclose who the partners are. In an interview with Time Magazine published Friday, Trump said, "I've made 200 deals."

"If we are lucky, Trump will have many more meetings with the guy who started out stocking shelves and became an actual great businessman, not one who played one on television. The president would have done well to have ditched Musk and listened to McMillon from the start," the Beast article said.

Our Analysis:

The Art of the Deal? More Like the Art of the Denial

In an era where the lines between political theater and economic policy blur with unnerving frequency, the recent meeting between President Donald Trump and a triumvirate of America’s retail giants—Walmart, Target, and Home Depot—serves as a stark reminder of the chasm between the world as Trump sees it and the reality faced by businesses and consumers alike.

Doug McMillon, the CEO of Walmart, didn't mince words when he reportedly told Trump that his tariff policy is “not good for anybody.” This statement, coming from the head of the nation's largest retailer—a man who climbed the corporate ladder from a shelf-stocker to CEO, overseeing $648 billion in sales last year—carries the weight of experience and expertise. Yet, it seems to have fallen on deaf ears, or perhaps, ears that prefer an alternate version of reality.

The CEOs, including Brian Cornell of Target and Ted Decker of Home Depot, pointed out the elephant in the room: the 145 percent tariff on China. They warned of dire consequences, including empty store shelves across the country. This isn’t just about the bottom line for these corporations; it’s about the American consumer, the economy, and the global market at large.

However, in a twist that surprises no one, Trump’s recounting of the meeting diverges starkly from these reports. He claims these business leaders think what he’s doing is “exactly right.” This discrepancy isn’t just a matter of he-said-she-said; it’s a reflection of a troubling pattern of denial and distortion that has characterized much of Trump’s presidency.

The president’s assertion that he’s renegotiated 200 trade agreements remains as opaque as ever, with no details as to who these agreements are with or what they entail. His claim, “145% is very high and it won’t be that high,” followed by a vague promise to reduce it substantially yet not to zero, is as clear as mud. It’s a non-answer that leaves businesses, consumers, and policymakers in a state of uncertainty.

Trump’s comparison of the United States to a “department store” where he controls prices is a gross oversimplification of international trade dynamics and the intricate balance of global economics. It underscores a profound misunderstanding of how tariffs work and who they impact the most. Spoiler alert: it’s often the American consumer who foots the bill.

The closing thoughts of the article, suggesting Trump would do well to listen more to McMillon—a “great businessman”—rather than Elon Musk, touches on a deeper issue. It’s not just about choosing the right advisers; it’s about listening to the voices of reason, experience, and expertise, even when they tell you something you don’t want to hear.

In the end, this episode is more than just a clash of personalities or differing opinions on economic policy. It’s a case study in the dangers of governing by gut feeling over grounded facts, of choosing narrative over knowledge, and of the potential consequences of such choices on the American economy and psyche. As the CEOs of some of the largest retailers in the country have made clear, the path Trump is on with his tariffs is not just “not good for anybody”; it’s potentially catastrophic.

The real question is, will Trump listen, or will he continue to confuse the art of the deal with the art of denial?
