Trump DOJ may have telegraphed its secret 'slush fund' plans in new filing: analysis

The Fund They Won’t Kill on the Record

Context

The Justice Department helped create a $1.776 billion “anti-weaponization” fund as part of a settlement tied to Donald Trump’s lawsuit against the IRS, then backed away after bipartisan outrage and public condemnation of the setup as a slush fund for allies. But when U.S. District Judge Leonie Brinkema demanded sworn declarations that the fund would never exist, the department declined to make that promise under oath.

Power, Not Confusion

The people with actual institutional power here are not the headlines’ symbolic targets. They are the officials who signed the arrangement in the first place: acting Attorney General Todd Blanche, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, and Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward. They created the structure, and they are the ones now refusing to close it down in the strongest possible way the court asked for.

That matters. If this were truly dead, swearing to that effect would be easy. The refusal suggests something narrower and more revealing: the administration wants the political benefit of retreat without the legal cost of surrender.

The Familiar Fog

The DOJ’s position is dressed up as separation-of-powers doctrine, but the timing makes the argument look less like constitutional principle than tactical insulation. The department says declarations are unnecessary because it has repeatedly said it will not move forward. Yet it has no problem producing sworn declarations in other fights under Trump, including the Alien Enemies Act deportation litigation, the Epstein Files Transparency Act dispute, and the Kennedy Center battle over Trump’s name.

That contradiction is the story. The department is not defending a neutral rule. It is choosing when sworn truth is convenient and when it becomes an obstacle.

What The Silence Protects

The question raised by Lisa Rubin is the right one: whether the refusal to swear the fund is dead is covering a hope, or even an intent, to keep the door open. The DOJ’s own filing does not erase that suspicion; it invites it. Blanche says the fund is finished. The department says no money has moved in or out. But those are not the same as a binding, sworn commitment that the thing has been buried.

That gap is where political malpractice lives. Officials can issue calming statements while preserving a mechanism for future use. They can tell the public the danger is gone while refusing to nail the door shut.

The Pattern

This is not just a dispute about one fund. It is a pattern of executive power using legal process to create a reserve of favors, then using procedural language to avoid accountability once exposed. The original arrangement was condemned because it looked like a vehicle for patronage. The refusal to swear it dead now functions like an after-the-fact insurance policy for the same kind of corruption.

The larger lesson is simple: in a system already bent toward executive impunity, the real signal is often not what officials proclaim, but what they refuse to attest to under oath.
