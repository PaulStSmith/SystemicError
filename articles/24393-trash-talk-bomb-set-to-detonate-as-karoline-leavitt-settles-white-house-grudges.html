Trash talk bomb set to detonate as Karoline Leavitt settles White House grudges

Based on an article by Jason Miciak

Paulo SantosOctober 3, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedA bomb made of conjectureWhere the power sitsGossip as misdirectionAccountability sold as entertainmentThe systemic error

What happened

RawStory columnist Jason Miciak says former White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt is shopping a book containing behind-the-scenes details from her tenure in the Trump administration. Citing a Page Six report based on unnamed sources, he predicts that Leavitt will settle grudges and expose damaging internal conflict.

The reporting establishes no book deal, publication date, specific revelation, quoted passage, or confirmed target of Leavitt’s alleged grievances. It also provides no evidence that she resigned because she objected to lying or believed the administration was collapsing; Miciak presents those possibilities as speculation.

A bomb made of conjecture

The promised “explosion” exists almost entirely in the columnist’s imagination. Miciak reasons that publishers demand palace intrigue, press secretaries hear sensitive information, and Leavitt must have accumulated resentments. From those premises, he constructs a prospective scandal without identifying its substance.

That is not an account of what Leavitt knows. It is a sales pitch for disclosures she may never make. RawStory’s framing converts an anonymously sourced publishing rumor into suspense while leaving the central question—what she will actually reveal—unanswered.

Where the power sits

The relevant institution is the presidency, and the dominant actor described here is Donald Trump. A White House press secretary possesses access and influence, but serves an administration whose senior officials set policy, control information, and decide what the public is told.

Miciak recognizes that communications staff can be excluded from consequential decisions, sometimes to preserve deniability. But the column then shifts attention from presidential power to Leavitt’s personality, ambitions, competence, marriage, appearance, and imagined payday. The machinery of government becomes scenery for a personality feud.

Gossip as misdirection

The column’s personal contempt is not evidence. References to Leavitt becoming pregnant by “a rich guy,” her “mean-girl disposition,” and her appearance do nothing to establish what happened inside the White House. They replace scrutiny of official conduct with gendered derision.

Leavitt’s record as press secretary can be assessed through her public statements and documented decisions. This article does not do that. Nor does it identify a lie she refused to tell, a cover-up she witnessed, or a mistake she blamed on someone else. It speculates about each, then invites readers to enjoy the anticipated retaliation.

Accountability sold as entertainment

If Leavitt possesses evidence of misconduct, the political question is not whether it can earn a seven-figure advance. It is what officials did, who authorized it, who concealed it, and what consequences followed. None of those questions can be answered from the material presented.

A tell-all may eventually supply evidence. Until then, describing prospective gossip as a detonating “bomb” rewards insiders for withholding information until it becomes commercially valuable. Public service is reduced to access, access to grievance, and grievance to intellectual property.

The systemic error

This is how political coverage launders uncertainty into drama. Powerful institutions disappear behind court intrigue; unnamed sources generate anticipation; an official’s possible disclosures become more important than the conduct those disclosures might document.

The larger pattern is an accountability culture built around spectacle rather than proof. It asks who will betray whom, not who exercised state power and with what result. When reporting cannot yet answer that harder question, the honest conclusion is simple: a book is reportedly being marketed, and the supposed political bomb has not been identified.
